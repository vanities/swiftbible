# James Edgar Green — Voice Analysis

A close read of the one surviving sermon, **"The Day Christ Came"**
([speakers/james-edgar-green/sermons/the-day-christ-came.md](../../../speakers/james-edgar-green/sermons/the-day-christ-came.md)).
Built for one job: writing in Brother Jim's voice, or measuring a draft against it. Every quotation
is from that sermon.

> **Caveat.** This is a portrait drawn from a single text — and an *atypical* one at that: he tells
> us this story-sermon is "somewhat different from the manner in which I usually present lessons."
> So we are studying his style in its narrative mode, which may not be his Sunday-to-Sunday default.
> Hold these conclusions loosely until more recordings surface.

---

## 1. The shape: a single sustained story, not an outline

His ordinary sermons were apparently the points-outline kind (he flags this one as a departure).
Here the *whole* lesson is one continuous narrative with a beginning (a man wakes on an ordinary
Monday), a middle (the Lord returns mid-walk), and an end (judgment, the great white throne, the
invitation). Doctrine is never lectured; it is *dramatized*, then named only at the very end ("not
one thing… is based upon speculation… every word has been based upon a biblical truth").

**For drafting:** prefer narrative on-ramps to thesis statements. Put the reader inside a scene
before you put a verse in front of him. Save the explicit doctrinal claim for after the story has
done its work.

## 2. The engine: the "John Jones machine"

The dread is manufactured by a repeating four-stroke cycle. Spotting it lets you reproduce it:

1. **Ordinary, warm, specific detail** — autumn leaves, a cracked sidewalk, Mrs. Curry's curlers,
   bacon, the newlyweds' ribboned car, the radio news of world peace, the milk truck.
2. **The turn** — the same detail re-read as omen: the leaves are beautiful *because all of nature
   is dying*; the newlyweds are *"as in the days of Noah… married and given in marriage."*
3. **Direct apostrophe** — he breaks the frame to address the character: *"John, why can't you
   understand…?"*
4. **Scripture, KJV, from memory** — the warning is grounded in a text (Matt 24, 1 Thess 5, 2 Pet 3).

The cycle runs maybe five or six times, each turn a little darker, until the literal trumpet sounds
and the omens become the event. **The ordinary is the ammunition.** The more domestic and safe the
detail, the harder the turn lands.

**For drafting:** build dread out of specifics a reader recognizes from his own Monday, not out of
abstractions. Don't write "the world is fragile" — write the sidewalk that has "been here for years
and years… it's not going to burn up," then crack it.

## 3. Direct address — preaching *at* the character so the listener overhears himself

This is the single most distinctive tic. He does not say "we should heed the warnings." He turns and
speaks to John, by name, in the second person, mid-scene:

> *"And John, why can't you understand that this is perhaps an omen?… John, why can't you understand
> that all of nature is dying?"*

> *"These are warnings, John. Why can't you understand them?"*

> *"Why did you act and live as if your Lord had been crucified up on a totem pole instead of the
> cross of Calvary? Why did you wait so long, John?"*

The genius is indirection: the congregation is never scolded directly, so no one's defenses go up —
but everyone in the pew is John. Then, only at the end, he drops the character and turns the same
second person on the room: *"What if God should call you tomorrow morning? Where would you be?"*

**For drafting:** you can borrow this. Address the figure in the story, let the reader identify, then
turn the pronoun on him at the close. Use sparingly — it's a scalpel, not a hammer.

## 4. Repetition as a drumbeat

Lines repeat until they change meaning under your feet:

- *"How good it is to be alive. How good it is to be alive."* — joy, then dramatic irony.
- *"God has been good to him… God has been good to him despite the fact that he has given nothing
  back to God. God has been good to him."* — the third beat indicts.
- *"I must get home. I must get home."* — panic.
- *"All of this is just a story. All of this is just a story."* — relief, set up to be yanked away:
  *"But it will happen to you this morning."*
- The hymn seals it: *"Are you ready? Are you ready? Are you ready for the judgment day?"*

**For drafting:** pick *one* line per piece and let it recur two or three times, shifting weight each
time. Do not stack many different repeated phrases — that reads as ornament. One drumbeat.

## 5. Sentence rhythm and grammar

- **Polysyndeton — the piled "and."** Long chains joined by *and… and… and*, biblical and breathless:
  *"And John Jones is alive, and he is well, and he is happy, and he is grateful to be alive."* This
  is his default narrative gear; it gives the prose a KJV, oral, forward-leaning momentum.
- **Present tense for immediacy.** He slips into present tense at the tense moments — *"the bed is
  empty, unmade. Mary is gone"* — then back to past. The drift into present is a tell that the
  pressure is rising.
- **Short declarative for the hammer.** After a long chain, a stop: *"Nothing was left."* /
  *"When it happened."* / *"Mary is gone."*
- **Triads and fours of verbs at the climax:** *"to grope and to run and to crawl and to cry out and
  to fall, through all eternity."*

**For drafting:** alternate the piled-"and" long sentence with a three-word stop. The contrast is the
rhythm.

## 6. Humility and the limits of language

He opens by giving the credit away and keeping only the blame — *"Whatever good there might be in
this lesson, I owe to brother David Roper. And whatever bad there might be in this lesson, I owe to
myself"* — and by confessing the *"limitations… imposed upon human language,"* wishing he had "a full
command of all the words" so that "there wouldn't be a single person leave here this morning without
being firmly dedicated to the service of God." The bigness of the subject against the smallness of
the speaker is a posture he states plainly.

**For drafting:** a note of this — the preacher who knows the words are too small for the thing — is
true to him and keeps the hard sermon from sounding self-assured.

## 7. Terror named, then disowned

He preaches hell vividly, then immediately tells you why: *"The things I said this morning were not
in order to scare anyone. I know my heart. The things I said… are to cause you to stop and think."*
The fear is real and the love is the point, and he refuses to let the listener mistake one for the
other.

**For drafting:** if a piece in his voice goes to judgment and fire, it should also, near the end,
show its hand: this was love trying to wake you, not a man enjoying the fire.

## 8. The theology carried in the plot

- **The sin that damns is neglect, not scandal.** The verdict is the sermon's thesis: *"not guilty
  of murder, not guilty of adultery, not guilty of drunkenness — but guilty of neglect… guilty of
  thievery, for he had stolen all the grace and the goodness… and had given nothing in return."*
- **"Someday soon" is the mechanism of the neglect.** Bowling Wednesday, fishing Sunday, *"the next
  Sunday, or the Sunday after that."* He is literally mid-procrastination when the trumpet sounds.
- **Readiness is personal and non-transferable.** Mary's lips at the throne: *"we love you so much.
  But we can't help you now."* A Christian wife cannot carry an unbaptized husband across.
- **The destination is the invitation.** Believe, confess, repent, be baptized, be added to the
  body — *"while together we stand and sing the invitation song."*

## Ready-to-use anchors

Short, high-signal excerpts to prime a voice. Use as few-shot fuel; do not republish wholesale.

**The opening humility:**
> *"Whatever good there might be in this lesson, I owe to brother David Roper. And whatever bad there
> might be in this lesson, I owe to myself."*

**The on-ramp:**
> *"I'm going to tell you a story… The names in this story are assumed names, but the characters
> could well represent someone you know. And, alas, they could well represent you, or they could
> represent me."*

**The ordinary-as-omen turn:**
> *"And John, why can't you understand that this is perhaps an omen?… John, why can't you understand
> that all of nature is dying?"*

**The procrastination:**
> *"I can't this Wednesday night, because that's the night me and the fellas go bowling… And maybe
> the next Sunday, or the Sunday after that. And I'm going to obey the Lord. I'm going to show him."*

**The interruption:**
> *"And he was just raising his foot to put it upon the curb when it happened. When it happened."*

**The verdict (the thesis):**
> *"Not guilty of murder, not guilty of adultery, not guilty of drunkenness — but guilty of neglect…
> guilty of thievery, for he had stolen all the grace and the goodness that God had given him… and
> had given nothing in return."*

**The turn on the room:**
> *"Now, all of this is just a story… It hasn't happened to you yet. But it will happen to you this
> morning, just as surely as you sit here."*

## Two deliveries: what he keeps and what he changes

We now have the *same* sermon twice — Greensburg (~1986, YouTube) and Spring Hill Church of Christ
(1998-12-22, a clean FLAC). Comparing them shows his preaching habits better than either recording
alone.

**Verbatim-stable (his fixed script).** The John Jones frame; the names (Mary, Billy, Suzy); the
opening humility (*"whatever good… I owe to brother David Roper… whatever bad… to myself"*); *"how good
it is to be alive"*; the omen beats (dying autumn, the cracked sidewalk, Mrs. Curry's robe, the Ingram
newlyweds); *"raising his foot to the curb when it happened"*; the verdict (*"not guilty of murder…
but guilty of neglect… guilty of thievery… given nothing in return"*); the signature line *"crucified
upon a totem pole instead of the cross of Calvary"*; and the close on "There's a Great Day Coming."
These are the load-bearing beats — treat them as *his*.

**Localized / varied (live, era-specific).**
- **The radio news.** ~1986: a Lebanon ceasefire, Iran–Iraq settling, El Salvador talks. 1998: *"Iran
  and Israel and Palestine and Iraq… negotiated peace talks"* — he swapped in the October 1998 Wye
  River (Israeli–Palestinian) news. He re-skins the *"peace and safety"* beat to whatever the
  congregation last heard on the radio, which is itself the point of the beat (1 Thess 5:3).
- **Props.** The delivery truck is a *milk truck* in one and a *Coca-Cola truck* in the other.
- **The invitation.** The 1998 delivery adds a second appeal aimed at the *wayward Christian* who
  *"wandered back into the beggarly elements of the world"* — extending the altar call to the erring,
  not only the unbaptized.

**For drafting:** treat the stable beats as canon and the topical detail as the dial he turns for the
room. A devotional in his voice keeps the fixed verdict-and-neglect spine and localizes the *"peace
and safety"* news to the reader's own moment.

## How Brother Jim differs from Matt Bassford

They share a theology and a refusal to soften judgment, and either could be quoted on "obey now." But
do not blur them:

| | Brother Jim | Matt Bassford |
|---|---|---|
| **Form** | Sustained narrative; one long story | Short essays; aphorism and argument |
| **Person** | Third-person story + 2nd-person plea to the character | First-person confession ("I hold grudges") |
| **On-ramp** | A scene (a man wakes on a Monday) | A domestic detail (Boomer the chihuahua, a haircut) |
| **Engine** | Ordinary detail → omen → apostrophe → scripture → dread | Inverted aphorism; Bible-geek precision; wry aside |
| **Close** | The altar-call invitation, sung | A verdict-imperative ("Create. Give. Lose.") |
| **Humor** | Gentle, observational (Mrs. Curry's "antennas") | Dry, sardonic, aimed at secular pieties |
| **Default mood** | A pleading pulpit warmth that turns to dread and back | Meditative, mortality-soaked, grace-emphatic |

When a draft wants a *story that ends at a decision*, reach for Brother Jim. When it wants a
*confession that ends at a verdict-line*, reach for Matt.
