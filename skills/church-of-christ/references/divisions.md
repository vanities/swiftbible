# Divisions and branches of the Stone-Campbell movement

A map of the fractures, for a writer who has to know which assumptions belong to which
branch. The audience for this skill writes devotional content in the voice of
**non-institutional** churches of Christ (see
[non-institutional.md](non-institutional.md)); this file exists so that the *other* branches
are described accurately rather than as caricatures.

> **Scope note.** The 1906 separation is treated here for its **doctrinal logic and
> aftermath**. For the nineteenth-century narrative (Campbells, Stone, Fanning, Lipscomb,
> the Civil War, the Sand Creek Address), see the history reference in this skill; this
> file does not repeat it.

## The ground rule for this subject

Every faction in this movement narrates the others uncharitably, and every widely
circulated summary — including the neutral-sounding ones — was written from inside one of
them. Three habits keep the map honest:

1. **State a position the way its own best advocates state it**, then note how opponents
   frame it, and say which is which.
2. **Never use a faction's exonym as if it were a description.** "Anti," "digressive,"
   "liberal," "change agent," "Bollite," "cupsite" are all fighting words somebody else
   coined.
3. **Treat every membership number as an estimate.** There is no central body, no required
   reporting, and the directories are compiled by partisans of one branch. Numbers below
   always carry a source and a year, and they frequently disagree with each other.

## 1. The 1906 separation — Churches of Christ / Disciples of Christ

**When it hardened.** The 1906 U.S. Religious Census listed "Churches of Christ" and
"Disciples of Christ" separately for the first time, after Census director S. N. D. North
wrote to David Lipscomb asking whether a distinct people existed and Lipscomb answered
that there was. Published reports of division go back to at least 1883; 1906 is the
bureaucratic ratification of a separation already complete on the ground
([Restoration Movement, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restoration_Movement);
[Disciples Historical Society](https://discipleshistory.org/wiki/churches-of-christ/)).

**1906 census counts** ([Restoration Movement, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restoration_Movement)):

| | Congregations | Members |
|---|---|---|
| Disciples of Christ | 8,293 (75.8%) | 982,701 (86.0%) |
| Churches of Christ | 2,649 (24.2%) | 159,658 (14.0%) |

**What the dispute was actually about.** Two practices — the instrument in worship and the
missionary society — but underneath them, one hermeneutical question: *what does the
silence of Scripture authorize?*

- **The Churches of Christ argument, as its advocates put it.** Scripture authorizes by
  direct command, approved apostolic example, and necessary inference. Where the New
  Testament describes the worship of the church and never mentions an instrument, and
  never shows a church working through a missionary society, silence is *restrictive*, not
  permissive. The society is a second organization doing the work Christ gave the church;
  the instrument is an addition to a specified act. Both are innovations, and innovation in
  worship and organization is how the apostasy started the first time.
- **The Disciples/Christian Church argument, as its advocates put it.** What Scripture does
  not forbid, Christian liberty permits. The instrument is an *expedient* — an aid to a
  commanded act (singing), no more an addition than a songbook, a pitch pipe, or a church
  building. The society is likewise a method of doing commanded work, not a rival to the
  church. To bind where God has not bound is itself the sectarian sin the movement was
  founded to end.

**Aftermath.** The line ran along social fault lines as much as doctrinal ones. Churches of
Christ were disproportionately rural, Southern, poorer, suspicious of professional clergy;
Disciples disproportionately urban, Northern, college-educated, wealthier. David Edwin
Harrell Jr. established this "sectional" and social reading of the split, and it is now the
scholarly consensus rather than a hostile claim
([H-Net review of Harrell](https://networks.h-net.org/node/15697/reviews/15892/foster-harrell-social-history-disciples-christ-vol-1-quest-christian)).

**Fellowship today.** Churches of Christ and the Christian Church (Disciples of Christ) are
separate bodies with essentially no institutional relationship. Cordial dialogue exists;
shared work does not.

## 2. 1927 / 1968 — Disciples and the Independent Christian Churches

The instrumental wing then divided against itself over exactly the question the a cappella
wing had raised: *may congregations be organized into a denomination?*

- **1920s.** Conservatives within the Disciples organized the **North American Christian
  Convention** as a rallying point independent of the denominational agencies (the
  [Restoration Movement article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restoration_Movement) dates
  its formation to 1926; it is commonly associated with 1927).
- **1968.** The International Convention adopted the *Provisional Design of the Christian
  Church (Disciples of Christ)*. Every congregation in the 1968 yearbook was automatically
  enrolled in the restructured denomination
  ([Disciples Historical Society, "Restructure"](https://discipleshistory.org/wiki/restructure/)).
- **The independents' objection, in their own terms.** A denominational structure with
  regions, a General Assembly, and recognized ministry infringes the freedom and
  sufficiency of the local congregation. They did not leave a denomination; they declined
  to join one.
- **The Disciples' answer, in their own terms.** Restructure only made visible and
  accountable a covenantal life the churches already shared, and it served the ecumenical
  vocation the movement was founded for — unity, not restoration, being the movement's
  first word.
- **Scale.** Congregations listed in the Disciples yearbook fell from **8,046 (1967) to
  5,278 (1969)**. By **1971** the independents were listed separately in the *Yearbook of
  American Churches* as **Christian Churches and Churches of Christ**
  ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Undenominational-Fellowship-of-Christian-Churches-and-Churches-of-Christ)).

**Where the a cappella Churches of Christ stand relative to both.** They regard both as
having departed in 1906 over the instrument, and they do not distinguish sharply between
them on that point. But they are doctrinally much *nearer* the Independent Christian
Churches — believer's immersion for remission of sins, weekly communion, congregational
autonomy, no creed — than the Disciples, whose ecumenism, open membership, and denominational
structure they see as a further departure. Consequently, every serious unity effort since the
1980s has been a *Churches of Christ / Independent Christian Churches* conversation. See
§7 below.

## 3. The premillennial division (R. H. Boll, *Word and Work*, the 1930s)

**The dispute.** Whether Christ will return to reign bodily on earth for a literal
thousand years before the final judgment — and, decisively, whether holding that view is
compatible with fellowship.

**Figures and organs.**

- **R. H. Boll** (German-born) was front-page editor of the *Gospel Advocate* **1909–1915**,
  where he began publishing premillennial expositions; after leaving, he took over
  ***Word and Work*** in 1916 and edited it until 1956, making it "the journalistic voice
  for premillennial Churches of Christ"
  ([Robert Henry Boll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Henry_Boll);
  [Word and Work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_and_Work)). The magazine was founded in
  1908 by D. L. Watson and still publishes ([wordandwork.org/about](https://www.wordandwork.org/about/)).
- **Foy E. Wallace Jr.** was the principal opponent. From the early 1930s he attacked Boll
  in the *Gospel Advocate* and then in the *Bible Banner*, whose first issue complained of
  a "general softness" in the church and blamed "the Bollistic blight." **In 1938, in the
  *Bible Banner*, Wallace declared premillennialists and their sympathizers no longer
  members of Churches of Christ** — the moment eschatology became a fellowship test
  ([Foy E. Wallace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foy_E._Wallace)).

**Each side in its own words.**

- **The mainstream (amillennial) argument.** The kingdom was established on Pentecost and
  *is* Christ's present reign; a future earthly kingdom implies the church was a substitute
  for a kingdom Jesus failed to set up, which impugns the cross and the New Testament
  itself. A doctrine that reorganizes the whole scheme of redemption is not an opinion
  Romans 14 covers.
- **Boll's side, in *Word and Work*'s account.** Christians should "study [the Bible]
  carefully and prayerfully for themselves, without having to agree fully with any church
  or human teacher"; prophecy is part of the whole counsel of God; and disagreement about
  the future is not a test of fellowship. Boll answered issues "rather than personalities"
  and declined to name opponents: *"We call no names unless we can commend"*
  ([Word & Work, "R. H. Boll as a Writer and Editor"](https://www.wordandwork.org/2010/09/r-h-boll-as-a-writer-and-editor-2/)).

**Why eschatology became a fellowship test at all.** Two readings, and a writer should know
both:

- *Non-institutional reading* — the millennial doctrine itself was serious enough, but the
  deeper fracture was over **whether doctrinal error is covered by grace**. Premillennialists
  "framed their position through 'grace' language, suggesting God's grace would cover
  deviations about doctrinal matters such as questions about God's kingdom"
  ([Truth Magazine, "The Impact of Premillennialism on the Church"](https://www.truthmagazine.com/the-impact-of-premillennialism-on-the-church)).
  On that account the millennium was a proxy for the fellowship question.
- *Scholarly reading* — before 1910 moderate historic premillennialism was common and
  uncontroversial among Churches of Christ leaders. What changed was the rise of
  **dispensational** premillennialism among fundamentalists (rapture, restored national
  Israel, Armageddon). Mainstream leaders recoiled from the whole family of ideas, and
  Wallace "pushed premillennial advocates such as Robert Henry Boll into a separate
  fellowship" ([Disciples Historical Society](https://discipleshistory.org/wiki/churches-of-christ/)).

**Size and current status.** Always small. A non-institutional account puts the loss at
"about 100 churches located mainly in KY, IN and LA"
([aubeacon.com, *History of the Institutional Controversy*, lesson 2](https://www.aubeacon.com/Sermons2011/HistoryOfInstitutionalDivisionLesson2of3.pdf) — partisan).
Concentrated in Louisville, Kentucky and its orbit; the Portland Avenue Church of Christ in
Louisville, the movement's historic center and *Word and Work*'s address, has recently
closed ([Word & Work, Portland Avenue history, 2025](https://www.wordandwork.org/2025/03/portland-avenue-church-of-christ-louisville-ky-history/)).
By the late twentieth century premillennial congregations had **asked directories to stop
distinguishing them**, which is itself the clearest signal of how far the heat has gone out
of it ([Disciples Historical Society](https://discipleshistory.org/wiki/churches-of-christ/)).

## 4. One cup and non-Sunday-school (1900s–1930s)

**These are two questions, not one.** Outsiders — and plenty of insiders — say "one-cup
churches" and mean "the ones with no classes and one cup." The overlap is real but partial:
the *Encyclopedia of the Stone-Campbell Movement*'s figures, as reported by
[Disciples Historical Society](https://discipleshistory.org/wiki/churches-of-christ/) and
[Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churches_of_Christ), count roughly **1,100
non-class congregations** against roughly **550 one-cup congregations** — which means most
non-class congregations use individual cups. Keep them separate.

### 4a. The Bible-class (Sunday-school) question

- **Timeline.** The modern Sunday school reached the movement in the nineteenth century and
  was contested from the start; Alexander Campbell objected to the *abuse* of the system
  before endorsing the system itself (Millennial Harbinger, 1847). Texas disputes appear
  from **1875**. Lines of fellowship over the class method were not generally drawn until
  about **1918**.
- **The non-class argument, as advocates put it.** The assembly of the whole church is
  what the New Testament shows; dividing it into age-graded classes with multiple
  simultaneous teachers — including women teaching — is an unauthorized method, and the
  women-teaching problem follows from the arrangement rather than being incidental to it.
  J. T. Showalter, *Gospel Advocate*, 21 April 1910: *"Whenever any man proves the Sunday
  school to be of divine authority, he can prove missionary societies to be of divine
  authority... I emphatically deny that there is any divine authority for Sunday-schools
  either by precept or precedent, hint or allusion."*
- **The mainstream reply.** Teaching is commanded; the arrangement of the teaching is an
  expedient, exactly like meeting times, buildings, and translations. Barton Stone had
  already urged that "a part of the day be devoted to the instruction of our children in the
  scriptures" under appointed teachers (*Christian Messenger*, 1828).
- **Key figure.** J. Ervin Waters credited **N. L. Clark** as "the father of the non-class
  movement in Texas," while noting pointedly that Clark "never made it a test of
  fellowship" and co-edited the *Firm Foundation* with G. H. P. Showalter, whom he debated
  on the subject; others "began to draw lines and create parties" (*Restoration Review* XII,
  March 1971, p. 39).

### 4b. The cup question

- **Timeline.** The individual-communion-cup set was invented in **1893** by J. G. Thomas, a
  minister-physician, and first used at a Congregational church in Ohio. It entered Churches
  of Christ in the 1900s–1910s: J. W. McGarvey opposed it (February 1910), C. E. Holt argued
  for it on sanitary grounds in the *Gospel Advocate* (11 June 1911), and **G. C. Brewer**
  introduced it at the Central Church of Christ in Chattanooga, Tennessee, in **1915**.
  Over three decades it became dominant
  ([Ronny F. Wade, "History of Individual Communion Cups"](https://fossilcreekchurchofchrist.org/history-of-individual-communion-cups-by-ronny-f-wade/) — partisan, one-cup).
- **The one-cup argument, as advocates put it.** *"Either there is a pattern for the
  observance of the communion or there is none"* (Ronny F. Wade, arguing from Heb. 8:5).
  Jesus took *a* cup and said "drink ye all of it"; the one loaf and the one cup jointly
  signify the one body (1 Cor. 10:16-17). Multiplying containers is not an expedient of a
  commanded act — it changes the act.
- **The mainstream reply.** "The cup" is a metonym for its contents ("this cup is the new
  covenant in my blood"); the container is not the ordinance. Distribution is an expedient.

### 4c. Organs, and the internal fracturing

**Papers.** The *Apostolic Way* (1913–1930, Clarence Teurman publisher from 1916, later
R. F. Duckworth) carried both objections; when Duckworth's views shifted, **H. C. Harper**
began ***The Truth*** in **1928**, joined by **J. D. ("Doug") Phillips** and, from 1930,
**Homer L. King**. In **1931** it was renamed the ***Old Paths Advocate***, with
**Homer A. Gay** added to the masthead. It is still published and is what "the old-timers
call 'the Standard'" (Leroy Garrett). Later editors include Don McCord and Don King
(Lebanon, MO); Ronny F. Wade (1936–2020) was assistant publisher. Other one-cup papers
include the *Christian's Expositor* and *The Light*.

**Size.** Leroy Garrett (*The Stone-Campbell Movement*, rev. ed. 1994, p. 437) put the main
one-cup group at "as many as 30,000 members in upwards of 530 churches, most of which have
30–60 members," and described the cluster as "fractured five ways." Clovis T. Cook, writing
*in* the *Old Paths Advocate* (May 1996), gave "approximately five hundred congregations in
the United States." The ESCM figure of ~550 is consistent with both.

**Internal fracturing.** The one-cup churches divide further, and their own papers document
it: over whether the loaf is broken or pinched, whether the fruit of the vine is wine or
grape juice, whether Matthew 19:9's exception clause applies to Christians, and — most
consequentially — over whom one may fellowship. These lines are drawn between congregations
that otherwise agree on cup and class. The taxonomy above is assembled by a **critic** of
the movement, though from quotations of its own periodicals: see
[*A Brief History of the One-Cup and Non-Sunday School Movement*](https://freedominchrist.net/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/PAGE-11-Chapter-2-A-Brief-Hisotry-One-Cup-JC-4-08-08.doc.pdf)
(hostile in tone; useful for its primary citations).

**Fellowship posture.** These congregations generally do not extend fellowship to churches
using classes or cups, and are generally not in fellowship with mainstream or
non-institutional churches either. They separated well before the 1950s division and are
**almost always also non-institutional** on the treasury question, which is a frequent
source of confusion — see the note in §8.

### 4d. Mutual edification churches

A smaller cluster (**c. 130 congregations**, ESCM figures via
[Disciples Historical Society](https://discipleshistory.org/wiki/churches-of-christ/)) that
opposes the located, salaried preacher in favor of teaching shared among the men of the
congregation. Also almost always non-institutional. The national directory has historically
coded them separately
([Christian Chronicle, 6 Dec 2011](https://christianchronicle.org/historically-a-cappella-churches-with-instrumental-services-returned-to-national-directory/)).

## 5. The institutional division (1938–1960s) — the central one for this repo

**The question.** Not *whether* to do benevolence and evangelism, but **through what
arrangement the local church's treasury may do them.**

### Timeline

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| 1909–1928 | Church-of-Christ-associated orphan asylums begin: Tennessee Orphan Home (1909), Potter (KY, 1914), Boles (TX, 1927), Tipton (OK, 1928) |
| 1931, 1933 | G. C. Brewer argues at the ACC Lectures and in the *Gospel Advocate* for church support of the colleges |
| 1938 | Brewer's remarks at the ACC Lectures — widely heard as saying a church without Abilene Christian College in its budget had the wrong preacher — ignite the controversy; Foy E. Wallace Jr. and W. W. Otey publish rebuttals |
| 1939 | B. C. Goodpasture becomes editor of the *Gospel Advocate* |
| 1947 | N. B. Hardeman and others revive the push in the *Firm Foundation* |
| 1949 | Wallace closes the *Bible Banner* and revives the ***Gospel Guardian***, installing **Fanning Yater Tant** as editor (he served 22 years) |
| 1951 | Fourth and Groesbeck church, Lufkin, TX splits (Roy Cogdill / Cled Wallace) — an early epicenter |
| 1951–52 | ***Herald of Truth*** launched by the Fifth and Highland church, Abilene, TX (sources give 1951 and 1952) |
| 1954 | G. H. P. Showalter dies; **Reuel Lemmons** takes the *Firm Foundation* and turns it institutional. In December, Goodpasture publishes with approval an anonymous elder's letter calling for a **"quarantine of the 'antis'"** — the "yellow tag of quarantine" |
| 1954–59 | Formal debates across the country |
| late 1960s | Contact between the two groups essentially ends |

Sources for the timeline: [Churches of Christ (non-institutional), Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churches_of_Christ_(non-institutional));
[La Vista church of Christ, "The History of the Institutional Controversy"](https://www.lavistachurchofchrist.org/cms/the-history-of-the-institutional-controversy/) (non-institutional);
[aubeacon.com lessons 2 and 3](https://www.aubeacon.com/Sermons2011/HistoryOfInstitutionalDivisionLesson2of3.pdf) (non-institutional).

### The four disputed practices

1. **Church treasury support of human institutions** — orphan homes and homes for the aged
   organized as separate corporations with their own boards; and Christian colleges.
2. **The sponsoring-church arrangement** — many congregations sending funds to one
   eldership, which then oversees a work larger than itself. *Herald of Truth* (radio, later
   television) is the emblem: at its height "over 1,000 churches sending money" to a single
   eldership.
3. **Church-supported social and recreational activity** — fellowship halls, kitchens,
   church-provided meals, gymnasiums, "family life centers," camps, recreation programs.
4. **General benevolence** — church funds relieving the world's poor rather than needy
   saints.

### The institutional argument, as its own advocates state it

- **The parallel argument.** N. B. Hardeman, *Firm Foundation*, 1947, on the colleges and
  the orphan homes: **"The same principle that permits one must also permit the other. They
  must stand or fall together."** If a church may send money to a school that teaches the
  Bible, it may send money to a home that shelters children; if it may do neither, then the
  brotherhood has been inconsistent for fifty years.
- **The expediency argument.** The church is commanded to teach and to do good. The *method*
  is unspecified and therefore free. A children's home is a method of caring for children,
  as a church building is a method of assembling and a radio network a method of preaching.
  Congregations pooling funds under one eldership violates no autonomy, because no
  congregation is compelled and the overseeing eldership oversees only its own work.
- **The scale argument.** Some good works — worldwide radio, post-war evangelism in Germany
  and Japan, a licensed child-placing agency — cannot be done by a single small congregation
  at all. To forbid cooperation is to forbid the work.
- **The James 1:27 argument.** "Pure religion... is to visit orphans and widows in their
  affliction." That is laid on the church, not merely on individuals.
- **A caution from inside.** Guy N. Woods, at the ACC Lectures in 1939 — *before* he became
  a leading institutional defender and editor of the *Gospel Advocate* — warned: *"The ship
  of Zion has floundered more than once on the sandbar of institutionalism... All such
  organizations usurp the work of the church, and are unnecessary and sinful."* (quoted by
  [aubeacon lesson 2](https://www.aubeacon.com/Sermons2011/HistoryOfInstitutionalDivisionLesson2of3.pdf);
  the irony is the non-institutional writer's point, and it is a fair one.)

### The non-institutional argument, as its own advocates state it

- **Authority.** God revealed a pattern for the work and worship of the church. Generic
  commands allow expedients; specific commands restrict. The question is never "is it
  useful?" but "is it authorized?" — *"It's not enough for us to hypothesize that some
  practice may possibly be authorized... Possibility and proof are far from the same thing"*
  (Matt Bassford, ["A New-to-Me Argument for Church Cooperation," 14 Nov 2017](https://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2017/11/a-new-to-me-argument-for-church.html)).
- **Organization.** *"There is no authority in Scripture for human organizations or
  super-church arrangements through which local churches may do their work"* (La Vista).
  The local church is itself the only organization God gave for doing the church's work; a
  separate corporation with a board is a second one.
- **The universal-church distinction.** Local churches have organization; the church
  universal does not. Any arrangement that gives one eldership oversight of a
  brotherhood-wide work creates a structure the New Testament does not have — which is the
  missionary-society argument of 1849 applied consistently.
- **The individual/church distinction.** Galatians 6:10 ("do good to all men") and James 1:27
  are addressed to individual Christians; 1 Timothy 5:16 ("let not the church be burdened")
  explicitly distinguishes what a believer must do from what the church must do. So general
  benevolence, hospitality, recreation, and social life are *individual* obligations, and
  offloading them onto the treasury is a way of not doing them.
- **The nature of the church.** *"The church Jesus died to purchase is a spiritual
  institution and was not intended to provide for the recreational or social needs of its
  members, nor to be a worldwide benevolence organization"* (La Vista).
- **The pattern of support for preachers.** Philippians 4:15-17 and 2 Corinthians 11:8-9 show
  churches sending **directly** to the evangelist, not through an intermediary eldership.

### The quarantine, and what it did

Goodpasture's December 1954 endorsement of the "quarantine of the 'antis'" converted a
doctrinal dispute into a social one. Non-institutional accounts report preachers fired and
meetings cancelled, employment conditioned on public renunciation, congregations pressured
to put a token sum in the budget for an institutional cause so as not to be tagged, want-ads
reading "No anti need apply," business boycotts, lawsuits over buildings. Harrell's line is
the best one-sentence account of the difference in method between the two chief antagonists:
**"Foy Wallace scorched heretics; Goodpasture warned them they would lose their position
within the brotherhood."**

Non-institutional writers also fault Goodpasture for closing the *Gospel Advocate*'s pages
to the discussion, and argue — this is worth registering, because it is a serious internal
critique rather than a triumphal one — that this suppression is partly responsible for the
mainstream's later drift, since the arguments were never actually answered
([La Vista](https://www.lavistachurchofchrist.org/cms/the-history-of-the-institutional-controversy/)).

### How large, and what a mainstream historian said at the time

Bill Humble, an institutional historian, in *Story of the Restoration* (1968), p. 74:
*"The most serious issue that churches of Christ have faced in this century is church
cooperation and 'institutionalism'... This is the most serious division, numbers-wise, that
churches of Christ have suffered."*

**Fellowship today.** Effectively none at the congregational level: separate directories,
separate lectureships, separate preacher rosters, separate colleges. Relations have
softened from the 1950s. Florida College's president told the *Christian Chronicle* in 2008
that "time has helped heal the bitterness and hard feelings," and its lectureship director
said, *"We're far enough from the personal acrimony … that we can at least treat each other
cordially"*
([Christian Chronicle, 2008](https://christianchronicle.org/a-non-institutional-institution/)).
Wikipedia reports a **15 November 2019** dialogue between Doug Burleson (mainstream) and
Kyle Pope (non-institutional) under Freed-Hardeman auspices as the first serious unity
conversation since the 1950s, with no agreement reached.

## 6. Crossroads / Boston / the International Churches of Christ

A genuinely separate body today — not a wing of the Churches of Christ, and not to be
confused with them in a devotional context.

- **Origin.** **Chuck Lucas** began "Campus Advance" in **1967** at the 14th Street Church of
  Christ in Gainesville, Florida (later **Crossroads**), built on intensive discipling —
  "soul talks" and "prayer partners." **Kip McKean**, baptized by Lucas, took the model to
  the Lexington (later **Boston**) Church of Christ in **1979** and grew it from 30 to 3,000
  in little over a decade — the "Boston Movement."
- **The discipling controversy.** Every member was paired with a more mature "discipling
  partner" with authority over life decisions, and critics alleged the use of public shaming.
  A 1985 Myers-Briggs study by **Flavil Yeakley** found Boston members' personality scores
  converging toward a group norm — the single most-cited piece of evidence against the
  system.
- **Separation.** Tensions ran through the 1980s; the movement **formally separated from
  mainstream Churches of Christ in 1993** (c. 103 congregations, c. 50,000 Sunday attendance
  in 1992) and was thereafter counted as an independent body.
- **2001–2003 crisis.** McKean took a sabbatical in November 2001 and resigned in November
  2002, acknowledging "arrogance, anger and an over-focus on numerical goals." In **February
  2003** London leader **Henry Kriete** circulated a 39-page open letter alleging financial
  mismanagement, legalism, dishonest statistical reporting and abusive teaching; 42 Los
  Angeles ministry leaders issued a similar apology within weeks. The **2003 restructuring**
  moved toward congregational autonomy, unpaid local elders and deacons, sharply reduced
  full-time staff, and consensus rather than a single central leader
  ([Christian Research Institute](https://www.equip.org/articles/icoc-international-churches-of-christ/);
  [Christianity Today, June 2003](https://www.christianitytoday.com/2003/06/boston-movement-apologizes/);
  [WRSP](https://wrldrels.org/2016/10/08/international-churches-of-christ/)).
- **McKean afterward.** Rebuffed in an attempt to reassert leadership, he was disfellowshipped
  in 2006 and founded the **International Christian Church** (ICC, the "SoldOut" movement) —
  a *third* body, distinct from both the ICOC and the Churches of Christ.
- **Size.** Reported peak **135,000 (2002)**; **89,000 (2006)**; **97,800 in 610 churches
  (2012)**; **112,000 across 144 nations (March 2024)**
  ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Churches_of_Christ)). The ICOC's
  own site currently claims **107,000 members in 767 congregations in 148 countries** and
  describes itself as "a family of congregations" with "no overall legal entity," tracing
  itself to "the Campus Evangelism movement of the Churches of Christ as lived out in the
  Crossroads Church of Christ in Florida in the 1970s"
  ([disciplestoday.org](https://disciplestoday.org/about/), retrieved August 2026).

## 7. Late-20th- and 21st-century fault lines *within* the mainstream

These run inside the institutional/mainstream body. Non-institutional churches watch them
closely and read them as the predicted downstream of 1955 — a reading a writer should
recognize as an argument, not a fact.

- **The grace debate.** K. C. Moser (1893–1976) and G. C. Brewer (1884–1956) pressed a
  grace-centered soteriology that was not widely accepted until the 1980s
  ([Disciples Historical Society](https://discipleshistory.org/wiki/churches-of-christ/)).
- **The hermeneutics debate (1980s).** Traditionalists arguing from "command, example, and
  necessary inference" were challenged by advocates of a reading centered on the character
  of God and the narrative of Scripture. Opponents named it "the new hermeneutic." This is
  the deepest of the fault lines: nearly every other item on this list follows from it.
- **The 1986 "Expression of Concern."** Hundreds of mainstream preachers signed a statement
  aimed chiefly at theistic evolution at Abilene Christian University but also naming
  "diluted and polluted preaching," efforts "to restructure the organization, worship and
  work of the church along sectarian lines," fellowship with "those in blatant religious
  error," and "the typical emphasis of the denominational world on recreation, entertainment,
  and solving the social ills of society" (quoted in
  [aubeacon lesson 3](https://www.aubeacon.com/Sermons2011/HistoryOfInstitutionalDivisionLesson3of3.pdf)).
- **"Change agents."** A pejorative coined for ministers and writers pressing renewal —
  Rubel Shelly (Woodmont Hills, Nashville) and the *New Wineskins* magazine are the usual
  examples. The label is contested; the people it names do not use it of themselves.
- **Worship style and praise teams.** Microphone-led worship teams spread in the 1990s and
  are rejected by many congregations as an unauthorized performance model — an argument that
  parallels, in miniature, the instrument debate.
- **Instrumental music.** In January **2007** the **Richland Hills** church in Texas —
  6,400 members, the largest a cappella Church of Christ — added an instrumental assembly
  with communion on Saturday nights. Minister Rick Atchley: *"We must become a both/and
  church with regard to instrumental and a cappella"* and *"We've already lost too many over
  a question that's way too unimportant."* Two of seventeen elders resigned
  ([Christian Chronicle](https://christianchronicle.org/nations-largest-church-of-christ-adding-instrumental-service/)).
  The directory fight followed: in 2009 the publisher dropped 21 instrumental congregations;
  by 2012 it reinstated them and reworded its scope to "**historically** a cappella"
  ([Christian Chronicle, 6 Dec 2011](https://christianchronicle.org/historically-a-cappella-churches-with-instrumental-services-returned-to-national-directory/)).
- **The role of women.** Expansion through the 1990s into teaching and program leadership;
  then, at the margin, ordination. **Culver Palms** church in Los Angeles appointed two
  women elders in **2018**
  ([Christian Chronicle](https://christianchronicle.org/journey-church-female-elders/)).
  Most mainstream congregations do not, and the question is a live internal division.
- **Divorce and remarriage** and **the indwelling of the Holy Spirit** (personal indwelling
  vs. indwelling through the word) are perennial and cut across both mainstream and
  non-institutional churches; neither maps to the institutional line.
- **Unity with the Christian Churches.** Restoration Forums brought a cappella and
  instrumental heirs together annually; the 25th and final one drew about 1,100 and produced
  a "Pact for Peace" signed by more than 500. A Freed-Hardeman forum on **14 October 2006**
  put Ralph Gilmore (FHU) opposite David Faust (Cincinnati Christian University) before more
  than 1,500 — cordial, unresolved. Gilmore: *"There can be no genuine unity without truth."*
  Faust likened the division to "two lifeguards who get in a fistfight on the beach while a
  swimmer is drowning"
  ([Christian Chronicle](https://christianchronicle.org/unity-discussion-takes-center-stage-at-freed-hardeman/)).
- **Bible classes and fellowship halls.** Universal in the mainstream and entirely
  uncontroversial there. This matters for a writer: a fellowship hall, a church-run coffee
  bar, a family life center, a church-funded youth trip, and a church-supported university
  are all ordinary mainstream furniture and all unavailable in a non-institutional voice.
- **Decline.** Christian Standard, summarizing *Christian Chronicle* data in 2021, reported
  Churches of Christ losing "more than 2,000 people and nine congregations a month since
  2015" ([Christian Standard](https://christianstandard.com/2021/10/noninstrumental-churches-of-christ-facing-uncertain-future/)).

**Not a division:** the historically **Black Churches of Christ** (c. 1,240 congregations,
c. 172,000 members, per [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churches_of_Christ)) are a
distinct stream shaped by segregation, not a doctrinal party, and they span the same
doctrinal positions as everyone else. Do not list them as a branch.

## 9. Commonly gotten wrong

1. **"Non-institutional means anti-orphan."** It does not, and this is the single most
   frequent error. The objection is to the **church treasury funding a separate human
   institution**, not to caring for orphans — which they insist is an individual Christian
   obligation, pressed hard. See [non-institutional.md](non-institutional.md).
2. **"'Anti' is just shorthand."** It is a fighting word coined by opponents in the 1950s
   and is regarded as pejorative. So is "non-cooperative." Use "non-institutional."
3. **"They're the ones who don't use instruments."** No — *all* Churches of Christ in this
   family are a cappella. The institutional divide is about money and organization.
4. **Conflating one-cup with non-class.** Two questions; the non-class group is roughly twice
   the size of the one-cup group, which means most non-class churches use individual cups.
5. **Conflating one-cup/non-class with non-institutional.** One-cup and non-class churches
   are almost always non-institutional *too*, but they separated decades earlier and over
   different questions, and non-institutional churches are not in fellowship with them.
   Jackson Heights, for instance, has Bible classes for all ages.
6. **"Church of Christ" = the ICOC.** A common outside error, and an insulting one to both
   groups. The ICOC has been a separate body since 1993.
7. **"Conservative" and "liberal" are neutral descriptors.** Inside the non-institutional
   churches, "conservative" means non-institutional and "liberal" means mainstream. Inside
   mainstream churches, the same two words describe an entirely different internal spectrum.
   Every use of them is ambiguous; prefer institutional / non-institutional.
8. **"The 1906 division was about the instrument."** The instrument was the presenting
   issue; the missionary society was equally central, and the real question was whether the
   silence of Scripture permits or restricts.
9. **"Premillennialism was a fringe eccentricity."** Moderate historic premillennialism was
   respectable among Churches of Christ leaders before 1910. What made it a fellowship test
   was a combination of dispensationalism's rise and Wallace's 1938 declaration.
10. **Treating directory numbers as census data.** They are voluntary, partisan-compiled,
    and disputed — including over who belongs in them at all, as the 2009–2012 instrumental
    listing fight shows.
11. **Assuming the mainstream is monolithic.** In 2026 a mainstream congregation may have a
    praise team, a Saturday instrumental service, and women reading Scripture — or none of
    those, and a preacher who signed something like the 1986 Expression of Concern.
