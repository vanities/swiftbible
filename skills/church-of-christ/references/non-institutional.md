# Non-institutional churches of Christ

This is the branch **Matt Bassford** and **Josh Tolbert** belong to, and the branch the
Jackson Heights church of Christ in Columbia, Tennessee belongs to. If you are drafting
devotional content in either man's voice, this file is the one that keeps mainstream
assumptions out of it. For where this branch sits on the wider map, see
[divisions.md](divisions.md).

Matt named the tradition himself, and named it carefully:

> "All my life, I've been associated with (for lack of a better descriptor) the
> non-institutional churches of Christ. That was the way my parents taught me when I was
> young, and I've adopted it for myself as an adult."
> — ["Caring for Orphans," 12 Nov 2015](https://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2015/11/caring-for-orphans.html)

"For lack of a better descriptor" is the right note to strike. Nobody in this fellowship
loves the name.

---

## 1. What to call them

| Term | Status |
|---|---|
| **non-institutional** | The standard, least-objectionable term. Use this. |
| **non-institution churches of Christ** | Variant of the same; also fine. |
| **conservative churches of Christ** | Their most common **self**-description — but ambiguous, because mainstream churches use "conservative" for a party *inside* the mainstream. Fine inside the family, confusing outside it. |
| **churches of Christ** (unqualified) | What they actually call themselves most of the time. They do not think of themselves as a branch; they think of themselves as churches of Christ, full stop. |
| **"anti" / "antis"** | **Pejorative. Do not use.** |
| **"non-cooperative"** | Also considered pejorative. |
| **"liberal"** | What they call mainstream churches. Loaded in the other direction; avoid in neutral prose. |

**Why "anti" is a slur and not shorthand.** It was coined by opponents in the 1950s and
1960s and it was designed to complete a sentence: *anti-orphan, anti-mission, anti-cooperation.*
Wikipedia records that "some consider pejorative the epithet 'anti' with which they have
been called by some in the usually larger mainstream Churches of Christ since the 1950s and
1960s"
([Churches of Christ (non-institutional)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churches_of_Christ_(non-institutional))).
Congregational statements say the same directly: the label falsely characterized them as
"anti-orphan, anti-mission work" and unwilling to help the needy
([Timberland Drive church of Christ](https://www.timberlandchurch.org/articles/what-is-a-non-institutional-church);
[Westside church of Christ](https://www.justchristians.com/resources/articles/2014/07/04/what-is-a-non-institutional-church)).
It is a word that carries the accusation inside itself, which is why it survived.

Note also what these congregations say when asked what they prefer: several answer that they
prefer simply to be called **Christians**, and that all the other labels are conveniences.

## 2. The commitment, in one paragraph

The local congregation is the only organization God authorized for doing the church's work.
Its treasury is for **evangelism, edification of its members, and relief of needy saints** —
and for nothing else. Anything larger than a local church, or other than a local church, that
handles the Lord's money to do the Lord's work is a second organization the New Testament
does not have. Everything else in this file follows from that.

Not an abstraction: it is what the Jackson Heights congregation tells anyone who wants to
give. Funds "will be used to **assist needy saints and further the spread of the gospel**,"
given weekly "in keeping with 1 Corinthians 16:1-2"
([thebibleway.org/give](https://thebibleway.org/give/), retrieved August 2026). That sentence
is the whole doctrine, stated without the label.

## 3. What they decline, and why — stated as they state it

### 3a. Church support of human institutions

**The practice declined:** a congregation's treasury sending money to an orphan home, a home
for the aged, a hospital, a mission society, or a Bible college — any organization with its
own charter and its own board.

**The argument.** The church itself is God's benevolent and evangelistic organization. To
build a second one and fund it from the treasury is to duplicate what Christ established,
and there is no New Testament instance of a church sending money to anything other than
another church or directly to a preacher. Guy N. Woods, before he became the *Gospel
Advocate*'s editor, put the principle in the form non-institutional preachers still quote:
"All such organizations usurp the work of the church, and are unnecessary and sinful"
(ACC Lectures, 1939, as quoted by
[aubeacon.com](https://www.aubeacon.com/Sermons2011/HistoryOfInstitutionalDivisionLesson2of3.pdf)).

**On colleges specifically.** They are not against Christian colleges; they are against
churches funding them. The distinction is load-bearing and is embodied in Florida College,
which "does not accept funds from churches" — see §5.

### 3b. The sponsoring-church arrangement

**The practice declined:** many congregations pooling funds under one eldership, which then
oversees a work larger than its own congregation (*Herald of Truth* being the emblem).

**The argument.** An eldership's oversight is bounded by its own flock (1 Pet. 5:2, "the
flock among you"). An eldership overseeing a brotherhood-wide work is exercising authority
God did not give it, and the churches sending money have surrendered work God gave *them*.
The New Testament pattern for preacher support is **direct**: Philippians 4:15-17 and
2 Corinthians 11:8-9 show churches sending to Paul, plural churches to one man, never
several churches through a clearing-house church.

Matt Bassford worked through the strongest institutional counter-reading of exactly those
two texts — Glenn Ramsey's argument that Philippi *was* a first-century sponsoring church —
and his conclusion is the cleanest short statement of the hermeneutic:

> "There's a reason why we speak of 'establishing' authority from Scripture. It's not enough
> for us to hypothesize that some practice may possibly be authorized... Possibility and
> proof are far from the same thing."
> — ["A New-to-Me Argument for Church Cooperation," 14 Nov 2017](https://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2017/11/a-new-to-me-argument-for-church.html)

Note also *how* he got there: an elder handed him a book of paired essays (*Pursuing the
Pattern*, ed. Jim Deason) and urged him to read the **institutional** essays, "correctly"
observing "that the only way to truly understand a position is to read defenses of that
position by those who believe in it. It's awfully easy for non-institutional Christians to
set up strawmen and knock them down." That instinct is part of the tradition at its best and
is worth imitating in anything written for this audience.

### 3c. General benevolence from the treasury (saints vs. the world)

This is the one outsiders get wrong most often, so state it precisely. **The church's
treasury relieves needy saints. Individual Christians relieve everyone.**

The texts, and the arguments made on them:

- **1 Timothy 5:16** — *"let not the church be burdened, that it may relieve them that are
  widows indeed."* The verse itself distinguishes what a believing family must do from what
  the church may do. If Scripture draws the line, the line is real; and it is drawn to keep
  the church from a burden it was not given.
- **Galatians 6:10** — *"do good unto all men, especially unto them who are of the household
  of faith."* Addressed to individual Christians ("as we have therefore opportunity"), and
  therefore the individual's charter for open-handed help to anybody. This is not a verse
  they minimize; it is the verse they say the church-treasury argument has caused brethren
  to under-preach.
- **2 Corinthians 8–9** — the great collection, which is the New Testament's one extended
  account of churches sending relief money. It went **from churches to churches, for
  saints** (8:4, "the ministering to the saints"; 9:1, "the ministering to the saints"),
  carried by messengers to Jerusalem, not through any standing agency. Non-institutional
  readers take 9:13's "your liberal distribution unto them, and unto **all men**" as "all
  the saints," on the ground that the whole context is the relief of saints.
- **James 1:27** — "pure religion... to visit the fatherless and widows in their affliction."
  Institutional brethren read this as a charge to the church. Non-institutional brethren read
  it as a charge to the individual Christian ("pure religion **before God and the Father**"
  describing a person's religion), and — see §4 — many of them will tell you their own side
  has preached this verse mostly as a negative.
- **Acts 11:27-30; Romans 15:25-27; 1 Corinthians 16:1-3** — every apostolic example of one
  church sending funds to another is relief of *saints*, sent directly.

### 3d. Church-funded social and recreational activity

**The practice declined:** fellowship halls, kitchens, church-provided common meals,
gymnasiums, "family life centers," church-run recreation and entertainment programs, church
camps funded from the treasury.

**The argument.** 1 Corinthians 11:22, 34 — "have ye not houses to eat and to drink in?...
if any man hunger, let him eat at home" — separates the assembly from the common meal. The
church is a spiritual institution; hospitality, friendship, food, and recreation are real
Christian goods and belong to *homes and individuals*, which is where they do the most
spiritual work anyway. Congregations state it plainly: social and recreational activities
"should be provided by individuals"
([Timberland Drive](https://www.timberlandchurch.org/articles/what-is-a-non-institutional-church)).

**Practical consequence for a writer:** a non-institutional congregation's building typically
has an auditorium and classrooms and not much else. There is no fellowship hall, no coffee
bar, no gym, no youth ministry budget line, no church-organized ski trip. Hospitality happens
in members' houses — abundantly, and that is the point.

## 4. What the objection is **not** — and the internal critique

**Not against orphans.** Not against caring for them; against the *treasury funding a
corporation* that does. Non-institutional writers press the individual obligation hard, and
have receipts: a survey of 60 preachers who opposed church support of benevolent institutions
turned up 450 orphans and widows being cared for personally; 17 children adopted or fostered
among 25 Florida College faculty families; extrapolated by that source to "nearly 19,000
orphans and/or widows cared for by Christians in conservative churches"
([aubeacon lesson 3](https://www.aubeacon.com/Sermons2011/HistoryOfInstitutionalDivisionLesson3of3.pdf) —
a partisan estimate, and an extrapolation, but the practice it describes is real).

**Not against missions.** They fund preachers directly rather than through agencies. Jackson
Heights' Outreach page names individual men in Missouri, Dominica, and Mozambique — eight
Mozambican evangelists by name — not a mission society
([thebibleway.org/outreach](https://thebibleway.org/outreach/)).

**Not against Bible classes.** Most non-institutional congregations have graded Bible classes
for all ages. Jackson Heights runs classes Sunday morning and Wednesday night. (The non-class
churches are a *different* group; see [divisions.md §4](divisions.md).)

**Not against cooperation.** Churches may and do cooperate — several supporting the same
preacher, several holding a joint gospel meeting — as long as no church surrenders oversight
of its own work and no funds are pooled under a single eldership. As one congregational
statement puts it, "churches could cooperate without pooling their resources through a
sponsoring church"
([Westside](https://www.justchristians.com/resources/articles/2014/07/04/what-is-a-non-institutional-church)).

**And the internal critique, which you should know because Matt wrote it twice.** The best
minds in this tradition say the individual/church distinction has produced an unintended
casualty: the individual half quietly stops being preached.

> "I say this even though I believe that the basic argument being made (that churches should
> not be involved in caring for the world's orphans) is valid... However, all too often, the
> idea that individuals *should* be doing this is relegated to a rhetorical footnote. As long
> as we keep the church out of the orphanage, that's the important thing! No; it's not the
> important thing."
> — ["Caring for Orphans," 2015](https://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2015/11/caring-for-orphans.html)

> "I learned that James 1:27 does not authorize the church to act, but I heard much less
> about what it meant for my actions... No, general benevolence is not a work of the church,
> but it has to be our work as individuals."
> — ["You—Yes, You!—Are Responsible for Caring for the Needy," 1 Oct 2021](https://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2021/10/you-yes-you-are-responsible-for-caring.html)

This is the single most useful thing in this file for devotional writing. **The doctrine is a
restriction on the church and an expansion of the individual's duty.** A devotional that
lands on "so give generously and get personally involved with the poor, yourself, this week"
is squarely inside the tradition — and is what its own preachers say it under-preaches.

## 5. Their institutions and habits

Naturally, a fellowship that objects to institutions has few. The ones it has are
deliberately **not** church-supported, and that is the whole design.

- **Florida College** (Temple Terrace, FL; founded **1946**). About 500 students, roughly
  **90 percent** from non-institutional churches. It is not legally or financially connected
  to any church or group of churches, **does not accept funds from churches**, and does not
  send groups to perform at congregations
  ([Christian Chronicle, 2008](https://christianchronicle.org/a-non-institutional-institution/)).
  It is the nearest thing to a shared institution — and its funding model is the doctrine
  made visible. Its **annual lectures** are the fellowship's largest gathering.
- **Preacher training happens in congregations, not seminaries.** Local churches run their
  own programs. Jackson Heights advertises one on its own site
  ([Preacher Training Program](https://thebibleway.org/contact/preacher-training-program/));
  Matt Bassford was trained at the Dowlen Rd. church of Christ in Beaumont, Texas in the
  mid-2000s (see the [matt-bassford skill](../../matt-bassford/references/biography.md)).
  There is no ordination, no credentialing body, no ministerial association.
- **Periodical culture is the connective tissue.** With no conventions, agencies, or
  headquarters, the papers *are* the brotherhood-wide conversation — which is why the
  1950s division was fought in them and why editorships mattered so much.
  - ***Truth Magazine*** (1956– ) — the principal surviving journal. Editors: Bryan Vinson Jr.,
    Cecil Willis (1962–77), Mike Willis (to 2016), Mark Mayberry (2017– ); published by
    Truth Publications, Inc. It describes itself as "a group of Christians aspiring to teach
    the word of God, to inform brethren regarding the Lord's work in various parts of the
    country, and to strengthen and encourage Christians"
    ([truthmagazine.com/about-us](https://www.truthmagazine.com/about-us/)).
  - ***Gospel Guardian*** (revived 1949 by Foy E. Wallace Jr.; Fanning Yater Tant editor for
    22 years) — the paper of the division itself.
  - ***Searching the Scriptures*** — Connie W. Adams edited it before becoming *Truth
    Magazine*'s associate editor.
  - ***Preceptor***, ***Christianity Magazine***, ***Focus Magazine*** — also non-institutional
    journals. (*Christianity Magazine*'s run and editorial roster, and the relationship of
    *Guardian of Truth* to *Truth Magazine* and the *Gospel Guardian*, were **not verified**
    in this research; do not state dates for them.)
- **Lectureships and gospel meetings** rather than conventions. A "gospel meeting" is a
  multi-night preaching series hosted by one congregation with a visiting preacher; it is the
  ordinary rhythm of the year, not a special event.
- **Directories.** There is no single authoritative non-institutional directory verified here.
  Two things exist: (a) the mainstream **21st Century Christian** directory, *Churches of
  Christ in the United States*, which codes subgroups (one-cup, mutual edification, and
  others) and is the origin of most quoted subgroup counts; and (b) practice-based online
  listings such as [churchzip.com](https://churchzip.com/about), run by Earle West, which
  includes congregations meeting four behavioral criteria — no denominational headquarters,
  weekly Lord's Supper, no clergy titles or vestments, baptism by immersion — and explicitly
  declines to sort by "institutional affinity."
- **No** conventions, no synods, no missionary societies, no benevolent corporations, no
  church-supported schools, no ministerial fellowship, no denominational name.

## 6. Size, geography, and the health of the numbers

**Read every figure below as an estimate.** There is no central body and no required
reporting; the directories are compiled by partisans of one branch or another; and the
estimates disagree.

| Figure | Source | Year |
|---|---|---|
| ~**2,055 congregations** | *Encyclopedia of the Stone-Campbell Movement*, as reported by [Disciples Historical Society](https://discipleshistory.org/wiki/churches-of-christ/) and [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churches_of_Christ) | ESCM 2004; underlying data undated in the secondary reports |
| "**nearly 2,000** non-institutional Churches of Christ" | [Christian Chronicle](https://christianchronicle.org/a-non-institutional-institution/) (mainstream) | 2008 |
| **1,898 congregations / 113,656 members** | cited by [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churches_of_Christ_(non-institutional)) | 2009 |
| **2,757 congregations** | cited by [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churches_of_Christ_(non-institutional)) | 2020 |
| ~**120,000 members**, ≈**9%** of US Churches of Christ members and ≈**15%** of congregations | cited by [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churches_of_Christ_(non-institutional)) | undated |
| "**nearly 21%** of the total," of nearly 12,000 US churches of Christ | David Tant citing Mac Lynn's directory work, [aubeacon lesson 3](https://www.aubeacon.com/Sermons2011/HistoryOfInstitutionalDivisionLesson3of3.pdf) (non-institutional) | 2003 |

The 9%-of-members / 15%-of-congregations pairing is the most quotable fact in the set: these
congregations are **numerous and small**. The 15% and 21% figures are hard to reconcile and
you should not split the difference; report a range.

**A defensible summary sentence:** *roughly two thousand congregations and somewhere around
100,000–120,000 members in the United States, on estimates from partisan-compiled directories
between 2003 and 2020, which vary widely.*

**Geography.** Churches of Christ as a whole are a Southern and lower-Midwestern body: Texas
1,901 congregations, Tennessee 1,406, Alabama 838, Arkansas 694, Oklahoma 557 — out of
11,905 US congregations total (21st Century Christian, data build 08-2021,
[stats sheet](https://www.21stcc.com/wp-content/uploads/ccusa/ccusa_stats_sheet.pdf),
compiled by Carl H. Royster). The non-institutional churches follow that distribution and
are conventionally described as concentrated in **Texas, Tennessee, Kentucky, Alabama, and
Florida**; the documented epicenters in the sources consulted here are East Texas (Lufkin,
Beaumont), Middle Tennessee, and Tampa/Temple Terrace, Florida (Florida College). **A
state-by-state non-institutional count was not verified in this research** — do not assert
one. There are many rural communities where "two or more small Churches of Christ" exist "as
the legacy of this division"
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churches_of_Christ_(non-institutional))), which is
the texture of the geography that actually matters.

## 7. Where they divide among themselves

Non-institutional does not mean uniform. The live internal questions:

- **Marriage, divorce, and remarriage.** The largest. **Homer Hailey** (1903–2000), the most
  revered teacher in the fellowship and the subject of Harrell's biography, argued in
  *The Divorced & Remarried Who Would Come to God* (1991) that people who divorced and
  remarried before conversion need not separate to obey the gospel. Ben F. Vick Jr. answered
  in *Truth Magazine* that "if all men are obligated to the law of Christ, which includes
  Matthew 19:3-9, then, all men are obligated to Matthew 19:3-9," and concluded that
  "Hailey's position is wrong"
  ([Truth Magazine](https://www.TruthMagazine.com/haileys-view-on-divorce-and-remarriage);
  the article is dated to *Truth Magazine* vol. 42 and reposted online in 2012 — **the
  original year was not verified**). The dispute was never only about Hailey's exegesis; it
  was about **whether error on this question breaks fellowship**, which is why it split
  papers, congregations, and friendships in the 1990s. Related: O. C. Birdwell Jr.,
  "Fellowship and Unscriptural Divorce and Remarriage" (*Truth Magazine*).
- **The head covering** (1 Corinthians 11): whether it is cultural or binding. A live but
  smaller disagreement
  ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churches_of_Christ_(non-institutional))).
- **Fellowship boundaries and Romans 14.** How wide is the circle of matters over which
  brethren may differ and remain in fellowship? Non-institutional papers have argued at
  length against what they call an over-extension of Romans 14 — e.g. Tom Roberts, "Romans 14
  and Fellowship: A Deadly Parallel" (*Truth Magazine*). This is the argument underneath most
  of the others.
- **"Unity in diversity."** The general name for positions that would extend fellowship
  across doctrinal difference. Rejected by the mainstream of this fellowship as a solvent
  that dissolves everything the 1950s stand was about; defended by others as the only way to
  keep making distinctions without making parties.
- **Grace and the fear of antinomianism.** Matt Bassford himself was pushed back on by
  brethren who feared he was drifting; his answer — *"Grace and obedience are two halves of
  the same whole"* — is the tradition's characteristic resolution (see the
  [matt-bassford theology reference](../../matt-bassford/references/theology.md)).
- **Institutional creep at the margins**: whether a church may support a preacher who also
  writes for a particular paper, whether a congregation may host particular joint efforts,
  how "individual" an individual work has to be before it is really a church work.
- **And a boundary that is not an internal division at all:** the **one-cup**, **non-class**,
  and **mutual edification** congregations are almost always non-institutional too, but they
  separated decades earlier over different questions and are not in fellowship with these
  churches. Do not merge them.

## 8. The internal range: irenic to hard-line

The fellowship runs from preachers who read the other side's best books on an elder's
recommendation and say so in print, to preachers for whom every question is a fellowship
question. Both are genuinely inside it. Matt's own diagnosis of the range is worth having in
front of you when you write:

> "To one group of people, [the phrase 'church of Christ'] stands for a collection of
> traditions based on proof texts. These traditions focus entirely on minutiae as compared to
> the weightier matters of the law, and they are enforced with grim perfectionism. To another
> group of people, the phrase 'church of Christ' stands for the notion that ordinary people
> are capable of reading, interpreting, and practicing the Scripture on their own. The whole
> Bible belongs to us, and it's wonderful!"
> — ["Tradition and the Churches of Christ," 9 Mar 2018](https://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2018/03/tradition-and-churches-of-christ.html)

He locates himself firmly in the second group and admits the first exists "even at Jackson
Heights." **Write for the second group.** Matt and Josh both belong to it: Matt reading the
institutional essays because an elder told him to; Josh auditing his own tradition's slogans
out loud in class — *"which is not always true, even though we have good intentions when we
say that"* (see the [josh-tolbert skill](../../josh-tolbert/SKILL.md)). A draft that sounds
like grim perfectionism is not more authentic to this tradition. It is authentic to the part
of it these two men spent their lives arguing with.

## 10. Writing checklist: what to avoid assuming

Run a draft against this list before it goes out in Matt's or Josh's voice.

**Do not write the church doing these things.** Each is ordinary in mainstream Churches of
Christ and unavailable here:

- the church supporting a children's home, a Christian college, a mission society, a
  crisis-pregnancy center, or a hospital;
- "our church's" fellowship hall, kitchen, potluck, gym, family life center, coffee bar,
  bookstore, or church-funded camp/retreat/ski trip;
- a "youth minister," "worship minister," "executive minister," "small groups pastor," or
  church staff beyond elders, deacons, and an evangelist;
- "the church" running a food pantry, homeless ministry, or community outreach program for
  non-Christians out of the treasury;
- churches pooling money under a sponsoring congregation, or a national radio/TV/mailing
  campaign;
- praise teams, worship bands, solos, choirs, "worship sets," instruments of any kind;
- women leading prayer, reading Scripture, or preaching in the assembly; women elders or
  women deacons;
- denominational furniture generally: "Pastor Smith," "Reverend," "the sanctuary," "joining
  the church," "our denomination," "getting saved by praying a prayer," "asking Jesus into
  your heart," "accepting Christ as personal Savior."

**Do write these, freely — they are the tradition's own emphases:**

- **the individual Christian's** personal, costly, inconvenient care for the poor, the
  orphan, the widow, the neighbor — fostering, adoption, hospitality in your own house,
  showing up. This is not a concession to make; it is what their own preachers say they
  under-preach.
- hospitality in homes; the congregation as named people, not an abstraction;
- direct support of preachers by name;
- weekly Lord's Supper; a cappella congregational singing as a shared act rather than a
  performance;
- baptism by immersion for the remission of sins;
- elders shepherding one flock; congregational autonomy;
- "brethren," "the brotherhood," "gospel meeting," "the assembly," "the Lord's money."

**Also avoid:**

- calling them "anti," "antis," or "non-cooperative";
- calling them a denomination, or treating "church of Christ" as a denominational name;
- flattening the tradition into rule-keeping. Its best writers are grace-emphatic and
  self-critical about legalism, and Matt spent his last two years insisting on exactly that;
- assuming premillennial, Calvinist, or "once saved always saved" framing (all rejected);
- assuming they are one-cup, non-class, or mutual edification (they are not; those are other
  groups);
- assuming they refuse to read or engage the other side. The tradition's own instinct, at its
  best, is Bassford's elder's: read the defenses written by the people who believe it.
