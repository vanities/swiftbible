# History of the Churches of Christ (the Stone–Campbell / American Restoration Movement)

Reference for getting the tradition's history right when drafting devotional or teaching
content. Companion file: [timeline.md](timeline.md) — a dated table of the same material.

This skill sits beside [matt-bassford](../../matt-bassford/SKILL.md) and
[josh-tolbert](../../josh-tolbert/SKILL.md), both **non-institutional** Churches of Christ.
That matters for reading this file: the non-institutional stream is a minority within a
minority, and much of what a general source says about "Churches of Christ" describes the
mainstream institutional churches instead. Where the two diverge, it is flagged.

---

## How to read the confidence markers

Every branch of this movement writes tendentious history about its opponents. That is itself
a documented fact and one of the most useful things to know about the field. So claims here
are tagged:

| Tag | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **F** | Established fact — dated document, census return, or uncontested record |
| **S** | Mainstream scholarly interpretation — defensible, but an argument, not a datum |
| **C** | Contested between branches — each side tells this differently; do not present either version as settled |
| **?** | Unverified in the research for this file — treat as a lead, not a citation |

**Rule for drafting:** an **F** can go in a devotional as a statement. An **S** needs
"historians generally argue." A **C** needs "in this tradition's telling" or naming of both
sides. A **?** should not be used at all until checked.

## Orientation: three streams from one root

The movement that began around 1801–1832 is now three distinct North American bodies, plus
several smaller fellowships. Confusing them is the single most common error in popular
writing about this tradition. **F**

| Body | Instruments | Polity | Rough US size | Self-description |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Churches of Christ** (a cappella) | No | Strictly congregational; no denominational structure | ~11,800 congregations, ~1.1M adherents (2020) | "Not a denomination"; often "the church of Christ" (lowercase *c* on church) |
| **Christian Churches / Churches of Christ** (independent) | Yes | Congregational; conventions but no hierarchy | Larger per-congregation, smaller congregation count | "Independent Christian Churches" |
| **Christian Church (Disciples of Christ)** | Yes | Restructured 1968 into a mainline denomination with General Assembly | Sharply declining mainline body | Openly a denomination |

Separation dates: **1906** for the Churches of Christ (census marker), **1927/1968/1971** for
the independents' separation from the Disciples. **F**

Within Churches of Christ there are further, older fellowships that do not commonly commune
across their lines — non-institutional, one-cup, non-class (no Sunday School), mutual
edification, and the premillennial churches. See "Sub-fellowships," below.

## Part 1 — Roots (1790s–1811)

### The often-omitted first "Christians"

Popular accounts jump straight to Stone and Campbell. Two earlier "Christian" movements were
already in the field and were absorbed into what became the movement's Stone wing. **F**

**James O'Kelly** (c. 1735–1826) withdrew from the Methodist Episcopal Church in **1792**,
protesting Bishop Francis Asbury's appointive authority. His group, centered in Virginia and
North Carolina, was first called **Republican Methodists**, and took the name **Christian
Church** in **1794**. **F**
([NCpedia](https://www.ncpedia.org/biography/okelly-james),
[Wikipedia: Christian Connection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Connection))

**Abner Jones** (1772–1841) of Vermont and **Elias Smith** (1769–1846) of New Hampshire
disavowed their Baptist identity in the same decade and led a parallel New England "Christian"
movement. **F** By about **1810** the O'Kelly, Stone, and Smith/Jones groups were in loose
fellowship as the **Christian Connection** (or "Connexion"), estimated at roughly 20,000
members. **F/S** (the 20,000 figure is an estimate, not a return)

This matters for two reasons. First, "we are just Christians, not a denomination" was already
a going concern in America before either Campbell arrived. Second, most of the New England
Christian Connection did **not** join the 1832 union; that wing went its own way and eventually
merged into the Congregational Christian Churches, and from there into the United Church of
Christ. **F** (merger dates unverified here — see Unverified)

### Barton W. Stone and Cane Ridge

**Barton Warren Stone** (Dec 24, 1772 – Nov 9, 1844), ordained a Presbyterian in **1798**,
was preacher at Cane Ridge, near Paris, Kentucky. **F**
([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Barton-W-Stone))

The **Cane Ridge Revival**, **August 6–12, 1801**, was the defining camp meeting of the Second
Great Awakening in the West. **F** Attendance estimates run from about **10,000 to 25,000** —
in a state whose whole population was around a quarter million. **S** *No one counted. Every
figure you will see is a contemporary guess.* Use "somewhere between ten and twenty-five
thousand, by contemporary estimate," never a hard number.
([Disciples Historical Society](https://discipleshistory.org/wiki/cane-ridge-meetinghouse/))

The revival's physical phenomena — falling, jerking, barking, dancing — are well attested and
were an embarrassment to the later, more rationalist movement. **F** This is worth remembering
when the tradition presents itself as purely a *reasoned* return to the text: its Stone-side
origin is a frontier revival, not a seminar.

**1803:** Stone and colleagues withdrew from the **Synod of Kentucky** rather than face censure
for departing from the Westminster Confession, and formed the **Springfield Presbytery**. **F**
The date of the withdrawal protest is given as **September 10** or **September 12, 1803**
depending on the source — **disputed**.
([Wikipedia: Springfield Presbytery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Springfield_Presbytery))

**June 28, 1804:** the **Last Will and Testament of the Springfield Presbytery** — the
presbytery dissolved itself after less than a year. **F** Six signers: Robert Marshall, John
Dunlavy, Richard McNemar, B. W. Stone, John Thompson, David Purviance. Richard McNemar is
generally credited as co-author with Stone. **F**
([Disciples of Christ Historical Society, foundational documents](https://digitalcommons.discipleshistory.org/all_foundationaldocuments/8/))

Its central line: the presbytery willed that "this body die, be dissolved, and sink into union
with the Body of Christ at large." **F** The group took the name **Christians**.

> **A detail insider accounts often skip:** the Springfield Presbytery signers did not hold
> together. Several left — some to the Shakers, some back to the Presbyterians — leaving Stone
> as the principal survivor of the group that signed the document now treated as a founding
> charter. The specific destinations of each signer could not be confirmed in the research for
> this file (**?**). If you use this point, verify it first; the general fact of the group's
> disintegration is standard in the histories.

### Thomas and Alexander Campbell

**Thomas Campbell** (1763–1854), a Seceder Presbyterian minister from Ireland, arrived in
Philadelphia in **1807** and was censured by his presbytery in western Pennsylvania for
offering communion across party lines. **F**

**1809:** he organized the **Christian Association of Washington** (Washington County, PA) —
explicitly *not* a church, but a society for reform — and wrote the **Declaration and
Address**, adopted in September and published in Washington, PA, before the end of the year.
**F**
([Wikipedia: Declaration and Address](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declaration_and_Address);
[primary text, ACU mirror](https://webfiles.acu.edu/departments/Library/HR/restmov_nov11/www.mun.ca/rels/restmov/texts/tcampbell/da/DA-1ST.HTM))

Proposition 1, the movement's most-quoted sentence:

> "That the Church of Christ upon earth is essentially, intentionally, and constitutionally
> one; consisting of all those in every place that profess their faith in Christ and obedience
> to him in all things according to the Scriptures…"

Note what that says. The founding Campbell document is a **unity** document, not a
restoration-of-the-pattern document. The restrictive strand is there too — Proposition 5's
demand for "a thus saith the Lord either in express terms, or by approved precedent" — and the
tension between those two propositions is, arguably, the whole subsequent history of the
movement. **S**

> ### The slogan that is not in the Declaration and Address
>
> **"Where the Scriptures speak, we speak; where the Scriptures are silent, we are silent"** —
> the single most-quoted line in Churches of Christ — **does not appear in the Declaration and
> Address.** Verified by reading the 1809 first-edition text. **F**
>
> It is traditionally attributed to Thomas Campbell at a meeting of the Christian Association,
> reported decades later in Robert Richardson's *Memoirs of Alexander Campbell* (1868). That
> attribution could **not** be verified in the research for this file (**?**). Do not write
> "as Thomas Campbell wrote in the Declaration and Address" — that is flatly wrong, and it is
> in a great deal of popular Church of Christ literature.

**May 4, 1811:** the Christian Association reconstituted itself as a congregationally governed
church, meeting at **Brush Run**, Pennsylvania. **F** (Some timelines date the congregation to
1810 — **disputed**; 1811 is the date for its constitution as a church.)

**1812:** Alexander Campbell (Sept 12, 1788 – March 4, 1866), having concluded that infant
baptism was without warrant, was **immersed by Matthias Luce**, a Baptist minister. **F**
(The commonly given day — June 12, 1812 — was **not** verified here.)
([therestorationmovement.com](https://www.therestorationmovement.com/_states/wv/acampbell.htm))

That immersion is the hinge. It pulled the Campbell movement into the Baptist orbit for the
next two decades and made believers' immersion — later, immersion *for the remission of sins* —
the movement's non-negotiable.

## Part 2 — The Baptist years, Walter Scott, and the union (1811–1832)

### Inside the Baptist associations

Brush Run affiliated with the **Redstone Baptist Association** (c. **1815–1824**), then moved
to the **Mahoning Baptist Association** in **1824** as relations with Redstone soured. **F**
([Wikipedia: Restoration Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stone-Campbell_Restoration_Movement))

This period is routinely under-told. For roughly fifteen years the Campbell movement was a
reforming faction *within* Baptist life, not a separate people. Its distinctives — weekly
communion, plurality of elders, a plea against creeds, rejection of the "clergy" — were
hammered out in Baptist association politics.

### The papers

Alexander Campbell's periodicals were the movement's nervous system, and the movement's
subsequent divisions all ran through papers rather than through any hierarchy — because there
was no hierarchy to run through. **F/S**

| Paper | Run | Editor |
|---|---|---|
| *Christian Baptist* | 1823–1830 | Alexander Campbell |
| *Christian Messenger* | 1826–1844 | Barton W. Stone |
| *Millennial Harbinger* | Jan 1, 1830 – 1870 | A. Campbell; W. K. Pendleton from the late 1850s |

([Wikipedia: Millennial Harbinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millennial_Harbinger);
[Christian History Institute timeline](https://christianhistoryinstitute.org/magazine/article/from-stone-and-campbell-to-the-great-communion/))

The *Christian Baptist* is the sharp-elbowed, anti-clerical, anti-institutional Campbell; the
*Millennial Harbinger* is the older, more constructive, more institution-friendly Campbell.
Partisans on both sides of every later division quote whichever Campbell suits them. **C**
The non-institutional stream leans heavily on the early Campbell; the institutional stream on
the later. Both are quoting a real Campbell.

### Campbell's debates

Campbell built national visibility through public debate — the frontier's mass medium. **F**

| Year | Opponent | Subject | Place |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1820 | John Walker (Seceder Presbyterian) | Baptism | Mt. Pleasant, OH |
| 1823 | W. L. Maccalla (Presbyterian) | Infant baptism | Washington, KY |
| 1829 | Robert Owen (socialist, freethinker) | Evidences of Christianity | Cincinnati |
| 1837 | Bishop John B. Purcell (Roman Catholic) | Protestantism vs. Catholicism | Cincinnati |
| 1843 | Nathan L. Rice (Presbyterian) | Baptism, Spirit, creeds | Lexington, KY |

([Library of Congress, *Debates That Made History*](https://www.loc.gov/item/21010601/);
[Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Alexander-Campbell-American-clergyman))

The debate is still the tradition's native genre. Its argumentative habits — proposition,
proof text, "book, chapter, and verse" — descend directly from this.

### Walter Scott and the five-finger exercise

**Walter Scott** (1796–1861) was appointed evangelist of the Mahoning Association in **1827**
on Alexander Campbell's nomination. **F** He is credited with turning a reform program into a
revival: the **"five-finger exercise"** — **faith, repentance, baptism, remission of sins, gift
of the Holy Spirit** — a conversion sequence a hearer could count on one hand. **F**
([Disciples Historical Society](https://discipleshistory.org/wiki/scott-walter-1796-1861/))

Traditional accounts credit Scott with over a thousand baptisms a year for three years and
3,000+ converts on the Western Reserve. **S** (movement accounts; not independently audited)

Scott's contribution is the most-omitted piece of the founding story, and arguably the most
consequential: the "plan of salvation" preached in Churches of Christ for two centuries is
Scott's, not Campbell's. **S**

**1830:** the Mahoning Association dissolved itself, ending the Baptist connection. **F**

### The 1832 union

Meetings at **Georgetown** in late December 1831 and at the **Hill Street meeting house in
Lexington, Kentucky**, culminating **January 1, 1832**, joined Stone's "Christians" with
Campbell's "Reformers"/"Disciples." Sealed by a handshake between **Barton W. Stone** and
**"Raccoon" John Smith**, chosen as spokesman for the Reformers. **John Rogers** (Christian)
and **John Smith** (Reformer) were commissioned to ride to the churches and commend the union.
**F** Estimated combined size: about **22,000**. **S**
([Wikipedia: Restoration Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stone-Campbell_Restoration_Movement))

**What each side gave up. C/S** — the branches narrate this differently, so state it carefully.

| | Stone's Christians | Campbell's Disciples |
|---|---|---|
| Gave ground on | Revivalist experience as the test of conversion; Stone's non-orthodox Trinitarian language; the moral-influence view of atonement as a *teachable* position; the exclusive claim on the name "Christian" | The exclusive claim on the name "Disciples"; the harder edge of Campbell's anti-Stone polemic; some of the *Christian Baptist* combativeness |
| Kept | The name "Christian"; a pietist, unity-first temper; suspicion of the state and of respectability | Immersion for the remission of sins as the settled terms of entry; weekly communion; a rationalist, text-first method |

Stone was accused (by Campbell, among others) of unorthodox views on the **Trinity** — he
denied being Arian — and held a **moral influence** view of the atonement against Campbell's
**penal substitution**. **F** The union did not resolve these; it declared them opinion rather
than faith. Campbell's formula, quoted in the histories: *"Our bond of union is not opinion,
nor unity of opinion. It is one Lord, one faith, one baptism."* **F/?** (widely quoted; the
primary citation was not located here)
([Disciples Historical Society: Stone](https://discipleshistory.org/wiki/stone-barton-warren-1772-1844/))

Two honest caveats worth carrying into any devotional use of "the union":

1. **It was regional.** It took in Kentucky, Ohio, and the West. Much of the eastern Christian
   Connection stayed out. **F**
2. **It was not tidy.** By 1832 there were at least three different understandings of the
   atonement inside the merged movement, and that diversity persisted through the century. **S**
   ([SBL lecture on atonement, ACU mirror](https://webfiles.acu.edu/departments/Library/HR/restmov_nov11/www.mun.ca/rels/restmov/texts/studies/whatdid.htm))

## Part 3 — The restoration ideal and its actual debts

This section is where devotional writing most often goes wrong, in both directions.

### What the ideal claims

- **Restoration, not reformation.** Not to purify an existing church but to reconstitute the
  New Testament church directly from the text, skipping the intervening centuries. **F**
- **"No creed but Christ, no book but the Bible."** Creeds are tests of fellowship humans
  invented; the New Testament alone is the constitution of the church. **F**
- **Silence as prohibition** (the Campbell/Scott side) vs. **silence as liberty** (a minority
  strand). The "regulative" reading — that what Scripture does not authorize is forbidden —
  became dominant and is the engine of nearly every later division. **S**
- **Unity through restoration.** Unity is achieved by everyone independently returning to the
  same text and arriving at the same conclusions. **F** (as a stated aim)

### What historians say it actually owes

The mainstream scholarly case — Richard T. Hughes, Nathan Hatch, Douglas A. Foster, and others
— is that the movement is a *product* of its moment far more than it recognized. **S**

| Debt | The argument |
|---|---|
| **Scottish Common Sense Realism / Baconian method** | Campbell read the Bible the way a Baconian naturalist reads nature: collect the facts, induce the pattern, and any honest observer will reach the same result. That is an 18th-century epistemology, not a first-century one — and it is *why* the movement believed unity-through-restoration was achievable at all. Hughes makes this central. |
| **Lockean epistemology** | Faith as assent to testimony, revelation as propositional information, "opinion" cleanly separable from "faith" — Locke's categories, in Campbell's mouth. |
| **Sandemanianism / Glasite / Haldane Scottish independency** | Weekly communion, plurality of unpaid elders, congregational independence, suspicion of a trained clergy, faith as assent to testimony rather than an inward experience. Campbell studied at Glasgow and knew Greville Ewing, a Sandemanian sympathizer. Lynn McMillon's *Restoration Roots* (1983, from a 1972 Baylor dissertation) is the standard treatment. |
| **Frontier revivalism** | Cane Ridge. Scott's five-finger exercise is a revival technique. The gospel meeting, the invitation song, and the counted response are camp-meeting inheritances. |
| **Jacksonian democratic populism** | Nathan Hatch's *The Democratization of American Christianity* places the movement inside a broad anti-elite, anti-clerical, every-man-his-own-interpreter surge. The plea "no clergy, no creeds, no councils" is also a political mood. |

([Hughes, *Reviving the Ancient Faith*, Eerdmans; 3rd ed. 2024 with James L. Gorman](https://www.eerdmans.com/9780802877291/reviving-the-ancient-faith-3rd-ed/);
[Rochester Christian University, "American Origins of Churches of Christ"](https://rcu.edu/american-origins-of-churches-of-christ/);
[Britannica: Sandemanians](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Sandemanians))

**Hughes's two-stream thesis** — worth knowing by name, because it structures most current
academic writing on Churches of Christ: the movement carried both a **Baconian-rationalist**
strand (Alexander Campbell) and an **apocalyptic** strand (Barton Stone, later David Lipscomb)
that viewed nations, denominations, and worldly power with deep suspicion. Hughes argues the
apocalyptic strand was gradually displaced across the 20th century, and that Churches of Christ
became more rationalist, more respectable, and more comfortably American as a result. **S**
([Word&Way review, 2024](https://wordandway.org/2024/02/27/review-reviving-the-ancient-faith/);
[Bob Cornwall review](https://www.bobcornwall.com/2024/02/reviving-ancient-faith-story-of.html))

### The insider counter-case — state it fairly

Do not present the scholars' case as a debunking. The serious insider reply has real force:

1. **Genetic fallacy.** That Campbell used the intellectual tools available to him no more
   discredits his conclusions than Augustine's Neoplatonism discredits Augustine. Every
   theology is written in some century's vocabulary.
2. **The claim was never "we have no context."** The claim was that Scripture, not tradition
   or council, is the final authority — a claim about *norm*, not about *sociology*.
3. **The scholars are insiders too.** Hughes, Foster, Harrell, and Garrett all wrote from
   inside the tradition, mostly from its colleges. This is a family argument, not an outside
   attack — which is part of why it is worth taking seriously.
4. **Common Sense Realism is not obviously wrong.** The confidence that ordinary readers can
   read a text and reach shared conclusions is out of fashion; it is not thereby refuted.

The non-institutional stream (Matt Bassford's and Josh Tolbert's) tends to be the most resistant
to the Hughes framing, and the most consistent in actually applying the early-Campbell,
silence-as-prohibition method. Josh Tolbert's habit of auditing his own tradition's slogans
from inside it is exactly the register to use if this material appears in a devotional.

## Part 4 — The Civil War and the sectional fracture

### The 1863 resolution

The **American Christian Missionary Society** (formed 1849 in Cincinnati) adopted a resolution
declaring loyalty to the United States government at its meeting in **Cincinnati, October
20–22, 1863**. **F**
([ACU digital commons: 1863 ACMS Proceedings](https://digitalcommons.acu.edu/sc_teaching_images/7/))

Because the meeting sat in Cincinnati during the war, only Northern members could attend.
Southern brethren had no vote and no voice. **F** Historians across all three streams treat the
1863 resolution as a decisive hardening of Southern opposition to missionary societies. **S**
([Disciples CUIM, "Historical Disciples Positions on War and Peace"](https://www.disciplescuim.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/09/FC-Module-2.pdf))

That is the sharpest available illustration of the movement's central irony: a body with no
national machinery, formed to end sectarian division, split along the same sectional line as
every American church that *did* have machinery.

### Harrell's argument

**David Edwin Harrell Jr.**, *Quest for a Christian America, 1800–1865* (vol. 1) and *Sources
of Division in the Disciples of Christ, 1865–1900* (vol. 2), argued that the division between
Churches of Christ and Disciples was driven far more by **sectional, economic, racial, and
class** forces than by the doctrinal questions the participants argued about. **S** Poor,
rural, Southern churches against wealthier, urban, Northern ones; the organ and the society
were markers of the divide as much as causes of it.
([H-Net review by Douglas Foster](https://networks.h-net.org/node/15697/reviews/15892/foster-harrell-social-history-disciples-christ-vol-1-quest-christian);
[University of Alabama Press](https://www.uapress.ua.edu/9780817350741/quest-for-a-christian-america-18001865/))

**How to handle this in writing. C** Harrell's thesis is broadly accepted in the academy and
resented in parts of the tradition, where it can read as saying the doctrinal arguments were
mere rationalization. Harrell does not quite say that. The honest formulation: *historians
argue that sectional and economic factors shaped which arguments persuaded whom, and that the
doctrinal debate cannot be understood apart from them.* Do not write "the split was really
about money" — that is a caricature of Harrell.

Note also: **Alexander Campbell himself defended the moral permissibility of slavery**,
arguing that Scripture nowhere forbids it, while treating American slavery as inexpedient.
**F**
([Wikipedia: Alexander Campbell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Campbell_(clergyman)))
This is not a footnote to be quietly dropped. A devotional that presents the founders as
purely disinterested Bible readers, in a movement that fractured along the Mason–Dixon line,
is not telling the truth.

### David Lipscomb's dissent

**David Lipscomb** (1831–1917) is the pivotal figure of the Southern churches. **F**

- Edited the **Gospel Advocate** from **1866** until his death in **1917**. **F** (The paper
  was founded in **1855** by **Tolbert Fanning**; it suspended publication in 1861 during the
  war and resumed in 1866.) **F**
- Wrote a series of articles on civil government in the *Advocate* in **1866–67**, published in
  book form as **_Civil Government: Its Origin, Mission, and Destiny, and the Christian's
  Relation to It_** in **1889**. **F**
- Held that the Christian's citizenship is in a different kingdom altogether: no military
  service, no voting, no holding office, no participation in the coercive machinery of the
  state. **F**
  ([Wikipedia: David Lipscomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Lipscomb);
  [Missio Dei: "David Lipscomb's Political Eschatology"](https://missiodeijournal.com/issues/md-13/authors/md-13-bookout-young))
- Co-founded the **Nashville Bible School** with **James A. Harding** in **1891** — later David
  Lipscomb College, now Lipscomb University. **F**

Lipscomb's pacifism and political abstention are, in Hughes's reading, the fullest inheritance
of Stone's apocalypticism. **S** They also faded: by the mid-20th century Churches of Christ
were, in most places, a patriotic and politically engaged people. That reversal is one of the
most substantial and least-taught facts about the tradition. **S**

## Part 5 — The long divorce and 1906

### The issues, in the order they mattered

1. **The missionary society** (from 1849). Not primarily about missions — about whether any
   organization larger than a local congregation may exist to do the church's work. **F**
2. **Instrumental music** (from the late 1850s). *L. L. Pinkerton*'s congregation at **Midway,
   Kentucky** is the standard first documented case, a melodeon in use by about **1859–60**.
   **F/?** (date given variously; the Midway attribution is standard)
3. **The "located preacher" / pastor system**, choirs, paid fundraising, and church festivals —
   the Sand Creek list. **F**
4. **Rebaptism** — a genuinely intramural fight. **Austin McGary** founded the **Firm
   Foundation** in **1884** (Austin, TX) largely to argue that a baptism performed without
   understanding that it was *for the remission of sins* was invalid. Lipscomb held the
   opposite: immersion for any scriptural reason was scriptural baptism. **F** McGary's
   stricter position substantially prevailed in 20th-century Churches of Christ. **S**
   ([John Mark Hicks on McGary and rebaptism](https://johnmarkhicks.com/2011/03/15/mcgary-on-rebaptism-reason-to-divide/))

### Sand Creek, 1889

At the **Sand Creek** meeting house in **Shelby County, Illinois**, an **Address and
Declaration** — principal author **Daniel Sommer** (1850–1940), read aloud by **P. P.
Warren** — condemned "innovations" and closed with the line that made it notorious:

> "…if they do not turn away from such abominations, that we can not and will not regard them
> as brethren."

**F**
([primary text, ACU mirror](https://webfiles.acu.edu/departments/Library/HR/restmov_nov11/www.mun.ca/rels/restmov/texts/dsommer/dec_v1.html))

Signatories included officers of the Sand Creek and Liberty churches and representatives from
Ash Grove, Union, Mode, Gays, and Stricklyn. **F**

**Date: disputed.** The document itself is dated **August 17, 1889**; some accounts (including
Wikipedia's Daniel Sommer article) give **Sunday, August 18, 1889**. Cite it as "August 1889"
unless you have checked the primary.

The document is a deliberate inversion of Thomas Campbell's *Declaration and Address* — same
title elements, opposite purpose: the 1809 document proposed terms of union, the 1889 document
proposed terms of separation. **S** Attendance is traditionally given as around 6,000; that is
a partisan estimate. **C**

**Contested significance. C** Non-institutional and conservative accounts treat Sand Creek as a
faithful line-drawing that named what was already true. Disciples and progressive accounts
treat it as the movement's first formal call to schism. Sommer himself was inconsistent by
anyone's standard: he fought societies, colleges, the pastor system, and instruments, while
defending Sunday schools and individual communion cups, which others were dividing over.
([Disciples Historical Society: Sommer](https://discipleshistory.org/wiki/sommer-daniel-1850-1940/))

### J. W. McGarvey — the position that did not survive

**J. W. McGarvey** (1829–1911), the movement's leading biblical scholar, is the essential
counter-example to a tidy story. He **opposed instrumental music** on the silence-of-Scripture
argument — his 1864–65 exchange with A. S. Hayden is among the earliest public discussions —
while **supporting the missionary societies** as an expedient. **F** In **November 1902**, when
his own Broadway Christian Church in Lexington moved to introduce the instrument, he left. **F**
([Wikipedia: John William McGarvey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_William_McGarvey);
[Waddey, *Life and Lessons of J. W. McGarvey*](https://www.restorationlibrary.org/library/LLJWM/LLJWM_html.html))

Both sides claim him and neither can. His combination — anti-instrument, pro-society — had no
institutional home after 1906, and its disappearance is good evidence that the split hardened
into a package deal rather than a set of independently reasoned positions. **S**

### 1906: a bureaucratic marker, not an event

This is the single point most worth getting right.

- The **1906 Census of Religious Bodies** was directed by **Simon Newton Dexter North**. **F**
- **William J. Campbell** of Marshalltown, Iowa, alerted the Bureau that some 3,000 "churches
  of Christ" no longer considered themselves connected with the Disciples, supplying a preacher
  list from the **McQuiddy Publishing Company**. **F**
- North wrote to **David Lipscomb** asking whether a distinct body existed; the exchange was
  published in the **Gospel Advocate of July 18, 1907**. **F**
- Lipscomb replied that there was "a distinct people taking the word of God as their only and
  sufficient rule of faith, calling their churches 'churches of Christ'…distinct and separate
  in name, work and rule of faith from all other bodies," and identified instrumental music and
  the missionary society as "a subversion of the fundamental principles on which the churches
  were based." **F**
- **James Walton Shepherd**, Lipscomb's office manager, was authorized as a special census
  agent for Churches of Christ. **J. H. Garrison**, editor of the *Christian-Evangelist*,
  fought the separate listing and accused Lipscomb of sectarianism. **F**
- Data were published in a **1909** bulletin and **1910** volumes. **F**

([Christian Chronicle, Douglas Foster, "What really happened in 1906?"](https://christianchronicle.org/what-really-happened-in-1906-a-trek-through-history-reveals-role-of-census/);
[Christian Standard, "1906 Census of Religious Bodies"](https://christianstandard.com/2006/06/cs_article-331/))

**The numbers, with a caveat.** Two tabulations circulate and they do not match:

| Source | Churches of Christ | Disciples of Christ |
|---|---|---|
| Foster (Christian Chronicle / Christian Standard) | 2,642 congregations; 159,123 members | 7,799 congregations; 923,698 members |
| Wikipedia (Restoration Movement) | 2,649 congregations; 159,658 members | 8,293 congregations; 982,701 members |

Use approximations — "roughly 2,650 congregations and about 160,000 members, against roughly
1 million Disciples" — and note that the discrepancy exists. **F/?**

**Foster's verdict, and the line to remember:**

> "The division did not begin or happen in 1906 — it was nearing its end. The government did
> not declare the division, the Census Bureau simply published data it received."

**F** (direct quotation, Foster)

Popular Churches of Christ writing sometimes implies that the federal government "declared"
the two bodies separate in 1906, or that the split happened that year. Both are wrong. The
divergence ran roughly from the 1860s to the 1900s; 1906 is the year a bureaucracy printed
what had already occurred. Say "the 1906 census is a marker, not an event."

## Part 6 — The twentieth century

### The papers, the schools, and the absence of a hierarchy

With no synod, conference, or convention, Churches of Christ have been governed in practice by
**editors and colleges**. **S** This is the structural fact that explains everything that
follows: to win a controversy you had to win a paper, and to hold ground long-term you needed a
school.

> institutions. Non-institutional churches do not object to the schools' existence — they
> object to *congregational treasuries funding them*. Matt Bassford and Josh Tolbert's stream
> holds this line. Do not write as though "Church of Christ colleges" are a shared inheritance
> of the whole fellowship.

### The premillennial controversy (1910s–1940s)

**R. H. Boll** was front-page editor of the *Gospel Advocate* from **1909**, and his name was
dropped from the masthead on **July 22, 1915**; **M. C. Kurfees** explained the removal in an
editorial of **August 12, 1915**. **F** The separation was **staged, not a single event**: the
board (McQuiddy, F. W. Smith, Boles, A. B. Lipscomb, Kurfees, G. Dallas Smith) forced his
resignation, **reinstated** him after he promised to hold his millennial views as private opinion
and not publish them, then called for and accepted his resignation again when he resumed writing
on the theme. **F** Popular accounts compress this into one dismissal. Boll went to **Word and
Work** in **1916** and edited it for forty years. **F**
([Wikipedia: Robert Henry Boll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Henry_Boll);
[Alex V. Wilson on Boll, ACU mirror](https://webfiles.acu.edu/departments/Library/HR/restmov_nov11/www.mun.ca/rels/restmov/texts/rboll/re/RHBWEC.HTM))

The substance: Boll held that the kingdom did not fully begin at Pentecost and that a future
earthly reign of Christ remains. The dominant position held that the kingdom *is* the church,
inaugurated in Acts 2 — the reading Matt Bassford holds and which the matt-bassford skill flags
as a pushback point.

**Foy E. Wallace Jr.** led the campaign against "Bollism" through the *Bible Banner*, debating
**Charles M. Neal** in **1933** at Winchester, KY, and Chattanooga, TN. **F** By the 1940s the
premillennial churches were effectively out of fellowship. **F**

Loss estimates of "about 100 churches, mainly in Kentucky, Indiana, and Louisiana" come from
**partisan anti-premillennial sources** and should be labeled as such. **C**
([Gospel Guardian, "Millennialism in the Restoration Movement"](https://www.wordsfitlyspoken.org/gospel_guardian/v21/v21n12p5b-9a.html) — partisan)

### Race

Churches of Christ were segregated, in the South by law and elsewhere by custom, and the
tradition's histories were slow to say so. **F/S**

- **Marshall Keeble** (1878–1968) was the most prominent Black evangelist in Churches of Christ,
  credited with thousands of baptisms, and president of the **Nashville Christian Institute** —
  a segregated school for training Black preachers — through the 1940s and 1950s. **F**
  (Sources differ on his start date: **1940** or **1942**; end date **1958**. **Disputed.**)
  ([Tennessee Encyclopedia](https://tennesseeencyclopedia.net/entries/marshall-keeble/))
- Keeble was an **accommodationist**: he preached to segregated seating and relied on white
  patronage, which drew criticism then and since. **F/C**
- **G. P. Bowser** (joined the movement 1897) was outspoken against segregation in church and
  society, and received markedly less white support in consequence. **F**
  ([Disciples Historical Society: African Americans in the Movement](https://discipleshistory.org/wiki/african-americans-in-the-movement/))

Black Churches of Christ remain a substantial and largely separate network of congregations.
Any history of this tradition that does not mention race is an incomplete one.

### The institutional / non-institutional division (1930s–1960s)

The most important 20th-century division for this repo's purposes, because both archived
speakers are on the non-institutional side.

**The question:** may a local congregation send money from its treasury to any organization
other than another local church — an orphan home, a college, a "sponsoring church" running a
national work? **F**

**The sequence. F** unless noted:

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| 1931 | **G. C. Brewer** argues at the Abilene Christian College lectures that churches may support schools from the treasury |
| 1933 | Brewer presses the case in the *Gospel Advocate* |
| post-1945 | GI Bill enrollment surge at the colleges; postwar European mission opportunity; the **sponsoring church** model emerges |
| 1949/1950 | *Gospel Guardian* launched — Cogdill publishing, Tant editing |
| 1951 | Fourth and Groesbeck church, **Lufkin, Texas**, splits into two congregations |
| 1952 | **Herald of Truth** national radio program begins, overseen by the **Highland Church of Christ, Abilene** — the emblematic sponsoring-church arrangement |
| late 1954 | *Gospel Advocate* editor **B. C. Goodpasture** calls for a **"yellow tag of quarantine"** against non-institutional preachers |
| 1954 | *Firm Foundation* under **Reuel Lemmons** moves to the institutional side |
| 1950s–60s | Congregation-by-congregation separation, preacher lists, cancelled meetings |
| by late 1960s | Separation essentially complete; contact across the line largely ceased |

([Wikipedia: Churches of Christ (non-institutional)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churches_of_Christ_(non-institutional));
[La Vista Church of Christ, "The History of the Institutional Controversy"](https://www.lavistachurchofchrist.org/cms/the-history-of-the-institutional-controversy/) — non-institutional, partisan)

**Contested framing. C** Institutional accounts describe the non-institutional side as
legalists who would let orphans starve over a technicality. Non-institutional accounts describe
the institutional side as men who abandoned the authority principle the moment it cost them
something, and who used orphans as an emotional lever to avoid the argument. Both
characterizations are in print. Neither belongs in a devotional as fact. The neutral statement
is: *the question was whether the church's treasury may fund organizations other than the local
church, and the two sides did not resolve it.*

One irony the non-institutional side records honestly: **Foy E. Wallace Jr.**, whose *Bible
Banner* work was foundational for the non-institutional side, had by the mid-1960s associated
himself exclusively with institutional churches. **F**

### Sub-fellowships

Several groups separated earlier than 1950s and remain distinct. **F**

| Group | Issue | Rough US congregations |
|---|---|---|
| Non-institutional | Church support of institutions and sponsoring churches | ~1,900–2,800 (estimates vary widely; see below) |
| Non-class / "no Sunday School" | Dividing the assembly into classes; women teaching | ~1,100 |
| One-cup | A single communion cup, against individual cups | ~550 |

[Wikipedia: Churches of Christ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churches_of_Christ) and

in other years — figures that are not mutually consistent. **?** Treat all of them as
order-of-magnitude only.

**G. C. Brewer** introduced **individual communion cups** at Chattanooga in **1915**; **G. A.
Trott, H. C. Harper, and W. G. Rice** organized the opposition to Sunday Schools. **F**
The one-cup and non-class congregations are almost always also non-institutional, but they

### The Crossroads movement and the ICOC

The most disruptive external episode of the late 20th century, and one many Churches of Christ
would rather not claim. **F**

|---|---|

**Handle with care. C** "Church of Christ" and "International Churches of Christ" are different
bodies and have been formally separate since 1993. Mainstream Churches of Christ generally
regard the ICOC as having left; ICOC accounts describe the parting differently. Reveal.org and
countercult sites are ex-member and apologetics sources — usable for chronology, not for
neutral characterization.

### The "change agent" controversies (1980s–2000s)

From the mid-1980s, a set of preachers and writers pushed for grace-centered theology, softer
boundaries with evangelicalism, and a re-examination of the a cappella tradition. Their critics
named them **"change agents."** **F/C** (the label is polemical and was not self-chosen)

- **Rubel Shelly**'s shift around **1986**; *I Just Want to Be a Christian*; *The Second
  Incarnation* (1992, with Randall Harris). **F**
  ([Wikipedia: Rubel Shelly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubel_Shelly))
- Watchdog papers — *Seek the Old Paths* and others — carried the countercase. **F** (partisan
  on the conservative side)
- **2007:** the Richland Hills church (Fort Worth; later **The Hills**) added an instrumental
  Saturday service. **Max Lucado**'s Oak Hills in San Antonio moved similarly. At least five
  large congregations added instruments to some services in that period. **F**
  ([Baptist News Global](https://baptistnews.com/article/some-churches-of-christ-re-examine-tradition-of-instrument-free-worship/))
- **2009:** the national directory **removed 21 congregations** using instruments in at least
  one Sunday service. **2012:** the directory **reversed** the decision, inserted the word
  "historically" into its definition ("historically a cappella Churches of Christ"), and added
  classification codes for partial instrumental use. **F**
  ([Christian Chronicle](https://christianchronicle.org/historically-a-cappella-churches-with-instrumental-services-returned-to-national-directory/))

That directory reversal is a small, concrete, and genuinely revealing fact: the boundary of the
fellowship is now maintained by a data-collection decision, and the collectors chose
self-identification over practice.

### Softening boundaries with the other two streams

- **Stone-Campbell Dialogue** and **Restoration Forums** brought leaders of the three streams
  into regular conversation from the 1990s. **F**
- **October 4, 2009** — World Communion Sunday — the **Great Communion**, marking the
  bicentennial of the *Declaration and Address*, observed by hundreds of churches in the US and
  87 other countries across all three streams. **F**
  ([Douglas A. Foster, "The Great Communion of 2009," ACU](https://digitalcommons.acu.edu/sumlec_audio/778/);
  [Christian History Institute](https://christianhistoryinstitute.org/magazine/article/united-we-shall-prevail))

This softening is real but uneven. It runs through the colleges, the academic conferences, and
some large mainstream congregations. It has essentially no purchase in the non-institutional,
one-cup, or non-class fellowships. **S** A devotional that says "Churches of Christ today are
warming to their Christian Church cousins" is describing one part of a divided fellowship.

### Decline

Churches of Christ in the US have been shrinking in absolute terms since roughly 1990, and
shrinking sharply relative to population. **F**

| Year | Congregations | Members | Adherents | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1906 | ~2,650 | ~160,000 | — | Census of Religious Bodies |
| 1926 | — | 317,937 | — | Census (via DCHS) |
| 1936 | — | 433,714 | — | Census (via DCHS) |
| 1990 | 13,174 | 1,284,056 | 1,684,872 | 21st Century Christian directory |
| 2015 | 12,300 | 1,183,613 | 1,519,695 | 21st Century Christian directory (Carl Royster) |
| 2020 | 11,790 | 1,113,362 | — | 2020 U.S. Religion Census / ASARB |
| 2021 | 11,875 | — (attendance 1,092,182) | 1,423,295 | 21st Century Christian database |

**1990 → 2015:** −100,443 members (−7.8%), −165,177 adherents (−9.8%), −874 congregations,
while the US population grew from about 250 million to about 320 million (+28%). **F**
([Christian Chronicle, "165,000 fewer souls in the pews"](https://christianchronicle.org/165-000-fewer-souls-in-the-pews-five-questions-to-consider/);
[2020 U.S. Religion Census national overview](https://www.usreligioncensus.org/sites/default/files/2023-10/Intro-NationalOverview-Grammich.pdf))

**Caveats you must carry with these numbers. F/?**

1. **The standard directory is compiled by partisans.** *Churches of Christ in the United
   States* is published by **21st Century Christian**, a Nashville firm inside the fellowship.
   It is the best source available and it is not an independent census.
2. **Print editions stopped in 2018.** COVID halted the triennial cycle. The 2020/2021 figures
   come from an ongoing database, not a published edition. A **Church Research Council**
   (21st Century Christian, Heritage21, the Siburt Institute, the *Christian Chronicle*) was
   formed to rebuild the directory, with a **new edition planned for 2026** — not confirmed as
   published as of this writing. **?**
   ([Christian Chronicle](https://christianchronicle.org/church-research-council-formed-to-renew-churches-of-christ-directory/);
   [21stcc.com/ccusa](https://www.21stcc.com/ccusa/))
3. **"Members" and "adherents" are different counts.** Members are baptized; adherents include
   unbaptized children and spouses. Popular writing mixes them constantly. The gap is roughly
   300,000.
4. **Boundary drift contaminates the trend.** Because the directory now includes "historically
   a cappella" congregations that use instruments, the 2012-and-later counts are measuring a
   slightly different population than the 1990 counts.
5. **Worldwide figures are unreliable.** Estimates range from **1.7–2 million** members in
   40,000+ congregations to **~3 million**, depending on who is counting and what they count.
   **?** Do not put a worldwide number in a devotional.
6. The often-repeated line that Churches of Christ are shrinking by "2,400 a month" comes from
   a **blog analysis** of directory data, not from the directory itself. **C/?**

Geographic concentration is stable and worth knowing: Texas and Tennessee alone account for
well over a quarter of US members. **F**
([Disciples Historical Society: Churches of Christ](https://discipleshistory.org/wiki/churches-of-christ/))

## Part 8 — Historiography: who writes these histories

If you take one thing from this file into a devotional, take this. **S**

| Writer / work | Position | What to expect |
|---|---|---|
| **Encyclopedia of the Stone-Campbell Movement** (Foster, Blowers, Dunnavant, Williams, eds.; Eerdmans, 2004/2005) | Ecumenical, all three streams collaborating | The reference standard. Where a claim in this file conflicts with the ESCM, prefer the ESCM |
| **Richard T. Hughes**, *Reviving the Ancient Faith* (3rd ed. 2024 with James L. Gorman) | Insider academic (Pepperdine/Lipscomb), critical | The apocalyptic-vs-rationalist thesis; the argument that Churches of Christ absorbed Enlightenment method while believing they had escaped history |
| **David Edwin Harrell Jr.**, *Quest for a Christian America*; *Sources of Division* | Insider academic, social historian; personally non-institutional | The sectional/economic/class reading of the divisions |
| **Douglas A. Foster** (ACU) | Insider academic, irenic | Careful primary-document work; the "1906 was not an event" correction |
| **Leroy Garrett**, *The Stone-Campbell Movement* (1981) | Insider, unity-leaning, self-consciously ecumenical | Popularized the neutral term "Stone-Campbell Movement" precisely to avoid choosing between "restoration" and "unity" as the movement's purpose |
| **Earl Irvin West**, *The Search for the Ancient Order* (4 vols., 1949–1993) | Insider, traditional Churches of Christ | Detailed and indispensable on 19th-century sources; frames the story as faithful people preserving the ancient order against digression |
| ***Gospel Advocate***, ***Firm Foundation***, ***Truth Magazine***, ***Gospel Guardian***, ***Seek the Old Paths*** | Branch papers | Primary sources for what a side actually said; never neutral about opponents |
| **Disciples of Christ Historical Society** (discipleshistory.org) | Disciples institutional | Excellent archives; frames Churches of Christ as the group that departed |
| ***Christian Standard*** | Independent Christian Churches | Good on the 1906 and 1927–71 material; its own stream's perspective |
| ***The Christian Chronicle*** | Churches of Christ, editorially independent | The best current journalism on the fellowship, including on decline |
| **reveal.org and countercult sites** | Ex-member / apologetics | Useful chronology on the ICOC; hostile framing |

**The naming question is itself a historiographical position. F/C**

- **"The Restoration Movement"** — the insider Churches of Christ term. Implies the goal was
  restoring the ancient order.
- **"The Stone-Campbell Movement"** — the ecumenical academic term, from Garrett (1981) and
  standardized by the ESCM. Implies a historical movement founded by two men — which is exactly
  what the insider account denies.
- **"Campbellite"** — a slur, used by 19th-century opponents. Never use it.

The choice of name signals whose story you are telling. In devotional writing addressed to
Churches of Christ, "the Restoration Movement" is the natural register; in anything making a
historical claim, "Stone-Campbell Movement" is the safer academic term. Saying both once, early,
costs nothing and buys credibility.

**The deepest historiographical tension.** The tradition holds, as a matter of principle, that
it has **no history** — that it is simply the New Testament church, reproduced. A history of
the movement is therefore, in a small way, an argument against one of the movement's own
premises. Insiders who write good history (Hughes, Foster, Harrell, Garrett, and locally, the
kind of self-auditing Josh Tolbert does in a Bible class) are performing a real act of
intellectual honesty, and are sometimes attacked for it. Say so if the subject comes up.

## Things commonly gotten wrong

| Common claim | The correction |
|---|---|
| "Thomas Campbell wrote 'where the Scriptures speak, we speak' in the Declaration and Address" | It is not in the document. Verified against the 1809 text. Traditional attribution is to a spoken remark, reported in Richardson's 1868 *Memoirs* — unverified here |
| "The government split the church in 1906" | The Census Bureau published data supplied by the churches themselves. Foster: "The division did not begin or happen in 1906 — it was nearing its end" |
| "The split was over instrumental music" | Instruments were the visible marker. The missionary society came first, and the sectional/economic divide runs under both |
| "Churches of Christ and the Christian Church are the same thing" | Three distinct bodies since 1906 / 1968–71 |
| "Church of Christ" = "International Church of Christ" | Separate bodies since 1993 |
| "The movement began at Cane Ridge in 1801" | Cane Ridge is one root. The O'Kelly (1792) and Smith/Jones "Christian" movements predate Stone's separation, and the Campbell movement had a wholly separate origin |
| "The founders were unified" | Stone and Campbell disagreed on the Trinity, the atonement, and the terms of the union, and the union was regional and incomplete |
| "The colleges are the fellowship's schools" | Non-institutional churches object to congregational funding of them. Roughly 2,000+ congregations do not participate |
| "Walter Scott is a minor figure" | The five-finger plan of salvation preached for two centuries is his, not Campbell's |
| "About 1.5 million members in the US" | Depends entirely on members vs. adherents, and on directory data that stopped being published in print in 2018 |

## Where devotional writing should be careful

1. **Do not make the tradition's founders into saints or into Enlightenment dupes.** Both
   flatten them. Campbell defended the moral permissibility of slavery; Stone freed his slaves
   and held views on the Trinity his own movement could not digest; Lipscomb refused to vote.
   They were serious, inconsistent, situated men.
2. **Name which Churches of Christ you mean.** For this repo, that will usually be
   non-institutional.
3. **When you cite a division, cite both sides' framing or neither.** The rule that got both
   archived speakers their credibility is the same one here: concede the strongest objection.
4. **Prefer "the churches of Christ" (lowercase) in insider register**, since the tradition
   reads the phrase in Romans 16:16 as a description rather than a proper name — but be aware
   that even this is a contested usage, and the directory, the papers, and most members
   capitalize it in ordinary writing.
5. **Numbers age fast and are partisan-compiled.** Cite the year and the source, every time.

## Unverified — do not use without checking

- The specific fates of the Springfield Presbytery signatories after 1804 (who went to the
  Shakers, who returned to the Presbyterians).
- The attribution of "where the Scriptures speak, we speak" to Thomas Campbell via Richardson's
  *Memoirs of Alexander Campbell* (1868), and the Andrew Munro anecdote usually told with it.
- The exact day of Alexander Campbell's immersion (year 1812 and the baptizer, Matthias Luce,
  are confirmed; "June 12" is not).
- The merger dates by which the eastern Christian Connection entered the Congregational
  Christian Churches and then the United Church of Christ.
- The primary-source citation for Campbell's "Our bond of union is not opinion…" — the quotation
  is widely reproduced in the histories but was not traced to its original here.
- Whether Marshall Keeble became president of the Nashville Christian Institute in 1940 or 1942,
  and the date and circumstances of the school's closure and the later litigation over its assets.
- Whether the Sand Creek Address and Declaration was read on August 17 or August 18, 1889.
- The exact 1906 census figures — two tabulations circulate (see Part 5).
- Current non-institutional congregation counts: 1,898 (2009), ~2,055, and ~2,757 (2020) all
  appear in directory-derived sources and cannot all be right.
- Whether the Church Research Council's new directory edition, planned for 2026, has appeared.
- Any worldwide membership figure.
