# Institutions of the Churches of Christ

Colleges, publications, missions, benevolence, media, lectureships, camps, and hymnody
organizations of the a cappella **Churches of Christ**.

> **The governing fact.** Churches of Christ have no denominational structure — no convention,
> no board, no headquarters. Every institution on this page is therefore **either (a) a
> privately-chartered corporation with its own board, or (b) a work overseen by the elders of one
> local congregation that other congregations send money to (the "sponsoring church" model).**
> Which of the two it is, is the question the 1950s division was fought over. Get it right for
> each institution and the whole landscape makes sense.

> **The branch question.** Non-institutional Churches of Christ hold that a local church's
> treasury may not fund human institutions — colleges, orphan homes, missionary organizations —
> nor pool its funds under another congregation's eldership. They are not against the *work*;
> they are against the *funding mechanism*. So they have colleges (one), publishers, lectures,
> and benevolence — but all supported by individuals, never by church contributions. Always
> mark which branch an institution serves.

Every fact below carries a source. Facts that could not be confirmed are marked **unverified**;
do not fill them in from memory.

### 1.2 Florida College — the non-institutional exception, explained precisely

This is the single most misunderstood institution in the tradition, and getting it right signals
that a writer understands the division.

- **Chartered 1944** by a group of Florida businessmen and church leaders; **opened in the fall of
  1946 with 100 students** as Florida Christian College; renamed **Florida College** in 1963.
  **SACSCOC-accredited since 1954**; 591 students in 2023-24; 17 bachelor's degrees plus an A.A.
- **It was a two-year junior college until 1996**, when it added its first four-year degree (in
  Bible). Many students still transfer out after two or three years. Do not describe it as a
  four-year college for any period before 1996.
- Its charter **requires every trustee to be an active member of a local, non-institutional
  church of Christ.**
- It **refuses donations from churches** — and the practice goes further than the checkbook: it
  "won't accept donations from churches **or even send a choral group to sing at a church**."
  It "supports itself entirely through the donations from individuals and the tuition paid by
  students."
- **~90% of its students come from non-institutional Churches of Christ.**
- **How it recruits, since it cannot recruit through congregations:** it runs **21 one-week summer
  Bible camps** across the country, drawing **4,000 young people** and involving about **500
  volunteers** — its major recruiting tool. Named examples: Carolinas Mountain Camp (Flat Rock,
  NC) and a camp at Oregonia, Ohio. Public-relations director Ralph Walker: "Since Florida
  College doesn't recruit through churches… we have to work a little harder to find out who our
  kids are and where they come from. That's why our camps are so important to us."
- **How the college itself frames the difference.** President **C. G. "Colly" Caldwell**
  (a student from 1961, president from 1991), asked whether institutionalism is a salvation issue
  or a matter of conscience: "We would consider it on the same basis that we would, say, with the
  Christian Church over instrumental music or the missionary society." That is a fellowship
  answer, not a conscience answer — worth knowing before characterizing the branch as relaxed
  about the question.
- **How it was treated.** By the end of the 1950s the *Gospel Advocate* "had targeted the college
  as a haven for radicals and marked its teachers and students as 'antis'"
  (*Encyclopedia of the Stone-Campbell Movement*, quoted in the Chronicle piece).
- It has **no legal or financial connection to any congregation** — which is exactly the point:
  a non-institutional Christian may give to a college **as an individual**; his congregation may
  not give to it **from the treasury**. The college is therefore not a "church institution" at
  all in the sense the 1950s controversy meant.

Sources: Bobby Ross Jr., **"A non-institutional institution," The Christian Chronicle, April 2008
print edition** — the live URL is dead; text read from the Internet Archive capture of
`christianchronicle.org/article2158290~A_non-institutional_institution` (snapshot 2008-08-28).
[Wikipedia: Florida College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_College);
[floridacollege.edu](https://www.floridacollege.edu/about).

**Consequence:** Florida College is the only degree-granting college associated with
non-institutional churches. There is no non-institutional equivalent of Harding or ACU, and there
is no non-institutional college system. Preacher training among non-institutional brethren
happens through the local congregation, through Florida College's Biblical Studies program, and
through individually-supported study programs — not through a brotherhood school. See §4.3.

## 2. Publications and publishers

**Why this section matters more than it looks.** With no bishops and no convention, **the editors
of the papers were the closest thing to authority the tradition ever had.** Doctrine was argued,
and fellowship was withdrawn, in print. B. C. Goodpasture's call in the *Gospel Advocate*
(December 1954) for a "yellow tag of quarantine" on non-institutional preachers is the classic
case of an editor functioning as a disciplinary body (Harrell, *The Churches of Christ in the
20th Century*, Univ. of Alabama Press, 2000). Richard Hughes describes Goodpasture's method as
cutting his losses and consolidating his assets (*Reviving the Ancient Faith*, p. 238).

**What the quarantine looked like on the ground** — from a retrospective written inside the
non-institutional wing, so read it as a partisan account of events it nonetheless documents:
Steve Wolfgang, ["History and Background of the Institutional Controversy (3): The Yellow Tag of
Quarantine," *Guardian of Truth* XXXIII:9 (May 4 1989),
pp. 272–275](http://www.truthmagazine.com/archives/volume33/GOT033130.htm). Wolfgang reports
cancelled gospel meetings and withdrawn outside support for preachers; deacons and treasurers
told to write a check to an institution or resign; congregations rationalising "a $10 or $20
monthly orphanage donation on the grounds of 'showing that we're not anti'"; "no anti need apply"
in preacher-wanted ads; "James 1:27" and "Galatians 6:10" painted on church signs as a loyalty
marker; property lawsuits reported in metropolitan dailies; and published "confessions" in the
*Gospel Advocate* by men who recanted the non-institutional position — he names Earl West, Pat
Hardeman, Hugo McCord, C. M. Pullias, and John D. Cox. He also notes the pressure fell hardest on
"the newer schools such as Florida Christian College," which could resist only at cost to
enrolment and donations.

**On Foy E. Wallace Jr. (1896–1979), a caution.** He edited the *Gospel Advocate* **1930–34** and
founded the ***Bible Banner*** in **1938**, and his 1 Peter 5:2 argument against "brotherhood
elders" is the sharpest early statement of the non-institutional case. But he is **not a clean
representative of either side**: by the mid-1960s he had abandoned the non-institutional position
and associated exclusively with institutional churches. He is also on record for an explicitly
segregationist 1941 *Bible Banner* article, "Negro Meetings for White People" (quoted in
`culture-and-politics.md` §5.2). Know both before citing him approvingly.

## 3. Missions, benevolence, and media

### 3.1 Herald of Truth — the flashpoint

**What it was.** A nationwide radio (later television) preaching program, produced under the
oversight of the elders of the **Fifth and Highland / Highland church of Christ, Abilene, Texas**,
and funded by contributions from congregations across the country.

- **First network broadcast: February 10, 1952**, on **31 ABC stations**, from Fair Park
  Auditorium, Abilene ([Christian Chronicle, Apr 1 2012, reprinting Loretta Fulton, *Abilene
  Reporter-News*](https://christianchronicle.org/60-years-of-herald-of-truth/); corroborated by
  Logan Buchanan, *Gospel Guardian* 5:34, Jan 7 1954). The **1951** date that circulates refers to
  the regional precursor network, not the national launch.
- **Originator: James Walter Nichols (1927–1973)**, a recent Abilene Christian graduate running a
  local program called *Herald of Truth* in Iowa, who took the idea to the Highland elders.
  **James D. Willeford** ran a parallel program in Madison, Wisconsin; the two merged. Both
  preached on it. Willeford is often loosely called "the founder" because he was the long-running
  radio speaker; **the organizing initiative is attributed to Nichols** in the verifiable sources.
- **Batsell Barrett Baxter** became the television voice and face in **1959** and remained until
  his **death in 1982**. Herald of Truth's own history page gives the succession of broadcasters
  as **Nichols → Willeford → Harold Hazelip → Baxter**.
- **It was not the first sponsoring-church work, only the biggest.** Earlier precedents: the
  **Broadway church of Christ, Lubbock** raising funds for work in Germany, and the **Union Avenue
  church of Christ, Memphis** for Japan.
- **Television from February 12, 1954** (DuMont network); **152 TV stations** by the 1970s.

**Why it split the fellowship.** Not the preaching — the *structure*. Highland's own signed
elders' statement (*Gospel Guardian* 5:39, Feb 11 1954) put the claim plainly:

> "The Herald of Truth radio program is a work of the church of Christ at Fifth and Highland,
> Abilene, Texas. The elders of this congregation direct and oversee every phase of this work…
> The Highland Church merely asks sister congregations to have fellowship with her in this work…
> The Herald of Truth radio program is not the church or one of the churches. **It has no
> organization of its own**… since this is a work of the Highland congregation, to maintain its
> autonomy or independence **the elders must make the decisions**."

The non-institutional reply, at its sharpest, is **James W. Adams, "Is The Herald Of Truth A
Missionary Society?", *Gospel Guardian* vol. 5 no. 43, March 11 1954**
([full text](https://www.wordsfitlyspoken.org/gospel_guardian/v5/v5n43p1,13.html)):

> "(3) Each has its official board. The society has a board made up of members of different
> churches. 'The Herald of Truth' has a board consisting of **the elders of one church**… the
> board of directors of the society did not have any more authority than the Highland elders
> possess.
> (4) The society has its contributing members. 'The Herald of Truth' has its contributing
> members, **over 1000 of them**…"

and the argument in one sentence:

> "The only practical difference being that in our 'cooperations' the contributors **surrender
> all right to the management of their money**, while in the 'cooperations' of 100 years ago, the
> brethren thought it more equitable to retain at least a voting privilege in the matter of
> management."

That is the whole objection: the Churches of Christ had separated from the Disciples in part over
the **American Christian Missionary Society** (Cincinnati, October 1849), on the ground that a
para-congregational agency for pooled evangelism has no New Testament authority. Non-institutional
brethren argued the sponsoring church reproduced the society exactly, and on accountability was
*worse*, since the 19th-century society at least let contributors vote. **Foy E. Wallace Jr.**
argued from 1 Peter 5:2 that elders may oversee only "the flock among you," and that sponsoring
churches create "brotherhood elders" and "a system of religious feudalism"
([Truth Magazine](https://www.truthmagazine.com/the-sponsoring-church-4)).

FY2021–FY2023 it spent well above revenue each year (FY2023: $806,355 revenue against $1,330,392

### 3.5 How non-institutional churches accomplish the same ends differently

The distinctions below are what a writer most often gets wrong. Non-institutional churches are
**not** against evangelism, benevolence, education, or preacher support. They are against a
particular *funding mechanism*.

1. **The individual/congregational distinction.** "What the individual may do, the church may not
   necessarily do." A Christian may give to a college, a children's home, or a magazine as an
   individual; the congregation's treasury may not. This is the load-bearing principle and it
   explains Florida College (§1.2), Sacred Selections (§3.3), and the entire non-institutional
   institutional landscape.
2. **Direct congregational support of preachers**, rather than routing money through a sponsoring
   church or a society. The proof texts are **2 Corinthians 11:8** ("I robbed other churches,
   taking wages of them, to do you service") and **Philippians 4:15–16** — churches sending
   *directly* to the man in the field. A non-institutional congregation supporting a missionary
   sends him a check; it does not send a check to another congregation's eldership to forward.
3. **No pooling under one eldership.** 1 Peter 5:2 — elders oversee "the flock among you," not a
   brotherhood work. This is the Wallace argument and it is still the operative one.
4. **Benevolence: a genuine internal disagreement.** Whether a congregation's treasury may
   relieve non-Christians, or only "saints," is **disputed among non-institutional churches
   themselves** — some hold to saints-only from Acts 11:29, 2 Corinthians 8–9, and Romans 15:26;
   others hold that Galatians 6:10 authorizes general benevolence from the treasury. **Do not
   state a single non-institutional position on this.** Individual Christians relieving anyone in
   need is not in dispute on any side.
5. **No fellowship halls, kitchens, or gymnasiums.** Most non-institutional congregations hold
   that the treasury and the building exist for worship, teaching, benevolence, and evangelism —
   not for social meals or recreation
   ([Wikipedia: Churches of Christ (non-institutional)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churches_of_Christ_(non-institutional))).
   Meals happen in homes.
6. **Preacher training without a brotherhood school.** **No formal school of preaching serving
   non-institutional churches exists** — a negative reached independently by two research passes,
   including a check of *Truth Magazine*'s site, which mentions no school, institute, or
   apprenticeship program anywhere in its content. Men are trained through local-congregation
   apprenticeship and through Florida College's Biblical Studies program on the
   individual-support model. **The absence is not an oversight; it is the doctrine applied to
   itself.** Every school in §1.4 is either a chartered corporation soliciting church money or a
   work under a sponsoring eldership — both of which the branch rejects.
7. **A separate sub-question, do not merge it with the above.** The **one-cup** and
   **non-class** churches broke away over the Lord's Supper and Bible classes and did so **well
   before** the 1950s institutional division (Williams, Foster & Blowers, *The Stone-Campbell
   Movement: A Global History*, Chalice Press, 2013, pp. 234–235). They are almost always
   non-institutional as well, but they are not the same body as the mid-century non-institutional
   churches, and *Truth Magazine*/Florida College brethren are not one-cup.

### 4.2 Camps, youth programs, and Bible Bowl

### 4.3 Hymnody

The a cappella requirement makes congregational song a craft the fellowship has to sustain
itself, and it has built real institutions to do it.

**Sumphonia** — "Composition and Publication of Congregational Hymns"
([sumphonia.org](https://sumphonia.org/)).
- Publishes the hymnal ***Psalms, Hymns, and Spiritual Songs*** — production began **2006**,
  published **2012**, **850 hymns** (550 familiar, 300 new), assembled by an editorial board of
  "30 hymn writers, tune composers, and worship leaders, as well as elders, evangelists,
  teachers"; now in its 5th printing, in Pew, PowerPoint, and FlexCase editions
  ([sumphonia.org/products/hymnal](https://sumphonia.org/products/hymnal/)).
- Runs the **Hymninar**, an annual week-long hymn-writing workshop: one day of instruction in the
  rules of hymn writing, then hands-on work, with every participant required to produce at least
  one publishable verse. Next virtual session June 15–19, 2026
  ([sumphonia.org/hymninar](https://sumphonia.org/hymninar/)).
- Produces the **Voices of Sumphonia** recordings, on Spotify and YouTube.
- ⚠️ **Sumphonia's own site makes no denominational statement**; its Churches of Christ context is
  evident from its participants and its a cappella premise, and from The Christian Chronicle
  having covered a Sumphonia recording session ("Voices only," christianchronicle.org). Founding
  year, leadership, and legal base are **unverified** — the /about/ page 404s.
