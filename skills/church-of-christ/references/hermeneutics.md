# How Churches of Christ Read the Bible

The interpretive method matters more than any single doctrine. Almost every distinctive
position in Churches of Christ — a cappella singing, weekly communion, baptism for the
remission of sins, elders rather than a pastor, the refusal of missionary societies — is
downstream of one hermeneutic. And almost every division *inside* the movement is a
disagreement about how to apply that same hermeneutic, not about whether to use it.

> **Scope note.** "Churches of Christ believe X" is usually a false sentence. The a cappella
> Churches of Christ are a family of sub-groups that share a method and differ on its output.
> Where the **non-institutional** position differs from the mainstream it is flagged, since the
> speakers documented in this repo ([Matt Bassford](../../matt-bassford/SKILL.md),
> [Josh Tolbert](../../josh-tolbert/SKILL.md), Brother Jim) are non-institutional.

**Related references:** [doctrine.md](doctrine.md) for the positions this method produces ·
[scripture-index.md](scripture-index.md) for the passages · [history.md](history.md),
[timeline.md](timeline.md), [divisions.md](divisions.md),
[non-institutional.md](non-institutional.md) for how the arguments played out in time.

## 1. The controlling question: "Where is your authority?"

The tradition's characteristic question is not *what does this passage mean to me* but
**by what authority is this done?** Every practice must be traceable to the New Testament
either directly or by an argument the tradition regards as valid. The standard proof text
for the principle itself is **Col 3:17** — *"whatsoever ye do in word or deed, do all in the
name of the Lord Jesus"* — read as "by his authority." **Col 3:17 is the hermeneutic's own
proof text**, and you will hear it quoted at the opening of any lesson on Bible authority.
Supporting texts: **1 Pet 4:11** (*"if any man speak, let him speak as the oracles of God"*),
**2 John 9** (*"whosoever transgresseth, and abideth not in the doctrine of Christ, hath not
God"*), **1 Cor 4:6** (*"not to think of men above that which is written"*), **Jude 3**
(*"the faith which was once delivered unto the saints"*).

A fair statement of the tradition's own self-understanding: it is not trying to be clever.
It is trying to do only what it can show God asked for, on the conviction that the alternative
is worship shaped by human preference.

## 2. CENI — Command, Approved Example, Necessary Inference

### The three modes

Scripture authorizes in three ways. If a practice is established by any one of them, it is
binding; if by none of them, it is unauthorized.

| Mode | What it means | Classic instance |
|---|---|---|
| **Command** (or direct statement/precept) | An explicit imperative or apostolic instruction | *"Repent, and be baptized every one of you"* (Acts 2:38) |
| **Approved (apostolic) example** | A recorded practice of the NT church that carries divine approval | The disciples at Troas came together *"upon the first day of the week … to break bread"* (Acts 20:7) |
| **Necessary inference** | A conclusion that *must* follow from what is stated — not merely one that may | The Ethiopian *"went down … into the water"* and came *"up out of the water"* (Acts 8:38-39), therefore baptism is immersion |

Often written **CENI** or **CEI**; older writers say "command, example, and necessary
inference." Some conservative teachers now prefer "**direct statement, approved example,
and implication**" — arguing that God *implies* and man *infers*, so what binds is the
implication in the text, not the reader's inference. The substance is unchanged.

### Where it comes from

- **Thomas Campbell, *Declaration and Address* (1809)** supplies the raw material.
  Proposition 3: *"nothing ought to be inculcated upon christians as articles of faith; nor
  required of them as terms of communion; but what is expressly taught, and enjoined upon
  them, in the word of God."* Proposition 4 makes the New Testament *"as perfect a
  constitution for the worship, discipline and government of the New Testament church … as
  the Old Testament was for the Old Testament church."*
  ([full text, ACU Restoration Movement Texts](https://webfiles.acu.edu/departments/Library/HR/restmov_nov11/www.mun.ca/rels/restmov/texts/tcampbell/da/DA-1ST.HTM))
- **Alexander Campbell** made inference load-bearing. Michael Casey's study argues Campbell's
  *increasing* use of necessary inference is what marked him as a moderate and that resistance
  to it split the movement long before his death in 1866
  ([*The Battle over Hermeneutics in the Stone-Campbell Movement, 1800-1870*, Edwin Mellen, 1998](https://archive.org/details/battleoverhermen0000case)).
- **J. S. Lamar, *The Organon of Scripture* (1859)** — the movement's first formal hermeneutics
  textbook, explicitly Baconian. **D. R. Dungan, *Hermeneutics: A Text-Book* (1888)** was still
  a classroom text at Freed-Hardeman into the 1970s
  ([John Mark Hicks](https://johnmarkhicks.com/2008/05/29/stone-campbell-hermeneutics-iii-baconian-hermeneutics-and-churches-of-christ/)).
- **J. D. Thomas, *We Be Brethren* (1958)** is the 20th-century codification and the version
  most preachers actually learned. As John Mark Hicks summarizes it, Thomas's "pattern
  principle" holds that what bound the New Testament characters binds us and what did not bind
  them does not bind us, with the pattern established by command, example, and necessary
  inference; Thomas supplied a "standard authority diagram" for deciding when a specific is
  binding, optional, or excluded, and a rule for when an example binds. Written during the
  institutional controversy — and criticized from the non-institutional side at the time
  ([Gospel Guardian review, 1959](https://www.wordsfitlyspoken.org/gospel_guardian/v11/v11n33p1,10-14a.html);
  [Hicks on Thomas](https://johnmarkhicks.com/2009/02/09/patterns-legalism-and-grace-j-d-thomas/)).

The **acronym** CENI is 20th-century shorthand; the three-part scheme is much older and has
Reformed and Puritan analogues. Do not write that "Alexander Campbell invented CENI."

### How it is actually applied

The honest description — which careful CoC teachers concede — is that not every example binds.
Everyone agrees Paul preaching from an upper room at Troas until midnight (Acts 20:7-9) does
not require upper rooms or midnight, while *first day of the week* and *break bread* do bind.
The working criteria used to sort them:

1. **Is the example tied to a command or a stated purpose?** Thomas's rule: an example binds
   when an implied command stands behind it.
2. **Is the detail incidental (circumstance) or essential (element)?** The *action* is
   binding; the *arrangements* for performing it are not.
3. **Is the practice uniform across the New Testament, or a one-off?**
4. **Does the practice belong to the nature of the act, or to the culture of the actors?**

Critics inside and outside the movement press exactly here: the sorting criteria are not
themselves stated in Scripture, so the hermeneutic imports a judgment it claims to avoid.
The standard counter is that this is true of *every* interpretive method, and that the
alternative — no criteria — is not humility but license.

**Common outsider misstatement:** that CENI means "if it isn't in the Bible you can't do it,"
full stop. It does not; see generic authority and expedients below (§6). The claim is
narrower and more interesting than the caricature.

## 3. Silence — the single most consequential principle

### The slogan

Thomas Campbell, summer 1809, at Abraham Altars' home near Washington, Pennsylvania:

> *"Where the Scriptures speak, we speak; and where the Scriptures are silent, we are silent."*

Robert Richardson's account reports the room fell into *"a solemn silence"* and that the words
were *"for ever engraven upon their hearts"*
([Christian Restoration Association](https://www.thecra.org/article/42)).
It is the most quoted line in the movement's history.

> **The slogan is not in the *Declaration and Address*.** Checked directly against the text: the
> sentence does not appear there, in that form or any close variant. It is a **spoken** remark
> from a meeting, preserved in Richardson's later account, that the movement retroactively made
> its motto. The printed document's own nearest equivalent is milder and is about *terms of
> communion*, not about acting: *"nothing ought to be received into the faith or worship of the
> church; or be made a term of communion amongst christians, that is not as old as the New
> Testament."* Both insiders and outsiders routinely cite the slogan as though it were a clause
> of the founding document. It is not, and a writer quoting it should say *Thomas Campbell,
> 1809* — not *the Declaration and Address*.

**A frequently repeated but poorly sourced detail:** many retellings add that Andrew Munro
immediately objected that the principle would end infant baptism. That anecdote circulates
widely in popular CoC and Christian Church histories; **treat the exact wording as
unverified** unless you can check Richardson's *Memoirs of Alexander Campbell* directly.

### The fork: does silence prohibit or permit?

The slogan is ambiguous, and the movement broke on the ambiguity.

| | **Regulative / prohibitive silence** | **Permissive silence** |
|---|---|---|
| Held by | Churches of Christ (a cappella) | Christian Churches / Churches of Christ (independent), Disciples |
| Rule | What God has not authorized is forbidden | What God has not forbidden is permitted, if edifying and orderly |
| Reading of the slogan | "silent" = *we do not act* where God has not spoken | "silent" = *we do not legislate* where God has not spoken |
| Applied to instruments | Not authorized → excluded | Not forbidden → a matter of liberty |

That one divergence generates most of the movement's divisions. It is the reason the same
family of churches contains congregations that sing without instruments and congregations
that do not.

Wikipedia's summary is accurate on the point: the instrumental Christian Churches *"view
scriptural silence on an issue more permissively"*
([Churches of Christ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churches_of_Christ)).

### The scriptural case for prohibitive silence

These four arguments recur in nearly every CoC lesson on authority. All four verses below
are quoted here from the KJV text in this repo (`ios/swiftbible/Text/bible.json`).

1. **Nadab and Abihu — Lev 10:1-2; Num 3:4.** They *"offered strange fire before the LORD,
   **which he commanded them not**"* and fire consumed them. The argument: God had not
   *forbidden* that fire; he had simply not authorized it, and that was enough. Num 3:4
   repeats the indictment. This is *the* silence text.
2. **Uzza and the ark — 1 Chr 13:9-10; 15:2, 13.** The cart was not prohibited; it was
   unauthorized. David's own verdict: *"we sought him not after the due order."*
3. **Hebrews 7:12-14.** *"For it is evident that our Lord sprang out of Juda; of which tribe
   Moses spake nothing concerning priesthood."* The Hebrew writer's argument turns on what
   Moses **did not say**. CoC teachers regard this as the New Testament explicitly reasoning
   from silence, and it is the strongest text for the principle.
4. **Jeremiah 7:31; Col 2:22-23.** *"which I commanded them not, neither came it into my
   heart"*; *"will worship."*

Wayne Jackson's [*The Silence of the Scriptures: Permissive or Prohibitive?*](https://christiancourier.com/articles/the-silence-of-the-scriptures-permissive-or-prohibitive)
(Christian Courier — mainstream, partisan) is a representative full statement and also quotes
Tertullian: *"what has not been freely allowed is forbidden."*

### The honest counter-pressure, stated fairly

- **Heb 7:14 argues from silence within a covenant that specified its priesthood.** Critics say
  the inference works because the Law positively assigned priesthood to Levi, not because
  silence is prohibitive as a general rule.
- **Nadab and Abihu / Uzza sit inside a legislated cultic system** with named penalties. Whether
  that transfers to the New Covenant is precisely the disputed step.
- **Thomas Campbell's own Proposition 6** cuts against the later practice:
  *"inferences and deductions from scripture premises, when fairly inferred, may be truly
  called the doctrine of God's holy word: yet are they not formally binding upon the
  consciences of christians farther than they perceive the connection."* The founding document
  denies that inference may be made a term of communion — which is exactly what 20th-century
  practice made it. Progressives cite this constantly; conservatives answer that Campbell was
  guarding against *human* inference wrongly drawn, not against implication genuinely present
  in the text.

## 4. The three dispensations — and why arguments come from the New Testament

### The scheme

**Patriarchal → Mosaic → Christian.** Alexander Campbell laid it out in the **Sermon on the
Law**, preached to the Redstone Baptist Association at Cross Creek, Brooke County, Virginia,
1 September 1816, and printed at Steubenville, Ohio, in 1816
([text, ACU](https://webfiles.acu.edu/departments/Library/HR/restmov_nov11/www.mun.ca/rels/restmov/texts/acampbell/mh1846/SOTL.HTM);
[Disciples Historical Society](https://digitalcommons.discipleshistory.org/all_foundationaldocuments/9/)).
Campbell compared the three to infancy/childhood/adulthood and to **starlight, moonlight,
sunlight**. Both figures are still used from CoC pulpits.

The claim: the Law of Moses was given to Israel, was fulfilled and removed at the cross, and
is not the Christian's law. Texts: **Col 2:14** (*"nailing it to his cross"*), **Eph 2:14-16**
(*"the law of commandments contained in ordinances"* abolished), **Gal 3:24-25** (*"the law was
our schoolmaster … we are no longer under a schoolmaster"*), **Rom 7:4**, **2 Cor 3:7-11**,
**Heb 8:6-13** (*"he hath made the first old"*), **Heb 9:16-17** (a testament is in force after
the death of the testator — the "the New Testament began at the cross" argument).

The counterweight, and the reason Old Testament preaching is not abandoned: **Rom 15:4** —
*"whatsoever things were written aforetime were written for our learning"* — and **1 Cor 10:11**.
The slogan version: **the Old Testament is for our learning; the New Testament is our law.**

### Consequences you will feel when drafting

- **Doctrinal arguments run almost entirely from Acts through Revelation.** An OT text is
  normally used for illustration, example, or the character of God, not to establish a
  practice. The exception is instructive: the silence argument itself leans on Leviticus and
  1 Chronicles — used as *precedent for how God treats unauthorized worship*, not as law.
- Tithing is generally not taught as binding (it is Mosaic); giving is taught from
  **1 Cor 16:1-2** and **2 Cor 9:6-7**, "as he prospers," "purposed in his heart."
- Sabbath is not transferred to Sunday; the first day is defended from **Acts 20:7** and
  **1 Cor 16:2** as apostolic practice, not as a Christian sabbath.
- Instrumental music in the Psalms and in the temple (**2 Chr 29:25**) is treated as belonging
  to a removed covenant. This is the hinge of the OT counter-argument (see doctrine.md).
- Historic critics — including some inside the movement — call the effect functionally
  **Marcionite**, an over-thin use of three-quarters of the Bible. Defenders answer that
  "not our law" is not "not our Scripture," and point to how heavily CoC preaching actually
  uses OT narrative.

## 5. Pattern, blueprint, and the ancient order

The restoration ideal: the New Testament contains a recoverable **pattern** for the church
— its worship, work, organization, and terms of entrance — and a congregation is faithful to
the degree it conforms to that pattern. Alexander Campbell's *Christian Baptist* series
**"A Restoration of the Ancient Order of Things"** (1825-1829) is the source of the phrase
*ancient order*.

Texts pressed into service for the idea of a pattern: **2 Tim 1:13** (*"hold fast the form of
sound words"*), **Heb 8:5** (Moses commanded to make all things *"according to the pattern"*),
**Phil 3:17**, **2 Thess 2:15**.

Critics call this **"blueprint hermeneutics."** John Mark Hicks — himself from within Churches
of Christ, and the most substantial internal critic — argues Paul never appeals to a
blueprint of church practice, but grounds obedience in *"the pattern manifested in the
incarnation, life, death, resurrection, and exaltation of Jesus"*
([*Searching for the Pattern*, 2019](https://johnmarkhicks.com/category/hermeneutics/)).

**Non-institutional note.** Pattern language is *strongest* in non-institutional churches,
because the institutional controversy was fought precisely over whether the NT pattern
authorizes church-supported institutions and inter-congregational structures. Where a
mainstream congregation may talk about "the pattern" loosely, a non-institutional preacher
means it as a working test.

## 6. Generic and specific authority; expedient vs. addition

**This is the workhorse.** Nearly every intra-movement argument — instruments, orphan homes,
sponsoring churches, kitchens, multiple cups, Sunday school classes, located preachers — is
argued on this distinction. Learn it and most CoC disputes become legible.

### The rule

> **Generic authority includes; specific authority excludes.**

When God specifies *what*, all alternatives to that *what* are excluded. When God commands an
end without specifying the means, any lawful means is authorized.

| Command | Specified | Therefore excluded | Therefore free (expedients) |
|---|---|---|---|
| *"Make thee an ark of **gopher wood**"* (Gen 6:14) | the material | oak, pine | saws, hammers, scaffolding |
| *"**Go** … preach the gospel"* (Mark 16:15) | the going, not the mode | nothing about transport | walking, ship, car, plane |
| *"**Sing** … psalms and hymns and spiritual songs"* (Eph 5:19; Col 3:16) | the kind of music — vocal | playing (a different kind of music) | song books, pitch pipe, song leader, meeting house |
| *"**Baptizing** them"* (Matt 28:19) | immersion in water | sprinkling, pouring | baptistry, clothing, heater |
| *"Upon the first day of the week let every one of you **lay by him in store**"* (1 Cor 16:2) | the day and the giver | fundraisers, raffles, selling to outsiders | collection plate, envelopes, online transfer |

### Aid vs. addition — the church-building-and-songbook argument

The perennial objection is: *"You have no verse for a church building, a songbook, or a
communion tray, so you're inconsistent."* The standard answer:

- An **aid (expedient)** helps perform the thing commanded without changing its kind. A song
  book aids *singing*; the congregation still sings. A building aids *assembling*
  (Heb 10:25); the assembly is still an assembly. A tray aids *distributing* the supper.
- An **addition** introduces a *different kind* of thing than what was specified. An
  instrument adds *playing* to *singing* — a second kind of music, not an aid to the first.

The classic condensed form: *a song book helps you do what God said; a piano does something
God didn't say.* Expedients must additionally be (a) within the scope of something
authorized, (b) genuinely helpful, and (c) not a stumbling block (1 Cor 6:12; 10:23; Rom 14).

Representative statements: [Focus](https://focusmagazine.org/generic-and-specific-authority.php),
[Knollwood church of Christ](https://www.knollwoodchurch.org/yr2025/e03_authority_aids_and_additions.html),
[Truth Magazine](https://www.truthmagazine.com/make-thee-an-ark-of-gopher-wood) (all partisan).

### Where it is contested

Everyone in the movement accepts the rule; the fights are over **what counts as the same kind
of thing.** The instrumental Christian Church says an instrument is an aid to singing, exactly
as a songbook is. Institutional churches say a benevolent society is an aid to the church's
benevolence. Non-institutional churches say both are additions — a *different organization*
doing the church's work is not an aid to the church doing it. The disagreement is never about
the principle. It is always about the classification.

## 7. Why the argumentation reads like a legal brief

### The historians' case

Scholars of the movement — Richard Hughes, Michael Casey, Thomas Olbricht, John Mark Hicks —
argue that the method carries a specific Enlightenment inheritance:

- **Lockean empiricism**: Alexander Campbell read Locke closely; faith is assent to testimony,
  and the Bible is a body of testimony to be examined.
- **Scottish Common Sense Realism** (Reid, Stewart): the plain sense of the text is available
  to any sincere reader; the Bible means what an ordinary honest person would take it to mean.
  This underwrites the movement's deep confidence that unity is achievable if people will just
  read.
- **Baconian induction**: gather all the facts, classify, generalize, deduce. Hughes describes
  Churches of Christ as formed by the fusion of *Campbell's Baconian Common Sense Realism* with
  *Barton Stone's apocalyptic theology*
  (*Reviving the Ancient Faith*, Eerdmans; 3rd ed. with James L. Gorman, 2024 —
  [review](https://www.bobcornwall.com/2024/02/reviving-ancient-faith-story-of.html)).
- The textbook expression is **Lamar's *Organon*** (1859), whose governing image is
  Scripture as scattered blocks of a temple to be arranged into *"the precise order designed
  by their Author."* Hicks notes the sting: Lamar himself abandoned the method by the end of
  his life, while the churches kept it for another century
  ([Hicks](https://johnmarkhicks.com/2008/05/29/stone-campbell-hermeneutics-iii-baconian-hermeneutics-and-churches-of-christ/)).

The practical result is the register you actually hear: propositions, numbered points, the
burden of proof, "book, chapter, and verse," the public debate as a genre, and a preference for
the syllogism over the story. Casey's contribution is to show this is not incidental style but
inherited rhetoric.

### The insider counter-case — state it, don't skip it

Conservative and non-institutional teachers answer, with some force:

1. **Genetic fallacy.** That a method resembles Bacon does not make it wrong. The question is
   whether Scripture *does* authorize by command, example, and implication — and the tradition
   answers yes, citing Heb 7:14 (implication), Acts 15 (apostolic reasoning from precedent),
   and Matt 22:31-32 (Jesus inferring the resurrection from a verb tense in Ex 3:6).
2. **Everyone infers.** Hicks himself concedes that hermeneutics is always inferential; the
   Trinity, the canon, and infant baptism are all inferred. The dispute is about *which*
   inferences bind, not whether inference is legitimate.
3. **The alternative is not neutral.** A "narrative" or "theological" hermeneutic still selects
   which parts of the story norm the church, and that selection is a hermeneutic too — one
   with fewer public checks.
4. **The plain-reading instinct is democratic, not rationalist.** Its point was to take the
   Bible out of the hands of clergy and confessions and put it in the hands of farmers. That is
   its own theological commitment, not a borrowed philosophy.

Both cases are real. Present them as a live argument, not as a debunking.

## 8. Internal critique — the hermeneutics debate

### 1857: the first round

The argument is older than the 20th century. **Robert Richardson** (Bethany College, Campbell's
physician and later biographer) ran a ten-part series, *Faith versus Philosophy*, in the
*Millennial Harbinger* beginning March 1857, charging that **Lockean empiricism had drained the
movement of spirituality**. **Tolbert Fanning** (Franklin College; founder of the *Gospel
Advocate*, 1855) attacked in reply, and Alexander Campbell was drawn in
([Richardson's text, ACU](https://webfiles.acu.edu/departments/Library/HR/restmov_nov11/www.mun.ca/rels/restmov/texts/rrichardson/mh/FVP05.HTM)).
Worth knowing because it shows the "new hermeneutic" complaint is not a modern import.

### 1980s-2000s: the main event

The debate that shaped the present landscape ran roughly from the mid-1980s into the 2000s.

**The critics' charge** (broadly, from the progressive wing):

- CENI plus prohibitive silence produces **legalism** and endless division.
- Scripture is mostly **narrative**, not a legal code or a construction blueprint; a hermeneutic
  built for statutes mishandles the genre it is applied to.
- Better to read **theologically** — through the character of God, the story of Israel, and the
  work of Christ — and let church practice follow the trajectory of that story.
- The tradition applies its own rule inconsistently (holy kiss, foot washing, meeting in homes,
  greeting with a kiss, women's head coverings).

**Key names and works**

| Figure | Contribution |
|---|---|
| **F. LaGard Smith**, *The Cultural Church* (1992) | A *conservative* intervention. Smith examines the call for a "new hermeneutic" and warns that the church risks becoming a "cultural church" — accommodating to the surrounding culture and losing the authority of the written Word. He is a critic **of** the new hermeneutic, not a proponent; do not file him with the progressives. ([Amazon listing](https://www.amazon.com/Cultural-Church-Winds-Change-Hermeneutic/dp/0890981310)) |
| **Thomas H. Olbricht** | Historical-theological work on how the movement has read Scripture; argued for reading the Bible as the story of God's acts. |
| **Michael W. Casey** | Rhetorical history of the movement's hermeneutics; showed inference was contested from the start. |
| **John Mark Hicks** | The most sustained internal alternative: from blueprint to *theological narrative*. See *Searching for the Pattern* (2019) and *Come to the Table* (2002). |
| **Richard T. Hughes** | *Reviving the Ancient Faith* — the standard history; argues the Baconian inheritance and the loss of Stone's apocalyptic gave the movement its legalist cast. |

Traditionalists named the proposal "**the new hermeneutic**" and its advocates "**change
agents**," and the label stuck as a term of criticism. The **1986 "Expression of Concern"** —
signed by hundreds of mainstream preachers, aimed chiefly at theistic evolution at Abilene
Christian University but also naming "diluted and polluted preaching," efforts "to restructure
the organization, worship and work of the church along sectarian lines," and "the typical
emphasis of the denominational world on recreation, entertainment, and solving the social ills
of society" — is a good single document for the temperature of the period
(see [divisions.md](divisions.md) §7).

### How conservative and non-institutional churches answered

The answers are substantive, and a devotional writer should be able to state them:

1. **It proves too much.** If the character of God replaces specific authority, nothing in
   practice is settled — and in fact the congregations that adopted it went on to adopt
   instruments and expanded women's roles, which conservatives read as confirmation.
2. **Narrative does not exclude command.** The Bible is a story that contains commands; noticing
   the story does not repeal the commands inside it.
3. **The inconsistency charge has answers, not just shrugs.** The holy kiss is a *generic*
   command (greet one another) with a culturally specific *form*; the day of the week for the
   supper is a specific. Whether that distinction holds is exactly the argument — but it is an
   argument, not an evasion.
4. **Fellowship consequences are the real stake.** If nothing can be established by implication,
   nothing can be a test of fellowship, and the historic reasons for a distinct people dissolve.
5. **Non-institutional churches were largely insulated.** The hermeneutics debate happened
   mostly in institutional and university circles (Abilene Christian, Pepperdine, Lipscomb,
   *Image* and *Wineskins* magazines). Non-institutional churches, having already fought a
   division *over* the strict application of authority in the 1950s, mostly regarded the 1990s
   debate as the predictable next step of the same error, and continued teaching CENI plus
   generic/specific authority essentially unchanged. Truth Magazine and Florida College
   lectureship material from this period reads as continuous with the 1950s, not reactive.

## 9. Compared with other traditions — briefly and fairly

| Tradition | Authority structure | Silence | Where it collides with CoC |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reformed / Presbyterian** | Scripture alone, but read through confessions (Westminster) and covenant theology | **Regulative principle**, WCF 21.1: God may not be worshiped *"any other way not prescribed in the Holy Scripture"* — nearest cousin to the CoC position | Shares regulative silence. Differs on covenant continuity (one covenant of grace, hence infant baptism), on election and perseverance, and on binding confessions |
| **Lutheran / Anglican / Methodist** | Scripture supreme, tradition ordering | **Normative principle** — what Scripture does not forbid may be retained if it serves order and peace (cf. Augsburg Confession, *Of Usages in the Church*) | Directly opposite on silence; the instrument question never arises |
| **Roman Catholic** | Scripture *and* Tradition as one deposit, interpreted authoritatively by the Magisterium (*Dei Verbum*) | The question does not have the same shape: the living teaching office resolves it | CoC rejects the whole structure — no post-apostolic development, no teaching office, no creed. CoC teachers usually appreciate patristic *witness* while denying patristic *authority* |
| **Baptist / broad evangelical** | Scripture alone; soul competency and local autonomy; congregational polity | Mostly permissive in worship, restrictive in soteriology | Very close on polity, autonomy, immersion, and believer's baptism — and the sharpest opponent on the *meaning* of baptism (sign of a salvation already received vs. the point at which one is saved). Historically the Baptist-CoC debate is the movement's home turf |
| **Restorationist cousins (Christian Churches / Disciples)** | Same founding documents, same slogans | Permissive | The whole division. Same Bible, same heroes, opposite reading of one sentence |

Do not caricature. Reformed Christians are not "adding to the Bible," Catholics are not
"ignoring it," and Baptists are not "denying that baptism matters." Each has a coherent account
of authority; the CoC disagreement is real and specific, and stating it accurately is more
persuasive than flattening it.

---

## 10. Using this when drafting devotional content

- **Establish practice from the New Testament.** An OT text used to prove a church practice will
  read wrong to this audience; an OT text used for narrative, character, or example is welcome
  and extremely common.
- **Prefer "authority" language to "preference" language** when touching worship.
- **Never write "the Church" as an institution.** Write the local congregation. This is
  ecclesiology, not style (see Matt Bassford's pushback flags).
- **Beware "just accept Jesus into your heart," "ask Jesus into your life," "say this prayer."**
  These are the single most common tonal misfires; see doctrine.md §1.
- **If a draft touches instruments, women's roles, divorce and remarriage, or institutional
  cooperation, stop and check.** Those are the four places a sentence written casually will land
  as a position taken.
- **Assume the reader knows the arguments.** This is a highly Bible-literate audience that has
  heard "book, chapter, and verse" its whole life. Vagueness reads as evasion.
