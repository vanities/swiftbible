# Culture, Demographics, and Politics of the Churches of Christ

Reference for the a cappella **Churches of Christ** — the Restoration Movement stream that
separated from the Disciples of Christ (recognized in the 1906 U.S. Religious Census) and
divided again over institutional support in the 1950s–60s.

> **Not the same as:** the United Church of Christ (UCC), the Christian Church (Disciples of
> Christ), the instrumental Christian Churches/Churches of Christ, the Church of God in Christ,
> or the International Churches of Christ (ICOC). Survey and census tables routinely confuse
> these. Check which body a figure actually measures before repeating it.

**Everything below carries a source and a year.** Where a survey does not break out Churches of
Christ separately, that is said in plain words rather than papered over with an evangelical
average. Where a figure could not be confirmed, it is marked **unverified**.

---

## 1. Why the numbers are hard

Churches of Christ have **no denomination, no headquarters, no convention, and no membership
rolls above the congregation.** Every congregation is autonomous. Nobody reports up. So the
national figures come from exactly two kinds of source, and they disagree:

| Kind | What it is | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Directory enumeration** | *Churches of Christ in the United States*, published by 21st Century Christian (Nashville); editors Mac Lynn, then Carl Royster from 2006 ([Christian Chronicle, Aug 21 2024](https://christianchronicle.org/church-research-council-formed-to-renew-churches-of-christ-directory/)) | Counts actual buildings and attendance; the field's own best instrument | Depends on congregations replying; **last print edition 2018**, halted by COVID in 2020 (same source) |
| **Census of congregations** | *U.S. Religion Census* / Religious Congregations and Membership Study, decennial, ASARB, distributed by [the ARDA](https://www.thearda.com/us-religion/census/congregational-membership) | Independent, decennial, comparable across bodies | Ten-year gaps; "adherents" is an estimate, not a roll |
| **Household survey** | Pew Religious Landscape Study 2007 / 2014 / 2023-24 | Measures *people who say they belong*, including non-attenders | Small n; captures anyone who self-describes as "Church of Christ," which is not identical to the fellowship |

A **Church Research Council** — 21st Century Christian, Heritage21, the Siburt Institute for
Church Ministry, and The Christian Chronicle — was formed in 2024 to rebuild the directory; a
new edition is [announced for 2026](https://www.21stcc.com/ccusa/). Its survey, designed by
sociologist Suzie Macaluso, went to roughly 11,000 churches from the 2018 database beginning in
late summer 2024 and had drawn responses from **over 2,500 congregations in all 50 states as of
September 2025** ([Christian Chronicle, Cheryl Mann Bacon, Jan 21 2026](https://christianchronicle.org/why-counting-the-sheep-is-so-difficult/)).

**Newest figures — use these first.** From that same reporting: an estimated **~10,100
congregations** and **850,000–950,000 regular attenders** as of 2025. In the two regions whose
survey work was complete (New England and West North Central), Stan Granberg found that **about
20% of the churches listed in the 2018 directory are no longer meeting** — a regional finding,
not yet a national one, and it should not be quoted as national. The article also notes that
1960s estimates of 2.5 million members were far above what later research supported.

## 2. Size and the trend line

### 2.1 U.S. Religion Census, decennial (independent, comparable)

Retrieved directly from the ARDA's national report for
[each census year](https://www.thearda.com/us-religion/census/congregational-membership?y=2020&t=4):

| Year | Congregations | Adherents | Adherents per 1,000 U.S. residents |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1980 | 12,719 | 1,600,177 | 7.11 |
| 1990 | 13,097 | 1,681,013 | 6.76 |
| 2000 | 13,027 | 1,645,584 | 5.85 |
| 2010 | 12,584 | 1,584,162 | 5.13 |
| **2020** | **11,881** | **1,422,331** | **4.29** |

Source: U.S. Religion Census / RCMS, ASARB, via the ARDA (accessed Aug 2026). "Adherents"
includes members, their children, and regular attenders.

**What that says.** Between 1990 (the peak in this series) and 2020 the Churches of Christ lost
**1,216 congregations (−9.3%)** and **258,682 adherents (−15.4%)**. Because the U.S. population
grew over the same period, their share of the population fell from **7.11 to 4.29 per 1,000 — a
40% decline in relative prevalence since 1980.** The 2010→2020 decade alone: −703 congregations,
−161,831 adherents (−10.2%).

Later editions: **2018 — 11,965 congregations, 1,447,271 adherents,** "a drop of more than
72,000 since the 2015 edition"
([Christian Chronicle, 2018](https://christianchronicle.org/), reported in
[Christian Standard, Oct 2021](https://christianstandard.com/2021/10/noninstrumental-churches-of-christ-facing-uncertain-future/)).
**2021 — 11,875 congregations, 1,423,295 adherents, 1,092,182 total attendance**
(21st Century Christian, cited in the same Christian Standard piece). **2025 (Church Research
Council estimate, not a completed directory) — ~10,100 congregations, 850,000–950,000 regular
attenders** ([Christian Chronicle, Jan 21 2026](https://christianchronicle.org/why-counting-the-sheep-is-so-difficult/)).

Note the definitional shift in that last row: "regular attenders" is a narrower measure than
"adherents," so the drop from 1.42M to ~0.9M is not all real decline. The congregation count
(11,965 in 2018 → ~10,100 in 2025) is the cleaner comparison.

**Peak.** Growth ran "rapidly up until the late 1980s"
([Christian Chronicle, Aug 21 2024](https://christianchronicle.org/church-research-council-formed-to-renew-churches-of-christ-directory/));
attendance peaked around 1985 and decline set in around 1990 (Christian Standard, Oct 2021,
citing Tim Woodroof and Stan Granberg, *Churches of Christ in 2050*). Flavil Yeakley of the
Harding Center for Church Growth put U.S. membership as plateaued from about 1980, with the
post-2003 decline statistically significant (Yeakley, *Good News and Bad News: A Realistic
Assessment of Churches of Christ in the United States: 2008*).

**Projection.** Granberg projects roughly **4,000 congregations and 400,000 members by 2050** —
about one-third of 1985 levels (Christian Standard, Oct 2021). Treat as a projection, not a
measurement.

**A myth worth correcting.** In the 1950s–60s Churches of Christ were widely described as the
fastest-growing religious body in America. That claim was later disputed and the actual growth
peak was the late 1980s (Christian Chronicle, Aug 21 2024).

### 2.3 Size of congregations

- Average congregation ≈ **100 members** (Foster & Dunnavant, eds., *Encyclopedia of the
  Stone-Campbell Movement*, Eerdmans, 2004, p. 213).
- Average known congregation size fell 3.2% between 2012 and 2018, to **121 adherents**
  (2018 directory, via Christian Chronicle).
- **91% of Church of Christ attendees are in congregations under 250**, and 55% of
  congregations average 34 in attendance (Granberg, 2006–2016 study, via Christian Standard,
  Oct 2021).
- In 2018 there were **30 congregations over 1,000 in attendance and 4 over 2,000**; the largest
  was The Hills Church, North Richland Hills, Texas (4,658) — versus 125+ congregations over
  1,000 among the instrumental Christian Churches (Christian Standard, Oct 2021).

**This is a small-church tradition.** A writer who pictures a megachurch has the wrong picture
for nine congregations in ten.

### 2.4 Share of the U.S. adult population

- **1%** of U.S. adults, Pew Religious Landscape Study 2023-24
  ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/religious-landscape-study/religious-denomination/churches-of-christ/)).
- Earlier estimates ranged **0.8%–1.5%** (ARIS 2008, Trinity College; Pew 2007).

## 3. Branches — never assert one position for the whole

Estimated congregation counts, compiled from Carl Royster's directory by Bobby Ross Jr.,
[The Christian Chronicle, March 1 2007](https://christianchronicle.org/who-are-we/):

| Branch | Congregations | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Mainstream / institutional** | **~9,800** | Supports colleges, orphan homes, sponsoring-church cooperative works |
| **Non-institutional** | ~2,000 | Rejects church-treasury support of institutions; separated as a distinct body by the 1960s |
| **Non-class** (no Sunday-school classes) | ~1,100 total | Of these, 510 use individual cups |
| **One-cup** | 554 | A subset of the non-class churches; a single cup in the Lord's Supper |
| **Mutual edification** | 124 | Mostly Midwest; no located preacher |

The mainstream figure of ~9,800 and the same one-cup (554) and non-class (510) counts appear
independently in Bobby Ross Jr., *A non-institutional institution*, **The Christian Chronicle,
April 2008 print edition** (live URL dead; read from the Internet Archive capture), which also
gives "the nation's nearly 2,000 non-institutional Churches of Christ."

**The scholarly version of the same table.** The *Encyclopedia of the Stone-Campbell Movement*
(Foster & Dunnavant, Eerdmans, 2004, p. 213) gives **non-institutional ~2,055**,
**no-Sunday-School ~1,100**, **one-cup ~550**, **mutual edification ~130**, noting that the last
three overlap heavily and all hold more conservative views than the non-institutional churches.
Prefer these figures when a citable scholarly source is wanted.

### The non-institutional count across seven vintages

This is the number most often quoted badly, so here is the whole set with its provenance:

| Vintage | Non-institutional congregations | Source and quality |
|---|---|---|
| 1987 | **2,541 "non-mainstream" congregations / 241,330 members** — against 10,165 mainstream congregations / 965,439 members, out of 12,706 / 1,206,799 total | Mac Lynn's *Where the Saints Meet* data, reported in Steve Wolfgang, *Guardian of Truth* XXXIII:9 (May 4 1989), pp. 272–275, n. 6. **"Non-mainstream" here is broader than "non-institutional"** — it sweeps in the no-class and one-cup churches |
| 1989 | **~2,265** | *Guardian of Truth Directory of Churches of Christ 1989* — a non-institutional self-directory |
| **1997** | **1,959 — "about 15 percent of the total congregations claiming to be 'churches of Christ'"**, out of 13,364 total / 1,275,533 members; no-class and one-cup a further **1,085** | Same Wolfgang footnote. The cleanest like-for-like figure available |
| 2004 | **~2,055** | *Encyclopedia of the Stone-Campbell Movement*, p. 213 — **the best scholarly citation** |
| 2007 | ~2,000; ~120,000 members; **~9% of members, ~15% of congregations** | Christian Chronicle, *Who are we?*, March 2007, from Royster's directory |
| 2009 | **1,898 congregations, 113,656 members** | Williams, Foster & Blowers, *The Stone-Campbell Movement: A Global History* (Chalice, 2013), p. 236 — the most rigorously published count |
| 2020 | 2,757 | goodfight.com self-listing directory, via Wikipedia — **the weakest of the set; unverified** |

**The share has been strikingly stable at roughly 15% of congregations and 9% of members for
thirty years**, even as the absolute counts drifted. That stability is the safest thing to assert.

⚠️ The Wolfgang piece is dated May 4 1989 but its footnote cites 1997 figures, so the text online
is an **updated reprint** of the 1989 article. Cite it as such.

**The International Churches of Christ** is a separate body, not a branch of this one; its
membership is reported at about **112,000 as of March 2024**, but that figure was **not confirmed
against a primary source in this pass — unverified.**

**Two things to keep straight.** The one-cup, non-class, and mutual-edification churches are
almost always non-institutional, but they became independent **well before** the 1950s
institutional division (Williams/Foster/Blowers 2013, pp. 234–235). And there is a smaller
**premillennial** stream (associated with the periodical *Word and Work*, Louisville) and the
separate **International Churches of Christ**, which grew out of the 1970s "discipling" movement
and is a distinct body (Foster & Dunnavant 2004, p. 418).

**Terminology.** Non-institutional churches generally accept the description "non-institutional"
but consider "anti" a slur; "non-cooperative" is likewise resented. Some non-institutional
members call mainstream churches "liberal," which confuses outsiders, because mainstream
churches use "liberal/conservative" for a different internal axis (Christian Chronicle,
[A conversation with Ferrell Jenkins](https://christianchronicle.org/a-conversation-with-ferrell-jenkins/),
June 1 2002).

The six core states (TX, TN, AL, AR, OK, KY) hold **~57% of U.S. adherents and ~50% of
congregations.** Yeakley found 70% of U.S. membership in thirteen states, a presence in 2,429
counties — fifth among U.S. religious bodies by county coverage, behind the United Methodists,
Catholics, Southern Baptists, and Assemblies of God — averaging 677 adherents per county
(Yeakley 2008).

**Rural intensity.** The densest counties in 2020 were **Clay County, TN (245 adherents per
1,000 — one in four residents), Jackson County, TN (182), Monroe County, KY (171), Hickman
County, TN (131), Cannon County, TN (130)**; the densest metros were **McMinnville, TN;
Florence–Muscle Shoals, AL; Searcy, AR; Lewisburg, TN; Lawrenceburg, TN**
([ARDA group profile, Churches of Christ](https://www.thearda.com/us-religion/group-profiles/groups?D=226)).
Note what those places are: small-town Middle Tennessee, north Alabama, and central Arkansas.
Compare Texas, which has by far the most adherents but a rate of only 10.71 per 1,000, and
California, with 568 congregations at 1.58 per 1,000.

**Historic center.** Tennessee had over 41,000 members in 1906 — nearly two-thirds of the
national total at the time — and Nashville became the movement's publishing and educational
capital ([Tennessee Encyclopedia](https://tennesseeencyclopedia.net/entries/churches-of-christ/)).

**Pew's regional split** for people who self-identify with the Churches of Christ, 2023-24:
South 53%, West 21%, Midwest 16%, Northeast 10% (Pew RLS 2023-24). The West figure is higher
than the congregational census would suggest and is one reason to treat the self-ID survey and
the congregation census as measuring different things.

## 5. Race, and the separate African American Churches of Christ

**Do not write about Churches of Christ without this section.** Black Churches of Christ are
large, old, institutionally distinct, and were made so by segregation practiced inside the
fellowship.

### 5.1 Scale
Approximately **1,240 predominantly African American congregations with 172,000 members**, and
**240 Spanish-speaking congregations with 10,000 members** (Foster & Dunnavant 2004, p. 213 —
a 2004 figure; no more recent enumeration was located, so treat as dated).

### 5.2 The history
- **Antebellum.** Barton W. Stone opposed slavery and called for immediate emancipation.
  Alexander Campbell treated it as a political rather than a moral problem and argued Scripture
  regulated rather than prohibited it. Assumption of white superiority was near-universal on all
  sides; separate seating for Black members was common (Foster & Dunnavant 2004, p. 619).
- **After the Civil War,** Black Christians in mixed congregations formed their own
  (ibid., p. 619).
- **David Lipscomb**, *Gospel Advocate* 49 (Aug 1 1907): 488–489 — "To object to any child of God
  participating in the service on account of his race, social or civil state, his color or race,
  is to object to Jesus Christ and to cast him from our association." Lipscomb had owned slaves
  and founded a school that stayed segregated until the 1960s
  ([Wikipedia: David Lipscomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Lipscomb), citing his
  biographers).
- **Marshall Keeble** (Dec 7 1878 – Apr 20 1968), the fellowship's most prominent Black
  evangelist, preached roughly fifty years and is credited with baptizing over 40,000. By January
  1919 he reported having "traveled 23,052 miles, preached 1,161 sermons, and baptized 457
  converts" (Foster & Dunnavant 2004, p. 620). From about 1920 he was funded by the white
  Nashville insurance executive **A.M. Burton** — support that came with the expectation of
  segregated congregations, and Keeble "refrained almost entirely from speaking publicly on
  matters of racial inequality" while facing racially motivated threats and assaults
  ([Wikipedia: Marshall Keeble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marshall_Keeble)).
- **G. P. Bowser** founded the **Southern Bible Institute** in Fort Worth in 1948; it moved to
  Terrell, Texas in 1949 and became **Southwestern Christian College**, a private HBCU affiliated
  with Churches of Christ, accredited by SACS in 1973
  ([Wikipedia: Southwestern Christian College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southwestern_Christian_College)).
  Keeble helped found and was first president of the **Nashville Christian Institute** in 1942
  (Wikipedia: Marshall Keeble).
- **Segregation defended from the pulpit and the college presidency.** Foy E. Wallace Jr. and
  George S. Benson of Harding denounced integration as contrary to divine order (Foster &
  Dunnavant 2004, p. 621; [Arkansas Times, June 6 2012](https://www.arktimes.com/arkansas/despite-school-sentiment-hardings-leader-said-no-to-integration/Content?oid=2276428)).
  Wallace, in the *Bible Banner* (Vol. 3 No. 8, March 1941), attacked white women who shook
  Keeble's hand after a sermon. N. B. Hardeman, president of Freed-Hardeman, refused to shake
  hands with Black Christians (ibid.).
- **Desegregation.** Abilene Christian admitted Black graduate students in 1961 and Black
  undergraduates in 1962; other campuses followed (Foster & Dunnavant 2004, p. 621).
- **Repentance, slowly.** A national meeting of thirty-two leaders in June 1968 signed proposals
  against discrimination in congregations and church activities. In 1999 the president of Abilene
  Christian "confessed the sin of racism in the school's past segregationist policies" and asked
  forgiveness at a lectureship held at Southwestern Christian College (ibid., pp. 621–622, 695).

### 5.3 Survey racial composition — read the caveat
Pew's self-identified Churches of Christ, race and ethnicity:

| | White | Black | Hispanic | Asian | Other/Multi |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2007 (n=564) | 76% | 13% | 6% | 2% | 3% |
| 2014 (n=498) | 68% | 16% | 10% | 1% | 4% |
| 2023-24 (n=355) | 48% | 24% | 13% | 7% | 6% |

(Pew Religious Landscape Study, denomination page, accessed Aug 2026.)

**Handle carefully.** The 2023-24 figures carry a ±7.5-point margin of error, and Pew warns that
2023-24 was conducted online and on paper while 2007 and 2014 were telephone surveys, so
"the results of the 2023-24 survey *should not* be compared with the earlier results"
([Pew Appendix A](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/02/26/appendix-a-comparing-results-across-religious-landscape-studies/)).
Separately, a self-ID label of "Church of Christ" can be given by people in unrelated bodies with
similar names. What is safe to say: **this is not a white tradition, has never been one, and the
self-identified population is markedly more diverse than the congregational directory suggests.**

## 7. Political alignment — what surveys actually measure

### 7.1 The only direct measurements

Pew RLS, members of the Churches of Christ:

| Party | 2007 | 2014 | 2023-24 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Republican / lean Republican | 43% | 50% | **61%** |
| No lean | 13% | 11% | 5% |
| Democrat / lean Democrat | 44% | 39% | **34%** |

| Ideology | 2007 | 2014 | 2023-24 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conservative | 53% | 51% | 53% |
| Moderate | 32% | 29% | 31% |
| Liberal | 12% | 12% | 12% |

**Read those two tables together.** Self-described ideology has been flat for nearly two decades
at roughly half conservative, a third moderate, one in eight liberal. Partisan identification, on
the same instrument, moved from an even split in 2007 toward a Republican advantage. Pew's own
warning applies to both: the 2023-24 study changed survey mode and **should not** be trended
against 2007 and 2014 (Pew, Appendix A). With n=355 and ±7.5 points, the 2023-24 party figure
alone spans roughly 54%–68%.

### 7.2 Turnout
Per the 2014 RLS, **73% of Church of Christ respondents reported being registered to vote** —
lower than Episcopalians (89%), Evangelical Lutherans (85%), and United Methodists (84%)
([Christian Chronicle, Sept 22 2020](https://christianchronicle.org/why-these-21st-century-david-lipscombs-wont-vote-for-trump-or-biden/)).
This is one of the few places where the tradition's apolitical inheritance (§8) shows up in a
number, and it should not be over-read: registration is not turnout, and the gap has several
plausible causes.

### 7.3 What is NOT measured
- **No published survey breaks out the branches.** There is no party, ideology, or turnout figure
  for non-institutional, one-cup, non-class, or premillennial churches specifically. Pew's
  category is anyone who says "Church of Christ." A non-institutional congregation and a
  progressive urban congregation land in the same cell.
- **No vote-choice data.** Pew's denomination-level tables report party identification, not
  candidate choice. Exit polls do not break out Churches of Christ; they use "white evangelical
  Protestant," a bucket that contains this group along with tens of millions of others.
  **Do not substitute an evangelical average for a Churches of Christ figure.**
- **In-house polling is non-scientific.** The Christian Chronicle's 2008 reader questionnaire
  drew responses from all 50 states and found McCain favored over Obama by roughly 2-to-1, but
  the paper stated plainly it was "not a scientific survey"
  ([Christian Chronicle, May 1 2008](https://christianchronicle.org/members-in-all-50-states-weigh-in-on-presidential-race/)).
  A self-selected reader poll of a newspaper is not a measurement of the fellowship.
- **No count exists** of Churches of Christ members holding public office, or of congregations
  that do or do not permit political material on premises.

### 7.4 Members' politics ≠ the tradition's teaching
This distinction does more work here than in almost any other American religious body.
Congregations have **no denominational body to pass a resolution**, no bishops, no synod, no
convention. There is no organized Churches of Christ lobbying presence in Washington, no
denominational public-policy office, and no mechanism by which the fellowship could take a
political position even if it wanted to. What exists is (a) what individual preachers preach,
(b) what individual periodicals print, and (c) how members individually vote. Those three
diverge, and the historic teaching (§8) points in almost the opposite direction from the current
survey numbers.

## 8. The pacifist and apolitical inheritance

**This is the single most surprising fact about the tradition, and it is often unknown to its own
members.** For roughly the first century of its existence, the dominant teaching of the Churches
of Christ was that a Christian should not vote, hold office, serve on a jury, sue at law, join
the military, or take any part in civil government.

### 8.1 The source text
**David Lipscomb** (Jan 21 1831 – Nov 11 1917), editor of the *Gospel Advocate* from 1866 to 1908
and co-founder of the Nashville Bible School (1891, later Lipscomb University), wrote
***Civil Government: Its Origin, Mission, and Destiny, and the Christian's Relation to It***.
It was serialized in the *Gospel Advocate* (1866–67), again in the *Christian Quarterly*
(1888–89), and collected as a book by Gospel Advocate Publishing in **1889**. Its argument: human
governments belong to a rebellious order; the kingdom of God and earthly kingdoms are
fundamentally distinct; the Christian therefore abstains from voting, office-holding, and warfare.
Lipscomb was regarded as disloyal by both the Confederacy and the United States for holding it
([Wikipedia: David Lipscomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Lipscomb)).

The strand runs back further. **Barton W. Stone**'s apocalyptic millennialism led many in his
movement "to adopt pacifism, avoid participating in civil government, and reject violence,
militarism, greed, materialism, and slavery" (Richard T. Hughes and R. L. Roberts, cited in
[Wikipedia: Restoration Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restoration_Movement)).
**Alexander Campbell** "persistently denounced the practice of war as absolutely incompatible
with Christian faith and commitment," rejecting the defensive/offensive distinction — the subject
of Craig Watts, *Disciple of Peace: Alexander Campbell on Pacifism, Violence and the State*
(Doulos Christo Press, 2005), reviewed in
[The Christian Chronicle, Feb 1 2006](https://christianchronicle.org/watts-gives-careful-analysis-of-churchs-pacifist-past/).
The British Churches of Christ split over the same question: a national conference was formed in
1916 for congregations opposing the First World War (Foster & Dunnavant 2004, p. 371).

### 8.2 How thoroughly it was abandoned
Almost entirely. By the late twentieth century the position survived only in scattered pockets,
and members of Churches of Christ served in the armed forces, held office, and voted at rates
that no longer distinguished them from their neighbors. The Christian Chronicle's own framing in
2020 — profiling the handful of preachers who still hold Lipscomb's view as unusual enough to be
newsworthy — is itself evidence of how complete the reversal was (Christian Chronicle,
Sept 22 2020).

**The standard scholarly explanations,** in the order historians usually give them:
1. **The World Wars.** Michael W. Casey documented federal surveillance of Churches of Christ
   during World War I precisely because of their pacifist stance ("Free Speech in Time of War:
   Government Surveillance of the Churches of Christ in World War I," *Free Speech Yearbook*,
   1996). The social cost of the position under wartime patriotism was severe, and the tradition
   moved from that stance toward civil religion over the two wars.
2. **The Cold War.** Anti-communism gave conservative Protestants, including this fellowship's
   college leadership, a reason to identify Christian faithfulness with American loyalty.
3. **Class.** This is **David Edwin Harrell Jr.'s thesis**, the most-cited sociological account of
   the movement: division within the Stone-Campbell movement tracked social class, and the
   Churches of Christ were the poorer, more rural, more Southern, more sectarian party — a
   position that made political withdrawal plausible. As that constituency moved into the middle
   class over the twentieth century, its sectarian and apolitical stance eroded with it. See
   Harrell, *Quest for a Christian America: The Disciples of Christ and American Society to 1866*
   (1966) and *The Social Sources of Division in the Disciples of Christ, 1865–1900* (1973), the
   two volumes of his *A Social History of the Disciples of Christ*; and *The Churches of Christ
   in the 20th Century* (University of Alabama Press, 2000).

**Do not state the mechanism as settled fact.** State that historians attribute it to the wars,
the Cold War, and upward class mobility, and name Harrell for the class argument.

### 8.3 Where it survives
- Individual preachers and members in mainstream congregations who decline to vote on Lipscomb's
  grounds — the Chronicle in 2020 named Sarah Blackstone (Broad Street, TX), Jonathan Storment
  (Pleasant Valley, AR), and Patrick Barber (Manchester, CT) (Christian Chronicle, Sept 22 2020).
- John Mark Hicks, a Lipscomb University theologian, is quoted there saying the current political
  climate has "awakened" interest in Lipscomb's approach.
- **Do not assume non-institutional churches are the pocket where pacifism survived.** The
  institutional/non-institutional division was about church funding of institutions, not about
  war or the state, and non-institutional churches are not systematically pacifist. Treat any
  claim linking the two as **unverified** absent a specific source.
- The 73% voter-registration figure (§7.2) is the only quantitative trace of the inheritance that
  was located; no survey measures how many members abstain from voting on religious grounds.

## 9. Contemporary social positions: taught, practiced, surveyed

Keep three columns separate: what is **formally taught** from pulpits and periodicals, what
members **actually do**, and what surveys **measure**. And never give one answer where the
branches differ.

### Abortion
- **Taught:** near-uniform opposition across branches; no branch has an institutional
  pro-choice position and no body exists that could adopt one.
- **Surveyed:** 55% of self-identified members say illegal in all/most cases; 42% legal in
  all/most (Pew 2023-24, ±7.5). The gap between the teaching and the survey is real and worth
  noticing.
- **Practiced:** unmeasured. Political activism on abortion is by individuals and by parachurch
  groups, not by congregations acting as congregations.

### Sexuality and same-sex marriage
- **Taught (mainstream and non-institutional alike):** homosexual conduct is sin, argued from
  Leviticus 18:22 and Romans 1:26–27; the common formulation distinguishes same-sex attraction
  from "acting on same-sex desires" (see e.g. [Christian Courier, "Straight Talk About
  Homosexuality"](https://web.archive.org/web/20200320203438/https://www.christiancourier.com/articles/1225-straight-talk-about-homosexuality),
  a mainstream-conservative outlet; and Apologetics Press). Both of those are **partisan
  in-house sources** — they show what is taught, not what is believed.
- **Surveyed:** 54% say homosexuality should be discouraged, 44% accepted; 57% oppose same-sex
  marriage, 42% favor (Pew 2023-24). In 2014 it was 65%/29%.
- **Range:** a small number of congregations, concentrated in large coastal and urban areas, have
  moved to affirming or partially affirming positions; these are a visible minority within the
  mainstream and have no counterpart among non-institutional churches. **No count of such
  congregations was located — unverified.**

### Gender roles and women's public participation
- **The mainstream default:** male-only eldership and deaconate; men lead singing, prayers,
  the Lord's Supper, and preach in the mixed assembly; women teach children and other women.
  The Wikipedia-summarized standard description of the fellowship names "local governance by a
  plurality of male elders" (Foster & Dunnavant 2004, drawn on there).
- **The range is wide and moving.** Some mainstream congregations have women read scripture,
  serve communion, lead prayers, serve as deacons, and in a small number of cases preach or
  serve as elders — The Christian Chronicle has reported on gender-inclusive congregations
  (Culver Palms, Los Angeles) and a woman preacher (New York Avenue, Arlington, TX)
  ([Christian Chronicle, Feb 24 2020](https://christianchronicle.org/womens-future-church/)).
- **Non-institutional congregations** hold the traditional practice essentially without exception.
- **Surveyed / counted: nothing.** No published survey or census quantifies how many
  congregations permit women to serve in any public capacity. Any percentage you see is
  someone's guess. Mark it **unverified**.

### Race and racial reconciliation
- **Taught:** racism is condemned across branches today; the Restoration principle that the
  church transcends race is standard preaching.
- **Practiced:** congregations remain substantially racially separate, an inheritance of §5.
  The formal apologies (1968 leaders' statement; ACU's 1999 confession at Southwestern Christian
  College) were institutional, not congregational, and did not merge the two streams. Black
  Churches of Christ retain their own colleges, lectureships, and networks.
- **Contested:** how much emphasis the pulpit should give to racial justice is an active
  disagreement in mainstream churches; non-institutional churches generally treat it as an
  individual rather than a congregational matter.

### Divorce and remarriage
- One of the tradition's **live internal disputes**, and one an outsider will underestimate.
  Positions range from "the innocent party in a divorce for fornication may remarry" (Matthew
  19:9, the most common view) through stricter readings that permit no remarriage while a
  former spouse lives, to broader views. It has produced congregational splits and periodical
  debates, especially among non-institutional churches. **Never state a single Churches of Christ
  position on divorce and remarriage.**
- Yeakley's 6.9% divorce rate (2008) is an in-house figure, not independently replicated.

### Alcohol
- **Taught:** total abstinence is the overwhelmingly dominant teaching across branches; social
  drinking is preached against. A minority position permitting moderate use exists in some
  mainstream circles.
- **Practiced:** unmeasured. No survey of drinking among Churches of Christ members was located.

### Education and homeschooling
- Congregations do not operate parochial school systems the way Catholics or Lutherans do; the
  educational institution is the **private Christian college** (see `institutions.md`).
- Homeschooling and small Christian day schools are common among conservative families in both
  branches, but **no figure quantifies the rate — unverified.** Do not assert a homeschooling
  percentage.

### Church and state
- **Congregational practice:** most congregations avoid endorsing candidates from the pulpit —
  partly conviction, partly the absence of any body that could endorse. Patriotic observance
  varies: some congregations sing patriotic songs near Independence Day or honor veterans;
  others treat this as contrary to the assembly's purpose. Non-institutional churches are
  generally stricter about keeping the assembly free of anything not authorized as worship.
- The Christian Chronicle has covered members debating "pros, cons of campaign involvement by
  congregations, individuals" ([The role of the church in politics?](https://christianchronicle.org/the-role-of-the-church-in-politics/)),
  which is a fair indication that no settled consensus exists.

## 10. Cultural texture — how these congregations actually feel

### Governance and offices
- **No hierarchy above the congregation.** No bishop, no district, no synod, no convention.
  Each congregation is governed by a plurality of **elders** (also called overseers, shepherds,
  bishops — the same office), assisted by **deacons**.
- **No clergy titles.** The preacher is not called Reverend, Pastor, or Father. "Pastor" means
  *elder*, not preacher, and calling the preacher "the pastor" marks a writer as an outsider
  immediately. He is "the preacher," "the evangelist," "the minister," or simply "brother
  [Surname]." In the local corpus of Church of Christ preaching archived in this repo,
  *preacher* appears 809 times across 354 files and *elders* 741 times across 205, while
  *pastor* as a title for the preacher does not appear as a norm.
- **The local congregation is the unit.** Not "the Church" in the abstract. Writers who reach for
  denominational language ("our denomination," "national leadership," "the church board") get it
  wrong.

### The assembly
- **A cappella congregational singing.** No instruments, no choir, no soloists, no praise band in
  the assembly. Everyone sings; a **song leader** stands in front and leads with his voice and
  often a hand. The singing is four-part and the congregation is expected to carry the parts.
- **The sermon is the center of the hour** — typically 25–40 minutes, expository or textual,
  with the text read aloud and heavy scripture citation.
- **The Lord's Supper every Sunday**, unleavened bread and fruit of the vine, served by men
  passing trays down the rows, usually with a short talk at the table.
- **The invitation.** At the sermon's close the preacher extends an invitation and an
  *invitation song* is sung while anyone who wishes to be baptized, confess sin, or ask for
  prayers "comes forward" to the front. Baptism by immersion often follows immediately, in a
  baptistry behind or above the pulpit. The corpus shows the idiom in use: "when the invitation
  was extended... I came forward and was baptized."
- **The contribution** is taken on the first day of the week, usually without envelopes or
  pledges, and is not solicited from visitors.
- **Three assemblies is the historic pattern:** Sunday morning Bible class and worship, Sunday
  evening, and Wednesday night. "Wednesday night" appears 87 times in 59 files of the local
  corpus; "Sunday evening/night" 68 times. Many mainstream congregations have folded Sunday
  evening into small groups or dropped it; conservative and non-institutional churches largely
  keep all three.
- **Bible class, not Sunday school.** The word "Sunday school" is nearly absent from the local
  corpus (1 occurrence) while "Bible class(es)" appears 404 times across 275 files. Use
  *Bible class*.
- **Auditorium, not sanctuary.** The room is the auditorium; the building is a *building* or
  *meetinghouse*, not a sanctuary or a temple. "Auditorium" appears 94 times in the local corpus.

### Gathering beyond the assembly
- **Gospel meetings** — a visiting preacher for several consecutive nights, the successor to the
  protracted meeting. Still common; typically shorter now (a weekend to five nights) than the
  two-week meetings of the mid-century.
- **Lectureships** — multi-day preaching and teaching programs hosted by colleges and large
  congregations. Central to mainstream institutional life (see `institutions.md`). Notably, the
  word does not appear in the local non-institutional corpus at all; non-institutional churches
  have their own equivalents (Florida College's annual lectures, Truth Lectures) and do not use
  the college-lectureship circuit.
- **Potlucks and fellowship meals** — the mainstream norm, often in a purpose-built
  **fellowship hall**. **Non-institutional churches object to this on principle**: the church
  treasury and building exist for worship, teaching, benevolence to saints, and evangelism, not
  for social meals or recreation, so non-institutional congregations generally have no fellowship
  hall, gymnasium, or kitchen, and members host meals in homes instead
  (see [Wikipedia: Churches of Christ (non-institutional)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churches_of_Christ_(non-institutional))
  and `institutions.md`). "Fellowship hall" appears 25 times in 12 files of the local corpus —
  usually being discussed rather than used.
- **Youth culture:** Bible Bowl and Lads to Leaders in mainstream churches; church camps; youth
  rallies; Vacation Bible School. Non-institutional churches typically do these through families
  and individual congregations rather than through brotherhood-wide programs.

### The periodical and debate tradition
This is central and easy to miss. With no denominational press and no bishops, **the editors of
the papers were the closest thing to authority the tradition ever had.** Doctrinal disputes were
conducted in print — *Gospel Advocate*, *Firm Foundation*, *Gospel Guardian*, *Truth Magazine* —
and in **public debates**, often multi-night, between named opponents before a mixed audience,
formally propositioned and moderated. B. C. Goodpasture's December 1954 *Gospel Advocate*
call for a "yellow tag of quarantine" on non-institutional preachers is the classic instance of
editorial power functioning as discipline (Harrell, *The Churches of Christ in the 20th Century*,
Univ. of Alabama Press, 2000, via the non-institutional history). Richard Hughes describes
Goodpasture's political style as cutting his losses and consolidating his assets (*Reviving the
Ancient Faith*, p. 238). A writer who does not understand the papers does not understand how this
body made decisions.

### Hymnody
- **Shape-note roots.** Southern singing-school culture — seven-shape notation, singing schools,
  all-day singings — shaped the fellowship's four-part congregational singing and its expectation
  that ordinary members can read parts. Hymnals are the property of the congregation and sit in
  the pew racks.
- The hymnody scene is active and self-conscious: hymn-writing workshops, new hymnals, and
  a cappella recording projects. See `institutions.md` for Sumphonia, its *Psalms, Hymns, and
  Spiritual Songs* (2012), and the Hymninar.

- **No liturgical calendar.** No Advent, Lent, Ash Wednesday, or Christmas services as religious

  whether Christmas is acknowledged at all.
- **A congregation is "the church at [Place]"** — "the Lord's church in Joliet," "the Jackson
  Heights church of Christ" — not a branch of anything.

## 12. How the tradition looks from outside, and where that's wrong

| Outside impression | What is actually the case |
|---|---|
| "It's a Baptist offshoot" or "a Southern Baptist variant" | Different origin (the Stone-Campbell Restoration Movement, not the English Baptists), different polity (elders, not congregational vote plus associations), different baptismal theology (baptism *for* remission of sins, not an ordinance following salvation), a cappella worship, and no convention. |
| "They're the United Church of Christ" | Opposite ends of American Protestantism. The UCC is a liberal mainline denomination with a national synod. |
| "Members of the Churches of Christ vote as a bloc for the Republican Party" | 61% Republican/lean and 34% Democrat/lean in 2023-24 (Pew, ±7.5) — a Republican advantage, not a bloc, and one in three leans Democratic. Ideology has been flat at ~53% conservative since 2007. |
| "They're a conservative Southern white church" | It is Southern and conservative-leaning, but never white-only: over a thousand predominantly Black congregations and an HBCU of its own (§5), and self-identified members were 48% white in Pew's 2023-24 sample. |
| "They've always been on the religious right" | For its first century the dominant teaching was that Christians should not vote, hold office, or serve in the military (§8). The reversal is one of the most striking facts about the tradition. |
| "Big churches, big money, TV preachers" | 91% of attendees are in congregations under 250; the median congregation is small and rural (§2.3). |
| "The pastor runs it" | There is no pastor in that sense. Elders oversee; the preacher preaches and can be dismissed by the elders. |
| "They think they're the only ones going to heaven" | A live internal disagreement, not a settled position, and it varies enormously by branch and by congregation. The exclusivist reading is real and is preached in some places; it is also argued against inside the fellowship. Do not state it as *the* position. |
| "It's a denomination that split three times" | The fellowship rejects the word *denomination* for itself and has no mechanism to split formally — the divisions are separations of fellowship between autonomous congregations, which is why the boundaries are fuzzy and no one can count them exactly. |
| "They're declining because they're strict" | The decline is real and well documented (§2), but no study located establishes the cause. Yeakley's 60% youth-retention figure is the closest thing to a mechanism, and it is one researcher's in-house number. |
