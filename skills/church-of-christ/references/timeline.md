# Timeline: Churches of Christ / the Stone–Campbell Movement, 1790s–present

Companion to [history.md](history.md), which carries the narrative, the historiography, and the
full source list. Every row here is dated; disputed dates are marked **⚠ disputed**; anything
not confirmed in research is marked **? unverified** and should be checked before use.

**Confidence tags** (same convention as history.md): **F** established fact · **S** mainstream
scholarly interpretation · **C** contested between branches · **?** unverified.

Where a row's date is a *publication* or *census* date rather than the date something actually
happened, the "Why it matters" column says so. This distinction is the single most abused thing
in popular accounts of this tradition — above all for **1906**.

---

## I. Antecedents and roots, 1792–1811

| Date | Event | Tag | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1792 | **James O'Kelly** withdraws from the Methodist Episcopal Church over Bishop Asbury's appointive power; forms the **Republican Methodists** in VA/NC | F | An American "no creed, no bishop" movement predating both Stone and Campbell |
| 1794 | O'Kelly's group takes the name **Christian Church** | F | The name "Christian" as a protest against denominational labels is already in play |
| c. 1800–1802 | **Abner Jones** (VT) and **Elias Smith** (NH) leave the Baptists; lead a New England "Christian" movement | F | The third independent "Christian" stream; largely stays out of the 1832 union |
| Aug 6–12, 1801 | **Cane Ridge Revival**, near Paris, Kentucky, under Barton W. Stone | F | The movement's revivalist root. Attendance estimates 10,000–25,000 — all contemporary guesses, none a count |
| Sept 10 or 12, 1803 | Stone, **Robert Marshall, John Dunlavy, Richard McNemar, John Thompson** withdraw from the **Synod of Kentucky**; form the **Springfield Presbytery** | F ⚠ disputed | Formal break with Presbyterian authority. Sources give both dates for the protest |
| **June 28, 1804** | **Last Will and Testament of the Springfield Presbytery** published at Cane Ridge; six signers (adding **David Purviance**); Stone and McNemar the credited authors | F | The Stone movement's founding document; wills the presbytery to "sink into union with the Body of Christ at large." The group takes the name **Christians** |
| after 1804 | Several Springfield signers depart — reportedly to the Shakers and back to the Presbyterians, leaving Stone the principal survivor | ? | Commonly told in the histories; specific destinations not verified here |
| 1805 | Stone publishes **_Atonement: The Substance of Two Letters Written to a Friend_** | F | Sets out the moral-influence view that Campbell later attacked; the theological gap the 1832 union papered over |
| 1807 | **Thomas Campbell** arrives in Philadelphia; censured by his Seceder Presbyterian presbytery for open communion | F | The offense that starts the Campbell movement is a *unity* offense, not a restoration one |
| 1809 | **Christian Association of Washington** organized in Washington County, PA — explicitly a society, not a church | F | Campbell's first attempt was to reform from within, not to start something |
| Sept 1809 (published Dec 1809) | **Declaration and Address** adopted and printed | F | The Campbell movement's founding document. Proposition 1: the church "is essentially, intentionally, and constitutionally one." Proposition 5 demands "a thus saith the Lord either in express terms, or by approved precedent" — the two propositions in tension ever since |
| 1809 | The slogan **"Where the Scriptures speak, we speak; where the Scriptures are silent, we are silent"** is traditionally attributed to Thomas Campbell at a Christian Association meeting, via Richardson's 1868 *Memoirs* | ? | **It is not in the Declaration and Address** (verified against the 1809 text). Popular Church of Christ writing routinely misattributes it there |
| c. 1810 | O'Kelly, Stone, and Smith/Jones groups in loose fellowship as the **Christian Connection**; est. ~20,000 members | F/S | "We are simply Christians" is already a national network before Campbell is a factor |
| **May 4, 1811** | The Christian Association reconstitutes as a congregationally governed church at **Brush Run**, PA | F ⚠ disputed | Some timelines say 1810; 1811 is the date of its constitution as a church |

## II. The Baptist years and the union, 1812–1832

| Date | Event | Tag | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1812 | **Alexander Campbell** (b. Sept 12, 1788) immersed by **Matthias Luce**, a Baptist minister | F | The hinge. Believers' immersion becomes non-negotiable and pulls the movement into Baptist life. (The commonly cited day, June 12, is **? unverified**) |
| Jan 1, 1812 | Alexander Campbell ordained by the Brush Run church | F | — |
| c. 1815–1824 | Brush Run affiliated with the **Redstone Baptist Association** | F | For roughly a decade the Campbells are a reforming faction *inside* Baptist life, not a separate people |
| June 1820 | **Campbell–Walker debate**, Mt. Pleasant, OH (baptism) | F | Debate becomes the movement's mass medium and its native argumentative genre |
| 1823 | **Campbell–Maccalla debate**, Washington, KY (infant baptism) | F | Campbell's national reputation; the argument that baptism is *for* remission of sins sharpens here |
| 1823–1830 | Campbell publishes the ***Christian Baptist*** | F | The combative, anti-clerical, anti-institutional early Campbell — quoted by every later conservative faction |
| 1824 | Campbell moves membership to the **Mahoning Baptist Association** | F | Redstone relations have soured; Mahoning becomes the launchpad |
| 1826–1844 | Stone publishes the ***Christian Messenger*** | F | The Stone wing's voice through the union period |
| 1827 | **Walter Scott** appointed evangelist of the Mahoning Association on Campbell's nomination | F | Turns a reform program into a revival |
| 1827–1830 | Scott preaches the **"five-finger exercise"** — faith, repentance, baptism, remission of sins, gift of the Holy Spirit; traditional accounts claim 1,000+ baptisms a year and 3,000+ converts | F (plan) / S (numbers) | **The plan of salvation preached in Churches of Christ for two centuries is Scott's, not Campbell's.** The most-omitted founding contribution |
| April 1829 | **Campbell–Owen debate**, Cincinnati (evidences of Christianity vs. Robert Owen's socialism) | F | Establishes Campbell as a national figure beyond sectarian circles |
| **Jan 1, 1830** | ***Millennial Harbinger*** begins (runs to 1870; W. K. Pendleton takes over from the late 1850s) | F | The older, more constructive, more institution-friendly Campbell — quoted by every later progressive faction |
| 1830 | **Mahoning Baptist Association dissolves** | F | The Baptist connection ends; the Reformers are on their own |
| late Dec 1831 | Preliminary unity meeting at **Georgetown, KY** | F | — |
| **Jan 1, 1832** | **Union of Stone's Christians and Campbell's Reformers**, Hill Street meeting house, **Lexington, KY** — handshake between Stone and **"Raccoon" John Smith**; Smith and **John Rogers** commissioned to ride to the churches. Est. ~22,000 combined | F (event) / S (number) | The movement's conventional starting point. **Regional and incomplete**: much of the eastern Christian Connection stayed out, and at least three views of the atonement persisted inside the merged body |

## III. Growth, institutions, and the first fault lines, 1837–1865

| Date | Event | Tag | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jan 1837 | **Campbell–Purcell debate**, Cincinnati (Protestantism vs. Catholicism) | F | — |
| 1840 | **Bethany College** founded by Alexander Campbell | F | The first of the movement's colleges; now in the Disciples stream |
| Sept 1843 | **Campbell–Rice debate**, Lexington, KY | F | The most substantial of Campbell's debates; Henry Clay moderated |
| Nov 9, 1844 | **Barton W. Stone dies** | F | The Stone wing loses its restraining, unity-first voice just as institutions begin forming |
| 1847 | Campbell, in Britain, defends the moral permissibility of slavery, arguing "there is not one verse in the Bible inhibiting it," while treating American slavery as inexpedient | F | The founders were situated men. A devotional that presents them as disinterested readers of a text is not telling the truth |
| **1849** | First national convention, **Cincinnati**; the **American Christian Missionary Society (ACMS)** formed; Campbell elected president *in absentia* | F | The organizational question that eventually splits the movement. Not really about missions — about whether any body larger than a congregation may act for the churches |
| 1849 | Musical instruments first discussed unfavorably in the journals; **L. L. Pinkerton** questions biblical inerrancy | F | Both fault lines surface in the same year |
| **1855** | ***Gospel Advocate*** founded in Nashville by **Tolbert Fanning** | F | The Southern churches acquire a voice; becomes the mainstream's flagship for 150 years |
| c. 1859–60 | A **melodeon** in use at Midway, Kentucky (Pinkerton) — the standard first documented instrument case | F ⚠ dates vary | The visible marker of the coming division |
| 1861 | *Gospel Advocate* suspends publication during the Civil War | F | — |
| **Oct 20–22, 1863** | ACMS meeting in **Cincinnati** adopts a resolution declaring loyalty to the United States government | F | Only Northern members could attend. Historians in all three streams treat this as the decisive hardening of Southern opposition to societies |
| 1860 | Movement membership est. **192,000** | S | Baseline for the postwar divergence |

## IV. The long divorce, 1866–1906

| Date | Event | Tag | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1866** | **David Lipscomb** takes over the *Gospel Advocate*, revived after the war; edits it until his death in 1917 | F | The single most influential figure in Southern Churches of Christ |
| 1866 | ***Christian Standard*** founded in Cincinnati by **Isaac Errett** | F | The progressive/Northern counterweight; later the independent Christian Churches' paper |
| 1866–67 | Lipscomb's articles on civil government run in the *Advocate* | F | The pacifist, apolitical strand — Stone's apocalypticism carried forward |
| March 4, 1866 | **Alexander Campbell dies** | F | — |
| 1864–65 | **J. W. McGarvey**–A. S. Hayden exchange on instrumental music | F | Among the earliest public discussions of the instrument |
| 1869 | The **Louisville Plan** reorganizes the ACMS | F | Proved divisive; effectively failed by 1872 |
| 1874 | **Christian Women's Board of Missions** organized | F | More societies; more of the same argument |
| 1875 | **Foreign Christian Missionary Society** organized | F | — |
| **1884** | ***Firm Foundation*** founded in Austin, TX by **Austin McGary** (with Elijah Hansborough) | F | Founded largely to press the **rebaptism** question against Lipscomb — an intramural fight over whether baptism is valid without understanding it is for remission of sins. McGary's stricter view largely prevailed |
| 1889 | Lipscomb's ***Civil Government*** published in book form | F | The fullest statement of Churches of Christ pacifism and political abstention |
| **Aug 17, 1889** | **Sand Creek Address and Declaration**, Shelby County, Illinois — principal author **Daniel Sommer**, read by **P. P. Warren** | F ⚠ disputed | "…if they do not turn away from such abominations, that we can not and will not regard them as brethren." A deliberate inversion of the 1809 *Declaration and Address* — terms of separation instead of terms of union. Wikipedia gives Sunday, Aug 18. Attendance traditionally ~6,000 (partisan estimate) |
| 1889 | **Clara Babcock** ordained by the Erie, IL Christian Church — first known woman Disciples preacher | F | The gender question enters the movement; still an active fault line |
| 1890 | Movement membership est. **641,051** | S | — |
| **1891** | **Nashville Bible School** founded by **David Lipscomb** and **James A. Harding** — later David Lipscomb College, now Lipscomb University | F | The first Churches of Christ school; the model for the rest |
| 1897 | **G. P. Bowser** joins the movement | F | The leading Black voice against segregation in church and society; consistently under-supported by white churches |
| 1900 | Movement membership est. **1,120,000** | S | — |
| Nov 1902 | **J. W. McGarvey** leaves Broadway Christian Church, Lexington, when it introduces the instrument | F | McGarvey was anti-instrument *and* pro-society. That combination had no home after 1906 — evidence the split hardened into a package deal |

## V. 1906: the census marker

| Date | Event | Tag | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1902 | Permanent U.S. Census Bureau established; **Simon Newton Dexter North** director | F | — |
| 1906 | **Census of Religious Bodies** conducted | F | The data-collection year |
| 1906–07 | **William J. Campbell** of Marshalltown, Iowa notifies the Bureau that ~3,000 "churches of Christ" are no longer connected with the Disciples, supplying a preacher list from **McQuiddy Publishing** | F | The separation is initiated *by the churches*, not by the government |
| **July 18, 1907** | North's letter to **David Lipscomb** and Lipscomb's reply published in the *Gospel Advocate* | F | Lipscomb: "a distinct people taking the word of God as their only and sufficient rule of faith, calling their churches 'churches of Christ'…distinct and separate in name, work and rule of faith from all other bodies." He names instrumental music and the missionary society as "a subversion of the fundamental principles" |
| 1907–08 | **J. H. Garrison** (*Christian-Evangelist*) opposes the separate listing, accusing Lipscomb of sectarianism; **James Walton Shepherd** authorized as census agent for Churches of Christ | F | Both sides fought over the listing, which is itself evidence the division was already real |
| 1909, 1910 | Census results published — Churches of Christ **~2,650 congregations / ~160,000 members**; Disciples **~7,800–8,300 congregations / ~925,000–980,000 members**. Churches of Christ listed as "formerly included with Disciples of Christ" | F ⚠ two tabulations circulate | **1906 is a publication date, not an event.** Douglas Foster: "The division did not begin or happen in 1906 — it was nearing its end. The government did not declare the division, the Census Bureau simply published data it received" |
| 1909 | **Centennial Convention** celebrates the *Declaration and Address* bicentennial-minus-100 | F | The Disciples' high-water mark of confidence, the same season the split is being recorded |

## VI. Consolidation, controversy, and colleges, 1906–1945

| Date | Event | Tag | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1906** | **Abilene Christian College** founded | F | Becomes the fellowship's scholarly center |
| 1909–1915 | **R. H. Boll** is front-page editor of the *Gospel Advocate* | F | — |
| **July 22, 1915** | Boll's name dropped from the *Advocate* masthead without comment; **M. C. Kurfees** explains in an editorial of **Aug 12, 1915**. The break was **staged** — forced resignation, reinstatement on a promise of silence, then resignation accepted on resuming the theme | F | Opening of the **premillennial controversy**. Boll held the kingdom did not fully begin at Pentecost; the dominant view held the kingdom *is* the church, inaugurated in Acts 2 |
| **1915** | **G. C. Brewer** introduces **individual communion cups** at Chattanooga | F | Origin of the **one-cup** division (~550 congregations today) |
| 1910s–1930s | **G. A. Trott, H. C. Harper, W. G. Rice** organize against Sunday Schools | F | Origin of the **non-class** division (~1,100 congregations today) |
| 1916 | Boll becomes editor of ***Word and Work***, holding the post ~40 years | F | The premillennial churches acquire their paper |
| 1917 | **David Lipscomb dies**, age 86 | F | The apocalyptic, pacifist strand loses its anchor |
| 1919 | Three Disciples missionary societies consolidate into the **United Christian Missionary Society** | F | Centralization that drives the independents out over the next decade |
| **1919** | **Freed–Hardeman College** named (institutional roots at Henderson, TN to 1869) | F | — |
| **1924** | **Harding College** formed by merger of Arkansas Christian (1919) and Harper College (1915); moves to **Searcy, AR** 1934; becomes Harding University 1979 | F | Named for **James A. Harding**, Lipscomb's Nashville Bible School co-founder |
| **1927** | First **North American Christian Convention**, Indianapolis | F | The independent Christian Churches begin separating from the Disciples |
| 1926 | Churches of Christ membership **317,937** (census) | F | Nearly doubled in twenty years |
| 1930 | **World Convention of Churches of Christ** established | F | The Disciples' internationalist turn |
| 1931 | **G. C. Brewer** argues at the Abilene Christian College lectures that churches may support schools from the treasury | F | **First shot of the institutional controversy** |
| 1933 | Brewer presses the case in the *Gospel Advocate* | F | — |
| **1933** | **Foy E. Wallace Jr.–Charles M. Neal debates** on premillennialism, Winchester, KY and Chattanooga, TN | F | Establishes Wallace as leader of the anti-premillennial campaign |
| 1930s–40s | Wallace's ***Bible Banner*** — "Devoted to the Defense of the Church Against All Errors and Innovations" | F | Drives the premillennial churches out of fellowship. Loss estimates of "about 100 churches, mainly KY, IN, LA" come from **partisan anti-premillennial sources** |
| 1936 | Churches of Christ membership **433,714** (census) | F | — |
| **1937** | **Pepperdine College** founded | F | — |
| 1940 or 1942 | **Marshall Keeble** becomes president of the **Nashville Christian Institute**, serving to 1958 | F ⚠ disputed | The most prominent Black evangelist in Churches of Christ; an accommodationist who preached to segregated seating and relied on white patronage — praised and criticized for it, then and since |

## VII. The institutional division, 1945–1970

| Date | Event | Tag | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| post-1945 | GI Bill enrollment surge at the colleges; postwar European mission opportunity; the **sponsoring church** model emerges | F | Prosperity and opportunity make the institutional question urgent |
| 1947 | "**Honor Roll of the Faithful**" lists conservative Disciples congregations | F | The independents' informal register before formal separation |
| 1949/1950 | ***Gospel Guardian*** launched — published by **Roy Cogdill**, edited by **Fanning Yater Tant** | F | The non-institutional side's principal organ through the 1950s |
| 1951 | Fourth and Groesbeck church, **Lufkin, Texas**, splits into two congregations | F | An early, emblematic local division |
| **1952** | **Herald of Truth** national radio program begins under the oversight of the **Highland Church of Christ, Abilene** | F | The emblematic sponsoring-church arrangement: one eldership overseeing a work funded by many congregations |
| 1954 | ***Firm Foundation*** under **Reuel Lemmons** moves to the institutional side | F | Non-institutional churches lose their Texas paper |
| late 1954 | *Gospel Advocate* editor **B. C. Goodpasture** calls for a **"yellow tag of quarantine"** against non-institutional preachers | F | The mainstream's formal exclusion mechanism, in a fellowship with no formal mechanisms |
| 1955 | Conservative Disciples publish their first ministerial directory | F | The independents build parallel infrastructure |
| 1950s–60s | Congregation-by-congregation separation; cancelled gospel meetings; preacher lists | F | The division happens locally, hundreds of times, without any central act |
| 1960 | Disciples authorize a **Commission on Brotherhood Restructure** | F | Sets up the 1968 restructure |
| by late 1960s | **Institutional / non-institutional separation essentially complete**; contact across the line largely ceases | F | ~1,900–2,800 non-institutional congregations today (estimates vary and are not mutually consistent) |
| mid-1960s | **Foy E. Wallace Jr.** — whose *Bible Banner* work was foundational for the non-institutional side — associates himself exclusively with institutional churches | F | The tradition's inheritances do not run in straight lines |
| 1967 | **Chuck Lucas** begins **Campus Advance** at the 14th Street (later **Crossroads**) Church of Christ, Gainesville, FL — "soul talks," "prayer partners," delegated discipling authority | F | Seed of what becomes the ICOC |
| 1968 | **Marshall Keeble dies** | F | — |
| **1968** | Disciples complete **Restructure**, becoming the **Christian Church (Disciples of Christ)** — a mainline denomination with a General Assembly | F | The independents formally exit |
| **1971** | Christian Churches / Churches of Christ obtain a separate listing in the *Yearbook of American Churches* | F | The three-stream structure reaches its present form |

| 2012 | ICOC ~97,800 members, 610 churches, 148 countries | F | — |

## Rows most often gotten wrong

| The date | What people say | What is true |
|---|---|---|
| **1906** | "The government split the church" / "the division happened in 1906" | A publication date for data the churches supplied about a division that ran roughly 1860s–1900s |
| **1809** | "'Where the Scriptures speak, we speak' is in the Declaration and Address" | It is not. Verified against the 1809 text |
| **1801** | "The movement began at Cane Ridge" | One of at least three roots; the O'Kelly (1792) and Smith/Jones movements predate it and the Campbell movement is wholly separate |
| **1832** | "Stone and Campbell united the movement" | Regional and incomplete; the eastern Christian Connection largely stayed out, and the theological disagreements were declared "opinion" rather than resolved |
| **1827** | (usually omitted entirely) | Walter Scott's five-finger exercise is the origin of the plan of salvation the tradition has preached ever since |
| **1863** | (usually omitted) | The ACMS loyalty resolution, adopted where only Northerners could vote, is the sharpest evidence for the sectional reading of the split |
| **1950s** | "The split was about orphans" | It was about whether a congregation's treasury may fund any organization other than a local church. Both sides' caricatures of the other are in print; neither belongs in a devotional as fact |
| **1993** | "Church of Christ" and "International Church of Christ" | Formally separate bodies since 1993 |
