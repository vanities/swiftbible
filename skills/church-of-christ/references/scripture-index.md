# Scripture Index — passage → argument → counter-reading

A lookup table for the passages that carry the weight in Churches of Christ teaching.
For each: what it is used to establish, the CoC argument in a sentence or two, and the
principal reading from other traditions. Read it in either direction — to find the argument
behind a proof text, or to find the proof text behind an argument.

**All quotations are KJV, verified against `ios/swiftbible/Text/bible.json` in this repo.**
Where the ASV or a modern version materially changes the argument, that is noted.

**How to use this when drafting.** These verses are load-bearing for this audience. Quoting
one of them in passing is not neutral — it invokes an argument. If you cite Acts 2:38 you have
said something about baptism whether or not you meant to. Conversely, a devotional that treats
one of these texts *devotionally* rather than *polemically* is usually welcome, as long as it
does not accidentally take the other side.

**Related references:** [hermeneutics.md](hermeneutics.md) for the method ·
[doctrine.md](doctrine.md) for the positions · [divisions.md](divisions.md) and
[non-institutional.md](non-institutional.md) for who holds what.

Abbreviations used in the counter-reading column: **RC** Roman Catholic · **EO** Eastern
Orthodox · **Ref** Reformed/Presbyterian · **Luth** Lutheran · **Bap** Baptist/broad
evangelical · **Disp** dispensational · **CC** Christian Church / Disciples (the instrumental
side of the same movement).

## 1. Conversion and baptism

### Acts 2:38
> *"Then Peter said unto them, Repent, and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus
> Christ for the remission of sins, and ye shall receive the gift of the Holy Ghost."*

**Establishes:** baptism is *for* (in order to obtain) forgiveness — the point at which sins
are remitted, not a sign that they already were.

**CoC argument:** This is the first gospel sermon, the first question ever asked about how to
be saved (*"what shall we do?"*, v. 37), and the first answer ever given. Repentance and
baptism are joined by *and* under one purpose clause, *eis aphesin hamartiōn*; whatever
"for" means for repentance it means for baptism. Nobody argues one should repent *because*
one is already forgiven.

**Counter-readings:** (Bap) *eis* can be causal — "because of the remission of sins" — a
proposal argued by **J. R. Mantey**, *JBL* 70 (1951): 45-48. Note honestly that **Ralph
Marcus** contested it (*JBL* 70: 129-30; 71 [1952]: 43-44) and the exchange is generally
regarded as leaving causal *eis* unproven in the NT; the standard lexicons (Thayer, BDAG)
give purpose. The stronger modern counter is grammatical: *"repent"* is 2nd person plural,
*"let him be baptized"* is 3rd person singular, and *"your sins"* is plural — so the
forgiveness clause may attach to the plural *repent*. CoC teachers answer that the whole
sentence is one response to one question. (Ref/Luth) baptism truly conveys what it signifies,
so the verse is not a problem, but faith is the instrument.

---

### Mark 16:16
> *"He that believeth and is baptized shall be saved; but he that believeth not shall be
> damned."*

**Establishes:** baptism belongs to the saving response, on the lips of Jesus.

**CoC argument:** Belief and baptism stand together on the saving side of the sentence.

**Counter-readings:** (all) the second clause conditions damnation on *unbelief alone*, which
is read as showing faith is the decisive term. (textual) Mark 16:9-20 — the "longer ending" —
is absent from Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus, and most critical editions bracket it;
many scholars will not build doctrine on it. CoC teachers generally accept the longer ending
(citing early patristic use and the mass of later manuscripts) but rarely rest the case on
this verse alone, precisely because of the textual question. **A devotional writer should be
aware the verse is textually contested** even though CoC congregations quote it freely.

---

### Romans 6:3-4
> *"so many of us as were baptized into Jesus Christ were baptized into his death … we are
> buried with him by baptism into death … even so we also should walk in newness of life."*

**Establishes:** the mode (burial → immersion) and the meaning (union with Christ's death and
resurrection) of baptism; also that grace is not a license to sin (v. 1-2).

**CoC argument:** Baptism is where a person contacts the death of Christ. This is the favorite
text for baptism preached *through the resurrection* rather than as a legal step — Matt
Bassford's framing.

**Counter-readings:** (Ref) genuine and central, but the union is effected by the Spirit and
received by faith; baptism is its sign and seal. (Bap) the passage describes what baptism
*pictures* for one already justified. Note that almost nobody disputes the burial imagery;
the dispute is over what the burial accomplishes.

---

### Galatians 3:27
> *"For as many of you as have been baptized into Christ have put on Christ."*

**Establishes:** one enters Christ in baptism.

**CoC argument:** Read with Rom 6:3 and 2 Tim 2:10 — every spiritual blessing is "in Christ"
(Eph 1:3), and this names how one gets in.

**Counter-readings:** (Ref/Bap) the same union-by-faith reading; some argue the baptism in view
is Spirit baptism (1 Cor 12:13). CoC answers that the immediately preceding verse makes faith
and baptism a single movement, not alternatives.

---

### 1 Peter 3:21
> *"The like figure whereunto even baptism doth also now save us (not the putting away of the
> filth of the flesh, but the answer of a good conscience toward God,) by the resurrection of
> Jesus Christ."*

**Establishes:** baptism saves.

**CoC argument:** The most explicit statement in the NT. Peter anticipates the objection —
it is not a bath — and answers it himself: what saves is the appeal to God made in the act,
grounded in the resurrection.

**Counter-readings:** (Bap) the parenthesis is the point: the *inward* appeal saves, and the
water is the figure. (Ref/Luth) baptism saves as God's instrument, not as a work. ASV renders
*eperōtēma* "interrogation"; modern versions "appeal"/"pledge" — the translation choice
matters and is contested in the commentaries.

---

### Acts 22:16
> *"And now why tarriest thou? arise, and be baptized, and wash away thy sins, calling on the
> name of the Lord."*

**Establishes:** Saul, three days after seeing the risen Christ, praying and fasting, was still
in his sins until he was baptized.

**CoC argument:** The narrative order is the argument. If sincere belief and prayer sufficed,
Ananias's urgency makes no sense.

**Counter-readings:** (Bap) *"calling on the name of the Lord"* is the saving act (Rom 10:13),
and the washing is its symbol; the participle may be antecedent ("having called"). CoC replies
that the Greek participle is contemporaneous with the imperatives and that the "washing" is
tied to "be baptized," not to the calling.

---

### Acts 8:36-38
> *"See, here is water; what doth hinder me to be baptized? … And they went down both into the
> water, both Philip and the eunuch; and he baptized him."*

**Establishes:** (a) baptism is immersion — down into and up out of the water (v. 39);
(b) preaching Jesus (v. 35) includes preaching baptism; (c) the response is immediate.

**Counter-readings:** (RC/Ref/Luth) *baptizō* need not require immersion for validity; going
into water is compatible with affusion. Note honestly: **v. 37** (*"If thou believest with all
thine heart…"*) is absent from the earliest manuscripts and is omitted or bracketed in modern
critical texts. It is still quoted from CoC pulpits as the confession proof text; a careful
writer should prefer Rom 10:9-10 for that point.

---

### John 3:3-5
> *"Except a man be born of water and of the Spirit, he cannot enter into the kingdom of God."*

**Establishes:** the new birth has a water element — baptism.

**CoC argument:** "Water and Spirit" is the plainest reading, and it is how the earliest
Christian writers took it.

**Counter-readings:** (Bap, some Ref) "water" is physical birth (contrasted with v. 6), or a
hendiadys for the cleansing-and-renewing work of the Spirit, echoing **Ezek 36:25-27**
(*"then will I sprinkle clean water upon you … and I will put my spirit within you"*), or
John's baptism as the entry into the movement Jesus was completing. (RC/EO/Luth) baptismal
regeneration, agreeing with the CoC reading of the text while differing on infants.

---

### Romans 10:9-10
> *"That if thou shalt confess with thy mouth the Lord Jesus, and shalt believe in thine heart
> that God hath raised him from the dead, thou shalt be saved. For with the heart man believeth
> unto righteousness; and with the mouth confession is made unto salvation."*

**Establishes:** for CoC, the **confession** step — the good confession made before baptism.

**CoC argument:** Confession is a required element of the response, not optional; and note that
the verse says confession is *unto* salvation, using the same construction CoC applies to
Acts 2:38 — so a reader who takes "unto salvation" as prospective here must do so there.

**Counter-readings:** (Bap) this is the classic sinner's-prayer text: believe and confess and
you are saved, with no mention of baptism. The disagreement is over whether Paul is giving an
exhaustive checklist or naming the heart of the response to a Jewish-Gentile question about
Israel; CoC teachers make the same "not a checklist" move and note that Romans has already
covered baptism in ch. 6.

---

### Acts 2:47 · Acts 2:41 · Acts 8:12 · Acts 10:47-48 · Acts 16:31-33
Conversion narratives, used as a set. **Acts 2:47** — *"the Lord added to the church daily
such as should be saved"* — is used to argue one does not *join* a church; God adds the saved.
The others are used to show the constant pattern: preaching, belief, immediate baptism,
whole households present and hearing (Acts 16:32 — *"they spake unto him the word of the Lord,
and to all that were in his house"*, which CoC uses against infant baptism, since all present
heard the word). **Counter-reading:** (Ref/Luth/RC) household baptisms plausibly include
children, and the covenant sign passes to households as circumcision did.

---

### Ephesians 4:4-6
> *"There is one body, and one Spirit … One Lord, one faith, one baptism, One God and Father of
> all."*

**Establishes:** one church (one body, cf. Eph 1:22-23; Col 1:18), and one baptism — water
baptism, the only one now in force.

**CoC argument:** Since there is *one* body and Christ is its saviour, denominational plurality
is not God's will; and since there is *one* baptism, the "baptism of the Holy Spirit" as a
separate later experience cannot be a present norm.

**Counter-readings:** (Ref/Bap) the one body is the invisible church of all true believers
across denominations — a unity of faith, not of organization; the one baptism is Spirit
baptism into that body (1 Cor 12:13), of which water is the sign. This is the text where the
CoC identity question is really fought.

---

### Titus 3:5 · Ephesians 2:8-9
Used together in CoC preaching to hold two things at once: salvation is *"not by works of
righteousness which we have done"* (Titus 3:5) and *"by grace … through faith … not of works"*
(Eph 2:8-9), yet is *"by the washing of regeneration"* (Titus 3:5). The standard CoC move:
baptism is not a *work of merit*; it is the appointed way of receiving a gift. Ananias did not
tell Saul to earn anything. **Counter-reading:** (Ref/Bap) any required act performed by the
sinner is functionally a work, and Eph 2:8-9 excludes it. This is the sharpest live
disagreement between CoC and evangelicalism, and both sides have heard the other's answer many
times.

## 2. Worship and the assembly

### Acts 20:7
> *"And upon the first day of the week, when the disciples came together to break bread, Paul
> preached unto them…"*

**Establishes:** the Lord's Supper on the first day of the week, weekly, as the purpose of the
assembly. The single most important **approved example**.

**CoC argument:** The stated purpose of the gathering is to break bread; the day is named; and
Paul, in a hurry (v. 16), waited seven days at Troas to be there. Weekly frequency is then
supplied by inference: the fourth commandment says "remember the sabbath day" without saying
"every week," and nobody doubts what it means; likewise "the first day of the week."

**Counter-readings:** (Ref/Bap/Luth) narrative, not command — the text records what happened
once, not what must happen weekly. "Breaking bread" may be a common meal (cf. Acts 2:46,
*"breaking bread from house to house"*). No NT passage commands a frequency. Many traditions
observe monthly or quarterly on exactly this basis. (Some inside the movement, e.g. John Mark
Hicks, argue the deduced rule "every Sunday and *only* Sunday" then wrongly forecloses Acts
2:46.) Note also that the sabbath analogy is an argument from the Mosaic law inside a system
that says the Mosaic law is not binding — critics press this; CoC teachers reply that they are
borrowing a rule of language, not a law.

---

### Acts 2:42
> *"And they continued stedfastly in the apostles' doctrine and fellowship, and in breaking of
> bread, and in prayers."*

**Establishes:** the content of the assembly — teaching, fellowship (usually read as including
the contribution), the Lord's Supper, prayer. Combined with Eph 5:19 / Col 3:16 (singing) this
yields the "**five acts of worship**": singing, praying, preaching/teaching, the Lord's Supper,
and giving.

**Counter-readings:** descriptive, not a liturgical rubric. And note the internal criticism:
the "five acts" list is a systematization, not a NT list, and some CoC writers themselves object
that it turns worship into an inventory. Non-institutional and mainstream conservative teaching
generally retains the five-acts framing; progressive congregations often drop it.

---

### Ephesians 5:19 · Colossians 3:16
> *"Speaking to yourselves in psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, singing and making melody
> in your heart to the Lord"* (Eph 5:19)
> *"teaching and admonishing one another in psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, singing with
> grace in your hearts to the Lord"* (Col 3:16)

**Establishes:** a cappella congregational singing.

**CoC argument:** God specified the kind of music — singing — and specified the instrument:
*the heart*. Under generic/specific authority, specifying one kind excludes the others. The
mutual-edification note (*"teaching and admonishing one another"*) also supports the
congregational, non-performance character of the singing.

**Counter-readings:** (CC/Bap/Ref) the verbs *psallō* (Eph 5:19, "making melody") originally
meant to pluck a string, and lexicons record instrumental associations in earlier Greek — so
the word does not exclude accompaniment. CoC scholarship (notably **Everett Ferguson**,
*A Cappella Music in the Public Worship of the Church*) answers that by the NT period *psallō*
had come to mean simply "sing praise," and that the object supplied in the text is *the heart*,
not a lyre. Others answer that these texts are about mutual instruction, not a worship
regulation at all, and that silence permits. See doctrine.md for where the historical argument
from the early church is strong and where it is weak.

**Related texts:** **James 5:13** (*"Is any merry? let him sing psalms"*),
**1 Cor 14:15**, **Heb 2:12**, **1 Cor 14:26**. The counter-texts are **2 Chr 29:25**
(instruments in the temple *"by the commandment of the LORD by his prophets"* — answered by
the three-dispensations scheme) and **Rev 5:8 / 14:2 / 15:2** (harps in heaven — answered by
noting the apocalyptic genre also contains incense, altars, and beasts with six wings, none of
which anyone imports into the assembly).

---

### 1 Corinthians 16:1-2
> *"Upon the first day of the week let every one of you lay by him in store, as God hath
> prospered him, that there be no gatherings when I come."*

**Establishes:** the weekly contribution, on the first day, proportional, by every member;
and (with Acts 20:7) that the church assembled on that day.

**CoC argument:** With **2 Cor 9:6-7** (*"as he purposeth in his heart … not grudgingly"*)
this is the whole doctrine of church finance. Tithing is Mosaic and not binding; fundraisers,
raffles, and soliciting from non-members are unauthorized because the specified source is the
members' proportional giving.

**Counter-readings:** (most) this was a one-time relief collection for the Jerusalem saints,
not a standing ordinance; *"lay by him in store"* may mean each person saving at home, which
would not describe a church treasury at all. CoC teachers answer that *"that there be no
gatherings when I come"* implies an accumulating common fund.

---

### 1 Corinthians 11:23-29
> *"this do in remembrance of me … For as often as ye eat this bread, and drink this cup, ye do
> shew the Lord's death till he come … But let a man examine himself."*

**Establishes:** the elements (unleavened bread, fruit of the vine), the memorial character,
self-examination, and the forward look ("till he come").

**CoC argument:** A memorial and a proclamation, not a sacrifice; *"this is my body"* is
figurative, as *"I am the door"* is. Discerning the Lord's body (v. 29) is the required inward
act, which is why the supper is taken thoughtfully and usually in silence.

**Counter-readings:** (RC) transubstantiation and the sacrifice of the Mass; (Luth) sacramental
union — the true body and blood in, with, and under the elements; (Ref) real spiritual presence
received by faith; (EO) mystery, no scholastic definition. Also note (from within the movement)
Hicks's argument in *Come to the Table* that the NT supper was a joyful communal meal, and that
the solemn individualized practice is a later development — an internal critique, not an
outside one.

**Intra-movement dispute:** the **one-cup churches** (c. 550 congregations) argue that "the
cup" is singular and that individual cups are an unauthorized change; most Churches of Christ
treat the cup as a container and multiple cups as an expedient. The same group often disputes
whether the bread must be broken during the service.

---

### Colossians 3:17
> *"And whatsoever ye do in word or deed, do all in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks
> to God and the Father by him."*

**Establishes:** the authority principle itself — nothing in religion without divine authority.

**CoC argument:** To act "in the name of" someone is to act by their authority (cf. Acts 4:7).
Therefore every practice needs authorization.

**Counter-readings:** (most) the phrase means "as his representative," "in a manner worthy of
him," or "with thanksgiving through him" — an exhortation about the *character* of the
Christian's whole life, not a formal legal test for church practice. The verse's own context
is household ethics, not liturgy. This is the most commonly challenged link in the CoC chain,
and it is worth knowing that the challenge is exegetical, not merely dismissive.

---

## 3. Authority and silence

### Leviticus 10:1-2 · Numbers 3:4
> *"Nadab and Abihu … offered strange fire before the LORD, which he commanded them not. And
> there went out fire from the LORD, and devoured them"* (Lev 10:1-2)
> *"And Nadab and Abihu died before the LORD, when they offered strange fire"* (Num 3:4)

**Establishes:** silence prohibits. **The** silence text.

**CoC argument:** God had not forbidden that fire. He had simply not authorized it, and the
text says so in exactly those terms — *"which he commanded them not."* Lack of prohibition is
not permission.

**Counter-readings:** (most) they violated a positive requirement — the incense fire was to be
taken *"from off the altar before the LORD"* (Lev 16:12) — so this is disobedience to a
command, not punishment for acting where God was silent. Further, the episode belongs to a
legislated priestly system with stated capital penalties; whether the principle transfers to
the New Covenant is precisely the disputed step. CoC teachers answer that the text's own
indictment is the omission, not a named violation.

---

### 1 Chronicles 15:13
> *"For because ye did it not at the first, the LORD our God made a breach upon us, for that
> we sought him not after the due order."*

**Establishes:** the same principle, with David's own retrospective verdict. Read with
**1 Chr 13:9-10** (Uzza) and **1 Chr 15:2** (*"None ought to carry the ark of God but the
Levites"*).

**CoC argument:** The new cart was not forbidden; it was simply not the appointed way. "The due
order" is the standard.

**Counter-reading:** as above — Ex 25:14 and Num 4:15 positively prescribed poles and Kohathite
bearers, so the failure was disobedience to a command. Note that CoC teachers usually
acknowledge this and reframe: the *specific* prescription is what made every alternative
unauthorized, which is the generic/specific rule, not bare silence.

---

### Hebrews 7:12-14
> *"For it is evident that our Lord sprang out of Juda; of which tribe Moses spake nothing
> concerning priesthood."*

**Establishes:** silence prohibits — and here the *New Testament itself* reasons that way.

**CoC argument:** The Hebrew writer's proof turns on what Moses **did not say**. If silence
were permissive the argument collapses.

**Counter-reading:** the inference works because the Law positively assigned the priesthood to
Levi; silence functions here inside an explicit assignment, not as a free-standing rule. Still,
this is the strongest text the CoC position has, and honest critics engage it rather than
brush it off.

---

### 1 Corinthians 4:6
> *"that ye might learn in us not to think of men above that which is written."*

**Establishes:** the "not beyond what is written" principle. Frequently quoted with
**2 John 9** and **Rev 22:18-19**.

**Counter-reading:** the context is factionalism over preachers (*"I am of Paul; and I of
Apollos"*, 1 Cor 1:12), and "that which is written" most likely refers to the OT scriptures
Paul has been citing — an argument against partisanship, not a hermeneutical rule. The Greek of
the clause is notoriously difficult, which most commentators concede.

---

### Galatians 1:6-9
> *"though we, or an angel from heaven, preach any other gospel unto you than that which we
> have preached unto you, let him be accursed."*

**Establishes:** doctrinal purity is not optional; there is one gospel and departures from it
are damnable — used to justify sharp lines on baptism, worship, and organization.

**Counter-reading:** Paul's "other gospel" in Galatians is specifically **justification by law-
keeping** — circumcision and Torah as terms of acceptance. Critics (including some inside the
movement) argue that applying it to disputes over church organization or instruments extends it
beyond its subject, and note the irony of using a text against legalism to enforce a practice
rule. The CoC reply: if adding circumcision perverted the gospel, adding or subtracting from
the terms of entrance does too.

## 4. The church, its organization, and fellowship

### Matthew 16:18
> *"Thou art Peter, and upon this rock I will build my church; and the gates of hell shall not
> prevail against it."*

**Establishes:** Christ built *one* church; it was still future in Matt 16 and began at
Pentecost (Acts 2); and it has never ceased to exist.

**CoC argument:** *"my church"* — singular, his, not ours to redesign. The rock is Peter's
confession (v. 16) or Christ himself, not Peter's person.

**Counter-readings:** (RC) the rock is Peter, and this text with Matt 16:19 grounds the
Petrine office and papal succession. (Ref/Bap) generally agree with CoC on the rock but read
"church" as the universal invisible body, not a restorable institutional pattern.

---

### Acts 14:23 · Titus 1:5-9 · 1 Timothy 3:1-13 · Acts 20:28 · 1 Peter 5:1-4 · Philippians 1:1
**Establishes:** the whole polity. Elders are (a) **plural** in each congregation
(*"elders in every church"*, Acts 14:23; *"elders in every city"*, Titus 1:5); (b) **qualified**
by named character requirements — *"the husband of one wife"*, *"having faithful children"*,
*"apt to teach"*, *"not a novice"*; (c) **identical with** bishops/overseers and pastors/
shepherds — Acts 20:28 calls the Ephesian *elders* those whom the Spirit made *overseers*, to
*feed* (shepherd) the flock, and 1 Pet 5:1-2 does the same; (d) **local only** — no elder has
authority beyond his own congregation. Deacons are a second, distinct office (Phil 1:1;
1 Tim 3:8-13), usually with **Acts 6:1-6** as the background pattern of servants appointed for
a specific work.

**CoC argument:** Because elder = bishop = pastor, "the pastor" as a title for the preacher is
a mistake, and a single pastor over a congregation is not the NT arrangement. Because elders
are local, no synod, convention, conference, or headquarters has authority over a congregation.

**Counter-readings:** (RC/EO/Anglican) the threefold order — bishop, presbyter, deacon —
distinguishes bishop from presbyter by the second century, with Ignatius as early evidence;
CoC replies that the NT usage is what binds and that the second-century development is the
problem, not the proof. (Bap/Ref) broadly agree on elder=overseer=pastor, and many practice
plural eldership; the difference is that most do not treat the arrangement as a test of
fellowship. On *"husband of one wife"* (*mias gynaikos andra*): read variously as "not
polygamous," "a one-woman man" (marital fidelity), "not remarried after divorce," or
"married" — the range exists inside Churches of Christ too, and whether an elder must have
more than one child (*"children"* plural, Titus 1:6) is a genuine congregational-level dispute.

---

### Matthew 23:8-10
> *"be not ye called Rabbi … call no man your father upon the earth … Neither be ye called
> masters."*

**Establishes:** the rejection of clergy titles — no Reverend, no Father, no Pastor-as-title.
Preachers are called "brother."

**Counter-reading:** (RC) the prohibition targets honorific self-exaltation, not all
respectful address; Paul calls himself a father to the Corinthians (1 Cor 4:15) and Scripture
uses "teacher" freely. (Most Protestants) agree substantively with CoC that titles are a danger
while not treating "Pastor" as a violation.

---

### Romans 16:16
> *"Salute one another with an holy kiss. The churches of Christ salute you."*

**Establishes:** the name. Congregations wear a scriptural designation rather than a
denominational one.

**Counter-reading:** the phrase is a description, not a proper name (Paul also writes "churches
of God," 1 Cor 11:16; "church of God," 1 Cor 1:2), and using it exclusively has itself become a
denominational marker — a criticism made from inside the movement as much as outside. Note also
that the same verse commands the holy kiss, which the tradition treats as a culturally variable
form of a generic command to greet — the standard example critics use to test the hermeneutic.

---

### Matthew 18:15-17
> *"go and tell him his fault between thee and him alone … then take with thee one or two more
> … tell it unto the church."*

**Establishes:** the procedure for discipline — private, then witnessed, then congregational.

**CoC argument:** The steps are sequential and binding; and the final step assumes a definable
local congregation that can hear a matter and act.

**Counter-readings:** (broad Protestant) essentially the same reading; the practical difference
is that Churches of Christ, especially non-institutional ones, still actually do it.
Some read the passage as addressing personal offenses only (*"trespass against thee"*), with
doctrinal and moral discipline handled under **1 Cor 5:4-5**, **2 Thess 3:6**, **Rom 16:17**,
and **Titus 3:10** — that scope question is a live intra-CoC dispute.

---

### 2 John 9-11
> *"Whosoever transgresseth, and abideth not in the doctrine of Christ, hath not God … If there
> come any unto you, and bring not this doctrine, receive him not into your house, neither bid
> him God speed: For he that biddeth him God speed is partaker of his evil deeds."*

**Establishes:** limits on fellowship; the ground for withdrawing from, or refusing support to,
false teachers. Also (via *"the doctrine of Christ"*) the general authority principle.

**CoC argument:** Fellowship is not unconditional. Endorsing error makes one a partaker in it —
which is why the tradition has been willing to divide.

**Counter-readings:** (most) the letter targets **itinerant teachers denying that Jesus came in
the flesh** (v. 7) — a christological heresy — and "receive him not into your house" concerns
hosting and funding traveling missionaries, not ordinary Christian association. Applying it to
disputes over instruments or orphan homes is, on this reading, a category error. **This is the
text most responsible for division inside the movement**, and how broadly "the doctrine of
Christ" is construed is itself one of the movement's oldest arguments — Carl Ketcherside and
Leroy Garrett built a whole unity movement on reading it narrowly.

---

### 1 Corinthians 1:10-13 · Ephesians 1:22-23
Used for the unity plea and the one-body doctrine: *"that ye all speak the same thing, and that
there be no divisions among you"*; *"the church, which is his body."* **Counter-reading:** the
unity in view is spiritual and does not require organizational uniformity — the reading that
lets other traditions accept the plea without accepting the conclusion.

---

## 5. Marriage, divorce, and remarriage

### Matthew 19:9
> *"Whosoever shall put away his wife, except it be for fornication, and shall marry another,
> committeth adultery: and whoso marrieth her which is put away doth commit adultery."*

**Establishes:** marriage is for life; one exception (*"fornication"*, Gk. *porneia*) permits
putting away and remarriage. Read with **Matt 5:32**, **Mark 10:11-12**, **Luke 16:18**,
**Rom 7:2-3**, **1 Cor 7:10-15**.

**CoC mainstream argument:** The innocent party who divorces for the cause of fornication may
remarry; the guilty party may not; anyone who divorces for any other cause and remarries is in
adultery.

**Intra-movement dispute — this is the most divisive live issue in the movement:**
- Whether the **guilty party** may ever remarry.
- Whether it binds those who **divorced and remarried before conversion**. **Homer Hailey**
  argued in *The Divorced & Remarried Who Would Come to God* (1991) that they need not
  separate to obey the gospel; the fight over whether that error breaks fellowship divided
  **non-institutional** churches through the 1990s, with Ed Harrell defending Hailey's
  fellowship and *Truth Magazine* opposing the position. See
  [doctrine.md](doctrine.md) §6.2.
- **"Mental divorce"** / the waiting-game position: whether a person put away for no scriptural
  cause may later remarry if the original spouse subsequently commits fornication.
- Whether **1 Cor 7:15** (*"if the unbelieving depart, let him depart. A brother or a sister is
  not under bondage in such cases"*) creates a second ground — the "Pauline privilege." Most
  CoC teaching says it does **not** permit remarriage; other traditions read it as
  a desertion exception.
- Whether **baptism** resolves a marriage entered before conversion.

**Counter-readings:** (RC) a ratified and consummated sacramental marriage is indissoluble;
*porneia* is read as unlawful union (incest per **Lev 18:6-18**) or as permitting separation
but not remarriage; annulment addresses defective consent. (EO) permits remarriage by
*oikonomia*. (Ref/Luth, "Erasmian") adultery and desertion both dissolve the bond and free the
innocent party. (Bap) the full range, from no-remarriage to permissive. Also the "betrothal
view": *porneia* refers to unfaithfulness during betrothal (as with Joseph and Mary, Matt 1:19).

**Handle with care in devotional writing.** This is the subject on which congregations in the
same fellowship refuse to work together, and where real people's marriages are at stake. Do not
resolve it in a devotional.

## 6. Women in the assembly

### 1 Corinthians 14:34-35
> *"Let your women keep silence in the churches: for it is not permitted unto them to speak;
> but they are commanded to be under obedience as also saith the law. And if they will learn
> any thing, let them ask their husbands at home: for it is a shame for women to speak in the
> church."*

### 1 Timothy 2:11-12
> *"Let the woman learn in silence with all subjection. But I suffer not a woman to teach, nor
> to usurp authority over the man, but to be in silence."*

**Establishes:** women do not lead, preach, teach men, or take a public speaking role in the
mixed assembly; elders and deacons are men.

**CoC argument:** The prohibition is grounded not in culture but in creation — 1 Tim 2:13-14
appeals to Adam being formed first — so it is not a first-century local rule. "Silence" is
understood as not being the one who leads or teaches with authority, not as literal
speechlessness (women sing, confess, and answer in Bible class in most congregations).

**Internal tension the tradition itself acknowledges:** **1 Cor 11:5** assumes women *"prayeth
or prophesieth"* — three chapters earlier in the same letter. Explanations offered inside the
movement: 11:5 concerns non-assembly settings; or it addresses the covering, not the propriety;
or the prophesying was in a different context. There is no consensus, and honest CoC teachers
say so.

**Range of practice inside Churches of Christ** (see doctrine.md): from women not passing the
communion tray or making announcements, through teaching children's and women's classes only,
to progressive congregations with full participation including preaching. Non-institutional
congregations are generally at the stricter end.

**Counter-readings:** (egalitarian) 14:34-35 may be a Corinthian slogan Paul quotes and rebuts
(v. 36), or — a minority text-critical proposal — a later interpolation, since some manuscripts
place the verses after v. 40; *authentein* (1 Tim 2:12) is a NT hapax whose sense ("to have
authority" vs. "to domineer") is debated; the letters address specific disorders in specific
churches. (Complementarian Ref/Bap) agree with the CoC conclusion in substance while often
allowing more latitude in practice — which is why "complementarian" is a fair label for the
mainstream CoC position but understates the stricter congregations.

## 7. Falling away, and the rejection of "once saved, always saved"

### Hebrews 6:4-6
> *"For it is impossible for those who were once enlightened, and have tasted of the heavenly
> gift, and were made partakers of the Holy Ghost … If they shall fall away, to renew them again
> unto repentance."*

**CoC argument:** The description is of genuine Christians — enlightened, partakers of the Holy
Spirit, tasters of the good word — and they can fall away. A hypothetical warning to people who
were never saved would be empty.

**Counter-readings:** (Ref) the warning is real and is one of the *means* God uses to preserve
the elect; those who finally fall away demonstrate they were never regenerate (1 John 2:19).
(Ref, alternative) the language describes covenant participation short of regeneration.
(Arminian/Wesleyan/RC) agree substantially with the CoC reading — worth noting, because CoC
writers sometimes present apostasy as their distinctive when it is the majority position across
Christendom.

---

### Hebrews 10:26-31
> *"For if we sin wilfully after that we have received the knowledge of the truth, there
> remaineth no more sacrifice for sins … who hath trodden under foot the Son of God, and hath
> counted the blood of the covenant, wherewith he was sanctified, an unholy thing."*

**CoC argument:** *"wherewith he was sanctified"* — the apostate was sanctified. And the address
is *"we."*

**Counter-readings:** as above; some Reformed readers take "sanctified" as ceremonial/covenantal
setting-apart rather than saving sanctification.

---

### 2 Peter 2:20-22
> *"For if after they have escaped the pollutions of the world through the knowledge of the Lord
> and Saviour Jesus Christ, they are again entangled therein, and overcome, the latter end is
> worse with them than the beginning … the dog is turned to his own vomit again."*

**CoC argument:** They escaped and then were re-entangled — the sequence requires a real prior
deliverance.

**Counter-reading:** (Ref) the proverbs are the point: a dog that returns to vomit and a washed
sow that returns to mud were always a dog and a sow — outward reformation, unchanged nature.

**Related CoC texts:** Gal 5:4 (*"fallen from grace"*), 1 Cor 9:27, 1 Cor 10:12,
Rev 2:10, 2 Pet 1:10, Acts 8:20-24 (Simon, a baptized believer, told to repent and pray).

## 8. Faith and works

### James 2:14-26
> *"What doth it profit, my brethren, though a man say he hath faith, and have not works? …
> Ye see then how that by works a man is justified, and not by faith only … faith without works
> is dead."*

**CoC argument:** The only occurrence of the phrase *"faith only"* in the Bible has *not* in
front of it. Rahab and Abraham are the models: their faith acted, and the acting was the
justifying. This is how baptism is fitted into a grace framework without becoming merit.

**Counter-readings:** (Luth) Luther's famous discomfort with James; the standard Protestant
harmonization is that Paul speaks of justification *before God* by faith apart from works of
the law, while James speaks of the *demonstration* of living faith before men, and that
*dikaioō* is used in different senses. (Ref) works are the necessary fruit and evidence of
justifying faith, never its ground. Note that most careful CoC teachers now say very nearly
this — the difference lies in whether baptism is counted among the "works" excluded by
Eph 2:8-9.

## 9. Last things

### Revelation 20:1-6
> *"he laid hold on the dragon … and bound him a thousand years … and they lived and reigned
> with Christ a thousand years."*

**Establishes:** the amillennial mainstream — the thousand years is symbolic of the present
church age between Pentecost and judgment; Christ reigns now (Acts 2:29-36; 1 Cor 15:24-26);
there is one resurrection and one judgment at his return (John 5:28-29; 1 Thess 4:16-17;
2 Pet 3:10).

**CoC argument:** The kingdom was established, not postponed — Christ is on David's throne now
(Acts 2:30-36), the kingdom was at hand and has come (Mark 9:1; Col 1:13), and the church is
that kingdom's present form. Premillennialism is read as requiring an unfulfilled kingdom
promise and a second earthly reign the NT does not teach. Numbers in Revelation are symbolic
throughout; a literal thousand is out of key with the genre.

**Counter-readings:** (historic premillennial) a real future reign of Christ on earth following
his return; the earliest post-apostolic writers (Papias, Justin, Irenaeus) held something like
it. (Disp) a distinct program for national Israel, a pretribulational rapture, and a literal
millennial kingdom — Churches of Christ reject dispensationalism thoroughly, including its
Israel/church distinction, since the three-dispensations scheme already assigns the Mosaic
covenant to the past. (Postmillennial) the movement's own 19th-century position, largely gone
by World War I.

**Intra-movement:** the **premillennial Churches of Christ** associated with **R. H. Boll**
(editor of the *Gospel Advocate* 1909-1915, then of *Word and Work*) are a small continuing
group; the controversy of the 1910s-1930s, in which **Foy E. Wallace Jr.** led the opposition,
hardened amillennialism into a test of fellowship for the mainstream. In most Churches of
Christ today, premillennialism is not merely disagreed with but treated as disqualifying — a
legacy of that fight rather than of ordinary exegesis.

## 10. Passages that carry weight in the non-institutional argument

These are the texts behind the **individual-vs-church-action** distinction, which is the
governing move in non-institutional reasoning. See doctrine.md for the full argument.

| Passage | Text | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **1 Tim 5:16** | *"If any man or woman that believeth have widows, let them relieve them, and let not the church be charged"* | **The** proof text: Scripture itself distinguishes what the individual must do from what the church treasury may do |
| **Gal 6:10** | *"as we have therefore opportunity, let us do good unto all men, especially unto them who are of the household of faith"* | Addressed to individuals — so it does not authorize general benevolence from the treasury (institutional churches read it as addressed to the church) |
| **James 1:27** | *"to visit the fatherless and widows in their affliction"* | Same dispute: pure religion of *a man* (v. 26-27) vs. a warrant for church-supported orphan homes |
| **Acts 11:29-30** | *"the disciples … determined to send relief unto the brethren which dwelt in Judaea: Which also they did, and sent it to the elders"* | The pattern for inter-congregational aid: direct, church to needy church, elders to elders, no intermediary organization or sponsoring church |
| **Phil 4:15-16** | *"no church communicated with me as concerning giving and receiving, but ye only"* | Churches sending directly to a preacher — cited against centralized missionary arrangements |
| **2 Cor 8-9** | the Jerusalem collection | Both sides claim it; the dispute is over whether the messengers constitute an authorized organization |

**Institutional counter-reading:** the church *is* the individuals; Gal 6:10 and James 1:27
bind the collective; and a home or college is merely an expedient method of doing an
authorized work, no different in kind from a church building. The non-institutional answer:
a separate organization doing the church's work is another entity, not an aid to the church
doing it — the classic aid/addition line.

## 11. Also worth knowing

| Passage | Used for |
|---|---|
| **Acts 17:11** | *"searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so"* — the Berean ideal; test every teacher, including this one |
| **Jude 3** | *"earnestly contend for the faith which was once delivered"* — the warrant for controversy |
| **2 Tim 3:16-17** | sufficiency of Scripture; *"throughly furnished unto all good works"* — used against continuing revelation and against creeds |
| **1 Cor 13:8-10** | *"whether there be prophecies, they shall fail … when that which is perfect is come"* — the standard CoC cessationist argument, "that which is perfect" read as the completed revelation. Contested: most non-CoC interpreters read it as the eschaton |
| **Rom 8:9 · 1 Cor 6:19 · Gal 4:6 · Acts 5:32** | the indwelling of the Holy Spirit — the texts on both sides of the internal "personal indwelling vs. word-only" dispute |
| **Acts 20:28-30** | elders as overseers, and the warning that apostasy will arise from within — a text behind the whole restoration impulse |
| **Heb 10:25** | *"not forsaking the assembling of ourselves together"* — attendance, and the authority for a meeting house as an expedient |
| **Ezek 18:20** | *"the son shall not bear the iniquity of the father"* — the standard CoC argument against inherited/original sin |
| **Matt 28:19-20** | the commission; baptism *"in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost"*; and the "go" that anchors generic authority |
| **2 Chr 29:25** | instruments *"by the commandment of the LORD by his prophets"* — the strongest OT counter-text on music, answered by the three-dispensations scheme |
| **Rev 5:8 · 14:2 · 15:2** | harps in heaven — the second counter-text on music, answered from apocalyptic genre |
| **Amos 6:5** | *"invent to themselves instruments of musick, like David"* — sometimes used as a rebuke of instruments; note the context is a woe on complacent luxury, and the reference to David is disputed. Weak argument; better to leave it alone |
