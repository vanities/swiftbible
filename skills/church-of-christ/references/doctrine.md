# Churches of Christ — Doctrine

Each position below is given as (a) **the position**, (b) **the scriptures**, (c) **where CoC
groups disagree among themselves**, and (d) **how it differs from broader Christendom**.

Two standing warnings:

> **"Churches of Christ believe X" is usually false.** The a cappella Churches of Christ are a
> family of sub-groups — mainstream/institutional, non-institutional, one-cup,
> non-Sunday-school, mutual edification, premillennial, progressive — that share a hermeneutic
> and differ on its output. Section (c) of every entry exists because of this.

> **This repo's speakers are non-institutional.** [Matt Bassford](../../matt-bassford/SKILL.md),
> [Josh Tolbert](../../josh-tolbert/SKILL.md), and Brother Jim
> ([james-edgar-green](../../james-edgar-green/SKILL.md)) belong to the non-institutional
> churches. Where that branch differs from the mainstream it is flagged **[NI]**.

Read [hermeneutics.md](hermeneutics.md) first if you have not — nearly every position here is
an application of the method described there. Passage-level detail and counter-readings are in
[scripture-index.md](scripture-index.md). For the history behind the positions see
[history.md](history.md), [timeline.md](timeline.md), [divisions.md](divisions.md),
[non-institutional.md](non-institutional.md), and [culture-and-politics.md](culture-and-politics.md).

## 1. The plan of salvation

### (a) The position

A person is saved by the grace of God, through faith, at the point of **baptism by immersion
for the remission of sins** — and then by continuing faithfully. The response to the gospel is
usually taught as five steps:

1. **Hear** the gospel (Rom 10:17)
2. **Believe** it (Heb 11:6; John 8:24)
3. **Repent** of sin (Acts 2:38; Acts 17:30; Luke 13:3)
4. **Confess** Jesus as the Son of God (Rom 10:9-10; Matt 10:32)
5. **Be baptized** — immersed — for the remission of sins (Acts 2:38; Acts 22:16)

…and then **live faithfully** (Rev 2:10; 1 John 1:7). The sixth item matters: preachers who use
the five-step outline nearly always add faithfulness, and criticizing the outline for "stopping
at baptism" usually misses that.

**Baptism is where salvation is received, not a sign that it was already received.** It is not
an ordinance of obedience following conversion; it is the point of conversion. Its subject is a
believing, repentant person old enough to believe — so **no infant baptism**, and **no original
guilt** to be washed away in an infant (Ezek 18:20). Its mode is immersion, from the meaning of
*baptizō* and from the burial imagery of Rom 6:4.

**The sinner's prayer is rejected.** Not because prayer is bad, but because no conversion in
Acts happens that way, and because the alternative on offer in Acts is always baptism. The
sharpest form of the argument — Matt Bassford's — is that the resurrection is the center of
Christianity and baptism is the only response that enacts it.

### (b) The scriptures

**Acts 2:38** (*"Repent, and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ for the
remission of sins"*) · **Mark 16:16** · **Rom 6:3-4** (buried with him by baptism) ·
**Gal 3:27** (baptized into Christ, put on Christ) · **1 Pet 3:21** (*"baptism doth also now
save us"*) · **Acts 22:16** (*"be baptized, and wash away thy sins"*) · **Acts 8:36-38**
(immersion) · **Col 2:12** · **Titus 3:5** · **John 3:5** · **1 Cor 12:13** · **Acts 2:47**
(the Lord adds the saved to the church). The conversion accounts of Acts are used as a set:
Acts 2, 8, 9/22, 10, 16, 18, 19.

Against merit: **Eph 2:8-9**, **Titus 3:5**, **Rom 4** — with **James 2:14-26** as the hinge
(*"by works a man is justified, and not by faith only"*).

### (c) Intra-movement disagreement

- **The "five steps" formulation itself is contested inside the movement.** It appears nowhere
  in Scripture as a list. Its ancestor is **Walter Scott's "five-finger exercise"** from his
  1827-1830 work as evangelist for the Mahoning Baptist Association on the Western Reserve.
  **But Scott's original was not the modern list.** It paired *human responses* — faith,
  repentance, baptism — with *what God gives in return* — remission of sins and the gift of the
  Holy Spirit (some accounts add eternal life as a sixth item). The 20th-century version dropped
  the divine half and converted remission of sins from a gift received into a purpose clause
  attached to a human step. One secondary account reports Scott complaining in an 1844 letter
  that people had been *"carried away wholly by the easier and more popular generalization of
  faith, repentance, baptism"* — **quoted here at second hand; verify against Scott's own
  writings before printing it.**
  ([Stoned-Campbell Disciple](https://stonedcampbelldisciple.com/2009/03/19/walter-scott-and-the-origins-of-the-five-finger-gospel/);
  [Disciples Historical Society on Scott](https://discipleshistory.org/wiki/scott-walter-1796-1861/))
  Progressive and some conservative preachers now avoid the phrase; conservative defenses of it
  are easy to find ([North Second Street](https://www.north2ndcofc.org/in-defense-of-the-five-steps-part-i/)).
- **Rebaptism.** Whether someone immersed in a Baptist church, who did not at the time
  understand baptism to be *for* remission, must be immersed again. Practice ranges from
  routine rebaptism to accepting the immersion if the person was a penitent believer. Stricter
  congregations, including many non-institutional ones, require the correct understanding at the
  time. This affects real people and comes up constantly.
- **How much a person must understand** at baptism — a related and unsettled question.
- **Whether the unimmersed can be saved at all** — see §5 on fellowship and identity, which is
  where this question actually lives.

### (d) Compared with broader Christendom

| | Position |
|---|---|
| **CoC** | Saved at baptism; immersion of believers; baptism *for* remission |
| **Baptist / broad evangelical** | Saved at the point of faith; baptism is a public sign of a salvation already received; immersion of believers. **The sharpest disagreement**, and the one most often argued |
| **Reformed / Presbyterian** | Justification by faith alone; baptism is a covenant sign and seal, applied to believers and their children; mode indifferent |
| **Lutheran** | Baptism genuinely conveys forgiveness and new birth — closer to the CoC reading of the texts than most — but administered to infants, with faith created by the Word |
| **Roman Catholic / Orthodox** | Baptismal regeneration; infant baptism; incorporation into the Church |
| **Pentecostal** | Adds a subsequent baptism in the Spirit with evidence; CoC rejects this outright (Eph 4:5, "one baptism") |

**Most common outsider misstatement:** "Churches of Christ teach salvation by works" or
"they think the water saves you." Careful CoC teachers say neither. They say salvation is by
the blood of Christ, received by grace through a faith that obeys, and that the water is the
appointed place of that reception. Whether that distinction survives scrutiny is a real
argument — but stating the position as bare works-righteousness will read as a caricature to
anyone in the tradition.

**Most common insider misstatement:** presenting the five steps as though they were a biblical
list, and forgetting that Acts 2:38 also promises *"the gift of the Holy Ghost."*

## 2. The rejection of Calvinism

### (a) The position

All five points of TULIP are rejected, and the rejection is thorough enough that
"anti-Calvinist" is part of the tradition's self-definition.

- **Total depravity / inherited sin — rejected.** Humans inherit mortality and a world bent
  toward sin, not guilt. Infants are safe. Sin is chosen, not inherited.
- **Unconditional election — rejected.** Election is *corporate and conditional*: God chose to
  save those who are in Christ, and individuals elect themselves into that body by obedient
  faith. Eph 1:4 says he chose us *"in him."*
- **Limited atonement — rejected.** Christ died for all.
- **Irresistible grace — rejected.** The Spirit works through the word, which can be resisted
  (Acts 7:51).
- **Perseverance of the saints — rejected, emphatically.** A child of God can sin so as to be
  lost. "Once saved, always saved" is probably the single doctrine most frequently preached
  against.

### (b) The scriptures

Against inherited guilt: **Ezek 18:20** (*"the son shall not bear the iniquity of the father"*),
Matt 18:3, 19:14, Rom 9:11.
For universal offer: **1 Tim 2:4**, **2 Pet 3:9** (*"not willing that any should perish"*),
**Titus 2:11** (*"the grace of God that bringeth salvation hath appeared to all men"*),
John 3:16, Heb 2:9, Rev 22:17.
Against irresistible grace: Acts 7:51, Matt 23:37.
For the possibility of apostasy: **Heb 6:4-6**, **Heb 10:26-31**, **2 Pet 2:20-22**,
**Gal 5:4** (*"ye are fallen from grace"*), 1 Cor 9:27, 1 Cor 10:12, 2 Pet 1:10, Rev 2:10,
**Acts 8:20-24** (Simon, already baptized, told to repent and pray).
On Romans 9: read as God's sovereign choice of *nations and roles* — Israel and the nations,
Jacob and Esau as peoples — not individual predestination to heaven or hell; and Pharaoh
hardened his own heart before God confirmed it (Ex 8:15, 32).

### (c) Intra-movement disagreement

- **How grace is preached.** A long-running tension, not a clean split: whether the tradition's
  emphasis on obedience has crowded out grace. **K. C. Moser** (1893-1976, *The Way of
  Salvation*, 1932) and **G. C. Brewer** (1884-1956) pressed a Christ-centered, grace-centered
  soteriology that was resisted in their lifetimes and not widely accepted until the 1980s
  ([Disciples Historical Society](https://discipleshistory.org/wiki/churches-of-christ/)).
  Moser's rehabilitation is a live theme in contemporary CoC scholarship.
  Matt Bassford is a useful case study: his late writing is grace-emphatic, and he was pushed
  back on by brethren who feared antinomianism, answering *"grace and obedience are two halves
  of the same whole."*
- **Assurance.** Some congregations teach that a faithful Christian can and should be confident
  of salvation; others cultivate a more anxious, examine-yourself register. This is a
  temperament difference with real pastoral consequences and no official line.
- **The security of the "faithful" Christian who dies with unconfessed sin** — handled by
  1 John 1:7 (walking in the light, the blood cleanses continually) in most teaching, but the
  weight given to that varies a great deal.

### (d) Compared with broader Christendom

The CoC position is essentially **Arminian** on election and perseverance, and it shares that
ground with Wesleyans, Methodists, most Pentecostals, Anabaptists, Roman Catholics, and the
Orthodox. **Note this when writing:** CoC material sometimes presents "you can fall away" as a
distinctive over against "the denominations," when in fact only the Reformed and much of the
Baptist world hold eternal security. The genuinely distinctive CoC positions are on baptism,
worship, and church organization — not on apostasy.

Where CoC differs from classical Arminianism: it does not generally teach prevenient grace as
a distinct doctrine, and it is far less interested in the whole framework — the tradition tends
to treat Calvinism as a system to be dismantled text by text rather than as a theology to be
answered systematically.

## 3. Worship

### 3.1 A cappella singing

#### (a) The position

Congregational singing without instrumental accompaniment, in the assembly. Everyone sings;
there is no choir, no soloist, no praise team in most congregations; the song leader leads
rather than performs.

#### (b) The scriptures and the arguments

Four arguments, in descending order of how much weight they actually bear:

1. **Authority / specific command.** **Eph 5:19** and **Col 3:16** specify *singing* and name
   the instrument — *"making melody in your heart," "singing with grace in your hearts."*
   Under generic/specific authority, specifying a kind excludes other kinds of the same thing.
   The standard supporting list: nine NT passages show Christians making music, and every one
   of them speaks of **singing** — Matt 26:30; Mark 14:26; Acts 16:25; Rom 15:9; 1 Cor 14:15;
   Eph 5:19; Col 3:16; Heb 2:12; James 5:13. (Not all nine are assembly settings — the Last
   Supper, a Philippian jail — so the list shows the uniform *practice*, not nine worship
   regulations.) This is the load-bearing argument.
2. **Silence.** Instruments are not authorized, therefore excluded (see hermeneutics.md §3).
   Functionally the same argument as (1) stated negatively.
3. **The nature of the singing.** *"Teaching and admonishing one another"* (Col 3:16) makes the
   singing mutual instruction, which frames worship music as congregational participation rather
   than performance — a genuinely attractive argument, and the one most likely to land with a
   general audience.
4. **Church history.** The early church sang without instruments; instruments came in later.

#### Where the historical argument is strong, and where it is weak

**Strong:** there is a real scholarly consensus that instrumental accompaniment was **not
normal in Christian worship for the first several centuries**, and that a number of early
writers speak against instruments or allegorize the Psalms' instruments. **Everett Ferguson**
(*A Cappella Music in the Public Worship of the Church*) is the serious version of this case,
and Ferguson is a recognized patristics scholar rather than a partisan pamphleteer — engage
that version, not the pamphlet version.

**Weak, and worth knowing:**
- **Early opposition was often aimed at pagan association and theatricality**, not at
  instruments as such — the same writers are frequently hostile to elaborate *vocal* music,
  drama, and dance. Using them as witnesses for "vocal only, as a rule of authority" reads a
  modern question back into them.
- **The date instruments entered is disputed and late**; the organ's introduction in the West is
  usually placed somewhere from the 8th to the 13th century, with the evidence thin and
  contested. Precise claims here are often overstated in popular CoC material.
- **The argument proves too much if pressed.** The same patristic corpus is unanimous on things
  Churches of Christ do not practice — and it establishes only what the early church *did*, not
  what it was *authorized* to do. Careful CoC teachers concede this: history is corroborating
  evidence, not the ground. Matt Bassford's own version cites Augustine and Clement as *witness*,
  with the authority argument doing the actual work.
- **The lexical argument cuts both ways.** *Psallō* (Eph 5:19, "making melody") is etymologically
  "to pluck," and in classical Greek carried instrumental associations. The CoC answer — that by
  the first century it meant simply "sing praise," and that Paul supplies the object himself
  (*the heart*) — is a good answer, not a knockdown one.

#### (c) Intra-movement disagreement

- **Instruments outside the assembly.** Nearly all Churches of Christ permit instruments in
  private life, at weddings and funerals held outside the assembly, in the car, in a school
  band. A small minority object to instrumental accompaniment of religious songs anywhere. Do
  not write as though CoC people think guitars are sinful.
- **Praise teams, choirs, solos, and "worship leaders."** Progressive congregations have adopted
  some of these; conservative and non-institutional congregations reject them as importing a
  performance model. **[NI]** Non-institutional congregations are near-uniformly against.
- **Instrumental congregations.** A small number of formerly a cappella congregations added
  instruments in the 2000s. The watershed was **January 2007**, when the **Richland Hills**
  church in Texas — about 6,400 members, then the largest a cappella Church of Christ, later
  renamed **The Hills** — added a Saturday-night instrumental assembly with communion. Its
  minister **Rick Atchley** said the church *"must become a both/and church with regard to
  instrumental and a cappella"*; two of seventeen elders resigned. A directory fight followed:
  in 2009 the publisher of the national directory dropped 21 instrumental congregations, and by
  2012 reinstated them under a reworded scope, "**historically** a cappella"
  ([Christian Chronicle](https://christianchronicle.org/nations-largest-church-of-christ-adding-instrumental-service/);
  [2011 follow-up](https://christianchronicle.org/historically-a-cappella-churches-with-instrumental-services-returned-to-national-directory/)).
  Max Lucado's Oak Hills in San Antonio moved similarly. This is the progressive boundary
  marker, not a typical congregation — see [divisions.md](divisions.md).

#### (d) Compared with broader Christendom

Almost every other tradition permits instruments. The nearest cousins are **strict-subscription
Reformed and Presbyterian bodies** (some of which are also exclusive-psalmody and unaccompanied,
on the same regulative principle), the **Old Regular** and primitive Baptists, and the
**Orthodox**, whose liturgical music is unaccompanied by long tradition — for different reasons
(Orthodox practice rests on tradition, not on a silence argument). The **Christian Churches**
use instruments while sharing the CoC's founding documents; that is the family split.

### 3.2 The Lord's Supper

#### (a) The position

Observed **every first day of the week**, in the assembly, as a **memorial and proclamation**,
with unleavened bread and fruit of the vine. Participants examine themselves; the mood is
usually reflective and quiet. It is not a sacrifice, and the elements do not change; *"this is
my body"* is figurative.

Most congregations practice open communion in the sense that they do not fence the table
institutionally — the invitation is to examine oneself.

#### (b) The scriptures

**Acts 20:7** (*"upon the first day of the week … came together to break bread"*) is the
approved example. **1 Cor 11:23-29** gives the institution, the elements, the memorial
character (*"this do in remembrance of me"*), the proclamation (*"ye do shew the Lord's death
till he come"*), and self-examination. **Matt 26:26-29**; **Acts 2:42**; **1 Cor 10:16-17**.
Weekly frequency is a **necessary inference** from Acts 20:7 plus 1 Cor 16:2, argued by analogy
with "remember the sabbath day."

#### (c) Intra-movement disagreement

- **One cup.** The **one-cup churches** (roughly 550 congregations) hold that "the cup" is
  singular and that individual communion cups are an unauthorized change; they also dispute
  among themselves whether the loaf must be broken during the service and whether the fruit of
  the vine must be fermented. Most Churches of Christ treat the container as an expedient.
  **Do not fuse "one cup" with "no Sunday school"** — they are two separate questions with
  partial overlap, and most **non-class** congregations (roughly 1,100) use individual cups.
  See [divisions.md](divisions.md).
- **Sunday evening / "second serving."** Whether the supper may be offered again on Sunday
  evening for those who missed the morning is a genuine disagreement — some see one assembly,
  one observance; others see the day, not the assembly, as what is specified.
- **Only on Sunday?** Whether it may be taken on another day (e.g. to a sick member midweek).
  Most say the day is specified; some allow taking it to the homebound the same day.
- **The mood.** Hicks's *Come to the Table* argues from within the movement that the NT supper
  was a joyful communal meal and that the silent, individualized practice is a later
  development. Conservative churches reject this as revisionism.

#### (d) Compared with broader Christendom

Weekly observance aligns CoC with Roman Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, and much Lutheran
practice, and separates it from most Baptist and evangelical churches (monthly or quarterly).
On the *meaning*, CoC is at the Zwinglian memorialist end and rejects transubstantiation
(RC), sacramental union (Lutheran), and generally speaks less warmly of real spiritual presence
than the Reformed do — though some CoC writers have moved toward a fuller communion theology.

### 3.3 The collection, prayer, preaching, and the assembly

- **The collection.** Taken **on the first day of the week**, from members, proportional
  (*"as God hath prospered him"*, 1 Cor 16:2) and cheerful (2 Cor 9:7). **Tithing is not
  taught as binding** — it belongs to the Law. Fundraisers, raffles, bake sales, business
  ventures, and soliciting from non-members are widely rejected as unauthorized sources, because
  the source has been specified. **[NI]** Non-institutional churches are strictest here, and the
  same reasoning drives the institutional dispute in §6.1.
- **Prayer.** Led by men in the mixed assembly (see §4.3). Prayers are extempore, not read;
  addressed to the Father, through Christ; congregation says amen.
- **Preaching/teaching.** Expository or topical, by a man, without vestments or liturgical
  form. The preacher is an evangelist, not a priest and not "the pastor."
- **The "five acts of worship."** Singing, praying, preaching/teaching, the Lord's Supper, and
  giving — a systematization drawn mainly from Acts 2:42 plus Eph 5:19 and 1 Cor 16:2. Widely
  taught, and criticized from inside the movement as an unbiblical inventory that turns worship
  into a compliance checklist. Conservative and non-institutional congregations mostly keep the
  framing; progressive ones mostly drop it.
- **No liturgical calendar.** Christmas and Easter are not observed as religious festivals in the
  assembly — every Sunday is the memorial of the resurrection. Individual Christians may keep
  Christmas as a family or cultural holiday; some congregations will not mention it at all.
  **This is a real trap for devotional writing.** A devotional built around Lent, Advent, Ash
  Wednesday, or "Easter Sunday" as a special worship occasion will read wrong. Seasonal
  *content* (the resurrection, the incarnation) is fine at any time; seasonal *observance* is
  not.

## 4. Ecclesiology

### 4.1 Congregational autonomy

#### (a) The position

Each congregation is **self-governing, self-supporting, and answerable to Christ alone**. There
is no headquarters, no synod, convention, presbytery, or diocese, no denominational
officialdom, and no binding authority above the local eldership. Churches cooperate only in the
limited ways the NT shows — and how limited that is, is exactly the institutional dispute (§6.1).

In place of formal structures the movement has been coordinated informally by **periodicals**
(historically *Gospel Advocate*, *Firm Foundation*, *Gospel Guardian*, *Truth Magazine*),
**lectureships**, and **colleges** — which is why editors have functioned as de facto power
brokers, and why divisions in the movement track editorial alignments. This is a genuine
historical observation, not a slur; it is standard in the scholarship.

#### (b) The scriptures

**Acts 14:23** (elders appointed *"in every church"*), **Titus 1:5** (*"elders in every city"*),
**1 Pet 5:2** (*"the flock of God which is among you"*), **Acts 20:28** (elders overseeing
*"the flock over the which the Holy Ghost hath made you overseers"*), **Phil 1:1**
(bishops and deacons in one city church). Rev 2-3 addresses seven separate churches
individually.

#### (c) Intra-movement disagreement

The scope of permissible cooperation is *the* dividing line of the 20th century — see §6.1.

#### (d) Compared with broader Christendom

Structurally close to **Baptist** and **congregationalist** polity, and completely opposed to
episcopal (RC, Orthodox, Anglican, Methodist) and presbyterian polity. The difference from
Baptists is that Baptists organize conventions and associations that Churches of Christ regard
as unauthorized.

### 4.2 Elders, deacons, evangelists, and the rejection of titles

#### (a) The position

- **Elders** — also called bishops, overseers, pastors, shepherds; the NT uses these for one
  office (Acts 20:17 with 20:28; 1 Pet 5:1-2; Titus 1:5 with 1:7). **Plural** in each
  congregation, **qualified** per 1 Tim 3:1-7 and Titus 1:5-9, **male**, with authority only in
  their own congregation. They oversee, shepherd, and teach; they do not "rule" in a corporate
  sense (1 Pet 5:3, *"neither as being lords over God's heritage"*).
- **Deacons** — a distinct office (Phil 1:1; 1 Tim 3:8-13), servants appointed to particular
  work, with Acts 6:1-6 as the background pattern. Not a junior eldership.
- **Evangelists / preachers** — a work, not an office of authority. A located preacher serves
  under the elders. He is not "the pastor."
- **No clergy titles.** No Reverend, no Father, no Pastor-as-title, no clerical dress. Men are
  called "brother." Grounded in **Matt 23:8-10** and in the priesthood of all believers
  (1 Pet 2:5, 9).
- Congregations without qualified men remain **unorganized** — led by "the men of the
  congregation" — rather than appointing unqualified elders. This is common and is not regarded
  as a defect to be papered over.

#### (c) Intra-movement disagreement

- **"Husband of one wife"** (*mias gynaikos andra*): not polygamous / faithful to one woman /
  not remarried after divorce / must be married. All four readings exist inside the movement.
- **"Having faithful children"** (Titus 1:6): must an elder have more than one child? Must all
  his children be Christians? A genuinely divisive congregational-level question.
- **How much authority elders have** over matters of judgment, and whether a congregation may
  ever overrule them.
- **[NI]** Non-institutional churches tend to a stricter reading of qualifications and a firmer
  insistence that elders have no authority beyond the local congregation — which is the point at
  issue in the sponsoring-church dispute (§6.1).
- **Deaconesses.** Rom 16:1 (*Phoebe*) and 1 Tim 3:11 are read by a minority as supporting a
  female servant role; the mainstream reads 1 Tim 3:11 as deacons' wives. Progressive
  congregations have appointed women as deacons; conservative and non-institutional ones have
  not.

#### (d) Compared with broader Christendom

Elder=overseer=pastor is shared with many Baptists, Presbyterians, and elder-led evangelical
churches. The distinctive is the *combination*: plural unpaid local elders, a separate paid
evangelist with no office authority, no titles, and no structure above the congregation.
Episcopal traditions distinguish bishop from presbyter from at least Ignatius (early 2nd
century) — CoC teachers regard that development as the beginning of apostasy, which Acts
20:29-30 predicted.

### 4.3 The role of women

#### (a) The position (mainstream)

Women do not lead the assembly: they do not preach, lead singing, lead public prayer, wait on
the table, or teach a class containing men. They are not elders or deacons. They sing, confess,
give, commune, teach women and children, and — in most congregations — participate in Bible
class discussion. The restriction is understood as concerning **leadership and authority in the
assembly**, not as a claim that women are lesser.

#### (b) The scriptures

**1 Cor 14:34-35** (*"Let your women keep silence in the churches"*), **1 Tim 2:11-12**
(*"I suffer not a woman to teach, nor to usurp authority over the man"*), with **1 Tim 2:13-14**
grounding it in creation rather than culture — the key move, since a creation ground is not
locally revisable. Also 1 Cor 11:3; Eph 5:22-24; Titus 2:3-5 (older women teaching younger
women).

The acknowledged tension: **1 Cor 11:5** assumes women *"prayeth or prophesieth."* Explanations
offered inside the movement — non-assembly setting; the covering, not the propriety, is the
subject; a different context — do not command consensus, and honest CoC teachers say so.

#### (c) Intra-movement disagreement — a wide range of practice

Ordered roughly from strictest to most permissive:

| Practice | Where |
|---|---|
| No audible participation beyond singing; no announcements, no scripture reading, no passing the tray, no leading in a mixed devotional | Strictest conservative and **[NI]** congregations |
| Women teach women's and children's classes; ask and answer questions in mixed Bible class | The broad mainstream, including most non-institutional churches |
| Women read Scripture, make announcements, serve communion, lead singing in a non-assembly setting | Some progressive institutional congregations |
| Women serve as deacons, pray publicly, teach mixed adult classes, preach | A small progressive minority; the flashpoint of the "change agent" controversies |

At the far margin, a very small number of mainstream congregations have appointed women as
elders — **Culver Palms** in Los Angeles appointed two in **2018**
([Christian Chronicle](https://christianchronicle.org/journey-church-female-elders/)). Most
mainstream congregations do not, and no reliable figure exists for how many permit women any
public role; treat any percentage you see as unsourced.

Whether a woman may pray aloud in a mixed home devotional, or lead in a class of women where a
man is present, or teach a teenage boy — these are argued at the congregational level, and
answered differently by neighboring congregations in the same fellowship.

**Matt Bassford's own position** is a useful calibration for this repo: complementarian, women
silent in the sense of not being the speaker or exercising teaching authority over men, but
able to speak in Bible classes if doing so submissively (see
[his theology notes](../../matt-bassford/references/theology.md)).

#### (d) Compared with broader Christendom

The mainstream CoC position is **complementarian**, shared in substance with conservative
Presbyterians, the Southern Baptist Convention, and confessional Lutherans — though many CoC
congregations are stricter in practice about non-teaching acts (announcements, reading,
serving) than complementarian churches in those bodies. Egalitarian readings —
1 Cor 14:34-35 as a Corinthian slogan Paul rebuts, or as an interpolation; *authentein* as
"domineer" rather than "have authority"; the letters as occasional correspondence addressing
local disorder — are known inside the movement and are exactly what the progressive
congregations adopted.

---

### 4.4 Identity: "the one true church"?

#### (a) The positions — plural

This is the most misunderstood thing about Churches of Christ, including by their own members.
There is a genuine **spectrum**, and both ends are historic.

**The exclusivist end.** Christ built one church (Matt 16:18; Eph 1:22-23; 4:4). Salvation is
in that body. One enters it by baptism for the remission of sins. Therefore those who have not
been so baptized are not in it, and denominations are human institutions without divine warrant.
At its hardest this becomes "only members of churches of Christ will be saved."

**The non-sectarian end.** The movement's founding aim was **unity**, not a new denomination.
Thomas Campbell's *Declaration and Address* Proposition 1: *"the church of Christ upon earth is
essentially, intentionally, and constitutionally one."* On this reading, "church of Christ" is a
description, not a brand; the plea is for all Christians to abandon party names and creeds and
be Christians only. The slogan version — **"we are Christians only, but not the only
Christians"** — is widely repeated inside the movement. **Its exact origin is commonly
misattributed** (it circulates variously as Barton Stone, Thomas Campbell, or an anonymous
19th-century formulation); **treat any specific attribution as unverified** unless you can check
it. **Verified negative:** the phrase does not occur in Thomas Campbell's *Declaration and
Address* — neither "Christians only" nor "not the only Christians" appears in that text, so the
common attribution to the movement's founding document is wrong.

Between the ends: most congregations teach that the NT pattern is knowable and binding, that
baptism for remission is essential, and that God — not they — will judge the sincere person who
never heard it. Most CoC members will say they belong to a *church* rather than a
*denomination*, and will resist "Church of Christ" being treated as a denominational label —
while acknowledging that from the outside it functions as one.

#### (c) Intra-movement history

- **Carl Ketcherside** and **Leroy Garrett** led a unity movement from the 1950s into the 1970s
  arguing for a much narrower reading of what breaks fellowship (2 John 9's "doctrine of Christ"
  as christological, not comprehensive). Both were themselves originally from strict
  backgrounds — Ketcherside from the non-institutional side. They were widely opposed and are
  now a standard reference point for progressive arguments.
- **Rubel Shelly** (Woodmont Hills, Nashville) is the best-known 20th-century example of a
  preacher who moved from a hard exclusivist position to an ecumenical one; he and the
  *New Wineskins* magazine are the standard referents of the pejorative "**change agents**."
  The label is contested and those it names do not use it of themselves.
- **[NI]** Non-institutional churches practice a narrower fellowship in a specific, concrete
  sense: they generally will not have institutional preachers in their pulpits or support
  institutional works, and directories of non-institutional congregations exist precisely
  because the two groups largely do not work together. Whether that constitutes "withdrawing
  fellowship" from institutional brethren is itself argued among them.

#### (d) Compared with broader Christendom

Exclusive claims are not unusual — Roman Catholicism (*Lumen Gentium*: the Church of Christ
*subsists in* the Catholic Church) and Orthodoxy both make one. What is unusual is the CoC
combination: an exclusive claim held by a body with **no central authority to define who is
in it**, so the boundary is drawn congregation by congregation, and often preacher by preacher.
That is why outsiders get contradictory answers to "do you think I'm going to hell" from
different Churches of Christ, and both answers are honestly given.

## 5. Eschatology

### (a) The position

**Amillennial**, and firmly so. The thousand years of Rev 20 is symbolic of the present age
between Pentecost and the return of Christ. Christ reigns **now**, on David's throne, from the
right hand of God (Acts 2:29-36). The kingdom **came** — it was not postponed. At his return
there is one general resurrection of the just and the unjust, one judgment, and eternity; no
rapture separate from the second coming, no future earthly reign, no rebuilt temple, no distinct
program for national Israel.

**Premillennialism and dispensationalism are rejected** — the latter thoroughly, since the
three-dispensations scheme (hermeneutics.md §4) already assigns the Mosaic covenant to the past
and reads the church as the fulfillment, not the parenthesis, of God's promises.

### (b) The scriptures

**Rev 20:1-6** read as apocalyptic symbol · **Acts 2:29-36** (Christ enthroned now) ·
**Mark 9:1** and **Col 1:13** (the kingdom came and Christians are in it) · **John 18:36**
(*"my kingdom is not of this world"*) · **John 5:28-29** (one hour, both resurrections) ·
**1 Thess 4:16-17** and **2 Pet 3:10** (one return, the elements dissolved — leaving no earth
for a millennium) · **1 Cor 15:24-26** (Christ delivers up the kingdom at the end) ·
**Luke 17:20-21**.

### (c) Intra-movement disagreement

- **The premillennial churches of Christ.** **R. H. Boll** — editor of the *Gospel Advocate*
  from 1909 to 1915, thereafter of **Word and Work** — advanced a premillennial reading; the
  resulting fight was a major source of division in the 1930s. **Foy E. Wallace Jr.** led the
  opposition, including debates with **Charles M. Neal** in 1933 at Winchester, Kentucky, and
  Chattanooga, Tennessee. A small group of premillennial congregations continues, associated
  with Word and Work and with Portland Avenue in Louisville.
  ([R. H. Boll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Henry_Boll); [Word and Work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_and_Work);
  [Foy E. Wallace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foy_E._Wallace))
- **The legacy is the point.** In most Churches of Christ premillennialism is not merely
  disagreed with but treated as **disqualifying** — a fellowship issue rather than an exegetical
  one. That is a direct inheritance of the Boll controversy, not of ordinary Bible study, and it
  is worth knowing that the reflex is historically produced.
- **The kingdom and the church.** Whether they are identical. Matt Bassford's position is
  instructive: linked but not identical, and Christians pray *"thy kingdom come"* because the
  kingdom keeps expanding as more people submit. Flattening kingdom to church is an
  over-correction he explicitly rebukes.
- **Hell.** Conscious eternal punishment is the overwhelming mainstream. **Edward Fudge**, from
  within the movement, argued for conditional immortality / annihilationism in *The Fire That
  Consumes* (1982) and was dismissed from his preaching work over it; the view remains a small
  minority and is treated as serious error by conservative and non-institutional churches.
- The movement was largely **postmillennial** in the 19th century (Alexander Campbell's
  *Millennial Harbinger* carries the optimism in its title); that collapsed around World War I.

### (d) Compared with broader Christendom

Amillennialism is shared with Roman Catholicism, Orthodoxy, Lutheranism, most of the Reformed
world, and Anglicanism — so on this point Churches of Christ sit with the historic majority and
against American evangelicalism's dispensational mainstream. **Do not write as though CoC
eschatology is idiosyncratic.** What *is* distinctive is the intensity: because of Boll, an
amillennial position that most traditions hold quietly is held here as a boundary marker.

## 6. Other live positions

### 6.1 The institutional question — and the individual-vs-church-action distinction **[NI]**

**This is the division that produced the churches this repo's speakers belong to**, and the
reasoning behind it governs how non-institutional preachers think about a great many other
questions.

#### (a) The dispute

In the 1950s Churches of Christ divided over whether a congregation may:

1. **Send money from the treasury to a human institution** — an orphans' home, a home for the
   aged, a Christian college — to do the church's work of benevolence or teaching.
2. **Operate through a "sponsoring church"** — one congregation's eldership overseeing a work
   funded by contributions from many congregations. The standard example named on both sides is
   the mass-media evangelism program **Herald of Truth**, sponsored from the early 1950s by the
   elders of a congregation in Abilene, Texas (usually given as Highland Avenue / Highland
   **Fifth and Highland** church in Abilene, Texas, beginning in **1951-52** (sources give both
   years).
3. **Provide social and recreational activities from the treasury** — fellowship halls, church
   kitchens, camps, ball teams, "church suppers."

Non-institutional churches answer **no** to all three. The result was a hard division: separate
directories, separate preachers, separate lectureships, and in many places no cooperation at
all. Current counts run around **2,055 congregations** (*Encyclopedia of the Stone-Campbell
Movement*), roughly 15% of congregations and 9% of members; other directories give figures from
about 1,900 to about 2,750, so cite a range rather than a single number.

**This section is a summary.** The full history, the four disputed practices, the arguments on
both sides, and the sociology are in [non-institutional.md](non-institutional.md) and
[divisions.md](divisions.md).

#### (b) The argument, and the scriptures

The governing move is the **individual-vs-church-action distinction**: some obligations are laid
on the *individual Christian* and some on the *church acting collectively*, and it is an error
to fund from the treasury what Scripture assigns to the individual.

- **1 Tim 5:16** is the pivot: *"If any man or woman that believeth have widows, let them
  relieve them, and let not the church be charged; that it may relieve them that are widows
  indeed."* Scripture itself distinguishes individual duty from church funding — so the
  distinction is not a human invention.
- **Gal 6:10** (*"as we have therefore opportunity, let us do good unto all men"*) and
  **James 1:27** (*"to visit the fatherless and widows"*) are read as addressed to individuals,
  not as warrants for the treasury.
- **Acts 11:29-30** and **2 Cor 8-9** show the pattern for inter-congregational aid: direct,
  church to needy church, to the elders, with no intermediary organization and no permanent
  structure.
- **Phil 4:15-16**: churches sent directly to the preacher — cited against centralized
  arrangements.
- On the sponsoring church: elders have oversight only of *"the flock … which is among you"*
  (1 Pet 5:2; Acts 20:28), so one eldership overseeing a nationwide work exceeds its charter.
- On recreation: the church's work is evangelism, edification, and limited benevolence
  (to needy saints); social and recreational activity is the individual's or the family's
  business, and 1 Cor 11:22 (*"have ye not houses to eat and to drink in?"*) is cited.

#### The institutional answer

The church *is* the individuals; Gal 6:10 and James 1:27 bind the collective; and an orphans'
home or a sponsoring arrangement is an **expedient method** of doing an authorized work, no
different in kind from a church building, a bank account, or a bus. Non-institutional churches
reply with the aid/addition line: a separate organization doing the church's work is a second
entity, not an aid to the first.

Both sides use the same hermeneutic. That is what makes the division so intractable — and it is
why a devotional writer needs to know it: a sentence like "the church runs a food pantry for the
community" is not neutral in this fellowship.

**Names, dates, and alignments** — Foy E. Wallace Jr. and the revived *Gospel Guardian* (1949)
with Fanning Yater Tant as editor; Roy E. Cogdill's *Walking by Faith* as the standard
book-length statement of the non-institutional position; B. C. Goodpasture at the *Gospel
Advocate* from 1939 and his December 1954 endorsement of a **"quarantine of the 'antis'"**;
Reuel Lemmons taking the *Firm Foundation* institutional in 1954 — are set out with sources in
[divisions.md](divisions.md) and [non-institutional.md](non-institutional.md). Use those rather
than popular accounts, which are partisan on both sides.

### 6.2 Divorce and remarriage

**The most divisive live issue in the movement**, and the one most likely to cause harm if
handled carelessly.

**(a) The mainstream position.** Marriage is for life. **Matt 19:9** permits putting away a
spouse for *"fornication"* (*porneia*) and permits the **innocent party** to remarry. The guilty
party may not remarry. Anyone who divorces for any other cause and remarries is living in
adultery, and repentance may require ending that marriage.

**(b) Scriptures.** Matt 19:3-12; **Matt 19:9**; Matt 5:32; Mark 10:2-12; Luke 16:18;
Rom 7:2-3; 1 Cor 7:10-15, 39; Gen 2:24; Mal 2:16.

**(c) The internal range — and it is wide:**

- **May the guilty party ever remarry?** Mainstream: no. A minority argues repentance restores
  the right.
- **Does it bind those who divorced and remarried before conversion?** **Homer Hailey**
  (1903-2000), the most revered teacher in the non-institutional fellowship, argued in
  ***The Divorced & Remarried Who Would Come to God*** (1991) that people who divorced and
  remarried before their conversion need not separate in order to obey the gospel. **Ben F.
  Vick Jr.** answered in *Truth Magazine* that "if all men are obligated to the law of Christ,
  which includes Matthew 19:3-9, then, all men are obligated to Matthew 19:3-9," concluding
  that "Hailey's position is wrong"
  ([Truth Magazine](https://www.TruthMagazine.com/haileys-view-on-divorce-and-remarriage)).
  **Ed Harrell** defended Hailey's right to fellowship — the dispute was never only about the
  exegesis but about **whether error on this question breaks fellowship**, and it split papers,
  congregations, and friendships through the 1990s. Harrell's *The Churches of Christ in the
  Twentieth Century: Homer Hailey's Personal Journey of Faith* (2000) is both the scholarly
  account and a document in the fight. See [non-institutional.md](non-institutional.md) §7.
- **"Mental divorce" / the waiting game:** whether someone put away for no scriptural cause may
  remarry if the original spouse later commits fornication. Sharply disputed.
- **1 Cor 7:15** (*"if the unbelieving depart … a brother or a sister is not under bondage in
  such cases"*): most CoC teaching holds this permits separation but **not** remarriage; a
  minority reads it as a second exception.
- **Does baptism resolve a prior unscriptural marriage?** Argued both ways.
- Whether a congregation must **withdraw** from members in a marriage it judges unscriptural.

**(d) Compared with broader Christendom.** (RC) indissolubility of a ratified and consummated
sacramental marriage; *porneia* read as an unlawful union (incest, Lev 18) or as permitting
separation only; annulment handles defective consent. (EO) permits remarriage by *oikonomia*.
(Ref/Luth, the "Erasmian" view) adultery **and** desertion dissolve the bond and free the
innocent party — the standard Protestant position, and closer to permissive than the CoC
mainstream. (Broad evangelical) the full range, with much looser practice.

**Do not resolve this in a devotional.** Write about faithfulness, forgiveness, and covenant
without adjudicating who may remarry.

### 6.3 The indwelling of the Holy Spirit

**A genuine, long-running internal dispute**, and one that surprises outsiders who assume
Churches of Christ are uniform.

- **Personal (literal) indwelling** — the majority position: the Spirit himself dwells in the
  Christian, given at baptism, distinct from the word, working providentially and in
  sanctification but not miraculously and not apart from the word in conversion.
  Texts: **Acts 2:38** (*"ye shall receive the gift of the Holy Ghost"*), **Rom 8:9-11**,
  **1 Cor 6:19**, **Gal 4:6**, **Acts 5:32**, Eph 3:16.
- **"Word only" (representative) indwelling** — a substantial minority: the Spirit dwells in the
  Christian *through* the word he revealed, so the indwelling is real but mediated, not personal.
  Texts: Eph 5:18 compared with Col 3:16 (*"be filled with the Spirit"* / *"let the word of
  Christ dwell in you richly"*), John 6:63, Rom 10:17. **Foy E. Wallace Jr.** is the name most
  associated with this position; **Z. T. Sweeney** wrote on the other side. *Verify individual
  attributions before naming anyone — this is a topic where positions are routinely misreported
  by opponents.*

Both sides agree on what actually matters in practice: **the miraculous gifts have ceased**,
there is no modern tongue-speaking, prophecy, or faith healing, and no direct operation of the
Spirit on the sinner's heart apart from the word. Cessationism is near-universal and is argued
from **1 Cor 13:8-10** (*"when that which is perfect is come"* read as the completed revelation)
and Eph 4:11-13, with the gifts understood as imparted by apostolic hands (Acts 8:14-18) and so
ending with that generation.

**Compared with broader Christendom:** the cessationism aligns CoC with confessional Reformed
and much of conservative evangelicalism and puts it in direct opposition to Pentecostal and
charismatic Christianity — which CoC teachers treat as one of the clearest modern errors.
The word-only view has no real analogue outside the movement.

### 6.4 Church discipline and withdrawal of fellowship

**(a) Position.** A congregation is obliged to withdraw from members who persist in sin or
in teaching error, after admonition, for the sake of the person and of the church. It is
practiced — not merely taught — more consistently in Churches of Christ, and especially in
non-institutional churches, than in most of American Christianity.

**(b) Scriptures.** **Matt 18:15-17** (private, witnessed, congregational) · **1 Cor 5:1-13**
(deliver such a one to Satan; *"with such an one no not to eat"*) · **2 Thess 3:6, 14-15**
(*"withdraw yourselves from every brother that walketh disorderly"* — and *"count him not as
an enemy, but admonish him as a brother"*) · **Rom 16:17** (*"mark them which cause divisions
… and avoid them"*) · **Titus 3:10** · **2 John 9-11**.

**(c) Internal range.** What triggers it (open immorality only, or also non-attendance, or
doctrinal error); how public the announcement is; whether it extends to social contact and
family; and — the largest question — whether it applies between *congregations* and *preachers*
as well as to individual members, which is how the movement's divisions were enforced. Practice
runs from near-disuse in some large mainstream congregations to regular, formal use.

**(d) Compared with broader Christendom.** Formally similar to Reformed and Anabaptist
practice; Anabaptist/Mennonite shunning is stricter. Most evangelical churches have the
doctrine and rarely use it. Roman Catholic excommunication is a canonical act of a hierarchy,
not a congregational one.

### 6.5 The Christian and civil government; pacifism

**(a) The historic position.** For roughly its first century the dominant teaching of the
Churches of Christ was that a Christian should not vote, hold office, serve on a jury, sue at
law, join the military, or take any part in civil government. **David Lipscomb** (1831-1917) —
editor of the *Gospel Advocate* 1866-1908, co-founder of the Nashville Bible School — set it
out in ***Civil Government: Its Origin, Mission, and Destiny, and the Christian's Relation to
It***, serialized in the *Gospel Advocate* (1866-67) and the *Christian Quarterly* (1888-89)
and collected as a book in **1889**. Human governments belong to a rebellious order; the
kingdom of God and earthly kingdoms are distinct; the Christian therefore abstains. **Do not
soften this to "he was against war."** Barton Stone's apocalypticism and Alexander Campbell's
denunciations of war carry versions of the same conviction.

**(b) Scriptures.** **Matt 5:38-44** (*"resist not evil… love your enemies"*) ·
**Rom 12:19-21** (*"avenge not yourselves"*) · **John 18:36** (*"my kingdom is not of this
world"*) · **2 Cor 10:3-4** · **Phil 3:20** (citizenship in heaven) · **Jas 4:1-2**. The
counterweight the tradition also has to hold: **Rom 13:1-7** (governing authorities ordained of
God, the sword) and **1 Pet 2:13-17**.

**(c) What happened to it.** The position eroded through the World Wars and had largely
collapsed by mid-century; Churches of Christ today are, on average, politically conservative
and strongly supportive of military service, with chaplains and veterans honored. A minority
of individuals retain the older position — the *Christian Chronicle* has profiled members who
decline to vote on Lipscomb's grounds — and it survives as a recognized historic position rather
than a live consensus. **Do not assume the non-institutional churches are where it survived:**
that division was about church funding of institutions, not about war or the state, and
non-institutional churches are not systematically pacifist. See
[culture-and-politics.md](culture-and-politics.md) §8. This is one of the clearest cases in
the movement of a position abandoned by cultural drift rather than by argument — historians
make this point, and it is fair to note.

**(d) Compared with broader Christendom.** The 19th-century position is close to the historic
peace churches (Mennonite, Brethren, Quaker); the modern position is indistinguishable from
mainstream American evangelicalism.

### 6.6 Giving and benevolence

- **Church benevolence** is directed primarily to **needy saints** — the NT examples are relief
  for Christians (Acts 2:44-45; 4:34-35; 6:1-6; 11:29-30; Rom 15:26; 1 Cor 16:1-3; 2 Cor 8-9).
  **[NI]** Non-institutional churches restrict treasury benevolence to needy saints on that
  ground and treat aid to the world as the **individual's** obligation under Gal 6:10.
  Institutional churches generally hold that the church may relieve non-Christians too.
- **The individual Christian's obligation to the poor is not disputed by anyone.** Do not write
  as though non-institutional churches are against helping the needy; they are against a
  particular *funding channel*, and they will typically say so with some heat.
- **No tithe.** Proportional, purposed, cheerful giving (1 Cor 16:2; 2 Cor 9:6-7).
- **The treasury's authorized works** are usually enumerated as evangelism, edification of the
  saints, and benevolence — the "three works of the church."

### 6.7 Other positions worth knowing

- **Instrumental music** — §3.1. The historic dividing line with the Christian Churches.
- **Sunday school.** The **non-Sunday-school churches** (roughly 1,100 congregations) hold that
  dividing the assembly into classes, and women teaching them, is unauthorized; they meet as a
  whole assembly with one teacher. They overlap heavily with the one-cup churches.
- **Mutual edification churches** (roughly 130 congregations) reject the located preacher in
  favor of rotating teaching by the men of the congregation.
- **The International Churches of Christ (ICOC)** traces to Chuck Lucas's discipling ministry
  at the Crossroads church in Gainesville, Florida; **Kip McKean** took the model to Boston, and
  the ICOC became a separate body in **1993**. It is organizationally and doctrinally distinct —
  discipling partners, central authority — and is **not** what "Churches of Christ" means.
  Mainstream and non-institutional churches disavow it, and conflating them is a common and
  insulting outside error. (McKean's later **International Christian Church / "SoldOut"**
  movement, post-2006, is a third body again.) See [divisions.md](divisions.md) §6.
- **Marriage, gender, and sexuality.** Traditional positions on marriage as between a man and a
  woman and on sexual ethics are held near-uniformly outside the small progressive wing.
- **Original sin, infant baptism, and inherited guilt** — rejected; Ezek 18:20 is the standard
  text (§1, §2).
- **Creeds and confessions** — rejected as human additions; "no creed but Christ, no book but
  the Bible." The standard counter-observation, made from inside as well as outside, is that
  the movement has a functioning unwritten creed enforced through periodicals and fellowship
  practice.
- **The name.** Congregations use "church of Christ" (often lowercase *c* on the sign, which is
  deliberate) from Rom 16:16, and resist the implication that this is a denominational brand.

## What is most often misstated

**By outsiders:**
- That CoC teaches salvation by works, or that "the water saves you." Neither is the taught
  position (§1d).
- That "if it isn't in the Bible you can't do it" is the whole hermeneutic. It isn't —
  generic authority and expedients are half the system (hermeneutics.md §6).
- That all Churches of Christ believe only Churches of Christ members are saved. A real position
  at one end of a real spectrum, not the uniform teaching (§4.4).
- That they are fundamentalists in the American revivalist sense. They are cessationist,
  non-emotional in worship, historically pacifist, amillennial, and hostile to the sinner's
  prayer, altar calls, and revival decisionism. The resemblance to Baptist fundamentalism is
  superficial.
- That the ICOC is the same movement (§6.7).
- Confusing Churches of Christ with the **United Church of Christ** (a mainline denomination,
  entirely unrelated) or with the instrumental **Christian Churches** (related, but a different
  branch).

**By insiders about each other:**
- **Institutional about non-institutional:** that they are "anti" everything, oppose helping
  orphans, and are legalists who divide over trifles. The actual position is about the funding
  channel and the organization, not about whether orphans should be fed (§6.6).
- **Non-institutional about institutional:** that they have abandoned the authority principle
  entirely. Most institutional congregations teach the same hermeneutic and reach a different
  conclusion on classification.
- **Conservative about progressive:** that "change agents" reject biblical authority as such.
  Most of the internal critics — Hicks, Olbricht — argue for a *different* account of authority,
  not for none, and the argument deserves to be stated as they state it.
- **Progressive about conservative:** that CENI is naive proof-texting with no self-awareness.
  The tradition has a developed doctrine of generic and specific authority and knows the
  standard objections; the songbook argument has an answer, whether or not it is a good one.
- **Everyone, about the "five steps":** treating a 20th-century mnemonic as a biblical list, and
  forgetting that Walter Scott's original was half about what *God* does (§1c).
- **Everyone, about the two famous slogans.** Neither *"Where the Scriptures speak, we speak;
  where the Scriptures are silent, we are silent"* nor *"Christians only, but not the only
  Christians"* appears in Thomas Campbell's *Declaration and Address* — both were checked
  against the text. The first is a remark reported from an 1809 meeting; the second has no
  confirmed origin at all. Attribute carefully.

### Flagged unverified
- The origin/attribution of **"we are Christians only, but not the only Christians"** (§4.4).
  It circulates as Barton Stone, Thomas Campbell, and as an anonymous 19th-century formulation;
  no positive attribution was confirmed. What *was* confirmed is a negative: the phrase does not
  appear in the *Declaration and Address*.
- Walter Scott's **1844 letter** quotation (§1c) — taken at second hand from a blog, not from
  Scott's own writings.
- Individual name-to-position attributions on the **word-only indwelling** question (§6.3),
  including Foy E. Wallace Jr. and Z. T. Sweeney. Positions on this subject are routinely
  misreported by opponents; check before naming anyone.
- Whether **1 Tim 3:11** refers to deacons' wives or to women deacons (§4.2) — genuinely
  ambiguous in the Greek and disputed by competent people on both sides.
- Precise **congregation counts** for the sub-groups. The figures above come from the
  *Encyclopedia of the Stone-Campbell Movement* and Wikipedia's syntheses of the 21st Century
  Christian directories; published figures differ by a factor of well over one, so give ranges.
- Whether the **historic pacifism survives anywhere as a congregational position** (§6.5); only
  individual cases were documented.
