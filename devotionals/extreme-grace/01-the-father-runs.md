# May 10 — Luke 15:20: The Father runs

**I went to boot camp a Christian. I let the Navy take what I should have held. I came back with a speech ready, and the Father didn't let me finish it.**

> *"And he arose, and came to his father. But when he was yet a great way off, his father saw him, and had compassion, and ran, and fell on his neck, and kissed him."*
> **Luke 15:20**

## A speech ready

You have a speech ready. An apology. Maybe for a parent you haven't called in years. Maybe for a spouse. Maybe for a brother you haven't spoken to since the wedding, or a friend you ghosted, or a kid you let down without meaning to. Maybe you've been working on it for years. Maybe just for the long drive home. Maybe you wrote it in a hospital chair, on a deployment, or at three in the morning when you couldn't sleep. You know it line by line, ready for the day you finally have the courage.

*I'm sorry. I don't deserve to be back in your life. Just let me earn it.*

You think the speech is humility. You think the long version is what they want to hear. You think the rehearsing matters.

The prodigal had a speech too.

> *"I will arise and go to my father, and will say unto him, Father, I have sinned against heaven, and before thee, And am no more worthy to be called thy son: make me as one of thy hired servants."*
> **Luke 15:18-19**

He had it word for word. He practiced it on the road, between the pig pen and the long walk home. By the time he got close enough to see the lights of his father's house, he could have said it in his sleep.

He never got to deliver the whole thing.

## Yet a great way off

The son had not said his speech yet. He had not gotten close enough to be heard. He was still *a great way off* — far enough that he was still rehearsing, still planning to grovel, still fully expecting to be turned into a hired servant.

The Father saw him.

The Father ran.

In first-century Middle Eastern culture, a dignified man did not run. Running required hiking your robe up, exposing your legs, looking like a fool. Old men did not look like fools in public. The Father did not care.

He ran. He hugged his disgraced son in front of the whole village. He kissed him. He cut off the rehearsed speech with a robe and a ring and a fattened calf and a party. The son tried to deliver his line — *"Make me as one of thy hired servants"* — and the Father wouldn't let him finish.

## Who he was telling it to

It is tempting to read this and stop. The Father runs. The speech is cut off. You are received. Beautiful.

But Jesus is not telling this parable to *you.* He is telling it to the people grumbling about you.

> *"Now the tax collectors and sinners were all drawing near to hear Him. And the Pharisees and the scribes grumbled, saying, This man receiveth sinners, and eateth with them. And he spake this parable unto them..."*
> **Luke 15:1-3**

Read that again. The tax collectors and sinners are *already coming.* They do not need the parable to convince them — they are at his feet. The parable is for the religious people who are upset about it.

Jesus tells three parables in a row — the lost sheep, the lost coin, the prodigal son. All three end the same way. *Heaven rejoices. Don't you?* And the third parable does something the first two don't. There is a second son in this one. He stands outside the party and refuses to come in. The Father comes out — *a second time.*

> *"Therefore came his father out, and intreated him."*
> **Luke 15:28**

Twice in one chapter the Father leaves the house. He runs to the prodigal. He pleads with the older brother. The grace is the same. The indignity is the same. The party is the same.

Jesus stops the parable before we know whether the older brother comes in. He left it that way on purpose. He left the question hanging in front of the Pharisees. He left it hanging in front of us.

## Come in to the party for sinners

You read this devotional thinking it was about coming home.

You may be the one being asked to come *in.*

Some of us have been the prodigal. The boot camp, the deployment, the season we don't talk about, the thing we did when we thought no one was watching. We came back. The Father did not make us earn it. He still won't.

But coming back can become a list. *I returned. I am here every Sunday. I am not like the people I used to be.* And quietly, somewhere underneath, we resent that grace seems to come cheaper for the ones who didn't have to claw their way home. We do not say it. We don't have to. The Father knows.

Look at what God thinks of sin in the meantime. Read Ezekiel 16. Read Ezekiel 23. The language is almost pornographic. Many Christians wonder why God put it in the Bible. He put it there because he wants us to feel in the pit of our stomach what *he* feels when he looks at our sin. He hates it. It disgusts him.

> *"But God commendeth his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us."*
> **Romans 5:8**

*While we were yet sinners.* Not after the speech. Not after we cleaned up. Not after we got tolerable. *While we were still disgusting* — Christ died.

We can never be so disgusting that God will cease to love us. We can never be so dutiful that we don't need this Father coming out the door.

The faith-extreme grace position is this: the Father is still on the doorstep. He has come out twice. He may have run to you years ago, when you were a long way off. He is also out in the yard right now, asking you to come in to the party for sinners.

Coming in is not nothing. The Father puts a robe on the prodigal. He puts a ring on his hand. He brings him into the household. Grace does not turn the speech into nothing — it puts you under a different obligation. The obligation of a son. The household. The brothers and sisters at the table you didn't pick. *That* is the party.

Stop rehearsing. Come home unfinished. And then — come in to the party for sinners.

## A prayer

Father,

I have rehearsed my apology.
I have made it long enough to prove I take it seriously.
I have made it eloquent enough to feel I deserve a hearing.

You see me yet a great way off.
You have come out for me before,
and you are still on the doorstep.

Run to me again.
Run to the part of me still in the yard,
keeping a list,
refusing to come in.

Let me come home unfinished.
Let me come in to the party for sinners.
Let me be one of them.

I am still a long way off.
I am coming.

***Father, I have come home unfinished.***

Amen.
