# May 10 — Luke 15:20: The Father runs

**I have rehearsed my apology for years. I have always believed God forgives. I have not always believed he runs.**

> *"And he arose, and came to his father. But when he was yet a great way off, his father saw him, and had compassion, and ran, and fell on his neck, and kissed him."*
> **Luke 15:20**

## The rehearsal

You have a speech. You've been working on it for years.

Maybe not on paper — maybe just in your head, in the dark, when you can't sleep. The speech comes. The long list of all the things you mean to say to God when you finally have the courage. *I am sorry. I am not worthy. Make me a hired servant. I will earn my keep.*

You think the speech is humility. You think the long version of the speech is what God wants to hear. You think the rehearsing matters.

The prodigal had a speech too.

> *"I will arise and go to my father, and will say unto him, Father, I have sinned against heaven, and before thee, And am no more worthy to be called thy son: make me as one of thy hired servants."*
> **Luke 15:18-19**

He had it word for word. He had practiced it on the road, between the pig pen and the long walk home. By the time he got close enough to see the lights of his father's house, he could have said it in his sleep.

He never got to deliver the whole thing.

## Yet a great way off

Read Luke 15:20 again. Slowly.

> *"And he arose, and came to his father. But when he was yet a great way off, his father saw him, and had compassion, and ran, and fell on his neck, and kissed him."*
> **Luke 15:20**

The son had not said his speech yet. He had not gotten close enough to be heard. He was still *a great way off* — far enough that he was still rehearsing the words, still planning to grovel, still fully expecting to be turned into a hired servant.

The Father saw him.

The Father ran.

In first-century Middle Eastern culture, a dignified man did not run. Running required hiking your robe up, exposing your legs, looking like a fool. Old men did not look like fools in public. The Father did not care.

He ran. He hugged his disgraced son in front of the whole village. He kissed him. He cut off the rehearsed speech with a robe and a ring and a fattened calf and a party. The son tried to deliver his line — *"Make me as one of thy hired servants"* — and the Father wouldn't even let him finish.

> *"For this my son was dead, and is alive again; he was lost, and is found."*
> **Luke 15:24**

That sentence ends the rehearsal. He's not going to be a hired servant. He's a son. He always was.

## The other son

There are two sons in this story. We talk about the first one because he is dramatic. The second one matters more, because the second one is mostly us.

The older brother had not run away. He had stayed home, worked the land, earned everything. He wasn't rebellious in the obvious way. He was rebellious in the polite way — he believed his sonship was a wage. *"Lo, these many years do I serve thee, neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment."* He had been keeping a list. Same as you.

When the Father finds out the older brother is outside refusing to come in, what does the Father do?

He goes out.

> *"Therefore came his father out, and intreated him."*
> **Luke 15:28**

Twice in one chapter the Father leaves the house to go to a son. He runs to the prodigal. He goes out and *pleads* with the older brother. The grace is the same. The dignity-cost is the same.

The older brother's sin isn't his obedience. It's his belief that the obedience earned something the Father would have given anyway.

The parable ends without telling us whether the older brother comes in. Jesus left it open. He left the question hanging in front of the Pharisees, and in front of you. *Will you come in to the party for sinners?*

## Before you finish

The faith-extreme grace position is this: God doesn't run because you finished the speech. He runs because he saw you turn.

You have been preparing your apology for years. You think the long version is what makes it count. You think the *quality* of your repentance is what God responds to.

Read Luke 15:20 one more time. The Father's compassion comes *before* the confession. The compassion is what produces the confession that finally arrives. We have it backwards.

> *"But God commendeth his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us."*
> **Romans 5:8**

*While we were yet sinners.* Not while we were getting our act together. Not while we were almost ready. Not while we had cleaned up enough to deserve the conversation. *While we were yet sinners* — still drunk, still angry, still a long way off — Christ died.

We can never be so disgusting that God will cease to love us.

Stop rehearsing. Come home unfinished. Let him cut you off mid-speech.

## A prayer

Father,

I have been rehearsing my apology for years.
I have made it long enough to prove I take it seriously.
I have made it eloquent enough to feel I deserve a hearing.

But you see me yet a great way off.
You see what I am still rehearsing.

Run, and cut me off mid-sentence.
Put a robe on me before I finish saying I am not worthy.
Kiss me where the village can see.

I am still a long way off.
I am coming.

***Father, I have come home unfinished.***

Amen.
