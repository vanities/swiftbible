# July 5 — Jonah 4:2: The chapter after the happy ending

> *"Therefore I fled before unto Tarshish: for I knew that thou art a gracious God, and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness, and repentest thee of the evil."*
> **Jonah 4:2**

## The happy ending comes a chapter early

Most of us remember how the book of Jonah goes. The prophet runs, the fish eats him, the fish spits him out, and the wicked city repents at the eleventh hour. By the end of chapter three the whole thing is wrapped up. The king is in sackcloth. The fire is called off. God himself is satisfied.

> *"And God saw their works, that they turned from their evil way; and God repented of the evil, that he had said that he would do unto them; and he did it not."*
> **Jonah 3:10**

That is the happy ending. That is where we stop telling it.

The book has one more chapter. It is eleven verses long, almost nobody can quote it, and it is the reason the other three were written. Nobody illustrates chapter four for children, because there is no way to draw it without making the hero look terrible. The city is saved, and the preacher is furious.

> *"But it displeased Jonah exceedingly, and he was very angry."*
> **Jonah 4:1**

## The reason he ran

Angry at what? At the best day any prophet ever had. Isaiah preached his whole life to a nation that would not listen. Jeremiah got the stocks and the bottom of a cistern. Jonah preached one sentence, eight words in our Bible, and a city of more than a hundred and twenty thousand people fell on its face. Jonah watched it happen, walked out, and asked God to kill him.

Then he prays, and the prayer explains the whole book. He ran, he says, because he knew exactly who God is. *I knew that thou art a gracious God, and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness.* He was sure that if Nineveh turned, God would relent, and he could not stand it.

He is quoting, by the way. Gracious and merciful, slow to anger, abundant in kindness: that is what God declared about himself to Moses on Sinai. Israel had been singing it for centuries.

> *"The LORD is gracious, and full of compassion; slow to anger, and of great mercy."*
> **Psalm 145:8**

Jonah recites his nation's favorite song about God, word for word, as an accusation. He may be the only man in scripture who states the doctrine of grace perfectly and hates every word of it. His theology was flawless. His complaint was where the grace was pointed.

Because Nineveh was the capital of Assyria, the cruelest empire of the ancient world, and Israel was on its menu. Within a generation, Assyrian armies would erase Jonah's nation and carry his people away. God had asked a man to deliver mercy to the people who were going to bury his country.

Jonah ran from God's mercy the way other men run from God's wrath.

## Why make him go?

Here is the weirdest thing in the chapter. Nothing in it surprises Jonah. *Was not this my saying, when I was yet in my country?* He called the ending before he left home. From where he stood it was settled the moment God said *go*: gracious God, repentant city, fire called off.

Which means the errand itself must have felt like an insult. If God was going to be merciful anyway, why make Jonah get up and walk to Nineveh? Why must the man who wants them dead be the one to carry their lifeline? The grace was coming with him or without him. He knew it, and he was made to go anyway.

Stranger still, the king of Nineveh had no such certainty. *Who can tell if God will turn and repent?* he says. The pagan bet his whole city on a maybe. The prophet held a guarantee and went looking for a hill to die on.

So why send him? Because the city was never the only patient. Nineveh took one sermon. Jonah takes the rest of the book, and the book runs out of verses before he comes around. God routed the mercy through the man who resented it, because carrying grace to a doorstep is how a man learns to want it there. Jonah carried it the whole way, said his eight words, watched them work, and went up the hill to be angry.

## The gourd, the worm, and the question

God answers the angriest prayer in the Bible with a question.

> *"Then said the LORD, Doest thou well to be angry?"*
> **Jonah 4:4**

Jonah does not answer it. He walks out the east gate, builds himself a booth on a hill, and sits down to watch the city, still hoping for fire. Abraham once stood before the Lord and haggled him down to ten righteous men, trying to talk heaven out of destroying Sodom. Jonah found a seat with a view.

What God does next is so gentle it is almost funny. He grows a plant.

> *"And the LORD God prepared a gourd, and made it to come up over Jonah, that it might be a shadow over his head, to deliver him from his grief. So Jonah was exceeding glad of the gourd."*
> **Jonah 4:6**

Exceeding glad. It is the only time in the whole book Jonah is happy. A city walked out of its own grave and it displeased him exceedingly; a vine grew over his head and he was exceeding glad.

Notice the word *prepared,* too. A gourd at evening. A worm at sunrise, and the gourd withers. A scorching east wind by midmorning, and Jonah wilts along with his plant. God keeps preparing things for this one stubborn man, and every one of them is aimed at his heart. Then he repeats his question with one small addition: *Doest thou well to be angry for the gourd?* Jonah, sunstruck and furious, gives his last recorded words: *I do well to be angry, even unto death.*

Then God speaks, and the book ends.

> *"Thou hast had pity on the gourd, for the which thou hast not laboured, neither madest it grow; which came up in a night, and perished in a night: And should not I spare Nineveh, that great city, wherein are more than sixscore thousand persons that cannot discern between their right hand and their left hand; and also much cattle?"*
> **Jonah 4:10-11**

One detail the English hides: *pity* and *spare* there are the same Hebrew verb. You had pity on the gourd, God says. Should I not have pity on the city? He answers Jonah with Jonah's own word.

And there it stops. No verse twelve. Jonah never answers, the narrator never resolves it, and the book ends mid-conversation, with God's question hanging in the desert air. The last words of the whole book are *and also much cattle.* His pity runs so deep it has room for the livestock. His prophet could not find any for the people.

## The seat east of the city

The ending is on purpose. The question was never finally aimed at Jonah.

You have a Nineveh. A person whose repentance you would read with suspicion, whose forgiveness would feel like theft. The man who wrecked a family you love and then found Jesus in prison, which you find a little convenient. We love the creed, gracious and merciful, slow to anger. We just keep a quiet list of people it had better not apply to. Mercy for me, and a good long memory for them.

Here is what makes chapter four so uncomfortable. The grudge lives happily underneath right conduct. You can do everything God asked, stay polite, stay helpful, even pray for the person, and still be sitting east of their life with your arms crossed, waiting for the fire you privately believe they have coming.

The book will not pretend your feeling is shallow, and neither will I. Nineveh had earned its reputation. The king's own decree admits to "the violence that is in their hands." The wound may be real, and chapter four never asks you to call it nothing. The mercy followed their turning; the city tore its robes first. Grace agrees with you about what happened. It just refuses to hand you the torch. It asks you to stop grieving when the guilty repent and live.

Watch where the grudge finally goes, too, because Jonah's does not stay hot. It sinks. He asks God to take his life in verse three. He wishes for death again in verse eight, fainting in the heat after the worm. By verse nine he is telling God his anger is worth dying of. Anger at grace has nowhere to put itself. It cannot unsave the city, and it will not join the feast, so it turns on the man carrying it. Jonah kept the grudge, and the grudge was killing him.

There is a particular grief in being right, too. Every doctrine he believed was proved true in front of him, and the proof flattened him. He had the correct answer about God and no joy anywhere in it. Knowing the creed and wanting the creed are two different loves.

Now look at how God treats the man at the bottom. That may be the gentlest thing in the chapter. No fire falls on the prophet for the ugly prayer. The gourd, the text says plainly, was grown *to deliver him from his grief.* God answers depression about grace with more grace. If despair is a country you have visited, notice the order: shade first, then company, then a question. He shades him, questions him, comes out to the hill where his angriest servant sits, and explains his own heart to him, right up to the last sentence of the book. Every ounce of the conflict in this chapter belongs to Jonah. Every ounce of the compassion belongs to God, and some of it is landing on Jonah the whole time he resents it.

Jesus told this story again, you know. A father throws a feast for a son who came home, and the older brother stands out in the yard, angry at the grace. The father comes out to him too. The book of Jonah ends with the Father out in the yard, still talking.

And Jesus said the men of Nineveh will stand up at the judgment, *"because they repented at the preaching of Jonas; and, behold, a greater than Jonas is here."* The greater than Jonah also looked at a city that deserved fire, and he wept over it. Jonah walked out of a city gate to watch sinners die. Jesus walked out of one to die for them, praying for the men with the hammers: *Father, forgive them.* That is the prayer Jonah refused, prayed by the only one who ever had the right to refuse it.

So the question still stands. It is the last thing God says in the book, and he says it to whoever is sitting east of somebody's life, watching, hoping. *Doest thou well to be angry?*

I have sat in that seat. The book gets no chapter five until you and I answer him.

## A prayer

Lord,

I know your creed by heart.
Gracious and merciful, slow to anger, abundant in kindness.
I have loved every word of it when it was pointed at me.

There is a name I do not want you to forgive.
You have heard me pray around it for years.

I am the man under the dead plant,
grieving my own shade
with a city at my back.

You came out to the hill to talk to him.
Come out to mine.
If you route your mercy through me,
do not let me hand it over with my arms crossed.
Keep asking me your question, and do not let me leave it hanging in the air the way he did.

***Doest thou well to be angry? Stay with me until I can say no.***

Amen.
