# June 7 — Exodus 8:15: The respite he used

**I'm reading Exodus from the start again, and it isn't the plagues that scare me. It's the verse where Pharaoh sees the respite and uses it to harden his heart. I've made that move with God's patience more times than I want to count.**

> *"But when Pharaoh saw that there was respite, he hardened his heart, and hearkened not unto them; as the LORD had said."*
> **Exodus 8:15**

## You have time

You have time. You may not say it out loud, but you believe it. You felt something last Sunday, a small pull toward getting right with God about a specific thing, and you let it pass, because the pull has always come back before. There is a conversation you keep not having. A habit you keep meaning to break after this next stretch settles down. A part of your life you have quietly agreed to deal with later.

Later has been the plan for years.

And you are not worried about it. God has always been there. He was there last year when you meant to start, and the year before that. The door has been open every single time you finally got around to checking. So there is no rush. You can finish the thing in front of you first.

That is the most reasonable assumption in the world. It is also the exact place where Pharaoh lived.

## Ten times the door opened

I always read Pharaoh as the villain. The cartoon tyrant with his arms crossed, practically asking to be drowned. This time through Exodus I noticed something I had skimmed for years. God kept giving him outs.

The frogs come, and Pharaoh cracks. He calls for Moses. *Intreat the LORD, that he may take away the frogs from me.* Moses prays. The frogs die in heaps and the whole land stinks of mercy. And then this:

> *"But when Pharaoh saw that there was respite, he hardened his heart."*
> **Exodus 8:15**

Respite. The breathing room. The moment the pressure lifts. Pharaoh felt the relief and used it as his reason to dig back in. The mercy did not soften him. He spent it.

It happens again and again. The flies come, Pharaoh begs, God relents, Pharaoh hardens. The hail comes, Pharaoh actually says the words *I have sinned,* God stops the storm, and the second the sky clears he takes it back. God opens the door ten times. Ten times Pharaoh looks at the open door and decides he still has time.

## The line nobody drew but him

This is where people get nervous, because the text really does say, more than once, that God hardened Pharaoh's heart. Some read that and decide Pharaoh never had a chance. That God reached into his skull, took the wheel, and then punished him for the crash. If that were true, it should frighten all of us.

But read the order. For the first five plagues, every hardening belongs to Pharaoh. *He* hardened his heart. *His* heart was hard. God is not yet said to be doing anything at all. The first time the text says the LORD hardened Pharaoh's heart is in the sixth plague, at chapter nine, verse twelve. And Pharaoh is never once said to harden his own heart after chapter nine. The phrases are not scattered at random. They are stacked. First it is all Pharaoh. Then, only after Pharaoh has made himself unmistakably clear, it is God.

You do not have to imagine God hijacking a mind to make sense of that. I know how to start a fight with my wife in under five minutes if I want to. I do not need a miracle for it. I just need to know her and choose the wrong words on purpose. God knew Pharaoh. After enough refusals, God stopped pleading and started confirming. He gave Pharaoh fully over to the man Pharaoh had already decided to be. Paul has a phrase for it. God gave them up. The hardening was a sentence, finally handed down on a man who had spent ten plagues earning it.

Pharaoh ended up God's punching bag by his own doing. He drew the line himself, one respite at a time, and then God honored it.

## What the patience was for

So what was all that patience for? Paul says it straight.

> *"Or despisest thou the riches of his goodness and forbearance and longsuffering; not knowing that the goodness of God leadeth thee to repentance? But after thy hardness and impenitent heart treasurest up unto thyself wrath."*
> **Romans 2:4-5**

The patience was an invitation with your name on it. Every respite Pharaoh got was God saying, one more time, *come home.* He heard *take your time,* and treated the mercy as permission. And notice the word Paul reaches for. Hardness. The very same word. A goodness meant to lead you to repentance, treated instead as a reason to put repentance off, slowly turns your own heart to stone. You do the hardening. God just stops stopping you.

The writer of Hebrews knew exactly which story to reach for when he wanted to warn people, and he put it in the present tense:

> *"But exhort one another daily, while it is called To day; lest any of you be hardened through the deceitfulness of sin."*
> **Hebrews 3:13**

The deceit is the plainest, oldest lie you tell yourself. *There will be a tomorrow for this.* Proverbs puts the whole tragedy in a single line: *he, that being often reproved hardeneth his neck, shall suddenly be destroyed, and that without remedy.*

I can open Exodus and put my finger on the verse where Pharaoh passed the point of no return. I cannot do that with my own life. I do not know where my line is. I do not know where yours is. Let that take the word *later* out of your mouth.

## It is still called Today

Here is the grace, and it is bigger than you have been treating it. The patience you are standing in right this second is God refusing to be finished with you. He remembers the sin exactly, and he is holding the door open anyway.

> *"The Lord is not slack concerning his promise, as some men count slackness; but is longsuffering to us-ward, not willing that any should perish, but that all should come to repentance."*
> **2 Peter 3:9**

Not willing that any should perish. The respite you are in is mercy, the same mercy Pharaoh kept getting handed. The only thing ever in question is what you do with it. Pharaoh felt the breathing room and went back to bed. You do not have to.

There is a thing you have been meaning to make right. You know what it is. You knew before you opened this. The grace is that the door is still open, that it is still called Today, that you have not run out of rope. Do not spend this respite the way he did. Get up while the getting up is still yours to choose.

## A prayer

Lord,

I have read about Pharaoh and shaken my head at him.
I have made his move and called it being busy.

You have been patient with me about the same things for years.
I have heard the patience and called it permission.

I do not know where my line is.
I am not asking to see it.
I am asking you to take the word *later* out of my mouth.

The respite I am in today is your mercy.
Do not let me spend it the way he did.
Let me get up while it is still called Today.

***Lord, I have heard your patience as permission. Soften me before I finish the job myself.***

Amen.
