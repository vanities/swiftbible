# Come, Lord Jesus

Most Christians don't actually pray for Jesus to come back.

We say we believe it. We confess it in the creeds. We sing about it on Sunday morning. But if you're honest, when's the last time you knelt down and asked - *"Lord, come now"*? Not "soon" in some abstract way. Not eventually. But tonight. Today. Before the trip you've planned. Before the wedding. Before your kids grow up.

There's a reason we don't.

We have plans. The career we're still building. The marriage that's young. The grandkids we want to meet. The vacation we saved for. The book we mean to write. We've watched parents and grandparents grow old and die without seeing this day, and somewhere along the way we quietly accepted that we probably will too — that history will keep grinding on as it has for two thousand years, and we'll slip out of it like everyone else.

And honestly? We're not ready. There are sins we mean to deal with. Conversations we mean to have. People we mean to forgive. *Later. Not yet.*

So we don't pray for Christ's return. We pray around it.

---

But scroll your news feed for five minutes.

Wars in places that didn't have wars last year. Children buried in rubble. Women trafficked across borders. A loneliness epidemic at scales we don't have language for. Mental health crises in teenagers we used to call kids. Whole generations growing up without fathers, without hope, without any sense of God. Overdoses. Mass shootings. The slow erosion of dignity for human life.

If you're honest, the world is not getting better. It is groaning.

> *"For we know that the whole creation groaneth and travaileth in pain together until now."*
> — Romans 8:22

The early church looked around at *their* world — Roman occupation, persecution, plagues, families ripped apart for the name of Christ — and they cried one word: ***Maranatha*** (1 Cor 16:22). *Come, Lord!* They didn't pray it as a metaphor. They prayed it as a request. They wanted it to happen *to them*. They wanted to see his face in their lifetime.

> *"He which testifieth these things saith, Surely I come quickly. Amen. Even so, come, Lord Jesus."*
> — Revelation 22:20

Those are the last words of the Bible. The whole story closes on a plea.

---

Paul puts it like this:

> *"For our conversation is in heaven; from whence also we look for the Saviour, the Lord Jesus Christ."*
> — Philippians 3:20

*Look for.* Watching. Waiting. Wanting.

The faith-extreme position is this: our citizenship is in heaven, not here. Every prayer you've ever prayed — *thy kingdom come, thy will be done on earth as it is in heaven* — is asking for this. We say it on autopilot. Do we mean it?

This isn't about hating life or rejecting good things. The wedding is good. The children are good. The work is good. But Paul could write "***to die is gain***" (Phil 1:21) and mean it — not because he hated being alive, but because he saw what was on the other side.

You can love your wife and still want to see Jesus's face. You can want your kids to grow up *and* want him to come back today. These don't have to fight each other. The longing for Christ doesn't crowd out love for the people around you. It deepens it — because everything you love about them was made by him, and they will be more themselves, not less, when he returns.

But why hasn't he come yet?

Peter answers plainly:

> *"The Lord is not slack concerning his promise, as some men count slackness; but is longsuffering to us-ward, not willing that any should perish, but that all should come to repentance."*
> — 2 Peter 3:9

He tarries because he's still gathering people. Every day he waits is mercy for someone who hasn't met him yet.

So we pray *come* — and we live like the delay is grace.

Pray it tonight, even if it scares you. Even if part of you wants to hold it back.

***Come, Lord Jesus.***

If you mean it, the world looks different in the morning.

---

## A prayer

King Jesus,

I have prayed around your return for too long.
I have wanted you to come — *eventually, later, after I've finished my plans*.
But the world is groaning, and so am I,
and your church for two thousand years has cried *come*.

So let me cry it too.

Not in fear of losing what I love,
but in love of seeing your face.
Not because life is bad,
but because you are better.
Not because I am ready — I am not —
but because you are coming, and your coming is grace.

***Maranatha.*** Come, Lord Jesus.

Until then, make me ready.

Amen.
