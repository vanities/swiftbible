# [date TBD] — Luke 22:19: Remember me

**Somebody in the room is already thinking about lunch. Some Sundays it's me.**

> *"And he took bread, and gave thanks, and brake it, and gave it unto them, saying, This is my body which is given for you: this do in remembrance of me."*
> **Luke 22:19**

## Be honest

Who, during the Lord's Supper, is already thinking about lunch?

Come on. The tray comes down the row, somebody bows to pray, and somewhere between the bread and the cup you're deciding where everybody's going to eat after. The usual place, or somewhere new? Is it going to be packed? Will there be a wait with the after-church crowd? You're still half-annoyed about the thing your kid said in the car. Monday's already in your head, with that meeting in it you don't want to go to. You take the bread, you hold it, you eat it, you move on. If somebody stopped you in the parking lot and asked what you just did up there, the honest answer is *I'm not totally sure.*

The words have gone smooth on us. We've heard them so many times they're like a coin that's been in too many pockets, worn down to where you can't read the face anymore. We call this remembering him. Most weeks it's closer to forgetting him politely, with bread in our hands.

## The word he actually used

The word Jesus chose is sharper than the English lets on. Luke writes it in Greek as **anamnesis** (ἀνάμνησις). It's a compound. The back half is `mnesis`, memory, from the same root that gives us *mnemonic* and *amnesty*. The front is `ana`, which means *up* or *again*, a calling back up to the surface.

Now negate it. Swap the `ana` for the little privative `a`, the Greek way of saying *not*, and you get **amnesia** (ἀμνησία). The word for a man who wakes in a hospital bed and doesn't know his own name. *Anamnesis* is the exact opposite of that. It's the deliberate undoing of amnesia, memory hauled back up on purpose.

And this is a loaded word in the New Testament. The writer of Hebrews uses it for the old sacrifices, which couldn't actually take sin away:

> *"But in those sacrifices there is a remembrance again made of sins every year."*
> **Hebrews 10:3**

Same word, *anamnesis*. Year after year the blood of bulls only reminded Israel that the debt was still open. Jesus takes that loaded word, *anamnesis*, the one tangled up with a debt that never closed, and hands it to his church the night before he closes the debt himself. *This* do in remembrance of me. Not one more reminder of guilt. The memorial of the one death that worked.

He's also doing it at a very particular meal. This is Passover. He has the unleavened bread in his hands because the Jews had been keeping a memorial for fourteen centuries already:

> *"And this day shall be unto you for a memorial."*
> **Exodus 12:14**

The Hebrew there is *zikkaron*, a memorial, from the verb **zakar**, to remember. Every spring Israel ate the lamb and the bitter herbs so the children would ask why, and the parents would say, *because the LORD brought us out.* Jesus sits inside that ancient memorial meal, picks up its bread, and quietly makes himself the thing it was always pointing at. The Lamb is at the head of the table, refitting the oldest remembrance Israel had.

## When God remembers somebody

Here's where it got good for me, and it's the thing I heard preached once and never shook. In the Bible, when *God* remembers a person, it is never that he forgot them and it slipped back into his mind. God doesn't misplace people. When scripture says God remembered someone, it means the waiting is over and he is about to move. His remembering has hands.

Watch it happen. God remembered Noah, and a wind passed over the water and the flood began to sink.

> *"And God remembered Noah... and God made a wind to pass over the earth, and the waters asswaged."*
> **Genesis 8:1**

He remembered Abraham, and reached into a city that was already on fire to pull Lot out of it (Genesis 19:29). Rachel had been barren so long it had become her whole identity; *God remembered Rachel,* and she carried a son (Genesis 30:22). Hannah prayed in the temple until the priest thought she was drunk; the text says simply that *the LORD remembered her,* and the next person born is Samuel (1 Samuel 1:19). And when Israel was groaning under Egypt with no rescue in sight:

> *"And God heard their groaning, and God remembered his covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob."*
> **Exodus 2:24**

The very next thing in the story is a bush on fire and a man named Moses. Every time God remembers, the ground moves.

And people learned to pray it back to him. Hannah, before God ever remembered her, stood in the temple and begged him to: *remember me, and forget not thine handmaid* (1 Samuel 1:11). Blind Samson, chained between the pillars with his strength gone, prayed *remember me, I pray thee, and strengthen me only this once,* and brought the whole house down (Judges 16:28). Hezekiah, told to set his affairs in order and die, turned his face to the wall and asked God to *remember* how he had walked before him, and got fifteen more years of life (2 Kings 20:3). *Remember me* is the oldest prayer there is.

It reaches its rawest version on a hill outside Jerusalem, prayed by a man with nothing left to bring. A criminal, hours from death, with no baptism and no resume and nothing left to bargain with, turns his head toward the next cross over and asks for the one thing that saves a man:

> *"Lord, remember me when thou comest into thy kingdom."*
> **Luke 23:42**

*Remember me.* He's asking God to do the Genesis 8 thing, the Exodus 2 thing, for him. And Jesus answers him on the spot: *To day shalt thou be with me in paradise.* Remembered, with hands.

## Re-membering yourself

So look again at what's in your palm on Sunday morning.

Paul says the bread does something to all of us at once:

> *"For we being many are one bread, and one body: for we are all partakers of that one bread."*
> **1 Corinthians 10:17**

Notice the shape of our English word, too. Re-member. The opposite of dis-member, of being torn into pieces. The dictionary won't actually back me up that those share a root, so I won't pretend they do, but the picture is true even when the etymology isn't, because Paul just told us the one bread makes the scattered many into one body. I come to that table most weeks in pieces, half here and half three days down the road. And the table puts me back together. It re-members me into him.

That's why we don't do this once and hang the certificate on the wall. We come back every first day of the week, the way a body comes back to food, because a body that stops eating starves and a soul that stops remembering forgets whose it is. There is nothing magic in the bread. It is a memorial, not a medicine. All its weight sits in the remembering, which is exactly why drifting off mid-bite is not the small thing we treat it as.

And remember what Hebrews said the old sacrifices were. An *anamnesis* of sins, the same debt read aloud year after year and never closed. The new covenant runs the other direction:

> *"And their sins and iniquities will I remember no more."*
> **Hebrews 10:17**

That is God quoting his own promise from Jeremiah, and it means two rememberings meet at this table. You remember him. And the one thing the old bread kept dragging back up, your sin, is the thing he now refuses to recall. He remembers you, and your sins no more.

I won't pretend staying present is easy. Your mind is going to wander; mine does. Lunch really is waiting, and the line really does get long. But hold the two halves of this together. You ask him *remember me,* the way the thief did. And he hands you bread and says *remember me* right back. Every Sunday, the cure for amnesia gets set in your open hand, and you are asked to do the deliberate thing, to haul it back up on purpose: who died for you, and who you became when he did. Grace puts the bread there. The remembering is the part he left for you.

## A prayer

Lord Jesus,

I have held this bread a thousand times
with my mind already out the door and down the road.
Forgive me for forgetting you politely,
week after week, with the cure right in my hand.

You remembered Noah, and the waters went down.
You remembered Abraham, and pulled Lot from the fire.
You remembered Hannah, and heard a temple full of silence.
You remembered the thief, and opened the gate that same afternoon.

When the tray reaches me this Sunday,
remember me too.
Pull me back out of the fog,
gather the scattered pieces of me
into your body where they belong,
and teach me to remember you on purpose.

Amen.
