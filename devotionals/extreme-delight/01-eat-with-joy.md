# May 24 — Ecclesiastes 9:7: Eat with joy

**My wife will look at my plate, then hers, then mine again, and ask how I'm already done, and the honest answer is I never really slowed down enough to know what I was eating.**

> *"Go thy way, eat thy bread with joy, and drink thy wine with a merry heart; for God now accepteth thy works."*
> **Ecclesiastes 9:7**

## The meal you don't remember

You can love food and still not taste it. You can be grateful for food and still finish a plate without ever really meeting it. Most of us have built a whole quiet life around eating to get on with the next thing, and we tell ourselves this is fine because we said grace at the start.

There is the lunch eaten over the laptop, the coffee that cools next to the keyboard while the morning gets away, the really good chocolate hidden in the cabinet that you eat standing up so it doesn't count. There is the dinner table where the kids are loud and the conversation is moving fast and the food is just fuel for what's happening on top of it. None of this is gluttony. None of it is even disordered. It is just a quiet refusal to be present with the thing in your mouth.

## The Preacher's strange command

Ecclesiastes spends eight chapters insisting that everything under the sun is vanity, the work and the pleasure and the wisdom alike. And then in chapter nine, without warning, the Preacher hands down an imperative: *go your way, eat your bread with joy, drink your wine with a merry heart.* No softening, no "in moderation," no hedge. He just says do it, and he says God already accepts the works you bring to the table.

The Hebrew for "merry heart" is *lev tov*, literally "good heart." A heart at rest can hold a glass of wine the way a heart at war cannot. The verse is paying attention to the condition of the heart receiving the drink more than to the drink itself.

> *"Live joyfully with the wife whom thou lovest all the days of the life of thy vanity... Whatsoever thy hand findeth to do, do it with thy might."*
> **Ecclesiastes 9:9-10**

The argument is unembarrassed. *Because* life is fleeting, eat with joy. *Because* the grave is coming, drink with a good heart. The Preacher hands you joy as a direct command from the God who made the world you are living in.

And the New Testament does not soften this. Paul tells Timothy that every creature of God is good, and nothing is to be refused if it is received with thanksgiving (1 Tim 4:4). He tells the rich to set their hope on the God who *richly gives us all things to enjoy* (1 Tim 6:17). Luke records Paul telling pagans in Lystra that God has been filling their hearts with food and gladness all along (Acts 14:17). Gladness is the gift God is handing you across the table at every meal.

## The obedience is in the tasting

The reason this lands hard is that most of us have already done the easy version. We say grace. We thank God for the meal. We even tell each other the meal was good. What we do not do is *taste* it. Gratitude has quietly become a substitute for delight, and the substitute will not do. The Preacher commands the joy itself. He wants the actual tasting, the actual drinking, the wine reaching your mouth with your attention attached to it.

If you grew up being told that pleasure is suspicious, this will feel like permission you don't trust. If you grew up counting calories or stewarding macros or treating food as a problem to manage, this will feel like a setup. It is neither. The God who made your tongue capable of distinguishing salt from sweet from bitter from umami intended you to use those receptors. The eating is the worship. The tasting is the obedience.

Joy is for now. The Preacher will not let you defer it until the kids are grown, or the project ships, or you feel you have earned the right to slow down. *Whatsoever thy hand findeth to do, do it with thy might.* Including the eating and the drinking. Including the small good meal in front of you tonight that you are about to inhale on the way to the next thing if no one stops you.

## A prayer

*Father, you gave me a tongue that can taste and a body that needs feeding, and I have spent most of my meals on my way somewhere else. Slow me down at the next table. Help me put down the phone, see the plate, taste the bread, drink the wine, and call it what you called it on the sixth day. Good. Amen.*
