# May 17 — Exodus 16:20: The bread that breeds worms

**I have a six-month emergency fund. I have called it wisdom. I am not always sure it is trust.**

> *"...some of them left of it until the morning, and it bred worms, and stank..."*
> **Exodus 16:20**

## Just in case

You have a savings account.

You have a fridge stocked for the week. You have a pantry. You have an emergency fund — six months of expenses, the financial podcasts say, maybe more if you're cautious. You have a 401k. You have a freezer half-full of meals from a Sunday batch-cook. You have, if you're being honest, more food and money on hand right now than most of human history could have imagined.

You did not plan this as unbelief. You planned it as wisdom.

The logic is everywhere. Dave Ramsey says save. Suze Orman says save. The Proverbs say save — and they do, sort of, in places. The whole modern Christian life, especially in the comfortable West, is organized around the quiet assumption that *just in case* is the same thing as *trust God.*

But it isn't, not always.

Somewhere along the way, *wisdom* and *trust* started feeling like opposites and you didn't notice. You wouldn't put it that way, because it sounds bad. You'd say: *I'm being a good steward.* Or: *I'm not going to test God by being foolish.* Or, very quietly: *I'd rather not have to ask him for tomorrow's lunch. I can handle that one.*

When did you last actually pray for daily bread?

When did you last need to?

## Bread for today

There is a story in Exodus that the comfortable Christian has trouble with.

Israel is in the wilderness. There is no agriculture. There is no grocery store. A nation of former slaves, no food, and a God who promises to feed them. Every morning, when the dew lifts, the ground is covered in a fine, flake-like bread — manna. Each family gathers enough for the day.

Two unbreakable rules, both in Exodus 16:

> *"Let no man leave of it till the morning."*
> **Exodus 16:19**

And then, for the ones who tried anyway:

> *"...some of them left of it until the morning, and it bred worms, and stank..."*
> **Exodus 16:20**

Don't gather more than you need. Don't save it for tomorrow. The Israelites who tried found their stash rotting and crawling with worms by sunrise. The next morning, more manna. New every morning. Forty years of that — the miracle that taught a generation of slaves that God's provision was not a stockpile but a relationship.

The lesson is brutal and clear. The moment you start storing tomorrow's bread, you are not trusting God for it. You are trusting your storage.

Jesus assumed this, by the way. When he taught his disciples to pray, he did not say *give us this week our weekly bread* or *give us this year our 401k.* He said:

> *"Give us this day our daily bread."*
> **Matthew 6:11**

The prayer assumes daily provision. It assumes you will need to ask again tomorrow. Jesus did not pray as if God's manna was meant to be banked.

## The storage is not the source

Now — God is not asking you to be foolish. He is not asking you to throw out your savings. He is not asking you to cancel the insurance. The wilderness Israelites carried tents and tools and flocks and silver. Joseph stored grain for seven years in Egypt. Scripture does not despise foresight.

God is asking you to remember which one is the source.

The storage is not the source. The salary is not the source. The retirement account is not the source. They are *acquaintance* with the source — like a cup that has been filled but does not fill itself. The day you forget which one is which is the day the bread starts breeding worms, even if it does not show yet.

Most of us are too far from hunger to feel manna's lesson in our bodies. We have never gone a meal without a meal in sight. We have never lain down genuinely uncertain about whether tomorrow will be fed. The Israelites felt manna in their bodies because they had to. We don't have to, and so we don't.

But the lesson was never just for the hungry. It was for everyone who had ever confused the cup for the spring.

This is part of why Christians for two thousand years have practiced cessation — fasting, a day of rest, giving large sums away. Not because God commands miserly suffering. Because the body learns what the mind keeps forgetting. *The storage is not the source.* A day of doing no work teaches you the world keeps turning without your hands on it. A fast teaches you a meal you can do without. A bank account suddenly smaller because you gave away too much teaches you that the smaller account did not, in fact, end your life.

These disciplines are how the comfortable Christian stays close enough to remember.

## Today's portion

There is a phrase Israel learned to use, in the wilderness, to describe the day's manna: *today's portion.* Not your portion forever. Not your portion for the year. The portion for today. Gather it. Eat it. Go to sleep. Tomorrow, gather again.

That is what the Christian life actually looks like, under the surface of the suburbs. The 401k can stay. The emergency fund can stay. But under it, behind it, *prior to it*, must be the daily prayer — *give us this day our daily bread* — and the daily admission that the source is not the storage.

You have built a small fortress against tomorrow. You have called it stewardship.

It might be. Or it might not.

The way you can tell the difference is this: when the fortress trembles — when the markets drop, when the job goes, when the savings get cut in half by something you didn't see coming — do you panic? Do you lose your peace? Do you sleep badly?

If yes, then the fortress was the source. The fortress was the god. You did not know it until the fortress shook, but the fortress shook precisely so that you could find out.

An eminently respectable idol, but an idol nonetheless.

Manna does not breed worms only in pantries. It can breed worms in your soul.

## A prayer

Father,

I have a six-month emergency fund.
I have called it wisdom.
I am not always sure it is trust.

I have built a small fortress
against tomorrow.
I have called it stewardship.
You have not asked me for the fortress.
You have asked me for the morning.

Teach me to gather today's portion.
Teach me to eat it without saving the leftovers.
Teach me that the storage is not the source,
that the source has never failed,
that the morning always comes.

Give me this day my daily bread.
Not next week's. Not next year's.
Today's.

And when the fortress trembles,
let me find I was leaning on you all along
and only thought I was leaning on the fortress.

Amen.
