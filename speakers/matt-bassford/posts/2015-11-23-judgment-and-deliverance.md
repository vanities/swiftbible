The week before last, we took a 20,000-foot view of 1, 2,
and 3 John.  I think zoom-outs like that
give us a valuable perspective on Scripture, but sometimes, it’s important to
zoom out even further.  Even as we were
making our way through the Johannine Epistles, we were still talking about us,
about the things that we ought to be doing to follow Jesus as His disciples.

However, in the final analysis, the story of our lives as
Christians isn’t our story.  It’s God’s
story, about the way that He has ransomed and redeemed us, and all that He does
to lead us to dwell with Him forever. 
Without us, the story of God and His people would still go on, but
without God, our story would be meaningless. 
This theme of redemption is repeated throughout the Bible, each time a
little different, but still clearly recognizable.  This morning, then, let’s look at a story of
redemption that is only peripherally about us but is still very much about
God.  Let’s consider what Isaiah reveals
about judgment and deliverance.



**Predicting the Future**

Our text for the morning is going to be Isaiah 8-12, and the
first part of this context concerns God predicting the future.  This begins with a prophecy of **THE COMING JUDGMENT.** Here, let’s read from Isaiah 8:1-15.  Before we go any further, a bit of historical
context is in order.  This part of Isaiah
comes from the reign of King Ahaz of Judah, who is one of the most wicked kings
that Judah ever has.  As usually happens
to wicked kings of Judah, Ahaz has just gotten himself stomped by his enemies,
King Pekah of Israel and King Rezin of Aram. 
All of Judah is in a dither about how they’re going to fight off both of
these nations.

Isaiah tells them not to worry about it.  As fearsome as Israel and Aram may look,
they’re about to have an even bigger problem. 
The Assyrians are going to come down from the north and destroy both
nations.  In fact, Isaiah says, even
though Assyria right now doesn’t even have a border with Judah, they’re almost
going to destroy that nation too.  This
is the same Assyrian invasion that we looked at in connection with the book of
Micah.  It’s a big deal in the history of
Judah, and when Isaiah is writing this, it’s probably about 5 years before
Israel and Aram are destroyed, and about 20 years before Judah is almost
destroyed.

As Isaiah goes on to tell the people of Judah, from this,
they should learn not to fear conspiracies between kings, but to fear God.  The Lord is the one behind all of this, and
even the most powerful nations are dancing to His tune.  Today, we would do well to remember the same
thing.  There’s a presidential election
coming, people are already getting crazy about it, and it’s by no means certain
that the new president, whoever he or she may be, will be able to solve our
country’s problems.  Rather than worrying
about that, though, we need to worry about serving God.

This grim prediction of disaster and near-disaster is
balanced by a prophecy about **THE COMING
LIGHT.** Look at Isaiah 8:16-9:7.  The first part of this text is taken up by a
comparison between Isaiah and his family on the one hand and various mediums
and spiritists on the other.  Not
surprisingly, in a time of national turmoil, the people of Isaiah’s time were
so confused and desperate that they’d begun to look for help from the
occult.  Isaiah is looking for God’s
deliverance, but the spiritists are looking for wisdom from the dead.  Rather than deliverance, though, all they’re
going to find is darkness.  Even today,
all the things that people turn to for reassurance other than God are going to
fail them too.  All the wealth and
knowledge of our society doesn’t have the answer.  Only God does.

In contrast to the darkness of the occult, Isaiah foresees a
time when God will send a great light. 
The setting for this is particularly interesting.  It’s the lands around the Sea of Galilee,
which were the first part of Israel that Assyria conquered.  Isaiah has already predicted that God is
going to judge these lands in the near future, but now he says that in the far
future, God is going to bless them.  The
conflict that so oppresses God’s people now is going to be done away with by
the coming of the Prince of Peace.  It
takes 700 years for this prophecy to be fulfilled, but when it is, it is
fulfilled in a way no one expects, by the preaching of a Jewish peasant named
Jesus of Nazareth, who begins His proclamation of the gospel in the very spot
where Isaiah said He would.

Before the Lord’s Supper, let’s reflect on these two
themes.  Even today, 2700 years after
Isaiah wrote, the judgment of God is still a real thing, and His wrath is
terrible.  We have invited it by our sin,
and we are powerless against it.  Our
only hope is Jesus, who surrendered His life so that we wouldn’t have to fear
the judgment any more.  As great as the
wrath of God is, the salvation that we have in Christ is greater.  Let’s remember that as we partake.

**The Anger of the Lord**

The second section of this text concerns the anger of the
Lord.  We see it first directed **TOWARD ISRAEL.** This appears in Isaiah 9:8-10:4.  You’ve probably realized by now that I’m not
always a huge fan of the chapter divisions in our Bibles, and this is one place
where they make no sense to me.  This
text is obviously a unit.  It’s a song
with four verses, if you will, and in 9:12, 9:17, 9:21, and 10:4, they all end
with “In spite of all this, His anger does not turn away, and His hand is still
stretched out.”  Ominous stuff, to be
sure!

In content, each of these four curses has a focus.  The first one is about pride.  The Israelites think that with determination,
they can outlast the judgment of God. 
Fat chance.  The second is about
foolishness.  The elders and prophets of
Israel should be leading the people wisely, but instead, they’re confusing them
and leading them astray.  The third curse
concerns greed.  Rather than trying to
help each other, all of God’s people are seeking to exploit and destroy each
other.  The final curse is about
oppression.  The judges of Israel are
plundering the weak and helpless, so they in turn will be plundered and taken
captive by the Assyrians.

It’s a spiritual equation. 
Pride, foolishness, greed, and oppression equals judgment.  I, of course, claim no prophetic gift, but
when I see these same four things become more and more prominent in our
national character, I cannot help but be afraid for what the future holds.  I do not believe that God has ceased to act
in history, nor that He will long allow the wicked to prosper.

Next, however, the anger of the Lord is going to turn **TOWARD ASSYRIA.** Read on in Isaiah 10:5-19.  Here, we see a classic case of
overreach.  God sent the Assyrians to do
a job:  punish the wicked nation of Israel.  However, the Assyrians started getting
ideas.  Because God was with them, they
conquered all of their enemies easily, but rather than crediting Him, they
decided that their own strength was responsible.  Instead of serving as an instrument of divine
vengeance, the Assyrians decided that they needed to found an empire, an empire
including Judah and Jerusalem, which God has not yet surrendered to her
enemies.

Because of their pride, God promises that He is going to
destroy them in spectacular fashion. 
Interestingly, because Isaiah’s prophetic career is so long, the book of
Isaiah contains both this prophecy about Assyria’s humiliation and its
fulfillment.  Isaiah 36-37 tells the
story of the Assyrian king Sennacherib’s invasion of Judah, and how God
repelled the Assyrian army from the very gates of Jerusalem by striking down
180,000 men in a single night.  Within
100 years of that event, the mighty Assyrian empire is completely destroyed,
never to rise again.

This is an important reminder to us that in a world governed
by God, evil never actually triumphs. 
Whether we’re concerned about ISIS, those who promote moral decay within
our own nation, or some other evil force entirely, we can be certain of one
thing.  Their day is coming.  History doesn’t belong to them.  It belongs to God, who guides its path for
unfathomable purposes of His own toward a conclusion that only He knows.  All the time we spend worrying is time
wasted.  In the end, it will be well with
the righteous, but it will not be well with the wicked.

**Returning From Exile**

The last part of Isaiah that we’re going to look at this
morning concerns the Israelites’ return from exile.  This starts with Isaiah’s description of **THE REMNANT**.  It appears in Isaiah 10:20-34.  Typically, when we see the prophecy, “A
remnant will return,” we want to apply that to the nation of Judah returning
from the Babylonian captivity.  I don’t
think, however, that this is specifically about Judah.  Instead, it seems to me to be discussing
Israel as opposed to Judah, the 10 northern tribes.  After all, the enemy under discussion here is
Assyria, not Babylon.  It points to
something we need to recognize about the “lost tribes of Israel”.  All of them weren’t lost either, any more
than all the Jews were.  For instance,
Anna the prophetess in Luke 2 is from the tribe of Asher.  Most of the Jews in the New Testament were of
the tribe of Judah, but not all.

We also need to remember, though, that the physical return
from exile was only an incomplete return. 
In his prayer in Nehemiah 9, Nehemiah still emphasizes the great
distress of the Jews under their Persian overlords.  Likewise, Simeon the prophet in Luke 2 is
still “looking for the consolation of Israel.” 
Even though the Jews had returned from captivity, there were a number of
important senses in which they were still in captivity, and they knew that very
well.  This explains the Messianic
expectation of Jesus’ day—they knew they still needed somebody to rescue them.

This explains the prophecy about **THE ROOT OF JESSE** that we next encounter.  Consider Isaiah 11:1-10.  As with Isaiah 9, this is one of the most
famous Messianic prophecies in the book, and it too only makes sense as a
reference to Jesus.  Nobody today knows
who the descendants of David are.  All
the Jewish genealogical records were burned up when the Temple was destroyed in
70 AD.  If this prophecy was to be
fulfilled, it had to be fulfilled before then, and only Jesus is a reasonable
candidate for its fulfillment.  None of
the other descendants of David rescued God’s people from exile.  None of the other descendants of David were
able to establish peace between people as different as a wolf is from a
lamb.  For that matter, none of the other
descendants of David could claim to have the seven spirits of God resting on
them.  Indeed, it is only through Jesus
that people from all over the world have found safety in the Lord.

Together, these two texts remind us about what we have and
about what we don’t have.  There’s no
question that thousands of years of Israelite and Jewish prophecy found their
culmination and fulfillment in Jesus. 
There’s no question that we are God’s holy nation now, that we possess
the blessings that Isaiah 11 promises.

However, as was the case with the literal Jewish exiles when
they returned from Babylon, there’s still a sense in which we’re in exile too.  This world is not our home.  Every day, we face the oppression of our
enemies, as the devil tempts us to do evil. 
Worse still, we continually are confronted by the last and most terrible
enemy of mankind, death itself.  The
here-and-now is not the fulfillment of God’s plan for us.

We need to remember that, friends.  Even though we’ve been brought closer to God
than any of His other people, we aren’t all the way there yet.  His victory is still incomplete, and His will
has yet to be worked out fully.  Like
Nehemiah, we too should be crying out for deliverance.

However, even in the midst of our longing, we should not
forget our **THANKSGIVING FOR
SALVATION.** Here, consider Isaiah
11:11-12:6.  Note first of all that in
11:1, Isaiah refers to this as the second time that God will recover the
remnant.  If the Messianic context of the
first part of the chapter leaves us in any doubt, this should dispel it.  We shouldn’t be interpreting this passage
literally.  We should apply it
figuratively, to the church.  It’s about
God’s true people being summoned from the four corners of the earth to serve
Him.  No matter the difficulties that
stand between them and God, God will make the pathway smooth, as He has indeed
done for us.

After this, we come to the song of thanksgiving that is the
climax and conclusion of this context of Isaiah.  What’s particularly interesting to me about
this text is that even though it’s in the Old Testament, this is a song for and
about us.  Contextually, this isn’t
something that the Jews would have sung in the day of Nehemiah or the day of
John the Baptist.  They hadn’t seen the
deliverance that Christ would bring, and they knew it.  Usually, when we sing a song from the Old
Testament, we’re singing a Jewish hymn that has been adapted for Christian
use.  Not so here.  This one is our property, even though it was
written 700 years before there even were any Christians at all.  For us to recall this hymn, then, every time
we sing “He Is in Our Midst”, could not be more appropriate.
