The English language is one of the richest in existence,
taking loanwords from dozens, if not hundreds, of other languages.  However, this polyglot heritage poses a
problem for hymnists (and formal poets too, I suppose).  The large variety of sounds in English makes
rhyming very difficult.

However, even though it’s hard, rhyming is still
important.  This is true for two
reasons.  First, it’s a structural
element.  Hymns need structure.  It functions as a mental roadmap that helps
the worshiper navigate through the content of a hymn.  Without structure, singers are prone to find
themselves wandering in a wilderness of words. 
Note that contemporary songs often use repetition instead of rhyme as a
structural element, but overuse of repetition dumbs down a hymn to the point
where it doesn’t offer enough content for the thoughtful worshiper.

Second, rhyme adds rhetorical and emotional force to a
hymn.  As I wrote [here](http://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2014/06/rhyming-in-hymns.html), scientific studies have shown that rhymed proverbs strike their audience as
“truer” than proverbs that don’t rhyme. 
This is weird, but it’s the way that God made us, and hymnists who want
their work to be sung would be foolish not to pay attention.



It’s not enough, though, to accomplish the difficult task of
rhyming in a way that makes rhyming look hard. 
There are plenty of hymns that do this. 
They tangle up the language, saying things that no native speaker would
ever say, in order to get the right word at the end of the line.  The distorted, hard-to-understand syntax
often found in traditional hymns is one of the main problems that critics of
the genre bemoan, and they have every reason to do so.

Instead, the hymnist who wants to master his craft must learn
how to rhyme in English, which is very difficult, in a way that makes it look
easy.  In imitation of Aaron in Exodus
32:24, they must maintain the pretense of, “I threw the words in the fire, and
out came this hymn!”  I said exactly what
I wanted to say in normal English, and it just happened to rhyme every eight
syllables!  How about that!

This isn’t an easy knack to pick up, but it’s
learnable.  I know how I do it, and the
other skilled lyricists I’ve talked to take a similar approach.  In fact, I see evidence in the writing of
great hymnists from hundreds of years ago that they were doing the same
thing.  Here’s how it works:

**Know, generally, what
you want to say.** It’s easy to rhyme
if you don’t have to maintain a coherent thought throughout the hymn, and it’s
easy to be led astray by a tempting rhyme that doesn’t relate to anything else
in the hymn.  The only solution to the
problem is to always maintain a clear picture of the overall idea behind the
hymn and the main sub-ideas (one per verse) that contribute to that overall
thought.  You should know what you want
to say at a level of specificity that gives you some flexibility in how you’re
going to say it.

**Find a powerful
phrase related to your content that will rhyme.**Every good hymn has powerful phrases in it.  These are the phrases that worshipers
remember, the phrases that motivate them to sing the hymn.  Craig Roberts calls them “hymn bites”.

Think, for instance, of “Shelter me safe in that haven of
rest” in “Nearer, Still Nearer” or “I know that my Redeemer lives” in the hymn
of the same name.  They are things that
Christians were aching to say until a hymnist put them into words so they could
say them.

If your idea is good, there will be powerful phrases and
lines like these associated with it. 
Sometimes, you can hide the power phrases in a non-rhyming location.  The very first words of a hymn, for instance,
are important enough to be a good hiding place. 
However, the music will nearly always emphasize the last lines of verses,
and you want your power statement to line up with the musical emphasis.  As a result, it really helps if your power
phrase is rhymable.

Some powerful ideas are not. 
I was talking the other day with a friend of mine about trying to rhyme
with “mercy”.  The word by itself is
powerful, and there are many strong Scriptural phrases that conclude with it.  However, the English language really has no
good rhymes for it, so if you’re looking to end the last line of a rhyming hymn
with “mercy”, good luck!

By contrast, if you find a hymn bite that ends in a word that
has several solid “church word” rhymes (it is no help to the hymnist that
“Lord” rhymes with “gourd”), you have a strong place to start.

**Find a rhyme that’s
related to the rhyme at the end of your power phrase.** Some words are about related ideas;
others are not.  It’s much easier,
though, to use a rhyme pair if the words in the pair are related.  After all, hymns are short, and you don’t
have much room to say things between any given pair of rhymes.  If the words in the pair have to do with
similar thoughts, you can connect them logically with little effort.

Take, for instance, one of the most common rhyme pairs in
the English language:  “night” and
“light”.  Both are about illumination, so
they go easily together in the same verse. 
It doesn’t take much thought to come up with a quatrain in which it
seems natural to discuss both light and night. 
“Light” and “parasite”, on the other hand, are not closely related ideas
(unless you are exposing parasites to the light for some reason), so there’s
much less opportunity to come up with a natural-sounding text.

**Connect your rhyme
pair with normal-sounding English with the appropriate rhythm and meter,
attempting to say spiritually useful things along the way.** If you can do this, you’ve succeeded in
writing a solid verse, and it is, I think, the only way to consistently write rhymed
verses that sound natural and conversational.

Perhaps the most technically accomplished hymnist of all
time was Frances Havergal, and there’s abundant evidence in her work that she
wrote this way.  Take, for instance, the
hymn “Is It For Me?”  Even though, as far
as I know, Havergal never described her thought process in writing it, that
process is plainly evident for those with eyes to see.

The core thought of the hymn is amazement that people as
sinful as we are could hope to spend eternity in heaven with God.  That was her main idea, and the first verse
that she wrote about it, chronologically speaking, is what is usually v. 2 in
our hymnals.  In fact, her core thought
appears as her power statement in line 4 of v. 2:  “For me, so full of sin?”

That’s kind of a problem. 
Though the thought is powerful, the rhyme is difficult.  Havergal eventually settled on “in” for her
rhyme with “sin”.  “In”, however, isn’t a
very good rhyme because English teachers get grumpy when you end sentences or
even clauses with a preposition.

Havergal therefore decided to conceal her line-ending “in”
with a quotation, “Enter in”.  However,
“Enter in” is not as clear as Matthew 25:21, where Jesus actually says, “Enter
thou into the joy of thy lord.” (KJV) 
Havergal solved her English-teacher problem but created a clarity
problem.

She solved the clarity problem, though, by making “Enter in”
part of a series of parallels.  “Thy
welcome,” = “Thy gracious ‘Enter in,’” = “Thy ‘Come, ye blessed,’”.  By itself, “Enter in” is not entirely clear,
but when surrounded by such parallels, it offers nothing that would confuse
anybody.

This left her with the quatrain,

*Is it for me, Thy welcome,*

*Thy gracious “Enter
in,”*

*For me, Thy “Come, ye
blessed,”*

*For me, so full of
sin?*

After that, Havergal surrounded her core-thought verse with
verses built around ideas that logically lead into and out of it.  The result sings so naturally that I doubt
very many singers have ever spared a thought for the deft technique that
created it.  Bad writing attracts
attention and jolts the worshiper out of worship.  Good writing attracts none and allows the
worshiper to focus on content.  Havergal
was a very good technician, but I think that if she knew that no one noticed
her technique when her hymns were sung, she would be pleased.  If we learn to write like her, we can
similarly deflect notice, and with that, we should be pleased too.
