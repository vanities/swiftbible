We’ve all got problems. 
At least, we all think we’ve got problems.  Some of them are uniquely our own; others, we
share with just about everybody else in this high-stress, low-peace era.

We also have another kind of problem in common.  Every single one of us is dealing with some
sort of challenge or weakness in the spiritual realm.  Maybe there’s a good thing we know we should
be doing, but we can’t quite bring ourselves to do it.  Maybe there’s a wicked thing that we know we
shouldn’t be doing, but we can’t quite bring ourselves to give it up.  If these conditions endure long enough,
trying to solve the problem may well be what we give up on!

Before we get too self-pitying, though, we should consider the
problems that our brethren in the first century faced.  We all have our struggles, but I don’t think
any of us have been faced with an angry mob! 
Nonetheless, despite the severity of these difficulties, early
Christians were able to overcome them because they learned to rely on God.  If we similarly lean on the Lord, we’ll be
able to overcome the obstacles before us too. 
Let’s see what we can learn, then, from the story of the triumph of the
gospel in Philippi.  As we work through
this text, let’s pay particular attention to the way in which God’s will
repeatedly confounds human expectations.

 **The Journey**

Logically speaking, our story begins with Luke’s account of
the journey to Philippi.  This trip,
however, starts on a sour note with a conflict between **PAUL AND BARNABAS.** This sad
story unfolds in Acts 15:36-41.  Usually,
when we study the account, we get caught up in a discussion of whether Paul and
Barnabas should have argued with each other like that, etc.  However, I want to take a slightly different
tack this morning.  Let’s focus on what’s
at the heart of the matter here.  It’s
not like Paul and Barnabas are arguing over what they should have for
breakfast.  Instead, they’re arguing
about John Mark’s spiritual failure and how they should handle it.

This points to something that in my experience, seems to be
pretty generally true.  It is that bad
behavior often leads to difficult choices for others.  Think about it.  If John Mark hadn’t chickened out on the
first missionary journey, Paul and Barnabas may well have stayed together
through the end of Acts 28.  Only because
of his weakness were they faced with a challenge to their unity that they
weren’t able to overcome.

I think there are a couple of applications we need to make
here.  First of all, we need to watch out
for other people in our lives whose bad behavior may do this to our
relationships.  Even when we agree with
somebody else that a third party is in the wrong, we can disagree passionately
about what we ought to do about it.  If
we’re not careful, that disagreement can lead to a Paul-and-Barnabas-style
blowup.  In a situation like that, let’s
take care to tread lightly.

Second, let’s make sure that we aren’t the kind of person
who causes trouble for others.  Too
often, we consider our sin only through the lens of the bad effects that it has
on us, but its effects can be equally bad on those who have to figure out how
to deal with us.  Let’s be sure not to
become that kind of spiritual stumbling block for those close to us.

Once Paul and Barnabas part ways, we see Paul with his new
companion Silas **RETURN TO LYCAONIA.** We see what happens next in Acts
16:1-5.  Unlike Paul’s last visit to the
region, this time nobody tries to stone him to death.  Instead, Paul engages in some pretty uneventful
work in helping the churches of the area grow. 
The only event that stands out during this time is that Paul makes a
young disciple named Timothy part of his team. 
He’s destined to be a major part of Paul’s ministry, but this is his
first appearance.

In the story of Paul and Timothy, there are lessons for
older and younger Christians alike.  For
the former, it teaches us that we should look for a Timothy to help.  When I look back today at where I was
spiritually 15 years ago or even 10 years ago, when I first came to Joliet, I
almost have to roll my eyes at how little I understood.  Older Christians, then, have something vital
to share with those who are younger in the faith, and like Paul, we should look
for opportunities to do that.  We can all
think back to mistakes that we made in the past.  Let’s be willing to help those who now are
where we were, so that they can benefit from our wisdom and not do the same
thing.

The obvious flip side to the coin is that if we are more
like Timothy in age and experience, we should look for a Paul.  We live in a society that does not value the
wisdom of age.  However, just because our
society is foolish doesn’t mean we have to be. 
Young folks, get to know Christians who are older than you are.  Learn from them.  That might not be the popular path, but it
will save you all kinds of misery and heartache.

This takes us to the story of **THE MACEDONIAN CALL**.  Here,
let’s read from Acts 16:6-10.  We’re used
to this phrase, but this is actually one of the stranger stories in Acts
16.  After all, it’s not only about Paul
being told to preach the gospel in Macedonia. 
It’s about Paul being told *not* to
preach the gospel in Asia and Bithynia.

Why in the world would God do that?  Why would God keep the people of those provinces,
who needed to hear the truth as much as any sinner does, from having the gospel
preached to them?  Apparently, Paul is
not given a reason, and even today, we still don’t know.

This points us to something crucial that we all must
understand about God.  His ways are not
our ways, nor are His thoughts our thoughts. 
All the way through the Bible, God does things that make no sense to
human understanding for reasons of His own. 
He is different.

We must be careful, then, not to believe that because we’re Christians,
the narrative of our lives is necessarily going to make sense.  We need a job, and we’ve prayed and prayed
and prayed for it, but we still don’t have one. 
I’ve been there, and in that situation, it’s tempting to start assigning
motives to God.  Maybe I don’t have a job
because I’ve been sinning, so if I become a better Christian, then I’ll have a
job!  Maybe God’s plan for my life is for
me to become a lute repairman!

Brethren, that’s nothing more than projecting our thoughts
on God’s thoughts.  Sometimes things
happen to us because we were foolish or evil, because Satan is powerful,
because God is working in our lives, and even because of pure blind
chance.  Contrary to popular belief,
everything doesn’t happen for a reason! 
Our job is not to figure out why we are where we are.  It’s to serve God as best we can wherever we
are.

**Responses to the
Gospel**

As is so often the case in Scripture, once Paul, Silas,
Luke, and Timothy arrive in Macedonia, they encounter two very different
responses to the gospel.  The first comes
from **LYDIA.**  Her story unfolds in Acts 16:11-15.  The first thing that strikes us about this
story is that now Luke is writing in first-person plural.  This actually started back in v. 10, from
which we can conclude that Luke was originally a resident of Troas who will now
accompany Paul to Philippi.

Second, it’s obvious that Paul and his companions aren’t in
Kansas anymore.  All the way through the
first missionary journey, we see Paul and Barnabas doing their Sabbath preaching
in synagogues.  All of the places where
they had gone before had enough of a Jewish community that the Jews were able
to build and maintain a building of their own. 
Not so here.  Instead, the few
worshipers of God in the area have to meet on a riverbank for lack of anywhere
better.

Likewise, Lydia herself is not typical for the disciples
we’ve met in the gospel story so far. 
She is a woman rather than a man. 
In fact, she’s probably a widow. 
She’s a Gentile, even though she does fear God.  She is a seller of purple, which means she’s
rich, where most disciples aren’t.

However, these unpromising circumstances conceal an honest
heart.  She hears the gospel, accepts it
at once, is baptized, and prevails on the rest of her household to be
baptized.  She even talks Paul and his
companions into staying with her for the remainder of their time in Philippi.

All of this should remind us once again about the dangers of
judging somebody’s heart by their outward appearance.  Lydia is different from Paul in just about
every way imaginable.  In fact, women
like Lydia were responsible for persecuting Paul in Antioch of Pisidia.  However, she obeyed the gospel where many who
seemed to be more promising prospects did not. 
We need to remember that if we assume somebody we know won’t be
interested in the truth because they aren’t like us, we may be writing off a
Lydia.  Let’s be wiser than that and
share the truth impartially.

Sadly, we see an example of exactly the opposite kind of
behavior when Paul heals **A DEMON-POSSESSED
GIRL.** Read with me from Acts
16:16-21.  This story is atypical in a
couple of different ways.  First, Paul’s
motivation for casting the demon out of this girl appears to be irritation.  The demon wouldn’t shut up about how Paul and
his friends were God’s servants, which annoyed Paul like it used to annoy
Jesus, so he commanded it to leave her to get it to be quiet.

Second, and even more interestingly, this is the only time
in the New Testament that I can think of where persecution arises because of
the results of a miracle.  The Pharisees
got on Jesus plenty of times for healing somebody on the Sabbath, but I can’t
remember them ever getting mad because somebody they didn’t want healed was
healed.  Here, that’s exactly what
happened.  The slave girl’s owners were
using her demonic insight to get rich, so no more demon meant no more
money.  They don’t care about the benefit
to her at all.  As a result, they seize
Paul and Silas and accuse them of doing some kind of shadowy, illegal thing.

Before the Lord’s Supper, let’s reflect that every one of us
is someplace in one of these two stories. 
Either our hearts are open to the word of God, or there is something in
them that is leading us to reject or even oppose it.  What it is doesn’t matter.  What matters is that it is keeping us from
Jesus.  He gave His life for us in a
magnificent act of self-sacrifice that will continue to echo down from Calvary
for as long as the world exists.  He
invites us all to give ourselves to Him in return.  Every one of us is either showing our love
for for Jesus by doing as He asks, or we are showing our love for the roadblock
by rejecting His will.   Where are
we?  Let’s consider this as we partake.

**Persecution and
Vindication**

The final portion of Acts 16 relates a story of persecution
and vindication.  It begins with **PAUL AND SILAS IN PRISON.** Here, consider the account of Acts
16:22-26.  Here, first of all, we see the
nastiness shown by the owners of the slave girl continuing.  Even though Paul and Silas have done nothing
wrong—indeed, quite the opposite!—they’re beaten, thrown into prison, and even
put into stocks in the inner prison.  The
jail in Philippi is one of those places in Scripture that archaeologists have
identified, and the photographs from today make clear that it was a nasty, dank
hole.  However, in the middle of the
night, God proves that He hasn’t forgotten Paul and Silas by sending an
earthquake that frees them, along with all the other prisoners.

What stands out to me most about this account, though, is
what Paul and Silas are doing when the earthquake comes.  In this terrible place where they can’t even
get up to relieve themselves, they’re praying and singing hymns.  I don’t think they were crazy.  Instead, they believed that even in their
present situation, somehow God was going to deliver them.

Today, even though hopefully none of us are going to be put
in stocks in an inner prison, we still encounter various trials, and our
reaction to those trials reveals every bit as much about us as Paul and Silas’
reaction revealed about them.  Weak faith
will succumb to despair, but strong faith will continue to rejoice in God, not
for the deliverance we have seen already, but for the deliverance we will
see.  God has never forgotten His own, so
we can be certain that He won’t forget us.

This takes us to the climax of the story of Paul in
Philippi:  **THE CONVERSION OF THE JAILER.** Consider
Acts 16:27-34.  There are some pretty
dramatic personal transformations in Scripture, but few so dramatic as
this.  In v. 27, the jailer is preparing
to kill himself.  By v. 34, he has been
baptized along with his entire household. 
Suicide to salvation is quite a trip!

Because this is one of the foremost conversion stories in
the entire Bible, it’s not surprising that a host of misconceptions and false
doctrines has grown up around it.  The
first concerns Paul’s answer when the jailer asks, “What must I do to be
saved?”  Because Paul tells him to
believe in Jesus, some make the argument that only faith is necessary to
salvation.

The problem with the argument, though, is that Paul isn’t
giving an exposition of the entire plan of salvation.  Instead, he’s meeting the jailer where he
is.  At this point in time, the jailer
may never even have heard of Jesus.  He
certainly doesn’t believe that Jesus is the Son of God.  When that’s where the jailer’s mind is, it
would have been pointless for Paul to teach him about baptism for forgiveness
of sins.  Paul’s answer to his question
has to start in faith.  However, Paul’s
answer clearly doesn’t end there.  The
jailer and his household are baptized that same hour of the night.

Second, advocates of infant baptism will often seize on the
fact that the jailer’s whole household was baptized to prove infant
baptism.  However, this too is a flawed
argument.  The mere fact that the jailer
has a household tells us nothing about whether that household contains
children.  After all, there are plenty of
households represented here this morning that have no children in them.  If anything, the evidence supports the
conclusion that the jailer’s household didn’t have any children.  After all, we also see the same “whole
household” that was baptized rejoicing because they believe in Jesus, and faith
is not the province of the child.

The story concludes with **PAUL’S RELEASE.** Luke
explains in Acts 16:35-40.  After a
night’s rest, the authorities in Philippi conclude that they don’t really have
a reason to imprison Paul and Silas, so they send orders to the jailer to let
them go.  At this point, though, they
discover that they have a problem.  Paul
and Silas are both Roman citizens, and it’s illegal under Roman law to beat,
whip, or degrade Roman citizens, period, much less without a trial.  Magistrates who ignore this law are subject
to severe penalties, possibly even the death penalty.  In other words, the Philippian town fathers
know that if Paul and Silas say the word, they’re doomed.

Paul and Silas don’t say the word, but neither are they
interested in letting the authorities get off lightly.  After all, disrespect shown to the messengers
of Jesus is disrespect shown to Jesus.  Now,
instead of Paul and Silas being humiliated, it’s the civic officials, who have
to come to the jail, apologize, and escort them out.  After this, they don’t want to see anything
further from the two preachers of the gospel, so they beg them to leave town,
and Paul and Silas graciously oblige.

From this, we learn again the importance of using our legal
rights as Christians.  We’re not supposed
to take our personal revenge, but it’s perfectly legitimate for us to turn our
problems over to the government, which is God’s messenger for good.  That’s leaving the resolution of those
problems to Him.
