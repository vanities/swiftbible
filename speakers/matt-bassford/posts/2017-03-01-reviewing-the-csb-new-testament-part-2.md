In [the last post in this series](http://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2017/02/reviewing-csb-new-testament-part-1.html), I embarked on a review of
the new Christian Standard Bible. 
Admittedly, this review has a decidedly negative cast;  I was/am interested in finding
translation-killers, renderings in the CSB that would keep me from using
it.  This is irrespective of the other
merits of the translation but entirely in keeping with Ecclesiastes 10:1 (one
of my father’s favorite passages, incidentally).



During our last episode, I made it through all the
objections up to the book of Hebrews. 
For whatever reason, I had more problems with the CSB in Hebrews than
anywhere else.  It could be that the
translator was different, that Hebrews is particularly difficult, that I’m more
familiar with Hebrews than any other book of the Bible (thanks to my dad, this
is probably true), or just that I happened to be cranky when I was reading
it.  Regardless, issues were had.

**Hebrews 2:9**

*But we do see Jesus—**made lower than the angels for a short time**
so that by God’s grace he might taste 
death for everyone—**crowned with
glory and honor** because he suffered death.*

This was my first disappointment.  I was so happy when I saw that the CSB’s
light gender-neutrality led it to keep Hebrews 2:6-8 in third person singular
(in my opinion, recasting the passage in third-person plural precludes the most
logical reading), but then I got to 2:9, and my happiness evaporated.

In both the Greek and the formal-equivalent translations,
the verse has this logical form that Jesus:

- is made lower than the angels for a little while

- suffers death

- is crowned with glory and honor because He did

- consequently, by God’s grace tastes death for everyone.

In other words, it is Jesus’ crowning with glory and honor
that allows Him to taste death for everyone. 
It is because He has been exalted that He can help us.  The CSB rendering obscures this critical
nuance.  Yes, it’s true that Jesus tasted
death for everyone on the cross, but that’s not what the passage is saying.

**Hebrews 4:2**

*For we also have
received the good news just as they did. 
But the message they heard did not benefit them, since they were not
united with those who heard it in faith.*

Don’t like this one at all.  The NIV84 rendering, which is typical here,
reads, “For we also have had the gospel preached to us, just as they did, but
the message they heard was of no value to them, because those who heard did not
combine it with faith.”  To put things
slightly differently, hearing does you no good if you don’t believe.  However, with scant textual support, the CSB
argues that the Israelites in the wilderness did not benefit from the gospel
because they weren’t united with those who did believe (though in the OT account
of the wilderness wandering, I’m not sure who these believers would have
been).  This places the emphasis on unity
rather than faith, which is not a perspective that the Hebrews writer shares.

**Hebrews 5:11**

*We have a great deal
to say about this, and it is difficult to explain, since you have become too
lazy to understand.*

Not a big deal here, but I think the substitution of “too
lazy to understand” for “dull of hearing” loses something.  People who are dull of hearing aren’t merely
lazy; they’re uninterested and unconcerned. 
An infusion of energy into such people wouldn’t solve the problem as it
would if they were lazy.  Instead, their
lack of interest in spiritual things would divert that energy to something
else.

**Hebrews 12:28**

*Therefore, since we
are receiving a kingdom that cannot be shaken, let us be thankful.  By it, we may serve God acceptably, with
reverence and awe.*

I think this one is an editing error.  In the HCSB, Hebrews 12:28 reads, “Therefore,
since we are receiving a kingdom that cannot be shaken, let us hold on to
grace. By it, we may serve God acceptably, with reverence and awe. . .”  For whatever reason, the translators decided
to sub in “let us be thankful,” for “let us hold on to grace.”  However, this change left the “it” in “By it”
without an antecedent, and nobody caught the problem.  I assume that later editions of the CSB will
correct this.

**2 John 9**

*Anyone who does not
remain in Christ’s teaching but goes beyond it does not have God.  The one who remains in that teaching, this
one has both the Father and the Son.*

I appreciate the desire to simplify and clarify the language
here, but I’m afraid that the simplification misses the point.  “Christ’s teaching” does not carry all the
same meanings that “the teaching of Christ” does.  Among other things, the “teaching of Christ”
can mean “the teaching about Christ”, and I think that’s the meaning it has
here.  Specifically, the teaching about
Christ is that He came in the flesh, which the deceivers and antichrists up in
v. 7 have been denying.  “Christ’s
teaching” has a much more general meaning and misses that contextual nuance.

Overall, this is a respectable showing.  When I started using the ESV, which thumps
its chest about being a formal-equivalent translation, I hated its renderings
of Ephesians 3:6 and Hebrews 10:20 so much that I crossed out the offending
portions and inserted my own version in ballpoint (incidentally, I think the
CSB gets it right in Ephesians 3:6, and it gets a pass on Hebrews 10:20 because
of the ESV).  Today, I would probably add
1 Thessalonians 4:4 to the list (where the CSB falls down too, alas, though
both provide useful marginal renderings). 
Note that I found these trouble spots even without going through the ESV
looking for trouble, as I did with the CSB. 
There are no perfect translations, and there aren’t any perfect readers
either!

I suspect that as time goes by, I and others will encounter
more passages in the CSB that we’re not thrilled with (and if you see such
passages, note them in the comments below, please!).  However, despite adopting a more liberal
translation philosophy than the formal-equivalent trio, it seems to me like the
CSB has largely done a good job of preserving the meaning of the text while
simplifying its language.

For me, at least, adopting the CSB for my primary
translation would take a lot of getting used to.  It doesn’t read the same as the NASB and ESV,
so when I’m trying to locate a text by scanning for key words, those specific
key words and phrases often aren’t there to be found.  However, that’s because I’ve spent 30 years
of my life accumulating bias in favor of the NASB and ESV!  As with many older Christians and the KJV,
that’s how the Bible sounds to me.

There are many who don’t share that bias.  They come to the word of God in ignorance,
eager only to learn what He wants them to do. 
For people like that, I can see the CSB being more useful, and if it’s
more useful for them, maybe it’s the translation I should be using too.
