One of the nice things about following a yearly
Bible-reading plan is that it takes you not only to the parts of the Bible
where you go all the time but also to the parts where you never go.  In my case, this means that I’ve spent a lot
more time in the book of Job than I ever had before, and that’s a shame,
because Job has quite a bit to say to all of us.

What’s more, even when we do look at Job, I think we spend
most of our time in the wrong part.  Job
is a frame tale.  It starts with two
chapters of prose describing all the terrible things that happen to Job, moves
through 40 chapters of poetic discussion among Job, his friends, and God, and
concludes with half a chapter of prose that describes all the good things that
happen to Job.

Generally, when we study Job, we study those two-and-a-half
chapters of prose that bookend the massive amount of poetry.  However, the prose isn’t the point, any more
than the vase is the point of a floral arrangement.  The point isn’t the happily-ever-after of Job
42.  The point is Job’s attempt to make
sense of his misery while he’s still neck-deep in it.  Today, his questions are no less relevant.  All of us have known, or perhaps even been,
good, righteous, God-fearing people who suffered greatly.  Let’s consider, then, what we can learn about
Job and suffering.



**Job 28-29**

Even though I would certainly encourage all of you to read
the entirety of Job on your own time, this morning, we’re only going to be
considering the climax of Job’s final speech, Job 28-31.  Within our chosen context, the first thing
that Job discusses is **THE ELUSIVENESS OF
WISDOM.** Here, let’s read the
entirety of Job 28.  A few weeks ago,
Matt Sterba and I were talking, and he observed that sometimes, we work too
hard at making everything in the spiritual realm make sense to us.  We want to rationalize, quantify, and put
everything in the Bible into nice neat little logical boxes so that we are
satisfied with our understanding of God and His works.

In some areas, this is appropriate.  We are responsible, after all, for obeying
God, and we can’t do that without knowing His will for us in the first
place.  In others, though, it is absurdly
ambitious.  Who of us can hope to know
the mind of God?  Who of us can hope to
know all that He does or why He does it? 
Job’s point here is that our efforts to search out the hidden things of
God are vain.  God alone possesses
wisdom, and apart from what He tells us, we do not.

Next, Job touches on a topic that we hardly ever talk
about:  **THE COMPLACENCY OF THE RIGHTEOUS.**Consider Job 29.  Here, we see
Job returning to the happy days before Job 1, when he was righteous, wealthy,
and honored.  He had no idea that
disaster was headed his way.  In fact, he
assumed the opposite, that as long as he remained righteous, he would be fine.

That, friends, is a terribly seductive illusion for all of
us.  So far as I know, even though we all
have our problems, few of us face great misery and suffering on a daily
basis.  It’s easy for us to make the same
assumption that Job does, that as long as we stay faithful, the good times are
going to keep on rolling indefinitely. 
If the earth continues, just about every one of us is going to reach a
point in our lives when we look back on today and say, “I didn’t know how good
I had it then!”  We live in a fallen
world, and suffering is inevitable.  If
we deny that, we are allowing Satan to take God’s good blessings and use them
to deceive us.

**Job 30-31**

After these things, we come to Job’s account of **HIS SUFFERING.** Consider Job 30.  As Job makes clear, his anguish has several
dimensions.  Part of it’s social.  Now even the outcasts of society scorn him.  Part of it’s physical, as he continues to
struggle with illness.  Most of all,
though, it’s emotional and spiritual. 
Job has done everything right, he cries out to God for release, and yet,
from his perspective, God only seems determined to add more misery to his
life.  This bewilders Job.  It’s like he went up to his best friend to
shake hands, and the friend slugged him in the face.

This should remind us, friends, of the fragility of earthly
happiness.  If things like these could
happen to Job despite his righteousness, how can any one of us assume that we
are safe from disaster?  The truth is
that most of us have it so good we don’t even know how far it’s possible for us
to fall.  All it takes is one diagnosis,
one accident, one job loss, and off we go.

Finally in Job, let’s consider what he has to say about **HIS RIGHTEOUSNESS.** Look at Job 31.  We talk about check-the-box Christianity, but
it’s clear that Job has all of his boxes checked.  He’s honest, he’s faithful to his wife, he’s
gracious to his servants, and he’s generous. 
He’s free from idolatry, free from hatred, and free from hypocrisy.  I don’t think Job is being boastful in saying
all these things.  He’s being
honest.  How could he possibly do all that
and get this?

The interesting thing about the book of Job is that Job
never gets an answer to his question. 
Even when God shows up, He points out the limits of Job’s understanding
rather than expanding that understanding. 
The reason for Job’s suffering remains mysterious.

Likewise, friends, the reasons for suffering in our lives
remain mysterious and unknowable.  A lot
of our denominational friends want to quote Jeremiah 29:11, about how God has
plans for us to give us a future and a hope. 
Sadly, however, that passage is addressed to the exiles of Judah 2500
years ago, not to us.  It might be
comforting to believe that God is going to solve all of our earthly problems
and wrap them up in a neat little bow, but it’s not true.  God acts or does not act for reasons of His
own, and we may never learn those reasons this side of Jordan.

**Consolation in Christ**

Even though the world is a dangerous and uncertain place, we
can still find consolation in Christ. 
This begins with **HIS
COMPASSION.** The Hebrews writer
explores this subject in Hebrews 4:14-16. 
If ever we are tempted to cry out, “It’s not fair!”, we should remember
that Jesus had more reason than we ever will. 
Not even Job could claim literal sinlessness, but Christ could.  As His reward for a flawless life, He should
have expected to live forever in prosperity and contentment, right?  Instead, the opposite happened.  God did not merely allow Christ to suffer; He
actively planned for it to happen. 
Jesus’ experience with suffering and death left Him not only able to
help us, but willing to help us.  Because
He has been where we are, because He suffered even more unjustly than we ever
will, He understands us and is sympathetic toward us.

In addition, we can anticipate to the **COMFORT** of Christ.  Paul
explores this subject in 2 Corinthians 1:3-5. 
Remember, brethren—even when Jesus was on earth, He never said, “Blessed
are those who mourn, for God shall fix all of their problems and send them away
smiling.”  Instead, it has always been,
“Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted.”

God’s promises to His people today are about spiritual
rather than physical gifts.  He offers us
strength to bear what must be borne, wisdom to look past the troubles of today,
and love to brighten our darkest hours.  In
this life, we are not comforted because God takes away our suffering, but
because even in the midst of our suffering, He never takes away Himself.

Finally, through Christ we can hope for **VICTORY** over suffering. 
Peter points this out in 1 Peter 5:10. 
I’ve heard it said that people can stand anything if they know it’s only
for a limited time.  The good news is
that this life with all of its suffering can only last for a limited time, and
for most of us, the day will come when we are glad of that.

However, our hope is not the hope of the atheist.  We do not anticipate only an end to
suffering, an end to pain.  We anticipate
triumph over it, that through the grace of Christ, the suffering of this life
will be replaced with an even greater joy. 
As Paul observes in 2 Corinthians 4, nothing that we face here is even
worthy to be compared to the glory there.
