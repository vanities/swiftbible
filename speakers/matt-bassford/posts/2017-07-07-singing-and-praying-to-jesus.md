A couple days back, [I posted](http://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2017/07/hymns-and-doctrinal-correctness.html) about doctrinal correctness in
hymns.  Honestly, the post attracted much more
attention than I was expecting, along with a number of comments.  I thought many of them were interesting, but
one of the most intriguing asked, “Have you written content re: the topic of
whether songs addressed to Jesus (and by the same metric, prayers addressed to
Jesus) are Scriptural?”

This is obviously an important question.  There are hundreds of hymns in our hymnals
that are addressed to Jesus, some that consist of praise only, others that go
so far as to ask Him to do something.  We
need to make sure that it’s lawful to sing those before we do.



Because I’m not only a worshiper but also a writer, this is
a question that is particularly significant to me.  If I write a hymn that prays to Jesus when
we’re not supposed to do that, and the hymn gets sung (certainly no sure
thing), I’ve led my brethren into error. 
Personally, I’d rather not have to explain that kind of debacle on the
Day of Judgment!  As a result, I’ve put
some thought into the subject, and I’ve concluded that it is godly both to sing
and to pray to Jesus.

First of all, it’s obvious from the gospels that during His
ministry, Jesus accepted worship.  John
9:38 is only one of many examples here. 
Philippians 2:5-11 is certainly a passage of Scripture that praises
Christ, and scholars believe that this text was originally used as an early
Christian hymn.  Similarly, in John
20:28, Thomas directly addresses Jesus as Lord and God.

As the thousands of heavenly beings in Revelation 5:12
proclaim, Jesus is worthy to receive “power and wealth and wisdom and might and
honor and glory and blessing”.  Surely
the people who belong to Christ are responsible for honoring, glorifying, and
blessing Him!  Nor does there seem to be
a Scriptural basis for arguing that hymns about Jesus are acceptable while
hymns addressed to Jesus are not.

However, most of the questions about directly addressing the
Lord do not concern worshiping Him but rather making requests of Him.  Is it appropriate to pray to Jesus (though of
course Biblical prayer includes adoration, thanksgiving, and so forth in
addition to supplication)?

First of all, it’s worth observing that the work of Christ
did not end with His ascension.  To this
present day, Christ remains with His people (Matthew 28:20), abides in them
(John 15:5), stands by and strengthens them (2 Timothy 4:17), and rescues them
from every evil deed (2 Timothy 4:18). 
This is hardly a comprehensive list; there are many other passages that
describe the work Christ does today.  If
He is already working for us in all these ways, why not appeal for His
attention in the areas of our lives where we particularly need His help?

Second, there are at least three examples in the New
Testament of Christians praying to Jesus:

* The replacement-selection prayer of Acts 1:24-25 is
  addressed to Jesus.  While it is true
  that “Lord” is used in Acts to describe both the Father and the Son, “Lord” in
  this context is identified as Jesus in Acts 1:21.
* The last two things that Stephen says are directed to Jesus
  (Acts 7:59-60).
* So too are Paul’s three prayers about his thorn in the flesh
  in 2 Corinthians 12:7-8.  Note that in
  12:9, the Lord replies by saying “My power is perfected in weakness,” and Paul
  describes this power as “the power of Christ”.

Of course, such prayers to the Son are not nearly as common
in the New Testament as prayers to the Father, but they do exist, and we have
no justification for being dogmatic about such an issue when Christians in the
first century clearly weren’t.

Today, then, I think it’s entirely appropriate for the
church belonging to Christ to sing a wide variety of hymns and spiritual songs
to and about Christ.  He is, after all,
God, which makes Him both worthy of worship and able to come to our aid.  If we pour out our hearts in songs that call
upon Him, our involvement cannot help but draw us closer to Him.
