One of the more surprising debates that came out of my
recent series of posts on “Sanctuary” was the discussion about which version of
“Sanctuary” we should be examining.  Many
people were familiar with versions of the hymn that didn’t contain some of the
lines I found most problematic, which led to some confusion.



At a guess, this is attributable to the fast-and-loose way
that copyrights are often handled in the praise-and-worship world.  Every contemporary hymn begins with the
author’s original version.  However, over
a decade or two, those original versions have a way of morphing.  Additional verses get added (I’ve done this
myself with “Servant Song”, though I did not know the hymn was copyrighted when
I did so) and problematic language in existing verses gets changed.  Sometimes, these changes take place
concurrently in several different places, with the result that several
different versions of the same hymn evolve. 
This can create the same kind of confusion in worship that I saw in the
discussion of “Sanctuary”!

In fact, during that discussion, the same issue arose.  I was asked why I didn’t rewrite the hymn if
I thought parts of it were inadequate, and a sister actually did rewrite it and
posted the rewrite in the comments section.

As much as I might want to endorse such a revision, there
are legal problems with it.  According to
17 U.S.C. § 106(2),
the owner of a copyright has the exclusive right to prepare derivative
works.  In other words, if you own a hymn,
you’re the only one who has the legal right to alter it.  You can grant permission for somebody else to
do so, but without that permission, it’s against the law for them to make any
changes.

This is the sole reason why I copyright my hymns.  I don’t have any ambition of making money
from them (though I believe I have the right to do so).  Indeed, the opposite is true.  I want them to be as widely used as possible,
and a free hymn will achieve a wider distribution than one that is not.

However, I emphatically do not want others altering my
work.  These days, it’s not uncommon for
me to sink 100 hours into a hymn before I declare it finished.  This includes lyric-writing, arranging,
correspondence with composers, endless bouts of editing, and trial sessions.

Before I am willing to take a hymn final and release it into
the wild, it has to be as good as I can possibly get it.  Literally every word has to be the precise
word I want there, evaluated for sound, meaning, and match with music.  For this reason, my finals often contain few
if any words from the first draft.  Sometimes,
God is gracious and that first draft is pretty much right, but usually not.

I invest this level of work for several reasons.  First, I’ve released hymns before without
working on them so much, and that comes back to haunt me every time I sing a
word or a line I know I should have changed. 
Second, if I’m going to ask brethren to go to the effort of learning my
hymn, the least I can do is make that hymn as good as I can.  Finally, I serve a God who deserves the very
best I have to offer.  How can I offer
less?

As a result, even the thought of someone altering my hymns
is enough to make me turn white.  There
are brotherhood hymnals that contain examples of hymns by skilled writers being
defaced by editors with much less skill, and it’s not a pretty sight!  I shudder to think of somebody taking five
minutes to red-pen something I wrestled with for months, and copyrighting my
hymns is the best protection I have against that.

As a result, it also makes me unhappy when I learn that
someone has removed my name and copyright information from a hymn I’ve
written.  I don’t put my name on my hymns
so that people will be impressed with me. 
I do it so that people will know the hymn belongs to me.  That, plus the copyright notice, should
convey the message that though I’m delighted for people to sing my hymns, I
don’t want anybody messing with them.

The short version of all this is:  Don’t alter other people’s hymns.  It’s both illegal and disrespectful.

This leads me into another question I received recently,
pertaining to my posts a few weeks ago about plagiarizing sermons.  A brother from Texas wanted to know about
sermon outlines.  Is it acceptable to
take somebody else’s sermon outline, along with attendant Scriptures, and
preach that?

It is.  This is the
case for a couple of reasons.  First of
all, the definition of plagiarism requires that it involve the use of the words
of another (or closely related words) without attribution.  I think there’s also an analogy to copyright
law here.  17 U.S.C. § 102(b) provides that
copyright does not extend to ideas, only to expressions of ideas.  You can copyright *Twilight*, but you can’t copyright the idea of a novel about teenage
vampires (which is why *Twilight*
spawned an entire genre of such novels). 
Likewise, a sermon outline isn’t an expression (unless the borrowing
preacher just puts the whole outline up on the projector and walks back to his
pew).  It’s just an idea, which the
borrowing preacher will presumably flesh out using his own words (and possibly
even altering as he sees fit).

In my opinion, it’s still good form to give credit where
credit is due if you’re using an outline from another preacher.  I believe the brethren in the pews have the
right to know if that’s what you’re doing and the right to complain if you do
it too often.  However, it is not an
ethical violation, nor is it against the law, to take a sermon outline and
preach it, even without attribution. 
Plagiarism becomes a concern only if you are attempting to pass off
somebody’s actual work, not just their thought, as your own.
