A month or so ago, I got the
idea that I wanted to go see my dad, my sister, and her family over Memorial
Day weekend.  Because of my obligations
in the pulpit, I can’t exactly take a traditional three-day weekend, so I
decided that my family and I were going to leave Joliet after morning services,
drive down to St. Louis, and worship with the saints in Kirkwood Sunday night.

I figured, “Hey, as long as
I’m down there, I might as well see if they want me to preach,” so I got in
touch with L.A..  He replied that the 29th
is actually their monthly singing night. 
I expressed my willingness to help with that, so a few days later, he
suggested that I put together a presentation on what makes a good hymn.  Tonight, then, we’re going to work on the attributes
of a good hymn.



**Content**

The first attribute of a good
hymn is content.  In other words, in
order for a hymn to be useful in congregational worship, it has to say
something.  This much is evident from 1 Corinthians
14:15.  In the Lord’s church, we
recognize that thoughtless, repetitive rote prayers have no place in
worship.  Instead, our minds should be
engaged as we pray.  A good hymn will
likewise engage the mind.  It will be
full of sound Biblical teaching so that we can learn as we sing.

The first hymn that I’ve
chosen as an example of good content is “Hallelujah!  What a Savior!”  Back in 2007, the editors of the hymnal *Psalms, Hymns, and Spiritual Songs*
prepared a survey that asked respondents to rate the 1400 hymns that had been
published in brotherhood hymnals over the past 75 years.  Three groups took this survey:  the editors themselves, the associate editors
who would also work on the hymnal, and interested Christians who took the
survey on the Internet.  Of those 1400
hymns, the hymn that scored the highest across all three groups was
“Hallelujah!  What a Savior!”  There’s a strong argument, then, that it is
the greatest hymn of all time.

What makes this hymn great is
its content.  When we consider the death,
burial, and resurrection of our Lord, we are struck by the richness of those
events.  They represent both the greatest
tragedy and the greatest victory that this world has ever seen.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEh1ylcbVWY11goV-vfqAH3zH3CCPHkKk080hItQtfm0RgVGSHMZBdwHtRIH3Arlmmx9dRRBP2EqHciyo7ONcxoyPFd1oABUYW8SVPqQg538Ic3FmOuXhkDzqbCyQJ8_HaUYQ6PAtutkLA/s640/HWaS.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEh1ylcbVWY11goV-vfqAH3zH3CCPHkKk080hItQtfm0RgVGSHMZBdwHtRIH3Arlmmx9dRRBP2EqHciyo7ONcxoyPFd1oABUYW8SVPqQg538Ic3FmOuXhkDzqbCyQJ8_HaUYQ6PAtutkLA/s1600/HWaS.jpg)



“Hallelujah!  What a Savior!” does an excellent job of
capturing that richness.  As we sing it,
we find ourselves rejoicing that Jesus was known as the Man of Sorrows, that He
stood condemned in our place, that He died as the atonement for our sins, and
that He will return to bring us to heaven to praise Him forever.  This is a hymn that in five short verses
captures the most profound mysteries of the gospel.

Our second content-rich hymn
is “Nearer, My God, to Thee”.  Sadly,
though, most Christians don’t get to fully appreciate this hymn because of some
heavy-handed editing by American hymnal editors.  I myself never understood what the hymn was
about until I saw it in a British hymnal called *Hymns Ancient and Modern*.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgzeUUvdmqmY488ATipYqOmee_rWtfEjPIwP_AYNorRDs-xk9Js7os2aW71ZelscYM6KyTNreNbTa4-IAIAt-sKSq2Z0tfZRoSPEGLX-F_jMJLSHRAayL-llm50VyTO5yUf7zVwsB5RYg/s640/NMGtT.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgzeUUvdmqmY488ATipYqOmee_rWtfEjPIwP_AYNorRDs-xk9Js7os2aW71ZelscYM6KyTNreNbTa4-IAIAt-sKSq2Z0tfZRoSPEGLX-F_jMJLSHRAayL-llm50VyTO5yUf7zVwsB5RYg/s1600/NMGtT.jpg)



When we look at the hymn’s
original vs. 2-4, it becomes obvious that it’s not just a personal expression
of the desire to be close to God. 
Instead, it’s a retelling and a re-application of the story of Jacob’s
ladder in Genesis 28.  Jacob wandering
till dark, Jacob taking a stone for his pillow, Jacob dreaming, Jacob seeing a
ladder reaching to heaven with angels on it, Jacob waking, and Jacob turning
his stony pillow into an altar, it’s all in the hymn.  It uses this strong Biblical content to
remind us that just as God was with Jacob at his lowest point, so He is with us
at ours.

**Structure**

The second main attribute of
good and even great hymns is structure. 
This might sound like a dry, boring topic, but in reality, it’s
extremely important.  Perhaps the best
way to understand its importance is by considering not hymns, but sermons.  At some point in time, I think all of us have
had the experience of listening to a sermon we couldn’t follow.  The preacher seemed like a good guy, he
seemed to be teaching the truth, but we couldn’t track what he was saying.  Generally, that’s a problem with
structure.  The preacher had good
content, but he didn’t know how to organize it.

A good sermon has to have
good structure, and so too does a good hymn. 
1 Corinthians 14:33 explains why. 
God isn’t a God of confusion, and His worship shouldn’t be confusing
either.  A hymn with good structure
minimizes confusion by presenting ideas in an orderly manner.

Our first hymn with excellent
structure is “Give Me the Bible”.  If you
put a gun to my head and made me pick a favorite hymn, I would probably pick
this one.  I love the message of the
hymn, but I particularly love the way it presents that message with an orderly
flow of thought.

“Give Me the Bible” takes its
primary idea from 2 Peter 1:19.  Here,
the word is compared to a lamp shining in a dark place until our spiritual day
dawns with the return of Christ.  The
first verse of the hymn makes that point by comparing the Bible to a radiant
star.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEjRoVjY5cQj1AogKLEpQf8mr7R0JiYt6K8bR86iXg0Y11AOZxE331b72tWTOhDLbQMPFDSvOAIfdtxHXzh7bexDU9lnPTBbRl6nHzq2DKdKvllhfVJ5fctTIVTwfYbWKqszRXwgGdU2ng/s640/GMtB.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEjRoVjY5cQj1AogKLEpQf8mr7R0JiYt6K8bR86iXg0Y11AOZxE331b72tWTOhDLbQMPFDSvOAIfdtxHXzh7bexDU9lnPTBbRl6nHzq2DKdKvllhfVJ5fctTIVTwfYbWKqszRXwgGdU2ng/s1600/GMtB.jpg)

Then, “Give Me the Bible”
goes on to explain how we need the Bible to illuminate our lives when we are
sorrowful, when we are tempted, and when we are dying.  If we wanted to, we could formally outline
the hymn.  Its meaning is so clear
because its structure is so good.

Our second hymn with great
structure is “We Saw Thee Not”.  Like
“Give Me the Bible”, “We Saw Thee Not” is based on a single Scripture, though
it uses many.  The text in question is
John 20:29.  Here, Jesus tells Thomas
that those who have not seen Him risen, yet believe that He has risen, are
blessed.  By singing this hymn, we’re
putting ourselves in the group of the blessed.

The hymn then focuses on four
main groups of events in the life of Christ that none of us saw.  We didn’t see His birth and ministry.  We didn’t see His crucifixion.  We didn’t see the empty tomb or meet Him
after His resurrection.  We didn’t see
Him ascend to heaven.  But, because of
the witness of Scripture, we believe that all those things happened anyway.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgO1GrFXyW25qO3bx1jVwN8fSyWSKuZQbUmOvxQ0wRc533pxFNwLHSvsM9gZrWk_vCLSU_xvWHyiKp7mhAE5dPdTN79rG6RCA365rSglLmy_eZRjsDZtsqsKssVAQjQX1f-qjPe-gOwUg/s640/WSTN.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgO1GrFXyW25qO3bx1jVwN8fSyWSKuZQbUmOvxQ0wRc533pxFNwLHSvsM9gZrWk_vCLSU_xvWHyiKp7mhAE5dPdTN79rG6RCA365rSglLmy_eZRjsDZtsqsKssVAQjQX1f-qjPe-gOwUg/s1600/WSTN.jpg)



It’s also worth noting here
how structure adds to emotion, rather than taking away from it.  Each verse of the hymn follows the same
pattern.  The first half, because it’s
about what we didn’t see, is quiet and subdued. 
The second half, because it’s about what we do believe, is
triumphant.  As a result, the composer,
Knowles Shaw, could write dramatic music for the hymn that starts out
super-soft and gets loud in the second half. 
The structure of the lyrics feeds the feeling of worship.

**Emotion**

The third attribute of a good
hymn is emotion.  Unless a hymn makes
worshipers feel as well as think, it’s dead on arrival.  Indeed, it’s evident from Scripture that God
intends for our song worship to be an emotional experience.  Look, for instance, at the famous opening of
Psalm 100:1-2.  Our Creator knows better
than anyone else that for humankind, singing is an emotionally powerful
action.  Every human society that has
ever existed has songs.  When He commands
us to sing, then, He knows full well that He is commanding us to express
ourselves emotionally.

This begins with what we
normally think of as “getting emotional”, emotions of sorrow or grief.  These emotions are ably expressed by our
first example of a hymn with powerful feeling, “Abide with Me”.  In this hymn, emotion is the product of
content and sincerity.  The repeated plea
of the hymn, for God to abide with us, is taken from the KJV rendering of Luke
24:29.  All of us have times in our lives
when we feel alone, when joys and relationships that we treasure leave us, and
when we feel fearful about the future.  In
those times, like the disciples from Emmaus, we want Jesus to be with us, a
rock of stability in a world where change is so often for the worse.

These are things that we feel
and mean, and somehow, when we read and sing “Abide with Me”, we know that the
author felt and meant those things too. 
He’s not saying these things because he thinks they would sound good in
a hymn.  He’s saying them because this is
where his heart is.  This characteristic
of sincerity is both indefinable and utterly necessary in a good hymn.

Another emotionally resonant
hymn is “It Is Well with My Soul”. 
Unlike many of the hymns we’ve studied, the core phrase of this hymn
isn’t taken directly from any one passage, but it is still a hymn that is
thoughtful and rich in Scriptural allusions.

However, what really gives
“It Is Well with My Soul” its emotional punch is the music.  P. P. Bliss was one of the greatest American
hymnists of the 19th century, and the tune to “It Is Well with My
Soul” may be his masterwork.  I could go
through and talk about all the technical reasons why this tune is so good, but
I think every one of us feels the power of the tune whether we know a thing
about music theory or not.  We sing “It
Is Well with My Soul” because we love to sing it.

There’s not a thing wrong
with that, either.  If a catchy tune is
paired with thoughtless, shallow lyrics, so that people love to sing it because
it’s fun to sing, that’s a problem. 
There, music has become the master of meaning instead of its
servant.  The words are nothing more than
an excuse to sing the tune.  However,
when the words are weighty and valuable on their own, that is when a great tune
adds tremendous emotion and produces the worship God wants.
