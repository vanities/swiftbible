Last week was the yearly Hymninar, a hymnwriting seminar
held in Columbia, MO.  During the course
of the week, I had many interesting discussions about hymns, but
some of the most interesting of them all didn’t occur during the actual
seminar.

My family and I spent the week staying with one of the
elders of the congregation there and his wife. 
They’re wonderful people whom I’ve known since I was a teenager, and I
spent a lot of the time I was out of class talking with them.  We worked our way through a number of
spiritual topics, but because they knew why I was there as well as I did, we
spent a fair amount of time talking about hymns.

This was unusual for me. 
I try to avoid riding my hobby horse through every conversation, so I
generally talk about hymns only with other hymn geeks unless somebody else
brings the subject up.

Neither one of my hosts, though, is a hymn geek.  They’re not even all that musically
inclined.  They sing in worship not
because they are singers, but because they are Christians.  As a result, they came to the topic with a
very different perspective.



For example, before services Wednesday evening, I was
invited to lead a hymn in the assembly. 
After some deliberation, I settled on “Ah, Holy Jesus” and briefly
discussed my decision with others in the class. 
We spent some time talking about the text but more time talking about
the tune.  In my opinion (and the opinion
of others in the discussion), it’s a masterpiece.  It has one of the most beautiful bass lines
in the hymnal.  Because we were all
singers, it was the kind of thing we couldn’t help but notice.

When my turn to lead came, I was surprised to find that the
congregation didn’t know “Ah, Holy Jesus”, but we worshiped together with it
regardless.  After the service, I talked
about the hymn with my host.  He was
enthusiastic about it.  In fact, he
wanted to talk to the other elders to make sure the congregation learned
it.

However, his enthusiasm had nothing to do with the bass
line.  He’s an intelligent and thoughtful
man, but I’m reasonably sure he doesn’t have the theory background to analyze
music above the this-is-pretty level (which, lest anyone misunderstand me, is a
perfectly godly way for a Christian to be). 
He didn’t talk about the music.

Instead, he talked about the content, especially the magnificent
beginning of the third verse:

“Lo, the Good Shepherd for the sheep is offered!

“The slave has sinned, and yet the Son has suffered!”

In fact, he grabbed his home copy of the hymnal and read the
couplet out, savoring every syllable. 
That was meaningful to him.  That
mattered.  He was determined that his
church needed to sing “Ah, Holy Jesus” not because of the beautiful tune, but
because of the content.  God needed to be
glorified by His people singing those words about the suffering of His Son.

Some Christians are singers. 
Most aren’t.  They would never
voluntarily sing in a choir or play in a band. 
Generally, they can’t read music.

However, because hymns have a musical component, the
discussion of worship repertoire in the church is ordinarily driven by
singers.  They get such an endorphin rush
out of singing that they eagerly seek out opportunities to sing.

There’s nothing wrong with being this way.  I love to sing myself.  I will be listening to a song on the radio
and start humming the bass line to it because hitting chords makes me happy
(this is also the reason why my wife doesn’t like to have the radio on when
she’s in the car with me).  In fact, I
believe that a hymn for the congregation must be enjoyable to sing; otherwise,
as a practical matter, it will not be sung.

However, a problem arises when “enjoyable to sing” becomes
the sole criterion for deciding whether or not to use a hymn in worship.  There are plenty of spiritual songs, both old
and new, that have gorgeous, lovely tunes but never manage to say much.  However, because they are enjoyable to sing,
singers like them and promote their use in worship.  It’s repertoire by endorphin rush.

That pleases the singers. 
They get their good-music fix. 
I’m less confident that it pleases God (if the Psalms are any
indication, God really wants His people to sing content!).  I’m quite confident, though, that it misses
Christians who don’t really enjoy singing all that much.

I can’t imagine my host getting excited about singing the
likes of “Did You Repent, Fully Repent”. 
Sure, you’ve got a fun alto lead, a high-flying tenor line, and some
positively sepulchral notes in the bass (“Bari-basses need not apply!  Only real men down here!”), but he doesn’t
sing any of those parts.  He will sing,
because he’s a courteous man and because he is commanded to, but he’s not going
to go home and read again to himself with relish:

“Christ the dear Lord, glorious Lord, speaks to us gently,
“By His great word that is our light each day.”

To my hymnist’s eyes, those two lines have more filler than
a dollar-store hot dog.  He might notice
that; he might not.  He will notice,
though, that there is nothing there that resonates with his deep understanding
of God’s word.  The singers may have had
a blast, but singing that song will for him always be a meh experience.

In the congregation, we should sing songs that are for the
congregation.  This usually means that
they will have been written for the congregation (rather than a quartet or a
praise band), but it also means that they will appeal to whole the
congregation, and not just part.

When it comes to song worship, singers are a vocal segment,
but they are still only a segment. 
However, Christians make up the vast majority of Sunday-morning
attendees.  They are defined not by their
love of singing, but by their love for God and His word.  When we select songs that appeal profoundly
to those things, songs like “Ah, Holy Jesus”, we don’t miss anybody who is
there for the right reasons.  However,
when we sing hymns that appeal to anything else, we cannot help but exclude.
