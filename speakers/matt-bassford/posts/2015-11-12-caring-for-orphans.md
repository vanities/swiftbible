All my life, I’ve been associated with (for lack of a better
descriptor) the non-institutional churches of Christ.  That was the way my parents taught me when I
was young, and I’ve adopted it for myself as an adult.  Even though members of non-institutional
churches of Christ have done all sorts of foolish and evil things (as human
beings are wont to do), sometimes even in the name of Christ, I still believe
in the soundness of the guiding principle: 
as churches and as individuals, we ought to be concerned with what Jesus
wants us to do, rather than with what we want to do.  That’s an idea that you can build a life on.

Even so, I can’t claim that I’ve always lived up to that
principle, nor have any of the churches I’ve belonged to always lived up to
it.  Sometimes we miss and don’t even
realize we’re missing.  I believe this is
the case when it comes to James 1:27.  Perhaps
others’ experience has been different, but most of the sermons I’ve heard
(preached?) on James 1:27 have been about what we should *not* do.

That’s not wrong. 
That’s badly wrong.


I say this even though I believe that the basic argument
being made (that churches should not be involved in caring for the world’s
orphans) is valid.  I don’t think churches
should be, and I think the reason goes deeper than “The Bible doesn’t say we
can.”  Scripturally speaking, the Lord
seems to be as much or more concerned with process as with results.  He instructs His disciples to share the
gospel with the lost rather than sending angels to do it, even though angels
might prove more effective.  In doing the
will of Christ, even if they aren’t best suited for it, Christians learn to be
more like Him.

I believe the same analysis applies to caring for orphans
and other children in need.  Maybe it’s
more efficient for Christians to drop a check in the plate and have the church
treasurer send another check to the orphanage. 
However, that check in the collection plate isn’t life-changing.  The most wicked, hypocritical so-called
disciple out there can write a check without changing his ways one iota.  Many of them do.  What is life-changing is when we involve
ourselves personally in caring for the needy and helpless, the more significant
the involvement, the better.  Such an
experience cannot help but teach us the compassion of Christ.

Three cheers, then, for rejecting church support of
orphanages!  It ought to be a work of the
individual, we note, and then move on. 
However, all too often, the idea that individuals *should* be doing this is relegated to a rhetorical footnote.  As long as we keep the church out of the
orphanage, that’s the important thing!

No; it’s not the important thing.  The important thing is that every Christian
ought to be actively and personally involved in caring for children who need
help.  I agree that it’s a spiritual
disaster when we start offloading the work that we should be doing onto the
church.  It’s easier that way; no messing
up our lives by getting involved with messy (in every sense of the word)
children.  It’s easy, but it’s
wrong.

However, it’s equally wrong to use church non-involvement as
a personal out.  I understand the reasons
for wanting a personal out.  Many
Christians have little experience with poor people in America.  Most of us have a snug, safe, middle-class
existence, so that our understanding of those who do not is shaped by media
reports of urban violence and by politicians who foment distrust of the
underclass for reasons of their own.

We don’t know those people, we’re afraid of them, and we’re
fearful that an excess of compassion will lead us to do something dangerous or
unwise.  “Bleeding-heart liberal” is not
commonly used as a compliment.  It’s much
easier, then, for us to stay uninvolved, to lavish our affection and compassion
only on those who are familiar to us.

However, the results of this so-natural way of thinking are
a spiritual disaster of their own. 
Instead of shining our light to the world, we focus our attentions
inward, so that each church becomes an enclave of prosperity and purity, its
members unconcerned with the physical and spiritual woes of the communities
around us.

That’s not what Jesus did. 
Not.  Not.  Not. 
Not.  Not.  Throughout His ministry, He went to the poor,
to the sinners, to the people that the religious elites of His day refused to
acknowledge.  He didn’t merely bring them
the gospel.  He brought them help.  He healed their sicknesses.  He cast out their demons.  He filled their bellies.  He received the children whom His disciples
thought were beneath His notice.  That’s
not a blueprint for an institution.  It’s
a blueprint for our lives.

I think that over the past several years, the light has
begun to dawn for us.  I commend Dana
Carozza and all who are involved with Sacred Selections for the work that they
do.  I am thankful for all the Christians
who have chosen to adopt, foster, or even host, particularly for those who have
helped children with physical or mental issues that had the world passing by on
the other side.  Of such is the kingdom of
heaven.

Of course, far more work yet remains to be done.  I saw a meme on Facebook the other day that
observed that if one family in every three churches took in a foster child,
that would solve the current child-placement crisis.  What the denominational world does is none of
my concern, but that sure looks like a golden opportunity for service for an
awful lot of us.

In saying this, I’m fully aware that right now, I’m not
doing much to solve the problem.  Lauren
and I have been talking about fostering for years, but we haven’t done it
yet.  Partly, I’ve been reluctant because
of logistical issues (Where in our overstuffed house would we put another
child???), but part of it is the same fear I described above.

As a pessimist by nature, I don’t have any trouble
concocting doomsday scenarios.  What if
the placement’s a disaster, and we can’t control the kid?  What if, despite our best efforts, our
charges end up as drug dealers or prostitutes anyway?  And so on. 
If you want reasonable-sounding justifications for doing nothing, I’m
your guy.

However, both the beauty and the burden of discipleship is
that none of us are responsible for results. 
It’s not the preacher’s fault if his listeners can’t endure sound
doctrine, nor is it the fault of the compassionate when their efforts
apparently go for nothing (though Tuesday’s post on providence may be relevant
here).

If our attempts to help make our lives messier and more
difficult, well, we are supposed to be following Jesus, after all.  “Messy” and “difficult”, daunting though they
sound to us, are hardly a fitting description for scourging and
crucifixion.  We have to go well beyond
mess and difficulty before we have filled up the sufferings of Christ!

Ultimately, I’m afraid it gets back to “don’t want to”,
which is a familiar problem for most disciples. 
With time, I hope Lauren and I can sort out the logistical issues, and
with study, meditation, and prayer, I hope I can sort out my spiritual issues
(not being a misgiving-y kind of person, Lauren doesn’t share mine).  I hope also that in the years and decades to
come, all Christians will learn to be James 1:27 Christians.

And perhaps, as we help others with their physical problems,
we will learn more about helping them with their spiritual problems too.
