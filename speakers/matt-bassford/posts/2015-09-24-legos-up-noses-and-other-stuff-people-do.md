A couple weeks back, I was sitting at my desk downstairs
with the children playing behind me.  My
four-year-old son, Marky, began to wail. 
He does that with some frequency.

“What’s wrong?” I asked.

“My nose hurts!” Marky sobbed.



I turned to survey the scene.  Marky was sitting on the floor by one end of
the futon.  My five-year-old daughter,
Empress Zoë, Queen of the World, was reading a book about halfway down the
futon.  I assessed the distance between
them and concluded that a sufficiently angry sister could have lashed out with
a heel to clobber her obnoxious brother. 
This was entirely thinkable. 
After all, sibling violence is my children’s love language.

“Marky, did Zoë kick you in the face?”

“Nooo. . .”

I reconsidered.  Marky
had juuust gotten a whole bunch of new toys for his birthday, including several
sets of Legos.  I thought for a moment,
and in a flash of fatherly insight, knew at once what had happened.

“Marky, did you stuff a Lego up your nose?”

“Ye-e-es.”

I beckoned him over and peered up the nostril in question
(always a risky operation with a four-year-old boy), and sure enough, there it
was, by now encrusted with a layer of snot and blood.  If left there for 20 years, it would probably
have turned into a pearl.  However, I
decided that the Lego had to go.  I
finally induced him to snort it into a Kleenex after 15 minutes of howling and
agony.  Marky cried some too.

Some perspective is in order here.  As is my paternal prerogative, I am convinced
that my boy is The Smartest, Strongest, Charmingest, Etc., Boy Ever.  However, this was not his finest hour.  He has not-so-finest hours sometimes, just as
any four-year-old boy does.  I’m not
going to list them all (or else he will hate me when he reads this a dozen
years from now), but they are certainly there.

However, frustrating and even infuriating though those
things may be, they aren’t the first things I think of when I think of my
son.  I think of the warm heart that he
got from his mother, the incorrigible hair that he got from me, and the
light-up-a-room smile that is uniquely his own. 
I make allowances for the rest, because he’s my son, and I love him.

Of course, that penchant for occasional foolishness is
something he comes by honestly too.  By
nature, I haaate to be embarrassed.  I
prepare meticulously for every sermon I preach. 
In part, this is because I am afraid that if I don’t, I will fail in
some disastrous and embarrassing way.     
However, despite this phobia of mine, I still have plenty of
cringe-worthy moments in my past, things I did or failed to do that are not
merely embarrassing but downright evil. 
I can remember things I did fifteen or twenty years ago and still feel
compelled to wince.

Those things are a part of the way that I see myself, but
they aren’t part of the way that God sees me. 
Psalm 103:13-14 reads, “Just as a father has compassion on his children,
so the Lord has compassion on those who fear Him.  For He Himself knows our frame; He is mindful
that we are but dust.”

God is not malicious. 
He’s compassionate.  He doesn’t
hold those who fear Him to a perfect standard. 
He’s mindful of us like I’m mindful of my preschooler.  He doesn’t expect something from us that
we’re not capable of producing.  Like any
good father, he makes allowances.

Even for the spiritual equivalent of Legos up noses.
