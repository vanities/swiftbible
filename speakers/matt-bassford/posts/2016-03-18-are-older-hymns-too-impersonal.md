Even though I’m a steadfast critic of many contemporary
praise songs, I enjoy talking with people who don’t agree with me on the
subject.  There is so much about hymnody
and worship that I don’t understand, and one of the best ways to learn more is
to listen to those who see things differently.

Often, these praise-song advocates will offer a critique of
older hymns.  I agree with many of the
points they make.  “Thee” and “Thou” make
hymns harder to understand.  The writing
in older hymns is frequently stiff and over-complicated.  Older hymn tunes are often uneventful and
boring to modern ears.

Sure.  “Great hymn”
does not mean “perfect hymn”, and in my experience, the latter does not exist
in any time period.  The finest of our
offerings to God is still flawed.



However, there’s one comment that I hear with some frequency
that puzzles me.  It is the argument that
praise songs are better because they are more personal.  They use “I” and “me”, older hymns don’t, so
worshipers connect more to contemporary songs.

There are certainly a number of hymns that we sing that
speak of God/Christ in the third person (“What a Friend We Have in Jesus”).  There are even more that address God from a
first-person plural perspective (“Holy, Holy, Holy”).  However, every era of English-language hymnody
that I can think of, from the early eighteenth century on, contains literally
hundreds of first-person, direct-address, personal-prayer hymns.  Indeed, a substantial number of the greatest
hymns ever written are personal prayers like this.  For instance:

* Nearer, My God, to Thee
* Abide With Me
* I Need Thee Every Hour
* Take My Life, and Let It Be
* I Am Coming, Lord
* Nearer, Still Nearer
* Higher Ground
* Rock of Ages

Not everything that hymnists of centuries past wrote was
personal expression, but a hefty chunk of it was.  So. . . why do those who like contemporary
praise songs suggest that we need praise songs so worshipers can express
themselves personally?

I think the answer may lie in the kind of expression that
older hymns offer, as compared to modern praise songs.  For instance, consider this overlooked gem from
1791:

*Gracious Lord, Incline Thine Ear*

*Gracious Lord, incline Thine ear,*

*My complaint vouchsafe to hear;*

*Sore distressed with guilt am I;*

*Give me Christ, or else I die.*

*Wealth and honor I disdain;*

*Earthly comforts all are vain;*

*They can never satisfy;*

*Give me Christ, or else I die.*

*Lord, deny me what Thou wilt;*

*Only take away my guilt;*

*Mourning at Thy feet I lie;*

*Give me Christ, or else I die.*

*All unholy and unclean,*

*I am sinful, vile, and mean,*

*But to Thee for mercy fly;*

*Give me Christ, or else I die.*

*O my God, what shall I say?*

*Take, oh take my sins away!*

*Jesus’ blood to me apply;*

*Give me Christ, or else I die.*

This comes to us from the pen of William Hammond, better
known for “Lord, We Come Before Thee Now”. 
It’s good enough to sing; I suspect we don’t sing it because it’s never
gotten the music it deserves.  Even
though the writing is fairly direct (lots of seven-syllable sentences), the
Thees, Thous, and occasional archaic words (“vouchsafe”) reveal its time of
origin.

However, I don’t think that its age is revealed only by its
style.  I think its age is revealed by
its content.  This is certainly a
soul-exposing hymn.  Every verse drips
intense sincerity, and each reveals the author's desperate longing for forgiveness of
sins.  “Give me Christ, or else I die” is
not an expression of his unhappiness without Jesus.  It is an expression of his conviction that
without Jesus, he is quite literally dead. 
This is the cry of a drowning man for a life preserver.

Hymns that reflect this deep sorrow for sin and deep fear of
spiritual separation are, to say the least, rare in the past 50 years’ worth of
hymns and praise songs.    Of the songs that I know, Twila Paris’s “Lamb
of God” and Stuart Townend’s “How Deep the Father’s Love for Us” (along with
some of the other Townend/Getty hymns) come closest.

Of course, when we’re talking about Townend and Getty, we’re
talking about some of the deepest and most thoughtful work contemporary hymnody
has to offer.  As writers, they are
exceptional, both in the sense of being good and in the sense of being
exceptions.  Most praise songs don’t come
within miles of this subject.  Their
theme is not, “I seek Jesus because He saves me from the death of my sin.”  Their theme is “I seek Jesus because He makes
me happy.”  They are the songs of people
who are infatuated with Jesus.

I think that worship like this both reflects and reinforces
the spirit of the age.  At some point
since World War II, “sin” became a dirty word. 
Modern American Christians don’t like to talk about sin.  They don’t like to acknowledge that the evil
that they personally have done would condemn them to an eternity in hell, but
for a single astonishing act of grace. 
What they practice, then, is not so much Christianity as a form of
neo-paganism in which Jesus has replaced Jupiter as the source of earthly
happiness.

In short, I think that people are right to say that older
hymns don’t speak for the modern worshiper like modern praise songs do.  However, the problem doesn’t have anything to
do with the presence or absence of first-person singular.  It has to do with the hearts of worshipers,
hearts that would rather not contemplate the ugly necessity of the cross.  Such worshipers will always choose a personal
expression that soothes rather than one that confronts.
