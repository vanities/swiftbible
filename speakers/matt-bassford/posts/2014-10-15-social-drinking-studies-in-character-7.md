*And Noah began to be a
farmer, and he planted a vineyard. Then he drank of the wine and was drunk, and
became uncovered in his tent.*

-- Genesis 9:20-21

During his days on earth, Noah did many great things.  He obeyed God when the world around him was
utterly wicked.  However, just like the
rest of us, Noah was fallible, and he bears the ignoble distinction of being
the first drunk recorded in Scripture.  Despite
his great wisdom, he succumbed to the lure of the vineyard, and alcohol reduced
him from one of the heroes of faith to a naked fool snoring in his tent.  If drinking can bring low a man like that,
what will it do to us?



We know that drunkenness is explicitly condemned under the
law of Christ, and few Christians, even those who have trouble with alcohol,
will dispute this.  However, many more
Christians will insist upon their right to drink, provided that they do not
become drunk.  A moment’s reflection will
reveal the danger of this position.

The Bible teaches that self-control is a virtue that
Christians should pursue, and as with any other virtue, we should seek to
promote it as much as possible. 
Drinking, even social drinking, lowers self-control.  Studies have shown that even one glass of
alcohol lowers our inhibitions and impairs our motor skills.

Because of its corrosive effect on our self-control,
drinking opens the door to any number of other sins.  First on the list is drunkenness.  The more we drink, the less likely we are to
stop drinking before we have become drunk. 
Few indeed are the social drinkers who can claim never to have been
intoxicated.

In fact, once alcohol has diminished our self-restraint,
every sin becomes more likely.  If we
struggle with lust, drinking will lead us to sexual immorality.  If we struggle with anger, it will provoke us
to angry words or even violence.  When we
drink, we become putty in the hands of the devil.

It is true that in situations where the local water supply
has become contaminated, the use of wine or other alcohol as a purifying agent
is allowed (see, e.g., 1 Timothy 5:23). 
This is much like our understanding that a Christian may take
painkillers for some injury if a doctor prescribes them, and it does not imply
that the recreational use of alcohol is wise or even godly.

In short, there is no good reason why a Christian in our
circumstances should ever use alcohol, and there are a host of reasons why we should not.  If we turn to drink, the costs of that decision
may well prove incalculable.  Let’s learn
from the example of Noah.  Let’s learn
never to believe Satan’s lies about drinking.
