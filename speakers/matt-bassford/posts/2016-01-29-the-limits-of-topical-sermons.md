Let’s start with the disclaimers first.  I do not believe it is wrong to preach a
topical sermon.  I recognize that
literally every sermon in the New Testament that uses Scripture is
topical.  Indeed, I myself have preached,
preach, and will continue to preach topical sermons.

However, I also believe that topical sermons are easy to
overuse, and, if overused, will stunt the spiritual growth of the preacher that presents them and the congregation
that hears them.



I have to confess that I started thinking this way out of
laziness.  About two-and-a-half years
ago, I started reviewing Bibles by taking them for test drives in the
pulpit.  Before that time, I hadn’t given
a great deal of thought to the balance of exegetical and topical sermons in my
preaching.  I pretty much preached on
what came to mind, and I used PowerPoint to project all the passages I used
onto the screen.

When I began rating Bibles, though, I wanted to rate them on
ease of citation in the pulpit, so I stopped reading Scriptures off the slides and
started reading them out of my Bible instead. 
It’s a lot easier to find one passage and put a ribbon marker there than
it is to find a dozen passages scattered across a thousand-plus pages of an
unfamiliar Bible, so I took to preaching more exegetical than topical sermons when
I was reviewing.  Like I said, laziness.

However, as I consciously began to prepare more exegetical
sermons, I noticed that (in my own estimation, at least), my preaching got
better.  I had more thoughtful things to
say, and I said them more powerfully.  As
a result, over the past year or two, what began as a quirk has evolved into a
strong preference for sermons that begin and end with the text.  In particular, I see three areas where
topical sermons can easily fall short.

**1. Topical sermons
generally miss the point of the context.**

Also as a result of my recent interest in Bibles, I’ve done *much* more reading of the Scripture than
I’d ever done in my life before.  2014
was the first year in which I read through the whole Bible; now, I’m currently
in the middle of a plan to read the Bible in 90 days.  I’ve also done more unplanned reading,
whether to evaluate a Bible for readability or simply for my own amusement.

This has led me to a very different view of the way the
Bible is constructed.  Like many whose
parents belonged to a church of Christ, I was raised with a verse-centric view
of Scripture.  Acts 2:38.  Colossians 3:16.  1 Peter 3:21. 
Without even realizing it, I treated the Bible like a giant reference
book in which I found the sound bites that would prove I was right.  I was basically a proof-texter.

Time for another disclaimer. 
There’s nothing inherently wrong with proof-texting either.  Jesus Himself did it.  However, if we only understand and use the
Scripture at the proof-text level, our understanding can only be
incomplete.  The more I read, the more I
figured out that I needed to study the Bible not merely at the level of the
verse, but at the level of the argument.

Let me give you an example of how this works.  When I was growing up, another one of those
beloved proof texts was Romans 6:3-4. 
You used it to show that you only rose to walk in newness of life after
baptism, so people who thought they could be saved without being baptized were
wrong.

I still agree with that point, but it is *not* the point that Paul is making.  He’s not dealing with a bunch of people who
think that they’re saved after praying the sinner’s prayer.  Instead, he’s addressing those who already
have been baptized for the forgiveness of sins. 
He’s concerned that they might argue that because they’ve been saved by
grace through faith, sin is at least irrelevant and at most something that
glorifies God.  Paul introduces
baptism—which he and his audience agree on—to argue that once you’ve been
baptized, you’re supposed to be dead to living like the old you and alive to
living like Jesus, which leaves no room for sin.

Romans 6:3-4 makes a point that outsiders need to hear.  Romans 6:1-23 makes a point that Christians
need to hear, and the very best way to make that point is to follow the logic
of the inspired apostle.  Once a topical
sermon starts doing that, it has ceased to be a topical sermon and has become
an exegetical sermon.

**2.  Topical sermons can never be bigger than the
preacher.**

This wasn’t true for the inspired preachers of the first
century.  They didn’t have to think about
what they were going to say beforehand. 
They could get up, open their mouths, and awesome would come out.  It sure must have been nice to have the Holy Spirit
do your sermon prep for you!

Today, however, I am not an inspired speaker, nor do I have
any hope of ever becoming one. 
Everything I say has to come from one of two places:  the Bible or my pointy little head.  In exegetical sermons, the “Bible” method
predominates; in topical sermons, the “pointy little head” method predominates.

This is evident from the very beginning of my sermon
preparation.  Let’s say that I decide to
preach a sermon on love.  I don’t start
reading the Bible; after all, there is no one place in Scripture where
everything God has to say about love is collected.  Instead, I stop and think.  I ask myself, “What do I already know about
love?”  I start jotting down subtopics on
a piece of scratch paper, and after I do that, I go back through and fill in
passages (probably one- or two-verse proof texts) that show that, yes, the
Bible does indeed make this point.

Again, there’s nothing wrong with this.  It is certainly a process that can produce a
100-percent true sermon.  However, what
it cannot do is exceed the limits of my understanding (or, if I’m using a bunch
of commentaries, the limits of the commentators’ understanding).  This is my outline, my thought, with bits of
the Bible pressed into service to support it.

However, if I’m preaching on a text (say, for symmetry’s
sake, 1 Corinthians 13), immediately after I decide on that text, I turn there
and start looking for the logical structure that is already present.  My sermon outline isn’t mine.  It’s Paul’s. 
In a topical sermon, the Bible is in the supporting role.  In an exegetical sermon, my own understanding
is playing second fiddle.

This has two particularly important results.  First, the Bible teaches me a lot more when
I’m writing a sermon from the text instead of from my head.  No matter how familiar the passage, it always
contains a point or application I hadn’t seen before.

Second, when I preach a topical sermon, it’s hard for me to
say more than I understand and intend. 
By contrast, when I’m preaching from the text, it is nearly certain that
I’m missing something.  God is way smarter
than I am.  However, I am still putting
all the pieces out there for the congregation to put together, even if I
haven’t put them together myself.  I
might miss an important connection in 1 Corinthians 13, but there’s a pretty
good chance that one of my hearers will make it.

**3.  Topical sermons too easily lead to hobbyism.**

Last year, in the midst of all the gay-marriage brouhaha, I
had an extended discussion on the subject with a friend of mine from high
school who has, to say the least, very different views about it than I do.  At one point, I observed that opposing the
practice of homosexuality isn’t at the heart of what it means to be a
Christian.  Things like love, faith,
etc., are.  He replied, “If this is a
minor point to you, you sure spend an awful lot of time blogging about it.”

Ouch.  What could I
say?  My posting history made it
perfectly clear that I was writing a great deal about a point that I admitted
was not among “the weightier matters of the law”.  My preoccupation with current events had led
me to lose my focus on what was more important. 
I wasn’t saying anything untrue, but I wasn’t proclaiming the whole
counsel of God either.

I suspect that every preacher struggles with this, at least
to some extent, when he writes topical sermons. 
All of those topics have to come from somewhere, be it current events,
Facebook, or a request from a church member. 
As that list of topics accumulates, it is highly unlikely that it will
match up exactly with the topics and emphases of Scripture.  Some part of the whole counsel of God is
going to be neglected, simply because the preacher doesn’t think about it.

Sometimes, this is necessary.  Jesus Himself sometimes drew on current
events to make a point.  However, if all
we ever talk about is the present, we aren’t preparing our hearers for the
future.

The word of God is timeless and complete.  If it is proclaimed, it will not return to
Him empty, without accomplishing the purpose that He had for it.  Maybe an exegetical sermon on a given context
isn’t exactly what people are eager to hear right now.  However, there will come a time when they
will need to have heard it.  We cannot
build on the foundation of the apostles and the prophets unless we allow them
all, though dead, to speak.  Topical
sermons can certainly have value, but the thoughtful, systematic exploration of
all of Scripture is essential.
