A couple of days ago, a substantial number of my Facebook
friends shared [this post.](http://entrustedministries.com/blog/look-away-my-son)  When I clicked on the link, I did so with
some anticipation.  Anything that so many
Christians like is going to be good, right?

However, once I started reading the article, my anticipation
quickly turned to dismay.  I didn’t even
finish it on my first read-through, but by the time I gave up, I felt like I
needed to bathe.  I couldn’t put my
finger on the reason, but something about the piece filled me with revulsion.

I forgot about it until a couple hours later, when I was
talking to my wife.  I mentioned my
reaction to her, and she replied, “Yes! 
I felt exactly the same way!”  At
that point, I decided that I needed to invest some time in figuring out what so
bothered both of us.  Here’s what I came
up with:



**It’s Not True.** The thrust of the post is that the author
trains her small boys not to look at immodestly dressed women because “They
need some privacy.”  She goes on to
explain, “Perhaps the woman doesn’t know she
needs privacy. That’s okay. As believers, we know she does. . .”  In other words, she is training her sons that
it is they, rather than some woman, who get to decide whether that woman needs
privacy or not.

The author makes a big deal
about respecting women, but that doesn’t strike me as very respectful.  In fact, I think it’s a perversion of the
very concept of privacy.  Within the
scope of law, people have the right to decide what they want to reveal of their
bodies.  They are the ones who decide
what they want to keep private, or not.

A bikini-clad woman on the
beach does not “need privacy”.  It’s not
like we’ve barged in on her when she’s using the restroom or something.  She has chosen to reveal her body publicly,
and those scraps of cloth are all the privacy she wants.  I disagree with her decision, but I have to
acknowledge that she has the personal autonomy to make it.  She decides what she wants to keep private,
not me.

If that is not true, if I get
to decide to “give her privacy” that she doesn’t want, does that mean that I
have the right to take away her privacy when she does want it?  After all, we’ve already established that she
doesn’t know enough to make the right privacy decision.  If a mother teaches her sons that they are
better equipped to make privacy decisions for women than those women are, that
conviction can lead them down all kinds of evil paths.

In short, there are serious
problems with the language here.

**It’s the Wrong Parent.**Throughout the course of the
post, the author quotes several times from the book of Proverbs.  The stated goal of the book is to provide instruction
in wisdom for young men.  However, with
the exception of Proverbs 31, throughout the book, the speaker is almost always
the young man’s father.  This is
particularly true in the early part of the book, which is where the bulk of
Proverbs’ discussion of sexual sin may be found.

I believe that today, it is
still primarily the responsibility of the father to teach his son about sex.  Men and women think differently, and there is
no area where this is more true than in the sexual realm.  Even the godliest mother cannot have the same
insight into her son’s sexuality that his father can.  She doesn’t know what it’s like to be a man,
and that makes a vast difference.

However, if the mother takes
that leading role anyway (and I know there are plenty of situations where she
has no choice, in which case she can only do her best), she runs the risk of feminizing her son’s perspective on
sex.  The original post opened with an
anecdote about how the author was over at her friend’s house, some immodestly
dressed woman came on TV, and her friend and the friend’s son looked at each
other and smiled until the image went away, then returned to watching TV.

“I sat in awe,” the author
gushed.  However, as I read the anecdote,
I wasn’t awed.  I was repelled.  When my son encounters sexual temptation, I
don’t want him to sit there passively and smile sweetly at (real or imagined)
Mommy.  That’s a feminine response.  It feels suffocating to me.

When Marky and I are watching
TV and we see something objectionable, I say, “Marky, turn that off.  I don’t want to look at that!”  Admittedly, my primary concern is for myself,
but it occurs to me that I’m also modeling behavior for him.  I want him to act, to reject the worthless thing
that is set before his eyes.  As for
smiling sweetly, generally the only time we exchange sweet smiles is when we’re
preparing to tackle one another.  That’s
as it should be.

**It’s the Wrong
Worldview.** Here, as elsewhere, it’s
important to consider the source of the post. 
I don’t know anything about the author personally.  However, Entrusted
Ministries, in the “Mission and Vision” heading on the site, says, “All people are
sinners not only by inheritance, but also by their own choice and therefore are
under just condemnation without defense or excuse. We believe that without
exception every man and every woman is totally depraved and needs a Savior.”

In other words, these folks are strongly Calvinist.  Think that their convictions about innate
depravity might influence their views about parenting small children?

My son is four.  If he were
presented with a nearly-naked woman, he would definitely stare at her.  However, his motivation for so staring would
be the same as his motivation for staring at any of the other people he stares
at (and he does lots of staring).  He’s
curious.  He sees something unfamiliar,
and he wants to figure it out. He is as innocent of sin as he could possibly
be, and he will remain so for years to come.

I don’t know, but I suspect that the author of the article would
disagree with my last statement.  At
least, other Calvinists of my acquaintance have not hesitated to describe the
nature of their three- and four-year-old relatives as sinful and the behavior
of those children as sin.  If that’s your
worldview, then sure, it’s important to get that four-year-old “protecting his
eyes”.  However, if I believe that my son
is as sin-filled as a puppy dog, there’s not anything to protect him from.

**It’s the Wrong
Approach.** One of the things
that struck me about the article was the difference between the author’s
teaching on modesty (big emphasis by her right now) and the author’s teaching
on sex generally (something her husband will discuss at some undefined point in
the future).  I find that awfully
strange.  Modesty is not necessarily a
sexual problem (showy, expensive clothing is a modesty issue too), but if we’re
talking about revealing dress, that’s definitely a sexual issue.  If the boys aren’t ready for the sex talk
yet, why the need to introduce the concept of modesty?  That seems like another relic of the
Neo-Victorianism that has bedeviled the church for decades when it comes to
sexual issues.

Honestly, I think it’s wiser to lead with the sex talk and get to
modesty issues second.  Of course, I
haven’t had a great deal of choice in this. 
Our daughter has a great interest in reading about the human body.  One day when we were on the way home from Wednesday-evening
services, she announced to us that babies started when the father put his sperm
cell in the mother’s egg cell.  She was
five at the time.

We discussed.  It has not appeared
to harm her.  I’m sure lots of other
discussions will be sprung on my wife and me in the future.

A discussion of revealing clothing and why men shouldn’t stare at
scantily attired women makes sense in the context of sex and sexual
attraction.  However, without that
context, it leaves the impression that there is something innately bad about
the female body, something vague yet evil. 
Rather than the “Not yet” that I think is essential to Christian
parenting on sex, that starts sounding a lot more like “No.”

In sum, though I certainly believe that Christians need to do a better
job of rising up and teaching their children about sexual issues, I don’t think
modesty should be the place to start with that. 
Even if it is, I don’t think the deceptive claim that “She needs privacy”
is the way to do it.  If you can’t properly explain what the problem is, now is probably not the time to
talk about it.
