Condemnation of the practice of homosexuality is not one of
the main themes of the New Testament. 
There are only a handful of texts in the law of Christ that even discuss
the subject.  As a result, religious
advocates of gay marriage, etc., commonly defend their position by explaining
that the few texts in opposition don’t really mean what they appear to mean on
their face:  “Paul wasn’t really talking
about the practice of homosexuality.  He
was talking about X instead.”



One of the most difficult passages to address in this way is
Romans 1:26-27.  This text is longer than
the others about the practice of homosexuality, and it not only defines but
describes homosexual behavior (“men committing shameless acts with men”).  There’s not much scope here for arguing that
this is actually about a particular kind of homosexual behavior (prostitution,
pedophilia, etc.).

Because of this, attempts to dismiss Romans 1:26-27 are
often much more wide-ranging in nature. For instance, a Facebook friend of mine
linked to [this article](http://www.patheos.com/blogs/unfundamentalistchristians/2015/09/the-punctuation-mark-that-might-change-how-you-read-romans/) about a week ago. 
 The author argues that throughout Romans 1:18-32, Paul is not actually
expressing his own convictions, but is simply quoting the bigoted arguments of
Gentile-hating Hellenistic Jews.  He does
this, the article continues, in order to set up his condemnation of Hellenistic
Jews as sinners in need of the grace of Christ.

I think this is partially correct.  It’s hard to deny that Romans 1:18-32
functions as a rhetorical trap.  Paul
wants to get his Jewish hearers nodding along as he blasts those bad, bad
Gentiles until the trap snaps shut in Romans 2. 
As Paul writes in Romans 2:1, “In passing judgment on another you
condemn yourself, because you, the judge, practice the very same things.”
(ESV)  Now that his audience has roundly
condemned the sins of others, he forces them to recognize that in that
condemnation, they have also condemned themselves.

So far, so good. 
However, it does not follow from this that Paul disagrees with the
condemnation of the Gentiles in Romans 1. 
It’s entirely possible that Paul simultaneously uses the sins of the
Gentiles to sucker the Jews in, while still meaning every word he says about
the Gentiles.  In fact, there are two
reasons to believe that this is exactly what Paul is doing.

First, it is demonstrably untrue that Paul disagrees with
everything in Romans 1:18-32.  He’s not
simply quoting somebody else whom he believes is wrong.  After all, the text condemns plenty of other
behaviors besides the practice of homosexuality, things that we know Paul also thought
were wrong.

Take, for instance, idolatry.  Romans 1:22-25 clearly condemns idolatrous
worship.  Certainly, any Hellenistic Jew
would have condemned idolatry too. 
However, this was a conviction that Paul himself shared.  1 Corinthians is an epistle that nearly
everyone agrees is genuinely Pauline, and 1 Corinthians 10:14-22 urges the
Corinthian Christians to flee from idolatry. 
Paul believed idolatry was a sin.

This knocks a big hole in the “Paul was quoting a
wrongheaded Hellenistic Jew” argument. 
Even if it is granted that Paul is quoting or paraphrasing from someone
or something else (and no such document has ever been found), he agrees at
least in part with what he’s quoting. 
There’s nowhere in Romans 1 where Paul says, “I agree with this, but not
that”, so either one of the greatest religious writers of all time (in his
greatest work) is being extremely sloppy, or he agrees with everything in
Romans 1, quoted material or not.

In fact, there is a strong contextual argument that Paul
does indeed mean every word of his condemnation of the Gentiles, including
Romans 1:26-27.  In the very next verse
after he springs his rhetorical trap on the arrogant Jews, he says, “We know
that the judgment of God rightly falls on those who practice such things.”

What things?  The
things he just got through describing in Romans 1:18-32.  Logically, the text does not allow for any
other possibility.  The problem of the
Jews is not that they were wrong in condemning the Gentiles for practicing
evil.  Their problem is that they were
right.  In condemning the Gentiles, they
also condemned themselves, because despite their pretensions to godliness, they
were actually doing exactly the same things. 
All of them were subject to the wrath of God because they practiced
those sins.

If the Gentiles weren’t actually sinning in their practice
in Romans 1:18-32, the Jews wouldn’t have been sinning in their practice
either.  Rather than proving that the
whole world is under sin, Paul would have proven that no one is.  The Savior would have come to rescue a world
that didn’t need saving.

In reality, of course, Jesus came to a world in which
everyone, Jew and Greek alike, desperately needed His salvation.  They had earned a spiritual death sentence
because of their sins.  The practice of
homosexuality, though not the only or even the greatest sin on the list, still
appears on it.

I feel for those who struggle with same-sex attraction.  Unless they are willing to join in what Paul
describes as the natural relations between man and woman, they must live lives
of celibacy.  That’s hard to bear.  However, it is the Lord, not I, who notes
that some will find it necessary to (figuratively) make themselves eunuchs for
the sake of the kingdom of God.  As He
says in Matthew 19:12, “Let the one who is able to receive this, receive it.”
