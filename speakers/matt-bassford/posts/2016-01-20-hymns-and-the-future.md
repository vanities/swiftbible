Most people who know me have probably already figured out
what makes me tick.  In addition to the
aspirations that most Christians have (Glorify God, provide for my family, be a
good husband and father, etc.), I have one ambition in life.  Before I die, I want to write a great
hymn.



I’ve written enough to know that this isn’t something I can
do on my own.  I don’t have the kind of
talent that can will a great hymn into existence.  Besides, I believe that when it comes to
hymns, it is not in the man who wills or the man who runs, but in the Lord who
has mercy.  I’m not going to write a
great hymn without God’s help.  However, I
am determined to invest enough time and effort in my writing that if He so
chooses, He will find me to be a useful vessel.

I’m not doing this for the fame (doubtful) or financial rewards (nonexistent)
that accompany the writing of a great hymn. 
If I do succeed, here’s what that will mean.  It will mean that 100 years from now, some
smart-alecky kid is going to see my name attached to my hymn and start making
fun of it, just like I spent my childhood doing to poor Horatius Bonar, Fanny
J. Crosby, and so many others. 
Basically, the personal reputation of even the most successful hymnist
is zero.

I don’t want to write a great hymn for me.  I want to write a great hymn for the
church.  I am convinced that in the years
to come, the church will need all the great hymns it can get.  The decline of religiosity in America means
trouble ahead for all denominations and religious traditions, even if they
aren’t among those most affected.  No
matter how strong your faith, your family, and your church may be, an
increasingly secular society is going to take its toll on all three.

There are likely going to be some dark years ahead, maybe
even some dark centuries.  My crystal
ball isn’t focused enough for me to predict whether that will mean difficulty
for Christianity in America or out-and-out destruction.  If it is to be the former rather than the
latter, though, I think that great hymns will be at least part of what makes
the difference.

There are few experiences that touch the human heart quite
like singing, and there is nothing else in a worship service that does.  I love a great sermon, and I appreciate great
prayers, but when a congregation is locked in on singing a great hymn, it will
make the hair stand up on my arms.  It
makes a huge difference that we don’t merely listen to the singing of great
hymns.  We get to participate in it.  When we do, our spirits are united with the
spirits of everyone else who is singing.

I’ve written at length about my unhappiness with some parts
of the contemporary praise-song repertoire. 
My greatest concern, though, is what the contemporary genre has done to
the tradition of participatory singing. 
Praise anthems tend to be both unfamiliar (maybe even written last week
by the band performing it) and technically difficult.  Both of those things are deadly to
participation.  It’s much easier for
congregants to sit and watch the praise band (or in other corners of my
religious tradition, the praise team) rather than attempting to join in,
especially when they know that few of the anthems will be presented enough to
create familiarity.

There is certainly emotion generated by a skilled praise
band, much like a rock concert generates emotion.  However, neither one generates emotional
connection.  I never was a great
concert-goer, but I’ve been to a few, and I didn’t feel any closer to the rest
of the audience when I left than when I first came in.  The crowd was never anything more than a
bunch of warm bodies occupying space between me and what I did care about.  Without the connection created by singing
together, no praise band can accomplish anything more.

A great hymn, by contrast, is written so that the ordinary
folks in the pews can sing it, want to sing it, and will sing it.  A great hymn does not depend on novelty for
its effectiveness.  Instead, it continues
in use for centuries because it speaks deeply to the soul.  Because it is familiar and beloved, it
renders all who join in singing it familiar and beloved.

This emotional connection is as powerful as it is
instantaneous.  Several weeks ago, while
worshiping with my sister’s home congregation, I was offered the opportunity to
lead “Oh, to Be Like Thee”:

*Oh, to be like Thee!*

*Oh, to be like Thee!*

*Blessed Redeemer, pure
as Thou art;*

*Come in Thy sweetness;*

*Come in Thy fullness;*

*Stamp Thine own image
deep on my heart.*

If you can sing that without meaning it, you are the rankest
hypocrite who ever lived.  If you sing it
while meaning it, it will shake you to your depths.  I meant it, and I had no doubt that the
congregation meant it too.  Even though I
was a visitor in their midst, we understood one another and together communed
with Jesus.

Like a unit of soldiers who bond during basic training,
Christians bond while singing together. 
They particularly bond while singing great hymns together.  Because of that emotional connection,
soldiers will remain united through great hardships.  I believe that Christians will need their
emotional connection too to remain united through what lies before them.

We have great hymns. 
We need more great hymns.  There
are profound Scriptural depths that remain unplumbed, veins of insight that
remain untapped.

Besides, the musical idiom of our day ought to be
represented in our repertoire, just as the musical idiom of days past already
is.  EVENTIDE, the tune for “Abide with
Me”, is clearly a specimen of Victorian parlor music.  To those with an ear to hear it, “In the
Garden” proclaims its *fin-de-siècle*
origin.  “Victory in Jesus” could not be
more Southern gospel if it tried (Not coincidentally, the chorus also sounds
like a really good college fight song.  I
can hear a marching band playing the final phrase as ESPN cuts to a commercial
break after a touchdown).  Why not have
hymns that reflect the styles of today, provided that they are singable and
worth singing?

Ultimately, though, new great hymns must supplement rather
than replace the existing tradition of hymnody. 
I understand the arguments about cultural relevance, but churches that
try to survive by keeping one step ahead of pop culture can only continue that
for so long before they get trampled and left behind.  The hat of even the most insightful church
leader only contains so many rabbits.

Instead, churches will survive, and perhaps even flourish,
by staying essentially true to who they are: 
accessible enough to provide entrée for seekers, yet different enough to
be worth seeking.  Traditional worship
doesn’t offer the things our society values, but that doesn’t mean that its
offerings are valueless.  Instead, it
means that our society isn’t as smart as it thinks it is.  A Facebook thread is no substitute for the
community that humankind has craved since the beginning, and there are few
better ways to build community than by singing great hymns together.

We need more great hymns. 
I want to write at least one of them, but even if I never succeed (God’s
call, not mine), I pray that someone will.  More than we know may well depend on it.
