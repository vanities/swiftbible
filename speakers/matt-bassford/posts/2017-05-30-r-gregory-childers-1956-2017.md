It’s often said that funerals and memorial services are for the living, not the dead.  I think there’s a lot of truth in that, but particularly when I’m delivering a eulogy for somebody I knew, I feel an obligation to them too, to remember them in a way they would have appreciated, to speak truth in love.

Greg Childers was somebody I knew, off and on for years, pretty well for the last year of his life.  I feel responsible, then, for doing justice to his memory, to saying about him the things that he would have wanted said.

First, I think it’s appropriate to remember his character.  Greg might not actually have wanted me to say what I’m about to say.  He was a humble man, not someone to crave the limelight.

Nonetheless, it’s still true that I thought highly of him.  One of his most obvious traits was his gentleness.  He was never pushy or loud.  He was kind and gracious in his dealings with everyone.  He was a man who could be hurt deeply by the bad behavior of others, but anger never came naturally to him.  It made him an easy man to love.

I was also impressed by the character he showed during his long struggle with cancer.  He faced a miserable ordeal of surgery and chemotherapy and radiation therapy not only with determination, but with joy.  When his health permitted it, he would come to church services and sit in the corner of that back pew back there, and even if he never said a word, you could look at him and know he was happy to be there.

The last time I ever talked to Greg was three or four days before he passed on.  He had some questions he wanted my advice on, but as we continued to talk, I asked him about how his health was going.  He replied with this horrible catalog of health problem after health problem, with an almost certainly grim prognosis at the end of it.  It hurt my heart to hear it.

At the end of this crushing litany of struggles, though, he added, “But I’m not complaining.  Please don’t ever think that I’m complaining.”  I told him that I didn’t, and he wasn’t.  It’s hard for me to imagine how a man could continue to count himself blessed even while dying of cancer, but Greg managed it.

Of course, Greg was not a perfect man.  In fact, in many ways, the story of his life is the story of his struggle with his flaws.  Perhaps some would speak harshly of those things, but I’m reminded instead of Hebrews 4:15, which reveals that rather than being harsh, which He has every right to be, Jesus is sympathetic toward our weaknesses.  He was sinless despite being tempted as we are, but because of His experience of temptation, He understands why we are not sinless.  Greg would and did acknowledge his mistakes and his flaws, but who of us is without them?  We too have all the understanding we need to remember him with sympathy and compassion.

Ultimately, though, Greg’s life story doesn’t end with struggle and stalemate and defeat.  It ends with victory, not a victory that he won for himself, but the victory that he won through the grace of Jesus Christ.

The last funeral that I preached was the funeral of my own father.  In earthly terms, he lived a very different life than Greg did.  He was a faithful Christian all his days, while Greg spent many years away from the Lord.  And yet, my father died no less in need of the grace of Christ than Greg did, and Greg died no more in need of it than my father.

Toward the end of their lives, I know that both men came to grips with that realization in their own ways because I talked with both of them about it.  Greg shared with me his regrets about the past, but he knew he couldn’t change those things.  All he could change was the future, to be faithful to God tomorrow in a way he hadn’t been yesterday.  I believe he succeeded in that, not because he suddenly got all of his spiritual ducks in a row, but because he put his trust in the One who justifies the ungodly.

The older I get, the more awed I become by the marvelous beauty of the grace of God.  With each passing year, my own flaws and failings come into sharper focus.  I understand better how short I fall of the glory of God because of my own sin.  Before His perfect righteousness, none of us can do more than cry along with the tax collector in Luke 18:13, “God, be merciful to me, a sinner!”  It is only by the grace of God that any of us can stand.

It is here, then, that we find the final meaning of Greg’s life.  It is a monument not to his successes and failures, but to the immeasurable riches of grace.  Greg wouldn’t want us to remember him as a perfect person.  It wouldn’t be true, for one thing.  But to remember him as a perfected person, yes, he would want that.

He would want too for each of us to seek perfection in the place he did, not in the proud rejection of the law of God, not in the hopeless struggle to justify ourselves, but in the inexhaustible fountain of the grace of Jesus Christ.  Greg drank from that fountain, and for eternity, he will never be thirsty again.  In measure with his love for all of us, he would want us, and indeed does want us, to share that eternity of joy with him.  If we seek our salvation where he did, we cannot fail.
