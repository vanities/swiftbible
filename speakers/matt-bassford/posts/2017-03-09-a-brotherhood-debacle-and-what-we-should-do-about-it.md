It seems like every few months, some cringe-inducing story
about Christians I know pops up on Facebook. 
Yesterday’s disaster was brought to my attention by Dan DeGarmo, who
posted [this link.](http://wate.com/2017/03/06/east-tennessee-preacher-sentenced-to-___-years-for-money-laundering-fraud/) It reports the sad news that Roger Williams, a brother in Christ and gospel
preacher, pleaded guilty to running a Ponzi scheme.  I know Roger, though not well, and I’ve
worshiped at the church in Pigeon Forge, TN, where he was the preacher.  The whole situation is inexpressibly awful.

However, Dan also expressed remorse that he hadn’t spoken
out earlier.  A friend of his had told
him years ago that Roger was committing fraud, and Dan hadn’t done anything
with the information but warn a church off of supporting Roger.  Understandably, Dan has found himself
wondering if he should have done more, despite the very real risk that he would
be accused of making trouble in the brotherhood.



As the conversation on Dan’s post continued, a brother named
Randy Baughn acknowledged that he was the friend in question.  Apparently, his parents had invested with
Roger, but it became apparent to Randy as early as 2010 that something shady
had happened to the investments.  He
encouraged his parents to go to the authorities, but they were unwilling to do
so because of their belief that 1 Corinthians 6:1 meant that the law shouldn’t
be involved.  Randy did not agree, but he
respected their convictions, and he chose to remain silent himself.

One of the sad truths about sin is that it often puts the
righteous in difficult positions.  We
want to trust our brothers and sisters in Christ.  We want to believe the best about them.  We don’t want to gossip or cause
trouble.  As a result, we are often
strongly inclined to keep secrets that should not be kept.

I’ll be plain.  I
think Randy should have made the matter public and gone to the police, and I
think that Dan should have encouraged him to do so, but that’s an INCREDIBLY
tough call.  I don’t have it in me to
criticize either one of those brothers. 
I know full well that in similarly difficult situations, I haven’t
always made the best decision either. 
Let him who is perfectly wise cast the first stone, and that ain’t me!

Having said that, I think this a situation that all of us
can learn from, and I’d like to outline some Scriptural principles that can
help us as we grapple with financial impropriety and other sins that tend to hide
from scrutiny, like sexual misconduct.

**Expose the
Truth.** Paul writes in Ephesians 5:11
that rather than participating in the unfruitful deeds of darkness, we should
expose them.  The righteous have nothing
to fear from the light; it is only the wicked who shrink from scrutiny.  This should, of course, start with us going
to our brother privately.  Randy and his
parents did that.  They e-mailed Roger,
seeking an explanation, but those e-mails went unanswered.

At that point, we have, I think, a responsibility to go
further.  This is extremely distasteful
because it feels so much like gossip. 
With all the benefit of 20/20 hindsight, it’s my judgment that Randy and
his family should have made contact with other brethren at Pigeon Forge and
gone from there, making the situation known brotherhood-wide if necessary.  I don't think it was Dan's place to act openly himself (after all, he had no direct knowledge of any problems), but he should have urged Randy to move forward.

**Involve the
Authorities.** I see where Randy’s
parents were coming from in their understanding of 1 Corinthians 6:1, and I
honor their determination to do right no matter the cost.  However, when the subject is fraud or other
criminal activity, I don’t believe the passage applies.  In context, Paul’s discussion in the first
part of 1 Corinthians 6 is about what we would call a civil lawsuit, and
Christians should look to resolve disputes like that outside of court.

However, a Ponzi scheme isn’t a civil matter.  It’s criminal fraud.  In such circumstances, the fraudster doesn’t
merely owe a debt to those he has defrauded. 
He owes a debt to society too.  As
a result, the text we should turn to is not 1 Corinthians 6, but Romans 13:4,
which advises us that the government is God’s minister for good and an avenger
for the punishment of wrongdoers.  In
punishing even Christians who have committed crimes, the government is doing
God’s work.

We therefore have a responsibility to help it do God’s work
(within the bounds of Acts 5:29, of course). 
A Christian who has knowledge that a crime has been committed needs to
take that knowledge to the police.  If,
God forbid, a brother came to me and confessed that he had molested children, I
would tell him that I appreciated his candor, but if he didn’t turn himself in,
I would turn him in.  Forgiveness is one
thing.  Obstruction of justice is
another.

This is important not merely for the sake of conscience, but
for the sake of other possible victims in the future.  Roger needed to be stopped in 2010 (not least
for the sake of his own soul).  As it
happens, though, I talked to a friend about this sad story yesterday and
learned that my friend was *still* sending
Roger money for support!  It’s terribly
tragic when good, honorable people continue to be exploited because of the
silence of others.

**Trust but
Verify.** As I observed above, we
generally believe the best of one another, especially when it comes to money.  We’re all Christians, and all Christians are
honest, so we’re all honest, right?  That
generalization is probably true 99.9 percent of the time when it comes to
brethren.  Sadly, though, there’s that
.01 percent out there that will take advantage of blind trust.

In this, we need to take yet another lesson from Paul.  In 2 Corinthians 8:21, he explains that in
the administration of the collection for the needy saints in Jerusalem, he was
at pains to do what was honorable not merely in the sight of God, but also in
the sight of men.

Paul knew that his intentions were pure with respect to that
money.  He knew that God knew it
too.  However, it was important to him
not merely to be righteous, but to be obviously righteous.  As a result, he invited representatives from
the churches that had contributed to accompany him back to Jerusalem.  There were multiple sets of eyes on that
money all the time, so that even if Paul had had the inclination to do evil, he
wouldn’t have had the opportunity.  Evil
thrives in the darkness but withers in the light.

In the same way, we should make sure that our financial
dealings with other Christians are exposed to the light.  This starts most obviously with church
treasuries.  No one man should count the
contribution and be responsible for depositing it.  No one man should have exclusive oversight
over the church books.  The preacher should not have primary control of church finances.  Et cetera.  Such arrangements
create too many shadows to be safe.

Similarly, we should use the same safeguards in our business
transactions with brethren that we would in our dealings with people in the
world.  It’s alluring to imagine that in
the church, we’re still living in the handshake-and-promise world of the 1950s,
but even if there ever were such a time, it certainly isn’t now.  If you’re going to buy insurance from a
brother, make sure the insurance company is legit.  If you’re going to invest your money with a
brother, make sure he’s registered with the appropriate state and federal
authorities (which Roger wasn’t, apparently). 
And so on.  No righteous man will
be offended by your desire to take precautions. 
He’s got nothing to worry about.

In conclusion, we need to acknowledge that the devil is
cunning enough to lure any of us not only into sin, but also into outright criminal
conduct.  I pray that Roger will repent
and deliver himself if he hasn’t already, and I know that God will forgive him
as He has so often forgiven me.  However,
Roger’s victims will not be so easily compensated.  Their loss is an expensive lesson, but it’s
one that we must take to heart.  We can’t
safeguard the souls of others, but with wisdom, forthrightness, and courage, we
can limit the damage they do if they are corrupted.
