A few days ago, one of the members at Joliet sent me [this link](http://www.acatholic.org/about-the-catholic-faith/catholic-the-apostles-creed/), which they had received in turn from a friend. 
The linked page contains the Apostles’ Creed, a statement of faith
developed after the writing of the New Testament, with each line followed by a
Scriptural citation that supposedly justifies the creedal statement that has
been made.  The implied argument is
clear:  because we know all of these
things to be true from the Bible, therefore the creed itself is true and ought
to be used in worship.

However, even though I acknowledge the truth of (most of, at
least) the claims in the Apostles’ Creed, I still think that there are problems
with the use of creeds in general.  Most
religious error, after all, contains at least some truth (and often a lot of
truth).  It’s the admixture of truth with
falsehood that makes the error dangerous. 
In particular, here are the problems I see:



**Creeds Replace
Scripture**

We see the pattern for how Christians should handle God’s
word in Acts 17:11.  There, we find the
noble-minded Bereans examining the Scriptures daily, testing the message of the
apostle Paul against them to determine whether he was telling the truth.  This is the responsibility of every believer
before God.  We can’t outsource our faith
to anybody else.  We have to know why we
believe what we believe, and we have to know it from the Bible.  Like the Bereans, we need to test every
preacher, every Bible-class teacher, by what the Scripture says.

Creeds short-circuit this vital process.  Unlike a sermon, a creed does not reflect
“reasoning from the Scriptures”.  Creed-writers
don’t explain how they reached the conclusions they reached.  In the words of my math teachers back in the
day, they don’t show their work.  They
just hand you a collection of truth claims and say, “Here; believe this.”

This can only lead to a dangerous habit of mind.  Once we start accepting that things are true
simply because somebody else says they are, where does that end?  If we uncritically trust the creed, we will
likely also uncritically trust the clergyman who gives us the creed in the first
place.  Nothing in this world outside the
word of God is worthy of that kind of trust (myself included), and it will
inevitably open the door for every kind of false doctrine.

**Creeds Deny the
Wisdom of God**

In 2 Timothy 3:16-17, Paul writes, “All Scripture is
breathed out by God and profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction,
and for training in righteousness, that the man of God may be complete,
equipped for every good work.”  In this
text, Paul makes two primary claims, both of which are vital.  The first is that the Scriptures are not of
human origin, but are instead divinely inspired.  If this is not true, why be a Christian?

Second, Paul argues for the sufficiency of the
Scriptures.  They complete us and equip
us for every good work.  If there is
something we need to know to glorify God and inherit eternal life, we can find
it between the covers of the Bible.  Of
course, the Bible is not exhaustive.  It
does not tell us everything we might want to know.  However, it is sufficient.

By their very existence, creeds deny the second of these
claims.  If the Bible is all we need, why
do we need a creed?  Why not allot the
time spent on creed recitation to, oh, I don’t know, reading and study of the
word?

The only answer the creed-defender can make to this is that
the creed provides something the Bible doesn’t. 
It’s a nice, neat statement of belief, of the sort that the writers of
the New Testament are notoriously reluctant to hand us.  What Christian has not wondered why all the
passages about salvation are not compactly gathered together in one place?

To human wisdom, that’s a compelling argument.  It’s the way we write human legal codes,
after all.  However, the evidence
indicates that this does not seem wise to God at all.  He certainly knows how to write like that; we
see many lists of statutes in the Pentateuch. 
However, the law of Christ does not imitate the style of the Law of
Moses, and we can only assume that an omniscient Being had His reasons for
doing things that way.  The extent to
which we understand those reasons is irrelevant.  When we replace Scriptural inquiry with a
list of bullet points, we cannot help but circumvent His wisdom.

**Creeds Privilege One
Interpretation of Scripture**

To the honest student of the word, there should be no point
of doctrine that is so firmly settled as to be beyond further study.  This is not because we cannot determine truth
from the word; instead, it is because in the search for truth, all of us are
limited and fallible.  When we deny the
possibility that we might be mistaken, that is not the voice of wisdom.  It is the appeal of pride.

However, once an interpretation makes it into a creed (and
all creedal statements are interpretations; otherwise, they would be
collections of Scripture), it becomes settled, beyond the reach of further
study.  It is now something you say and
accept without testing and challenging it.

This is bad enough when the creed-writer is correct, but
it’s a disaster when he’s wrong.  I said
above that I agreed with most of what is in the Apostles’ Creed.  However, I am not at all sure that I agree
that Christ “descended into hell”.

Hades, the abode of the dead?  Absolutely. 
He did die, after all.  Hell,
though, the place of eternal torment for the wicked?  I don’t see that in Scripture, not even in 1
Peter 3:19-20, which is where the Scripture-assigners of acatholic.org find
support for it.

However, as a result of the creed, there are doubtless
millions of people out there who believe that Jesus descended into hell, which
seems to me to be the sort of thing to give aid and comfort to a host of false
doctrines.  It fits very well with the
Calvinist teaching of imputation, for instance. 
Searching the Scriptures is challenging enough without these kinds of
stumbling blocks laid in our path!

**Creeds Usurp
Authority**

Finally, creeds are a challenge not only to the authority of
Scripture, but to the authority of God. 
In denominational worship, creeds are certainly presented as
authoritative.  A creed is Truth.  You should memorize it!

The problem is, though, who supplies the authority?  The Bible doesn’t.  The only authoritative document the Scripture
contemplates is. . . the Scripture. 
There are no instructions in the New Testament for forming a
creed-writing synod (and no, Acts 15 doesn’t count, not unless you want to
claim that the members of your synod are inspired like the men of Acts 15
were).

There are certainly those who claim to be the heirs of the
apostles, which, if true, would make them authoritative.  However, I think it’s appropriate to apply
the 2 Corinthians 12:12 test to them.  If
they want me to believe that they are true apostles, they’d better perform the
signs of a true apostle first.

We see evidence in Scripture that God wants sermons,
prayers, and hymns (none of which, by the way, are inherently
authoritative).  However, there is no
evidence that God wants creeds, which, when combined with the Scripture’s claim
to sufficiency, is evidence that He doesn’t want them.  If the word doesn’t equip us to write creeds,
creed-writing must not be a good work.

Ironically, then, the creed which expresses belief in “the
Father almighty” by its very existence presumes to usurp His authority.  Beneath a veneer of submissiveness, it hides
an attitude that isn’t actually very submissive at all:  “The word isn’t good enough.  God didn’t see that we needed something like
this, so, even though He didn’t tell us to, we went ahead and wrote it
anyway.”

We need the word of God to guide us to heaven.  We certainly don’t need creeds, which, if
anything, can only guide us in the opposite direction.
