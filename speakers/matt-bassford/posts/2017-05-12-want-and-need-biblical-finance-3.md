Every parent of a child capable of speech knows the cringe-inducing
sound of, “But I neeeeed it!”  In my
case, that particular appeal always generates a knee-jerk lecture on the
difference between “need” and “want”.  We
need food.  We do not need a new box of
Star Wars LEGOs.  We want it, even though
we already have enough Star Wars LEGOs to rebuild the Death Star if need be.

I don’t know whether other parents don’t do the knee-jerk
lecture.  However, it’s quite apparent
that there are plenty of adults in the United States who struggle with the
difference between “need” and “want” too. 
Among its other functions, the MIT Living-Wage Calculator [that I blogged about earlier](http://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2017/05/financial-self-assessment-and-living.html) does a pretty good job of spelling out existence in the modern-day United
States at the “need” level.  These are
the things that you have to have in order to function over time in our society.



People who don’t have income at the “need” level are going
to struggle to make ends meet, but there are also millions with incomes well
above that level who have terrible financial trouble too.  Either they’re meeting their needs at a want
level (I need a two-bedroom apartment; I want a four-bedroom house), or they’re
wrecking themselves over things they don’t need at all.

The Biblical answer here is not to adopt a grimly Spartan
existence.  It’s fine to have wants, and
it’s even fine to fulfill them.  Paul
observes in 1 Timothy 6:17 that God supplies us richly with all things to
enjoy.  More generally, the Bible does
not teach that there’s anything wrong with bodily pleasure *per se.* Christians are not
Buddhists.

Flawed though the world is, it was still originally created
by God and judged by Him to be good, and we are supposed to receive the good
things in our lives with gladness.  As
Solomon says in Ecclesiastes 5:18-19, “Behold, what I have seen to be good and
fitting is to eat and drink and find enjoyment in all the toil with which one
toils under the sun the few days of his life that God has given him, for this
is his lot.  Everyone also to whom God
has given wealth and possessions and power to enjoy them, and to accept his lot
and rejoice in his toil--this is the gift of God.”

In other words, there’s actually some truth to the old “I
deserve this!” justification for a shopping trip.  If you’ve earned it, can spare the money, and
would enjoy it, why not?

Wanting is not the problem. 
Fulfilling wants is not the problem. 
Instead, the problem comes when we treat wants as needs, when we cannot
distinguish between, “I need food to eat,” and “I need that box of Star Wars
LEGOs.”

Interestingly enough, I think that when we find ourselves
thinking in this way, we are correctly interpreting our emotional state.  We sincerely feel that the LEGOs are just as
important to us as food.  A want has
raised itself up to the level of need.

I don’t think this means that we have somehow added to the
constellation of needs.  I think it means
that we have identified a genuine need that we are attempting to fill with the
wrong thing.  I need clothing, especially
in Chicagoland in January.  However, no
matter how many clothes I accumulate, they will not satisfy my need for
food.  You can have a shoe closet like
Imelda Marcos and still be hungry.

Out-of-control consumerism represents the same kind of
category mistake.  We are trying to fill
a true need with the wrong thing.  God
has set eternity in our hearts, a yearning for Him.  If we seek Him, we will find Him and be
satisfied beyond measure.  Every earthly
joy is only a shadow of that greater joy.

When we feel our earthly wants as needs, we are mistaking
the shadow for the substance.  We are
lifting it up to a plane where it does not belong and asking it to do work it
can never accomplish.  He who drinks of
the water of Star Wars LEGOs will be thirsty again, but he who drinks of the
water that Jesus gives will never thirst.

Wants are fine, but if we are keeping them at the level they
belong, not getting them will also be fine. 
We can walk away from the Wal-Mart toy department with an empty buggy
and a light heart.  Maybe the time to
fulfill that want will come in another season, but even if it never comes, we
will be content.  In Philippians 4, Paul
reports that even the needs of the body pale in comparison to the contentment
of Christ (though it’s worth noting that Paul was a childless bachelor, which
changes the equation, as he himself acknowledges in 1 Corinthians 7).

However, if we must seek to satisfy those wants, even to the
point of our own financial ruin, that tells us that the joys of this life have
left their proper station.  We have the
fount of living water, but we’re trying to quench our thirst at the broken cistern.  Stuff will never bring lasting
happiness.

Just ask Solomon, or any of a long, miserable line of
lottery winners.  Seriously.  For a lot of people, winning the lottery is
just about the worst thing that can happen to them in this life.  Blessed is he who selects the wrong numbers!

More blessed, of course, is he who doesn’t select any
numbers at all because his heart is free from covetousness.  Whether we fulfill it or not, the lust of the
eye will leave us unsatisfied.  The
sooner we realize that and stop trying to replace God with things that perish
with use, the better off we will be.
