Some days, I think that God gave me my wife to make sure
that I would live a life with plenty of surprises.  I myself never say or do anything
surprising.  I generally consider novelty
to be about as appealing as a bout of stomach flu.  She, by contrast, comes up with all sorts of
wondrous and strange ideas on a regularly irregular basis.  (For those of you who are into Myers-Briggs
typology, I’m an INTJ, and Lauren is an ENFP. 
This means that our marriage can pretty much be summarized by the
cartoon above.)



A few months ago, she sprang one such wondrous and strange
idea on me.  She had just graduated with
her master’s degree in social work, but rather than going out and getting a job
as a social worker (as we had discussed and agreed on for LITERALLY YEARS), she
announced that she would rather spend her time home-schooling our children.

At first, I said “No”, just like I always say “No” to new
things.  Eventually, we agreed to use the
summer as a home-schooling trial period, ostensibly to see whether it would
work, in reality to give me a chance to sort out what I thought and how I felt
about home-schooling.  I don’t like to
make decisions quickly any more than I like to do anything else quickly.  I like to shovel all the information I can
get hold of into my brain and then let it ponderously grind its way to a
conclusion.

This is particularly true when it comes to complex issues,
and on its face, “Should we home-school our children?” is a complex issue.  What about socialization?  What about the soul-crushing
tests-and-homework focus of public schools these days?  What about extracurricular activities?  What about the rising tide of godlessness in
American society?  What about my own
miserable experience in public school? 
Et cetera.

The more I thought about the question, though, the more I
realized that all of those other issues were red herrings.  The purpose of an education is not to
socialize.  It is not to make children
happy.  It is to educate.  With that in mind, I was able to refine the
original question in much simpler terms. 
Did I believe that Joliet Public Schools would do a better job of
educating my children than my wife would, or vice versa?

At that point, the question answered itself.  My wife is intelligent, capable,
well-educated, a skilled teacher, and able to focus her attention on two
children instead of dividing it among 30. 
She would do a better job teaching my children than any public-school
teacher, no matter how well-intentioned, could. 
Therefore, I ought to give her the opportunity.

Of course, education is not the only area of life that is
rife with distraction.  The very
question, “How should I live my life?” quickly spawns a swarm of
sub-questions.  What about this?  What about that?  Most of those questions, though, however good
and pressing they seem, don’t really have anything to do with the larger
question.

As the purpose of education is to educate, so the purpose of
life is to glorify God.  Whenever we face
some dilemma, then, we should learn to re-frame the question as, “What should I
do in this to best glorify God?”  The
answers at which we arrive may surprise our worldly friends and relatives, but
that’s no more than we should expect from people who fundamentally don’t get
it.  Red herrings are red herrings, not
bases for decisions.  If we make our
decisions instead as part of a diligent search for God, we can’t go far
wrong.
