**OVERVIEW.** This is
a big Bible with a cover that’s made out of polyurethane, but is overall
well-constructed.  It offers respectable
value for money.



I got a copy of the Verse-by-Verse (henceforward the VbV) by
asking Crossway for one to review.  They
sent, and I reviewed.  They did so, of
course, without any conditions other than that I review it.  Besides, I don’t intend to keep the Bible
anyway, so I’m not exactly getting a lot out of the deal.

**VITAL STATISTICS**

**Translation:**  English Standard Version (2011)

**Cover:**  Polyurethane (“TruTone”)

**Binding:**  Sewn

**Trim Size:**  6 ½” x 9 ¼”

**Text Block Width:**  1⅛”

**Weight:**  2 pounds, five ounces

**Column:**  Double

**Layout:**  Verse-by-verse

**Font Size:**  9-point Lexion No. 1

**Words of Christ:**  Black

**Cross-References:**  Yes

**Ribbon Marker:**  1

Before I even get into the attributes of the Bible itself, I
think it’s worth talking about packaging. 
Unlike similarly priced Crossway Bibles of years gone by, the VbV isn’t
packaged in a sturdy clamshell box. 
Instead, it comes in a cardstock sleeve.

I think this is a good decision for Crossway.  No poly Bible is going to be a collector’s
item.  Nobody is going to keep a TruTone
VbV in a box and take it out to admire it twice a year.  Instead, people are going to use the Bible
and toss the box.  Why should Crossway
spend money (and pass its costs on to customers) for packaging that doesn’t
really serve a useful function?  I think
the cheaper sleeve does just as well here.

**SIZE.** The VbV is a full-size Bible, no doubt
about that!  Last year, I probably
wouldn’t even have asked to review it.  I
was prejudiced against Bibles larger than 6” x 9”.  However, since adopting the 7” x 9” Cambridge
NASB Wide-Margin as my Bible of choice, I’ve become much more relaxed about the
whole big-Bible thing.

No, the VbV is not going to fit in your glove box.  It certainly won’t fit in mine.  It’s not going to fit in your purse.  Even though it’s fairly thin for its size, I
still wouldn’t describe it as a thinline Bible. 
However, it does fit just fine in the 10-inch laptop sleeve that I use
to transport my WM, so it has that going for it.  Basically, if you want to lug the thing around
so you can preach out of it, you can.  By
no stretch of the imagination, however, is it a carry Bible.

**COVER.** From my perspective,
one of the most welcome changes in the world of Bibles over the past 10 years
has been the toning down of poly Bible covers. 
Yes, you can make a poly cover chartreuse with purple stripes if you
want to.  However, that doesn’t
necessarily mean that you *should*.  Generally, I take my Bibles to church, not to
rock concerts, and in the former environment, a certain amount of
unobtrusiveness goes over well.  Thankfully,
publishers have taken the hint here and are no longer publishing so many Bibles
that look like they escaped from Timothy Leary’s imagination.

The VbV I was sent is a marvelous example of this
trend.  The whole Bible is the same
color, a dappled medium brown obviously designed to look like high-end
leather.  It’s even been stamped with a
subtle leather grain that catches the light nicely.  In fact, I suspect that only a Bible geek
would be able to tell that the cover isn’t leather.  It won’t age like leather, of course, but the
beauty of a poly Bible is that if you wear one out (as a preacher, I will
shabbify one in about five years), buying another one isn’t too expensive.

Nor do I anticipate that the VbV would prove especially
fragile for a poly Bible.  Because the
whole Bible is one piece, it doesn’t have any stitched seams on the cover,
which often strike me as likely failure points. 
On the other hand, like most Crossway poly Bibles, the VbV has paper
rather than leatherette endpapers.  I
prefer leatherette, but at a guess, these endpapers will last as long as the
cover itself does, which is all they need to do.

No poly Bible cover is ever going to be as lovable as a
Bible cover made from high-end leather. 
It doesn’t smell like anything, for instance.  However, as poly covers go, this is a fine
representative of the breed.  One of the
guys I usually study with Monday nights, somebody who isn’t a Bible geek (and
in fact prefers using his tablet Bible), noted that he liked the looks of the
VbV, and it’s a Bible that I would expect to attract admiring comments wherever
it goes.

**TEXT BLOCK.** Does Crossway even make Bibles with glued
text blocks anymore, other than giveaway New Testaments?  Regardless of whether they do or not, the VbV
is certainly as sewn as sewn can be. 
When I open the Bible, it’s easy to see the signature lines on the page
edges.  Because the VbV is sewn, the text
block is likely to outlast the cover.  If
you use it a bunch, write in it a bunch, and the cover falls apart on you, you’ll
be able to get it rebound to save all your notes.

Now that I’ve used the VbV for two weeks, when I open it to
Genesis 1 and set it on my desk, it will stay open, but the cover on the left
side won’t touch the desk.  I would
imagine, though, that with more use, the cover would become as limp as a
reluctant three-year-old.

Interestingly, unlike most mass-market Bibles, the VbV is
printed with the grain instead of cross-grain. 
As a result, it doesn’t have any cockling in the gutter, which means
that the Bible opens silently instead of sounding like an arthritic grandmother
doing calisthenics.  There’s a little bit
of waviness along the top and bottom edges of the pages of my copy (which is
what happens with with-the-grain Bibles), but it’s not enough to bother me.

**VALUE.** According to the Crossway website at the
time of writing, the VbV retails for $59.99. 
In real life, you’d probably be able to find a copy online for $35-40
plus shipping.  By contrast, the
Foundation Large-Print Ultrathin Reference, another larger-print verse-by-verse
reference Bible, retails for $49.95 in genuine leather.  I’ve used both, and I think the paper in the
Crossway is a little better, but the genuine-leather cover from Foundation will
hold up better.

If you’re looking for the single best Bible value
irrespective of translation, the VbV probably isn’t it, but if you prefer the
ESV, and you’re looking for a verse-by-verse reference Bible, $59.99 is a
reasonable amount to pay.

**CONCLUSION.** Yes, it’s a big Bible, but it’s also
well-made for a poly Bible, and it’s pretty. 
My intuition says that the VbV probably deserves about an 8/10 in
Physical Form.
