As I had decided I was going to do late last year, I spent
the first three months of this year reading through the entire Bible.  My sword of choice was the Schuyler Caxton,
so my translation of choice was necessarily the NLT.  Even though I’ve read through the whole Bible
a couple of times before, this is the first time that I’ve done so using
nothing but one Bible and one translation. 
Here’s what I think about both:

**Schuyler Caxton**

Back when I reviewed the Caxton late last year, I thought it
was the best reading Bible I had ever used. 
Now that I have read the whole thing, my conviction has only
strengthened.  Even though this is a
Schuyler Bible, it’s not a Schuyler-developed setting.  Instead, it came from Tyndale, the primary
publisher of the NLT.  I think the
Tyndale design department did a magnificent job, both with the formatting of
the NLT and with the formatting of this Bible in particular.

The general layout of the Caxton is, I think, the best
possible for a reading Bible that isn’t on a screen.  Everybody acknowledges (even people like me,
who usually prefer double-column Bibles) that the poetry of the Old Testament
(and in the NLT, some passages in the New Testament) works best in a
single-column format.  It’s prettier, and
it reads more smoothly.

However, there are problems with single-column Bibles.  Because of all the white space in the poetic
sections, they’re longer than double-column Bibles, which use the page more
efficiently.  As a result, most single-column
Bible designers try to fit more words on each page than they should.  The Bibles so designed generally have too
many characters per line for comfortable reading (at least, for me).  They have big pages with long lines of small
print.

The designers of the Caxton (and its sibling, the Tyndale
Select) solved the problem in two ways. 
First, they put a reference column on the outside of each page.  This left less space for each line, which led
to shorter lines.  Second, they put a lot
of leading between each line, which keeps the eye from getting lost.

However, this solution led to another problem.  It left them with a Bible more than 2000
pages long.  Either they could print that
Bible on relatively opaque paper and have a text block two inches thick, or
they could print it on thin, less opaque paper and have serious problems with
ghosting—text from the other side of the page showing through.  The designers solved this problem by choosing
thin paper but line-matching the Bible, so that text on the opposite side of
the page was hidden beneath the text on this side.

This formula is not original.  It first appeared in the various Cambridge
Clarions.  However, I’ve used a Clarion,
and for me, the Bible simply doesn’t work as well.  The Select/Caxton is slightly taller, which
allows for more leading, which puts it over the top as a reader.

In practice, I didn’t notice any of these things.  I spent 90 days picking up the Bible and
reading the text without noticing the format, even though I geek out over Bible
format.  That’s not the mark of mediocre
design.  It’s the mark of superlative
design.  The layout of the Select/Caxton
is so good that it offers no obstacles between the reader and the text, and I
can’t say that about any other Bible.

There were also a number of little things that I noticed
about the formatting of the NLT that I liked. 
Lists like the list in Numbers 1:21-43 were formatted as lists instead
of as unreadable blocks of text.  Places
where Paul signed an epistle in his own handwriting, like Colossians 4:18, were
set in small caps so that they look like different handwriting.  Sections of the New Testament that scholars
believe were early Christian songs, like Ephesians 5:14, were set in poetry
like the Psalms.  All of those things help
the reader to appreciate the text.

Honestly, it’s hard to think if any design choices in the
Caxton, great or small, that I didn’t like. 
I wish every translation offered an edition in the Caxton format!

**NLT**

My impressions of the NLT as a translation were more
mixed.  There’s a lot that I liked.  I think the explanatory notes throughout the
NLT are generally well thought-out and helpful. 
Generally, I appreciated the NLT in the Old Testament.  Like most Christians, I’m much more familiar
with the New Testament than the Old Testament, and I’m not nearly as interested
in textual subtlety in the latter as in the former.  There were few places in the OT (one of them
being Genesis 22:18) where I said, “Man! 
They really whiffed on that one!” 
Through three-quarters of the Bible, then, the NLT is a smooth reader
that (from my perspective, at least) doesn’t sacrifice much to get the
smoothness.

However, there were times in my reading when I wasn’t nearly
as happy with the NLT, and many of those times came during my perusal of the
New Testament.  Again, the issue here is
familiarity.  I’ve done the great bulk of
my study out of the NASB and ESV.   I’m
used to the subtleties of the text.  The
NLT, by contrast, has adopted a much freer translation philosophy than either
of the above translations.  It sacrifices
nuance for the sake of clarity, but I don’t need the clarity, and I miss the
nuances.

Interestingly, my unhappiness with the NLT was not
consistent from book to book.  I found
the gospel of John to be fairly frustrating, but I didn’t have nearly as many
objections in Acts (with the memorable exception of Acts 22:16).  I chalk this up to differences in
translator.  When the NLT was translated,
the editorial committee parceled out the various books of the New Testament to
different groups of translators.  As the
NLT preface reveals, these translators were charged with keeping the literal
language of the original manuscripts where it was simple and clear, but
clarifying that language where it was complex or culturally foreign.

Of course, no two scholars are going to judge “clear” and
“complex or culturally foreign” in exactly the same way.  My impression is that the John group edited
the text with a heavier hand than the Acts group did.

This is not to say that I think the NLT translation of the
New Testament is useless.  Even if I
didn’t agree with a particular translation choice, I think I benefited from
having to stop and ask myself, “Now why don’t I like that?”  Fresh perspectives are generally important.  Similarly, when my wife does daily devotions
with my children (who are currently four and six), she uses the NLT.  That’s a situation where the added clarity is
important.  It helps those who aren’t as
familiar with the text to grasp its main points.  However, even though I probably will read
through the Old Testament in the NLT again, I can’t say that I will do the same
with the New Testament.

One final element of the NLT is worth discussing:  the choice of the translation committee to
adopt gender neutrality.  Generally, I
don’t think this changes the meaning of the text.  When I read Psalm 1:1 in the ESV (“Blessed is
the man who. . .”), I don’t conclude that the psalm is only about males.  Instead, I take it to mean more or less the
same thing as the NLT’s Psalm 1:1 (“Oh, the joys of those who. . .”).  There are places where I think gender
neutrality changes the meaning (as in Hebrews 2:6-8), but there aren’t many of
those places.

Instead, I object to gender neutrality in Scripture because
it leads to ugly, graceless renderings.  The
work-arounds that the translators adopt almost universally lack the cleanness
and beauty of the original.  Here is 1
John 4:20 in the ESV:

*If anyone says, ‘I
love God’ and hates his brother, he is a liar; for he who does not love his
brother whom he has seen cannot love God whom he has not seen.*

It’s epigrammatic in
its simplicity and punch. By contrast, here is 1 John 4:20 in the NLT:

*If someone says, ‘I
love God,' but hates a fellow believer, that person is a liar; for if we don’t
love people we can see, how can we love God, whom we cannot see?*

It’s an uncertain,
mealy-mouthed mess.  It says the same
thing, but it doesn’t have the same impact.

Translation quibbles aside, I very much enjoyed my 90-day
march through the Caxton.  It produced
one idea for a hymn (and good hymn ideas are rare things indeed!) and a number
of worthwhile spiritual thoughts.  I’m
not ready to turn around and dive into another 90-day reading program right
now, but I may well do so in future.  For
any who are considering it, I would both recommend the process and the Caxton
as its instrument!
