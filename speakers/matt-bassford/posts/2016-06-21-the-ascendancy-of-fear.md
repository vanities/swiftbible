*“And the one also who
had received the one talent came up and said, ‘Master, I knew you to be a hard
man, reaping where you did not sow and gathering where you scattered no seed.
And I was afraid, and went away and hid your talent in the ground. See, you
have what is yours.’”*

-- Matthew 25:24-25 (NASB)

Ours is an anxious age. 
Even though our society is sharply polarized, we all agree on one
thing.  *Somebody* out there is threatening us, and we need to protect
ourselves!  Liberals are terrified of
Donald Trump, his supporters, conservative Christians, and gun owners.  Conservatives are terrified of Hillary
Clinton, her supporters, militant LBTQ champions, and Muslims.

As a result, the national dialogue has taken on a sharply
negative tone.  Instead of voting for
this candidate, we need to vote against that one.  We need to actively defend our rights from
those who want to take them away.  And so
forth.  The things argued for and against
will change, depending on which side of the aisle you’re on.  However, the form of the argument does not.



There’s a sense in which this is hardly surprising.  As social scientists [have demonstrated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loss_aversion), aversion to loss is an extraordinarily powerful force.  Psychologically speaking, fear of loss is
twice as powerful as desire for gain. 
The great mass of humankind regards the threat of losing $100 as
equivalent to the opportunity to gain $200. 
When something that we have is threatened, our whole being rises up to
counter the threat.

However, if the Bible warns us against anything, it warns us
against the corrosive power of fear.  It
is true that the fear of the Lord is clean, enduring forever.  The fear of earthly loss, though, may well be
the most dangerous trial we ever face. 
As a rule, the more important a spiritual concept, the more it is
mentioned in Scripture.  In the New
Testament of the NASB, “lust” is used seven times, as is “greed”.  “Fear” is used in a negative sense 20
times.

It’s right for us to worry about sexual immorality.  It’s right for us to worry about
materialism.  However, the single
greatest temptation our culture places before us is the temptation to fear,
just as the people around us fear.

Sometimes, fear paralyzes us, and we see this clearly in the
case of the one-talent servant.  Despite
his words, it wasn’t really his master he was afraid of.  If he had truly feared his master, he would
have obeyed him.  Instead, he was afraid
of loss.  He chose disobedience over
risk.

Sadly, many Christians make the same choice. They are afraid
and shrink back to destruction.  I’ve
seen brethren fall away because they were afraid of what discipleship would do
to their marriage.  Others fear what life
would be like without their favorite sin, so they cling to the sin.  At one point or another, all of us have had an
opportunity to speak truth but chosen to remain silent because we were afraid
of the consequences.

Sometimes, though, fear animates us to do things we should
not do.  We feel threatened by a person
or group, so we become suspicious of their motives and goals, assuming the
worst about them and taking steps that we consider defensive but that they
believe are aggressive.  We assign the
characteristics of the worst, most extreme members of the group to the whole
group.  All Muslims are like Omar
Mateen.  All rural conservatives are like
Dylann Roof.

Once fear begins to control us, we become more prone to
hateful acts and more vulnerable to demagogues who seek to play on those fears.  “Extreme measures must be taken,” they cry,
“or else this thing that you’re afraid of will happen!”  Most of the great atrocities of history have
been committed by people who thought they were defending something they cared
about.  Fear is why we got the Thirty
Years’ War, the Cultural Revolution, and the gas chambers at Auschwitz.

I don’t have too much to say about fear to unbelievers.  If I were an unbeliever, I’d be afraid
too.  However, for Christians, fear is
precisely the opposite of what we’re supposed to be about.  According to Hebrews 2, Jesus came to set us
free from our slavery to the fear of death. 
Jesus Himself says in Matthew 10:28, “Do not fear those who kill the body
but are unable to kill the soul.”  Even
if our enemies are literally killing us, we’re not supposed to be afraid of
them, because that fear will distort the way we treat them.

Likewise for fear about our rights, fear for the future of
our country, and indeed fear of anyone but God. 
John tells us in 1 John 4 that perfect love casts out fear.  What, then, of the heart where fear rules
instead?  The more we fear, the more we
fall into the clutches of the devil.

Let outsiders be afraid. 
Let them rant; let them threaten. 
For that matter, let them act. 
They can’t touch us; our lives are hidden with Christ in God.  If we believe that this world is all there
is, fear makes sense.  If, however, our
hope rests on the world to come, fear is not an ally, but an enemy.  How terrible it would be if, like the
one-talent servant, we lived lives that were ruled by fear, only to see our
worst fears realized in the life to come!

I don’t think FDR meant it this way, but it is nonetheless
true that today, fear is what we have most to fear.  I know the American Civil War better than any
other area of history.  Fundamentally, it
wasn’t caused by slavery or states’ rights or any of that.  It was caused by fear.  Southerners feared so much that they split
the Democratic vote in 1860, handing the election to Lincoln.  They feared a Lincoln presidency so much that
they seceded and took up arms.  The
effects of fear will be no different today. 
The more afraid our country becomes, the more violence and death will
define us.

Like most sins, fear quite often has an ironic result.  Nazi fears that German Jews were working
against their country led to the ruin and dismemberment of Germany.  Southern fears of anti-slavery politicians
led to the downfall of slavery altogether, in a far more dramatic and
destructive way than anyone 10 years before could have imagined.  So too, of course, for the one-talent servant.  We can’t afford for our fears to make us like
him.
