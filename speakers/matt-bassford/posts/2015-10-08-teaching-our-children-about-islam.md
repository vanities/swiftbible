Over the past couple of weeks, there has been an increasing
amount of chatter on my Facebook feed about how schools are supposedly
attempting to Corrupt America’s Youth by teaching them about Islam.  Pictures of tests that ask students to
identify the Five Pillars of Islam are inducing all sorts of frothy-mouthed
comments.  “What’s next, Sharia law???”



Sometimes, I despair of the human race.  This isn’t one of those times, but it’s
close.  To be blunt, I think it’s a great
idea for American schools to teach students about Islam.  I say this not as a Muslim myself, nor as
some kind of secular humanist.  Instead,
I’m an extremely conservative evangelical preacher who believes that the Bible
is the inspired word of God, believes that Mohammed is a false prophet, and has
homeschooled children.  I control what my
children are taught, and when they’re a little older, I am entirely convinced
that they should learn about Islam, perhaps even in great detail (my wife and I
haven’t exactly designed the high-school curriculum for our kindergartener
yet).

First of all, I don’t think it’s possible to come to a full
understanding of humankind without understanding Islam.  Islam is one of the most important world
religions.  Its impact on the human race is
incalculable.  Trying to teach world history
without Islam is like trying to teach the Revolutionary War without George
Washington.

Second, there are more than 1.5 billion Muslims on the face
of the earth today, and their numbers are increasing, both absolutely and as a
share of the global population.  Islam is
becoming more and more important, not less, which makes it more and more likely
that my children are going to interact with Muslims, in college, in the
workplace, and even socially.  If I leave
them ignorant of Islam, they will be unprepared for those interactions and at a
disadvantage compared to those who are more knowledgeable.

Third, I think that anti-Muslim prejudice is a blight on
American society.  Yes, in the past 15 years,
we’ve seen self-identified Muslims engage in all kinds of atrocious acts, but
the vast majority of Muslims are neither extremists nor terrorists.  Indeed, most victims of Muslim extremism are
themselves Muslim.  To judge the many on
the basis of the few is rank bigotry.

Furthermore, even though the phenomenon of Muslim terrorism
is inexcusable, it is certainly understandable. 
From the CIA-backed overthrow of the Mossaddegh government of Iran in
1953 through the 2003 invasion of Iraq, U.S. foreign policy in the Middle East
has often been high-handed and imperialistic. 
Is it any wonder that inhabitants of the region resent us?  You don’t need a Ph.D. in foreign policy to
note that Muslim extremism is most prominent in the countries of the Middle
East that U.S. intervention has destabilized.

Also, let’s be clear about what the goal of instruction
about Islam is.  When I teach my
children, I am certainly not going to be training them to become Muslims, and
that isn’t the goal of schools either. 
Believe me; religious instruction isn’t that easy!

Are American students so credulous and Islam so impressive
that a couple of hours of classes (usually taught by a non-Muslim) are going to
win legions of converts?  If Islam is
that obviously superior, we should all become Muslims!  Are Christian parents doing such a terrible
job of teaching their children to know the Lord that they are that vulnerable
to novel religious doctrines?  If so, it
is not at all the schools’ fault.

Finally, ignorance of and disrespect for the religious
beliefs of others is antithetical to the spirit of Christianity.  How can we possibly reach the lost if we
don’t understand and respect them?

Consider, for instance, Paul’s sermon on Mars Hill in Acts
17.  Even though the Athenians were
idolaters and Paul was no friend of idolatry, he begins in v. 22 by commending
them for their religiosity.  As Paul
continues, he reveals that he has taken the time to explore their religious
practices and even read their religious poets. 
Even though he is a Jew, he doesn’t quote the Hebrew Scriptures to
people who have never read the Hebrew Scriptures.  Instead, he meets the Athenians where they
are and uses his understanding of their belief system to find common
ground.

Paul didn’t learn about the religion of the Greeks because
he cared about it.  He learned about it
because he cared about the people who believed in it.  As Christians today, our love for Muslims
should provoke us to do the same.

I am no promoter of Islam. 
If every single Muslim on the planet obeyed the gospel of Christ
tomorrow, I would rejoice (would I ever!). 
However, so long as we share a planet with Islam and Muslims, we and our
children both should take the trouble to learn about them.  Knowledge isn’t the enemy of the truth.  Ignorance is.
