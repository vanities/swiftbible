**OVERVIEW:** I liked using the LPPSR as a reader.  It was pleasant to hold, and the large print
was easy to read.  The only objection I
have to it is that the columns have fewer characters per line than most
double-column text Bibles do, which contributes to problems in the poetry
sections of the Old Testament.

At the time of writing this review, I’ve used the LPPSR as
my daily reader for about three weeks. 
Here’s what I think of it:



**SIZE.** The LPPSR is definitely at the upper end
of what I like in a reader.  With a
hand-sized text block and a weight that edges toward two pounds, it’s a Bible
that I notice a little bit when I pick it up. 
Reading the LPPSR is definitely not like reading a paperback, and I
think its designers knew going in that it would not be.  However, it’s still a Bible that I can
one-hand easily.

Having said that, the LPPSR has something important going
for it as a reader:  hand feel.  With its sparsely ornamented spine, brown cover,
and spine hubs, this is a fine-looking Bible. 
As good as it looks, though, it feels even better.  The cowhide of the cover is extremely soft,
with a dramatically different feel than any genuine-leather Bible of my
acquaintance.  Likewise, I enjoy the way
the spine hubs feel in my palm when I’m holding the open Bible.  It makes the weight of the Bible feel
dignified and authoritative rather than heavy.

**LAYOUT.** As is my preference, the LPPSR is a
double-column Bible.  Despite the
“Reference” in its name, it presents on the page much more like a text
Bible.  There is no center column of
references to clutter things up, it’s paragraphed rather than verse-by-verse, and
the few references at the end of paragraphs aren’t much of a distractor.

However, the LPPSR differs from most text Bibles in having
noticeably fewer characters per line. 
The typical double-column text Bible will have about 42 cpl; the LPPSR
has more like 38 cpl.  In prose, the
number of characters per line makes little difference.  However, in the poetry of the Old Testament,
fewer characters per line may well mean a problem.

In all the modern translations with which I’m familiar, the
translators elected to set OT poetry in poetic lines.  Ideally, each poetic line would make up one
line in the layout, followed by a hard return and then the next poetic
line.  This creates a problem in most
double-column Bibles, because many of the poetic lines are longer than the
width of the text column.  The typesetter
has to address the problem by moving the remainder of the poetic line, often
only a single word, down to the next line. 
Those one-word remainders are called “widows”.  They are unsightly, and they interfere with
reading comprehension.

Generally, center-column reference Bibles will have terrible
trouble with widows (the Pitt Minion is the only center-column reference
setting I know of that does not), but even a double-column text Bible will have
some issues there.  At 38 cpl, the LPPSR
falls between the usual text Bible and the usual center-column reference Bible
in line length, so we might anticipate moderate difficulties with widows.

It so happened that my daily readings during the review
period took me through parts of Isaiah and Jeremiah, both of which are
chock-full of poetic sections, so I had ample opportunity to see how poetry in
the LPPSR read.  The verdict:  not bad. 
There were a couple of times I found myself noticing widows, but it
didn’t happen much, and certainly not at a level that distracted me from the
text.

In the LPPSR’s favor, it adopted a layout feature in OT
poetry that I greatly appreciate: 
limited indentions.  Some text
settings of OT poetry, particularly in the ESV and NIV, indent so freely that
the text looks like it has been centered in the column.  This is unlovely, and it surrenders too much
real estate to the left.  The LPPSR,
however, never indents a line or line fragment more than one indention
over.  This does much to mitigate the
problems arising from reduced line length.

Also, the gutter in the LPPSR is worth commenting on.  When I reviewed the LPPSR’s little brother,
the Holman Large Print Compact Reference NKJV, the combination of tight gutter
and glued binding gave me fits.  On one
occasion, a chapter number (Ezekiel 3) disappeared into the gutter so completely
that I couldn’t see it and overlooked it. 
Thankfully, the LPPSR itself has a sewn binding, and the gutter is much
more generous than the gutter in its smaller companion, but it’s still not the
widest gutter in the world.  The gutter
problems aren’t as prominent as the gutter problems in the Cambridge Standard
Text REB, for instance, but they’re still present to some extent.   Another
sixteenth of an inch of inner margin per page would not have gone amiss.

**READABILITY.** Here, the LPPSR reigns supreme.  I’m not aware of another hand-sized Bible in
any translation that has 12-point print, and the slab-serif typeface 2K
developed and used (known, appropriately enough, as Bible Slab) is extremely
legible.  The opacity of the paper in the
Bible is nothing to write home about, but the line-matched setting minimized
ghosting difficulties.

I’m very nearsighted, so I don’t really have problems with
small-print Bibles.  I do prefer, though,
not to have to press my nose up against them in order to read them.  I tend to like Bibles that I can hold at
least eight inches away from my face and still read easily, even without my
glasses on.  I’ll be able to do that with
most thinlines.  I’m still able to read
the LPPSR without my glasses when it’s eleven inches away.  My crazy math skills, then, tell me that I
find the LPPSR  137.5 percent as readable
as the usual thinline, which compose the bulk of my reading Bibles.

**CONCLUSION.** Basically, the LPPSR has some minor
problems as a reader, but it also has some major advantages.  I’m not at all farsighted, but I would
imagine that for those who are, the LPPSR offers a badly needed alternative to
Bibles that are all the size of a coffee table.

Even for those who wouldn’t have to set up a Pitt Minion
across the room in order to be able to read it, the LPPSR is a quality
reader.  Its line length is not
everything that I might wish, but I’d still be happy using it as a daily
reader.  In this respect, I award it an 8/10.  Overall, the LPPSR nets a stellar 27/30.
