As of today, there are 129 days until Christmas and 448 days
until the next presidential election.  I
long for the latter even more fervently than my daughter longs for the
former.  This is not because I have a
favorite candidate (I don’t) and I long to vote for him (I most probably
won’t), but because I’m tired—already—of all the electioneering nonsense.



As I was contemplating this yesterday afternoon, I found
myself wondering how Jesus would fare in a presidential election.  There are several points in His favor.  He could certainly draw a crowd!  All through His ministry, the multitudes
wouldn’t leave Him alone.  He was the
best public speaker ever to walk the earth. 
In John 7:32-46, we are treated to the spectacle of Jesus so dazzling a
band of temple guardsmen sent to arrest Him that they return empty-handed.  In our age of focus-grouped faux oratory,
Jesus would stand out like a van Gogh at a third-graders’ art fair.  If Jesus were invited to a presidential
debate, I’d pity the other guys.

However, even if He were still on earth and eligible to run
for office (the whole natural-born citizen thing would be a problem), Jesus
would still be unelectable.  He had a bad
habit of telling the truth.  It proved
fatal to Him 2000 years ago, and it would be just as fatal today.  Instead of employing the puppies-and-rainbows promises of the politician, Jesus says things like,

* “If your right eye causes you to sin, tear it out and throw it away.  For it is better that you lose one of your
  members than that your whole body be thrown into hell.” (Matthew 5:29)
* “And you, Capernaum, will you be exalted to heaven?  You will be brought down to Hades.  For if the mighty works done in you had been
  done in Sodom, it would have remained until this day.” (Matthew 11:23)
* “You will be hated for all for my name’s sake.” (Matthew
  10:22)
* “If anyone would come after me, let him deny himself and
  take up his cross and follow me.” (Matthew 16:24)
* “You serpents, you brood of vipers, how are you to escape
  being sentenced to hell?” (Matthew 23:33)



That’s just the book of Matthew!  And that’s only a small percentage of the
harsh, inflammatory things that Jesus says in that gospel alone!  I see no reason to believe that Jesus would
deal any more gently with America circa 2015 than He did with Galilee and
Judea, circa 30.

People claim that they want to hear the truth, that they
love a straight shooter.  Generally, the
only truth they do want to hear is the truth that conforms to their
pre-existing convictions and prejudices, a truth that affirms, but never
demands.  I suspect that across all
demographic groups, a real live speaking-His-mind Jesus would be about as
popular as an outbreak of bubonic plague.

This reflection gives Christians an opportunity for self-congratulation:  “It’s a good thing that I’m not like all of
those wordly people who don’t love the truth. 
I love the truth and Jesus both!” Maybe.  However,
before we get too impressed with our own piety and integrity, we should
consider what Jesus says to the self-righteous of 2000 years ago, the
Pharisees.

Matthew 23:29-31 (still in the same gospel, you notice)
reads, “Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites!  For you build the tombs of the prophets and
decorate the monuments of the righteous, saying, ‘If we had lived in the days
of our fathers, we would not have taken part with them in shedding the blood of
the prophets.’  Thus you witness against yourselves
that you are sons of those who murdered the prophets.”

In other words, by acknowledging their kinship with their
fathers, the Pharisees were also confessing a deeper kinship than they wanted
to admit.  As events would soon prove,
both the Pharisees and their fathers liked their prophets the same way—dead.  Both the impulse to murder and the impulse to
build monuments to the murdered came from the same heart.

Likewise, once we start counting ourselves as exceptions to
our Jesus-rejecting society, that might be a sign that we aren’t as exceptional
as we would like to think.  Rather than
rejecting Jesus entirely, though, our great problem is rejecting the parts of
Jesus we don’t like.  The words of the
Lord should never leave anyone feeling comfortable, so if we are comfortable
with Jesus, we have most likely edited out the parts of Him that offend and challenge
us.  The gospels in our Bibles may be
intact, but the gospels in our hearts can have more holes than a colander.

Though the world would never elect the whole Jesus, we must
take care that we aren’t electing parts of Jesus.  When we read that Jesus carried His cross for
us, we find that compelling.  We must
find it equally compelling when He invites us to carry our crosses for Him.
