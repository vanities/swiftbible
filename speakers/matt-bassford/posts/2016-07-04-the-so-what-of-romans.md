Of all the books of the New Testament, few are as
doctrinally significant as Romans. 
Romans is where Paul proves that all people are condemned by their own
sins but can receive justification through faith in Christ.  He then explains how, even though God’s own
people rejected God’s Messiah, everything has worked according to the will of
God.

So, we have this great big proof of the core doctrines of
Christianity, but it only runs from Romans 1-11, and Romans has 16
chapters.  The remaining five chapters
are Paul’s answer to the question, “Now that we have received this great
salvation from God, how should we live?” 
They are the practical counterpoint to the doctrinal explanation, the
application of previously stated spiritual principles.  We don’t have time to make it through the
whole book of Romans in one sermon, but we can make it through this section.  Let’s look, then, at the so-what of Romans.



**Living Sacrifices**

The first section of our reading today concerns Paul’s call
to be living sacrifices for God, not sacrifices in the sense of giving things
up and suffering for God, but in the sense of being devoted entirely to
Him.  According to Paul, the sacrificial
life first involves a certain kind of **BEHAVIOR
IN THE BODY.** Let’s read here from
Romans 12:1-16.

Once Paul explains the overall objective, this text divides
up into two main sections.  The first
concerns our service in the church.  Paul
wants us to grasp that in our work for the Lord, we need to think of ourselves
not as individuals, but as sub-parts of a greater whole.  We are members of one another.  None of us can do everything, but every one
of us can do something.  Every one of us
has a God-given gift that we can use to help in His work.  Maybe we’re good at leading.  Maybe we’re good at service.  Maybe we’re good at teaching.  The list goes on and on.  Our responsibility is to figure out what
those gifts are and then use them to the best of our ability for His glory.

In the second half, Paul focuses not on our work but on our
interactions with other Christians. 
Notice how many times the phrase “one another” shows up in vs.
9-16.  The point is plain.  It doesn’t matter what wonderful work we’re
doing for the Lord if we act like jerks to our brethren.  That doesn’t help God’s cause at all!  Instead, we need to treat one another like we
believe we’re one body too.  There is no
room in the Lord’s church for antagonism or even indifference.  We need to work together, and we need to
reveal that spirit by our care for one another.

In the next section of Romans, Paul addresses the paired
topics of **LAW AND LOVE.** Here, look with me at Romans 12:17-13:14.  If you’ll notice, I didn’t follow the usual
pattern of dividing this text by splitting it at the chapter break.  Remember, the chapter breaks aren’t part of
the original text, and sometimes the guy who did the chapter breaks, a 13th-century
priest named Stephen Langton, broke the text in the wrong place.

I think this is one of those places.  There’s not a hard stop at the end of Romans
12.  Instead, Paul clearly means the end
of our chapter 12 to be a transition to the beginning of our Romans 13.  The key concept here is revenge.  He isn’t saying only that we shouldn’t take
our revenge.  Instead, he’s also saying
that we should leave our revenge in the hands of government.  We can count on the government to avenge
wrongdoing because government is established by God.  God works through human courts and human
laws, as imperfect as they are, to punish the wicked in this life.  Because government is established by God, it
follows that Christians should be good citizens, honoring the government’s laws,
paying taxes, and so on.

However, Paul’s discussion of revenge has another
aspect.  Not only are we to leave revenge
to human governments, but we are also to respond with love, even to those who
have wronged us.  When Christians give
people what they deserve, we don’t avenge ourselves on them.  Instead, we treat them according to the law
of Christ because we are motivated by love.

This raises a telling question.  How can I love everybody, even my enemies,
even those who have wronged me?  Paul
tells us that we are freed to love by the nearness of eternity.  For the Christian, getting upset about
earthly stuff is like getting upset at losing a penny when you’re going to get
a million-dollar inheritance tomorrow. 
Next to heaven, our problems here are meaningless.

There’s another aspect to that too, though.  If our earthly problems are meaningless, so
too are earthly temptations.  We need to
guard against sin because no sin is worth losing our reward.

**Handling Disagreement**

The next main section of Romans tells us how we ought to
handle disagreement in the church.  This
begins with an explanation of **MATTERS OF
CONSCIENCE.** Here, let’s read Romans
14:1-23.  This is a text on which I have
quite literally preached entire sermons, so we’re going to have to move quickly
here, but I still think it’s worth looking at some overall lessons.

The first is that disagreement can arise even between
brethren who are both sincerely trying to serve God.  In this case, some of the Roman Christians
thought it was acceptable to eat meat and drink wine; others did not.  Both, however, had hearts that were committed
to God.

So too for us. 
Whenever we find ourselves on the other side of an issue of judgment
from a brother or sister, we tend to assume that those with whom we disagree
are acting in bad faith.  Brother
So-and-So doesn’t think this is right because he’s a sour old Pharisee!  Brother Thus-and-Such only wants to do this
because he has a worldly heart and doesn’t really love God!  Now, both of those things may be true, but we
must not jump to conclusions.  Instead,
we must believe the best about our brethren until events make it impossible for
us to continue doing so.

Similarly, the way we handle those disagreements must
reflect our love for our brethren. 
Whether we take a stricter view about some judgment call or a more
lenient view, we have to pursue the things that make for unity and peace in the
church.  We don’t get to condemn
Christians as sinners because they do something that makes us
uncomfortable.

The flip side is that when brethren question our liberty in
some area, we don’t get to regard them with contempt.  In fact, we have to be careful lest our use
of that liberty lead them to do something they believe is wrong.  We should be worried not about ourselves, but
about them.

After this, Paul urges us on to **UNITY THROUGH DISCIPLESHIP.** Consider
Romans 15:1-13.  I think the key point in
this context appears in v. 7, where Paul urges us to accept one another as
Christ accepted us.  This is a powerful
idea!  After all, Christ did not accept
us because we were exactly like Him and it was easy for Him to do it.  Instead, He accepted us even though we had
corrupted ourselves with our sins, and the only way for Him to achieve reconciliation
was by taking those sins on Himself. 
That wasn’t Jesus pleasing Himself. 
It was Him pleasing us.

In consequence, Paul argues, we should adopt the spirit of
Christ in our dealings with one another. 
We should ask, “What can I do to please my brother, even when, and
especially when, he disagrees with me?” 
As Paul points out, this is only for our brother’s good and to his
edification.  We must never overlook our
brother’s sin because it will please him if we do.

Also, we must work on developing a spirit of
like-mindedness.  It’s too easy for us to
allow our relationship with a brother with whom we differ to be defined by that
difference.  Instead, we must remember
all the things that bring us together. 
In the church, we are bound together by God, and just as Jesus says of
marriage, what God has bound together, let no man separate!

Only when we determinedly seek unity and one another’s good
can we truly accomplish God’s will.  His
plan from the beginning has been to bring together all sorts of different
people into one.  It is only when we are
united in our work and worship that He is best glorified.

Before the Lord’s Supper, let’s reflect on His role in all
of this.  Christ is our great
Mediator.  He was so determined to be one
with us and see us made into one with His Father that He sacrificed His own
life to make it possible.  In the unity
that we share, He has done 99 percent of the work.  Let’s remember that, brethren.  Let’s remember His love and suffering for our
sakes.

At the same time, though, let’s remember that He isn’t only the
sacrifice for our sins.  He is also our
example.  He didn’t serve us so
selflessly only for our benefit.  He did
it so that we could learn to serve as He has served.  How are we doing in that?  Let’s reflect as we partake.

**Working for the Lord**

In the final part of Romans, Paul examines several aspects
of working for the Lord.  The first half
of this reading is about **HIS
MINISTRY.** Let’s look here at Romans
15:14-33.  In this section, Paul returns
to a theme he had introduced in Romans 1. 
In Romans, unlike many of the other letters, he is writing to brethren
in a city that he has never visited in person. 
As he observes, he’s done so pretty boldly.  I wouldn’t go around writing things like
Romans to congregations I’d never met with! 
As a result, Paul feels it appropriate to present his spiritual resume,
both his divine calling and all the work he had since done for the Lord.  The Romans should believe him not because of
his say-so, but because of his authority from God.

However, even though his absence from Rome has in some ways
complicated his work, he hasn’t been there and doesn’t have immediate plans to
go there.  First, Paul has a trailblazing
spirit and prefers to establish congregations rather than working with
established congregations.  Second, Paul
has a responsibility to carry a contribution for poor saints to Jerusalem.  This is the same collection we’re familiar
with from Paul’s other letters, so from this, we can be sure that Paul wrote Romans
in the middle of his third missionary journey, about 56 AD.  Paul asks for the prayers of the Romans as he
travels to Jerusalem so that he will be able to visit Rome later.

In this section, we see three important lessons about
service.  The first is that even though
the what of service is required of all Christians, the how isn’t.  Paul felt more comfortable working away from
established churches, and it was perfectly acceptable for him to do so.

We must recognize too, though, that service does not require
us to stand on the front lines.  The
Christians in Achaia who contributed financially to the needy saints in
Jerusalem did valuable work for the Lord. 
So too did the Christians in Rome who prayed for Paul to be safely
delivered to them, as he ultimately was, though not in the way anyone
expected!  Not every disciple can be a
preacher, but every one of us can be a servant.

Paul continues in this theme as he describes **SERVICE IN THE KINGDOM.** Take a look at Romans 16:1-27.  Here, he first asks the church in Rome to
help a sister named Phoebe, who is probably the one who carried the epistle to
the saints there.  Based on this text,
there’s some argument that Phoebe was a deacon of the church in Cenchrea.  Because of my reading of 1 Timothy 3, I don’t
think she was, but we shouldn’t allow that argument to obscure the point
here.  Whatever else Phoebe may have
been, she was a servant.  She was a
woman, but she didn’t allow that to undermine her usefulness to the Lord.  The same holds true for sisters today.  There are some things women can’t do, but
they can make a huge difference by the things they can do.

In fact, we see a broad-based lesson about usefulness in the
things that Paul says about the Christians in Rome whom he knows.  Priscilla and Aquila risked their lives to
save Paul’s, and they held assemblies in their house.  Many others are identified as workers or hard
workers.  In the first-century church and
in our churches today, every Christian needs to have a role in the work.

Finally, Paul warns the brethren in Rome against those who
cause trouble and dissension.  He
describes them as hindrances, those who will hamper Christians in their
work.  Even as we work in good faith with
one another, we must be wary of those who aren’t acting in good faith.  Only as we labor with wisdom as well as love
can we be a church where God is glorified.
