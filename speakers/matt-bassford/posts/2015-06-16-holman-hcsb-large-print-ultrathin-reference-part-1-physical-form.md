I got the Large-Print Ultrathin Reference (henceforth the
LPUT) in the same way I get most of my review Bibles these days.  I solicited a copy from Holman, and they sent
one along.



**VITAL STATISTICS**

**Translation:** Holman Christian Standard Bible (2009)

**Cover:** Cowhide

**Binding:** Sewn

**Trim Size:** 6 ¼“ x 9 ¼“

**Text Block
Width:** ⅞”

**Weight:** 1 pound, 15 ounces

**Column:** Double

**Layout:** Paragraphed

**Font Size:** 10.5

**Words of Christ:** Red

**Cross-References:** Yes

**Ribbon Marker:** 2

The LPUT arrived bound in black paper like what Allan uses
to train the yapp on its Bibles.  I
thought this was kind of odd; after all, the LPUT doesn’t really have any yapp
to train.  Similarly, some of the
advertising copy online crows about the “die-cut box” that is great for
storage.  I admit that it’s a nice box,
certainly much sturdier than the Holman norm. 
However, the Bible is too large for the box, so that you can’t put the
box lid all the way down with the Bible in the box.  Another eighth of an inch of depth would have
made the box the right size.  Neither of
these things is a problem; they just amused me.

**SIZE.** Next to pew Bibles and thinlines, the
LPUT is an exemplar of one of the most common Bible formats in existence:  the large-print reference thinline.  In addition to several from Holman, Crossway,
Zondervan, and Foundation also have added to the tribe.  These settings are also popular with the
makers of high-end Bibles.  Allan in
particular likes to rebrand them as “reader’s Bibles”.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEiANCzMaEsIBtndiO_i02F9jJt5jRGXDOSm40qe9AYpfXyN3pxchAsJ_mEv5vhIwEs6TQUzRcm8QOKVZv-RfCh92F0D-kt3F_zeLUn-5DZRBfF_w4R47MoIvluWlEAUpOI0iE6x7nb7ig/s640/PF+size.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEiANCzMaEsIBtndiO_i02F9jJt5jRGXDOSm40qe9AYpfXyN3pxchAsJ_mEv5vhIwEs6TQUzRcm8QOKVZv-RfCh92F0D-kt3F_zeLUn-5DZRBfF_w4R47MoIvluWlEAUpOI0iE6x7nb7ig/s1600/PF+size.jpg)



Generally, all of them share about the same form
factor:  tall, wide, skinny, and not
storable in a glove box.  All of these
things hold true for the LPUT.  I figured
it wouldn’t pass the glove-box test, but I shuffled out to the garage and tried
with it anyway.  Nope.  It wasn’t a terrible, embarrassing miss, but
this isn’t a grab-and stow Bible.  If you
want an A+ carry Bible, you probably shouldn’t be looking at large-print
thinlines.

**COVER.** When I asked for my review copy of the
LPUT, I went for the most expensive version Holman offers:  the one with the black cowhide cover.  Partially, this was because I had already
accumulated a couple brown-cowhide covers and wanted to try something
different.

Mostly, though, it was because I wanted an explanation for
the price difference.  Holman makes a
brown genuine cowhide LPUT that retails for $60.  The black genuine cowhide LPUT, on the other
hand, sometimes marketed as a “Legacy Edition” (though not on the box), retails
for $120.  So. . . what gives?  What does 60 extra bucks get you in a black
cowhide Bible instead of a brown cowhide Bible, besides black paper training
bands and a spiffy box?



I don’t actually own, nor am likely to own, a brown cowhide
LPUT, which makes a direct comparison impossible.  However, I do have a brown cowhide Holman
NKJV Large-Print Personal-Size Reference, and I’ve had other brown cowhide
Holman Bibles.  It’s pretty consistent
stuff, so I assume that a brown cowhide LPUT is going to have a cover that’s
similar to those.

If so, the black cowhide, despite the similar name, is going
to be quite a bit different from the brown. 
The black is considerably softer and considerably thicker.  Holman’s brown cowhide as always reminded me
of a pair of woman’s leather gloves; their black cowhide is much more like a
nice leather jacket.  It belongs at a
different price point than does the brown. 
Also, the black cowhide is a USA print, whereas the brown is probably a
China print.  At least, my LPPSR is a
China print, and I assume most Holman Bibles are.  More on the USA text block later.

When it comes to durability, I don’t see a single failure
point on the cover of the LPUT unless you do something like light it on
fire.  There shouldn’t be much of a
failure point where the cover attaches to the text block, either.  This isn’t an edge-lined Bible, so it has the
standard paste-down binding.  However,
the endpapers are leatherette rather than thick paper, which makes them more
likely to endure.  I suppose that if you
want to cram 15 years’ worth of church bulletins inside the cover of your LPUT,
you can make the binding fail (Note:  in
real life, you should not store anything inside the cover of any Bible.  It’s terrible for the binding!).  Other than that, though, the cover and
binding should be able to stand up to more than a decade of heavy, if
reasonably careful, use.

Also, this is a lovable cover.  As I mentioned above, it is thick and soft,
both thicker and softer than the calf-split cover on my Cambridge NASB Pitt
Minion.  Other Holman top-of-the-line
editions are described as published in top-grain cowhide, and this is probably
top-grain cowhide too.  It’s a pleasure
to hold, and it even has an appropriately luxurious leather smell.  Bible sniffers will not be disappointed with
this one.  As premium covers go, cowhide
or no, the cover of the LPUT is a fine effort.

**TEXT BLOCK.** In one of the reviews of the black
cowhide LPUT online, the reviewer asserted that the Bible has a glued
binding.  I doubted that from the first
time I read it; I’ve certainly had experience with Holman shenanigans with glued
bindings before, but Holman wouldn’t do that to a Bible that they clearly
consider a flagship edition.

They wouldn’t, and they didn’t.  I’ve seen signatures and binding thread with
my own two eyes (even though I spent long enough peering into gutters that it
took my eyes a little while to start focusing properly again).  This is a sewn Bible.  It’s even been printed with the grain, which
makes cockling minimal to nonexistent.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEh2eE_6hSbGIikSquWJNeA9aPpVjNady4ou0oE4Nzir9zjsUU_Hm7iE4GepAq3jlOUvvGHnJvFlIBYRCUx40mzLLFT4ELSjADSdRjdegY_9LO9ECWgLG7ywYuIo1sjq0FMY4Do2J_kPpQ/s640/PF+signatures.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEh2eE_6hSbGIikSquWJNeA9aPpVjNady4ou0oE4Nzir9zjsUU_Hm7iE4GepAq3jlOUvvGHnJvFlIBYRCUx40mzLLFT4ELSjADSdRjdegY_9LO9ECWgLG7ywYuIo1sjq0FMY4Do2J_kPpQ/s1600/PF+signatures.jpg)



It also possesses the curious characteristic of having been
printed in the USA.  That doesn’t happen
a whole lot these days; among my collection of Bibles, the most recent USA
print came off the presses in 2005. 
Jongbloed in the Netherlands claims a hefty chunk of the fine-Bible market, but
most mass-market Bibles are printed in the ironic realm of China, where they
are quite happy to print your Bibles for you, but not for you to come and
preach what the Bible says.

Irony aside, Chinese Bibles have a mixed reputation in the
Bible-enthusiast community, and American Bibles have a stellar one.  In particular, the Foundation Bibles printed
in the States on French-milled paper are the subject of much starry-eyed
swooning.  Oh, the opacity!  At its most extreme, this preference rises to
the level of chauvinism:  “If only we’d
publish Bibles in the good old U. S. of A. again, all of our Bibles would be
wonderful!”  That may be, but I must
confess that I’m not terribly thrilled with this USA print, especially not in a
premium-level Bible.

Let’s start, though, with what I like about it.  The print quality looks fine to me, but the
LPUT is printed in a sans-serif font that wouldn’t really showcase a high-end
printing press.  When I look at the
laser-sharp superscript letters and serifs in a Pitt Minion from Jongbloed’s
presses, they tell me all I need to know about Jongbloed’s technical
skill.  There simply isn’t the same kind
of evidence in the LPUT.

However, I can tell that the print is dark and even
throughout.  This is another red-letter
Bible, which, as always, is not my favorite, but even the red lettering is
quite readable.  It comes across as more
of a Words of Christ in Brown edition, which I find perfectly acceptable.

The paper, however. . . 
Almost as soon as the Bible arrived on my front doorstep, I began
breaking it in, and as I did so, I noticed that the paper had a vertical ripple
to it, like it had been exposed to moisture at some point during the printing
process.  It’s not like the paper was
left out in the rain or something, more like the humidity in a storeroom was
higher than it should have been.  This
rippling is most obvious as scalloping at the bottom of pages in the New
Testament.  I had hoped the ripple would
fade as the Bible warmed to room temperature, but it never did.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEil5F4VaNWTvGh6FZpxut0DnrXPrxbYilV_4QVIc3nGx6wTksZLKhTc04ZospgizbIZUFpAUi7CUw2wsZtYfahDGNs024zOOhMShU1cKxPRR52USiTwzsUPazZt3YswNODaw8xlQgKAiQ/s640/PF+rippling.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEil5F4VaNWTvGh6FZpxut0DnrXPrxbYilV_4QVIc3nGx6wTksZLKhTc04ZospgizbIZUFpAUi7CUw2wsZtYfahDGNs024zOOhMShU1cKxPRR52USiTwzsUPazZt3YswNODaw8xlQgKAiQ/s1600/PF+rippling.jpg)



This is definitely a first-world problem.  The Bible is perfectly functional and
perfectly readable.  The cover is
great.  If this were some $20 mass-market
edition, I would say that rippled paper sometimes goes with the territory.  However, if you’re going to sell a Bible for
more than $100 retail, I think you’d better get everything right.  Admittedly, the paper in the LPUT is quite
thin (that whole ultrathin thing again), but it’s still more substantial than
the paper in my Pitt Minions, and neither of them has any rippling (that whole
Jongbloed thing again).

Maybe I just drew the short straw here.  Maybe my Bible was the one out of a thousand
with rippled paper.  If so, I guess
Holman drew a short straw too, because the 1/1000 Bible went to somebody who
would write about it on the Internet.  If
not, though, if this is some problem with the production process, it’s a
problem that Holman really ought to correct.

**VALUE.** As always, I’m going to judge the LPUT
not just by what it brings to the table, but by what it brings to the table at
its price point.  I think the cover
belongs on a $120 Bible.  It’s not the
best deal in the whole wide world of Bibles, but it’s perfectly
acceptable.  Rippled paper, though,
doesn’t belong in a $120 Bible.

If I were going to buy an HCSB LPUT, I wouldn’t buy this
one.  I’d get the China-print brown
cowhide version instead.  It costs half
as much, and I’ve never had any problems with the paper in other Holman
China-print Bibles.  True, the cover
isn’t as luxurious. . . but it costs half as much.  Why pay $120 retail for a Bible with a
problem when you can pay $60 for a Bible with no problems?

**CONCLUSION.** As I come to the conclusion of this
review, I can’t help but be reminded of my review last year of the Holman NKJV
Large-Print Compact.  In both cases, so
much was right, but there was something that wasn’t.  Of course, the LPC’s problem was a glued
binding, which is one of the single worst traits a Bible can have.  The paper rippling in the LPUT isn’t nearly
as serious an issue.  However, combined
with the Bible’s lack of portability, it moves me to rate the LPUT at 6/10 in
physical form.
