The pleasant thing about studying the Bible is that the more
you study, the more you learn.  The
unpleasant thing about it is that the more you study, the more you learn you
were wrong.  Through the years, I’ve had
to eat crow about a lot of things that I missed in Scripture, but one of my
most embarrassing mistakes has been my misreading of the organization of Mark.

I could tell by comparing it with Luke that Mark wasn’t
strictly chronological, but I missed the organization that was there.  In recent years, I’ve learned that Mark isn’t
a loosely chronological collection of random stories about Jesus.  Instead, the gospel is thematically
organized, with intricate logical sequences that stretch for chapters and are
too complex for me to follow easily.

This means, then, that the stories about Jesus in Mark
aren’t self-contained units.  Instead,
every story in the gospel is a commentary on the stories around it, and you can
only figure out Mark’s point by considering the stories together.  At some point, I have to stop chasing the
connections if I want to keep my sermon to a reasonable length, but even in 45
minutes, there are still valuable lessons to be discussed.  Let’s look at one such lesson, the importance
of absolute commitment to Christ.



**Divorce**

Our text today comes from Mark 10, and its first subject is
divorce.  The story begins with **THE PHARISEES** asking Jesus a
question.  Read with me from Mark
10:1-4.  Unlike many of the other
questions that we see the Pharisees asking, I don’t think this one was intended
as a trap.  Instead, this is one of the
burning religious debates of the day, and they want Jesus’ take.

The center of the discussion is a convoluted text in the
beginning of Deuteronomy 24.  The point
of the passage is that you can’t remarry your ex-wife if she marries and is
divorced by somebody else in the interim. 
However, the religious leaders of Jesus’ day read it as divine
permission for a man to divorce his wife if he finds “some indecency” in
her.  Views about what constituted an
indecency ranged from committing adultery to burning the soup.  This is the text that the Pharisees are
referring to when they asserted that Moses allowed men to divorce their wives.

Of course, divorce is no less a hot topic in our society
today than in Jewish society 2000 years ago. 
Good statistics about divorce in America are hard to come by, but all of
us know that divorce is rampant simply because of all of our friends,
neighbors, and family members who have been divorced.  Most Americans, though, don’t bother to ask
whether the Bible permits the practice. 
In fact, most churches turn a blind eye to the marital records of their
members.

Jesus, however, makes the discussion about **GOD’S PERSPECTIVE** on divorce.  Consider here Mark 10:5-9.  Let’s be careful to note, friends, that
throughout this answer, it’s no longer man who is driving the action by writing
his wife a certificate of divorce.  It’s
God who is driving the action.  It is God
who created mankind male and female, implying that the two should be
united.  It is God who joins husband and
wife together in marriage.  Finally, when
man separates husband and wife, it is God whom he is defying.  The Pharisees shouldn’t be looking to
Deuteronomy 24 at all.  They should be
looking at Genesis 2 and defining their marriages by their commitment to their
Creator.

This tells us, friends, what our marriages are really
about.  Our marriages are not about sex, who
is going to do the dishes and run the laundry, or even whether we have a good
time talking to each other and connect well. 
Instead, our marriages are about whether we are going to honor God.  When in our marriages we conduct ourselves
according to His will, He is pleased, and with that we should be satisfied.  When we do not, or when we unlawfully
dissolve those marriages, He is dishonored. 
We don’t stay married because of our commitment to our spouses.  We stay married because of our commitment to
God, and God is faithful.  We ought to be
too.

We see this uncompromising statement followed up by a
question from **THE DISCIPLES.** It appears in Mark 10:10-12.  The exact question doesn’t appear in the
text, but it’s not hard to infer from Jesus’ answer what they asked.  Like many laws in Deuteronomy, the divorce
text in Deuteronomy 24 appeared to treat it like a legal and civil matter.  However, once Jesus brought God into the
picture, divorce couldn’t be a matter of human legality anymore.  What happens, they wanted to know, when man
does separate what God has joined together?

Jesus’ answer here is stark. 
I’m certainly aware that the parallel account in Matthew 19 contains an
exception, but it does not appear in Mark’s version of the story, so we’re only
going to focus on the rule.  The rule is
that when we join ourselves together with anyone other than the one to whom God
has joined us, we commit adultery.  This
is true regardless of the human legality of divorce.  This is true regardless of the human legality
of a second marriage.  If God has not
consented to the separating of a husband and wife, further marriage can only
result in sin.

This text too is about commitment.  This text too is about honoring God in the
most intimate relationships of our lives. 
If we’re in a position where getting married can only dishonor God, we
can’t get married and still be faithful to Him. 
It is true that for some Christians, following this passage will mean a
life that is lonely, sad, and difficult. 
I sympathize with that.  However,
it is also true that we follow a Master who died on a cross.  “Easy” is not part of the deal for any of us.

Finally in this section, consider Jesus’ words about **CHILDREN.** Consider Mark 10:13-16.  The story here is pretty simple.  A bunch of parents want to bring their
children to Jesus so He can bless them. 
The disciples think this is a waste of time and bark at them to go
away.  Jesus, however, intervenes,
inviting the children to come near Him and blessing them.

In isolation, this reads like one of those Precious Moments
Bible stories, but I don’t think we should read it in isolation.  Throughout Mark 10, Mark structures his
narrative with a big negative example, followed by a smaller positive
counter-example.  This isn’t only a story
about children.  This is the
counter-example to the icky discussion of divorce.

As elsewhere, Mark is subtle here.  He doesn’t spell his point out for us.  However, that point seems to be that the
children are the opposite of all those people who are off getting divorced
without even thinking about the One who bound them together.  Instead of seeking anything else or anyone
else, these children are seeking the blessing of Jesus, simply and only.

When we receive the kingdom of God like a child, then, we
receive it in God-centered simplicity. 
My children don’t have a very complicated mental landscape yet.  They aren’t worried about their retirement
funds or even their college funds.  They
live in the moment.

Mark is urging us to live in the moment with Jesus.  The devil wants to complicate our spiritual
lives, but they’re really very simple. 
Right now, with the single choice before me, am I going to make that
choice to glorify God, or am I going to make it to please myself?  Don’t worry about tomorrow.  Choose God now, and keep choosing Him.  Everything else will sort itself out.

**Riches**

Next, we see Jesus tackle the even more confounding subject
of riches.  This begins with a question
from **THE RICH YOUNG RULER.** He makes his appearance in Mark
10:17-20.  There’s a lot to like about
the guy.  He’s got his eyes on the prize.  Rather than throwing himself into all the pleasures
money can buy, he wants to know what he can do to inherit eternal life.  Basically, he’s the young man we want our
sons to become and our daughters to marry. If his modern-day equivalent showed
up here and wanted to place membership, we’d accept him in a heartbeat.

However, his impressive spiritual resume should not impress
us.  It should unnerve us.  Hopefully I’m not spoiling the story for
anybody, but this guy with just about everything going for him does not end up
following God.  He was 99 percent
committed, but he wasn’t 100 percent committed, and the 1 percent got him.

This is ominous news, because there’s a part of every one of
us that wants to be 99 percent committed. 
We will give almost everything in our lives to Jesus, but there’s this
one thing.  Probably none of our friends
see it.  We ourselves probably don’t even
see it.  Whether we do or not, we don’t
talk about it, but it’s there.  If we let
it linger, sooner or later, it’s going to get us too.

We see how the rich young ruler gets got when Jesus makes **A REQUEST.**Let’s look together at Mark 10:21-27.  Maybe the ruler doesn’t know what his 1 percent
is, but Jesus does, and He asks him for it. 
“Sell everything,” He instructs him. 
“Follow Me.”  There’s an old and
vaguely creepy Meat Loaf song called “I’d Do Anything for Love (But I Won’t Do
That)”.  Well, the rich young ruler will
do anything for God, but he won’t do that. 
Jesus always knew what his 1 percent was.  Now he knows it too, and he leaves with his
illusions shattered.

This is shocking for His disciples.  As so many in our day believe, many Jews 2000
years ago believed that wealth was a sign of divine favor.  There’s plenty of justification for that
belief in the Old Testament, too.  Over
and over again, God promises His people prosperity if they obey and foreign
invasion if they don’t.  Clearly, the
Jews of Jesus’ time had all the foreign invaders they wanted in the Romans, but
weren’t the people who were rich anyway doing something right?

Not so, Jesus says. 
Wealth is a blessing, but it’s more spiritually significant as a
distraction.  I was talking with my dad a
few weeks back about that huge Powerball jackpot, and I told him that even if I
won the 1.5 billion or whatever, I wouldn’t want it.  Right now, I have enough money that my life
isn’t about money, but if I won a billion dollars, my life couldn’t be about
anything else.  Whether we have a little
or a lot, if our lives are about money, we don’t have room for God.

Our positive counter-example for this section comes out of
the mouth of **PETER.** Read with me from Mark 10:28-30.  We always remember Peter for the times when
he blurted out something and got slapped down, but it’s equally interesting to
look at the times when he blurted out something and didn’t get slapped
down.  This is one of those times.  Peter points out that even though he was
never rich, he did what the rich young ruler wouldn’t do.  He left it all and followed.

Jesus doesn’t dispute this.  He was there when Peter, Andrew, James, and
John left the nets on the beach and walked away.  Don’t worry, He tells Peter.  Your sacrifices will be rewarded.  In the age to come, you’ll inherit eternal
life, but in this one, you’ll receive all the family and material blessings you
left behind, a hundred times as much, along with the persecutions that early
Christians regarded as a blessing too.

This is not the gospel of prosperity.  This is the gospel of belonging to God’s
people.  If we leave everything and
follow Jesus, He will take care of us, not least through His people taking care
of us.  There are plenty of things in
this life that money can’t buy but love can. 
If we put God before possessions, He will take better care of us than
wealth ever could.

**Pride**

The third story in the Mark 10 trio concerns pride.  It begins with a common element in this
section of Mark:  **A PROPHECY** from Jesus about what is going to happen to Him.  Look at Mark 10:32-34.  It’s immediately obvious that something funny
is going on here.  On the one hand, we
see Jesus rushing on ahead of His followers in His hurry to get to
Jerusalem.  On the other, we see Him
pulling the Twelve aside and revealing that when He gets to Jerusalem, He’s
going to be betrayed, tortured, and executed. 
Something here doesn’t add up!

I think there’s certainly a fair amount of “Get it over
with” going through Jesus’ mind at this point, but that’s not really what’s
driving Him.  Instead, He is animated by
His love for God and His love for others. 
He knows that only if He goes to Jerusalem will God be glorified and
mankind saved.  Even if the price for
that is His own suffering and death, He is eager to accomplish it.  His heart is so filled with love for others
that He has no room for consideration of Himself.

Quickly, though, we move from the sublime to the ridiculous
as **JAMES AND JOHN** ask a
question.  The resulting dialogue appears
in Mark 10:35-40.  This has to be one of
the most bizarre moments in the entire Bible. 
Jesus has just finished saying that He’s going to be taken and killed,
but James and John come trotting up to Him and ask that they be set at His left
and right hands when He is glorified! 
It’s like Jesus was the teacher in the Peanuts TV specials.  James and John heard sound, but clearly they
failed to grasp any of Jesus’ meaning.

Jesus’ mind, of course, is filled with the cross, and He
must have been amazed by their cluelessness. 
He starts off by telling them that they’re clueless and then asks them
whether they are able to receive what He’s about to get.  They remain clueless, so they unhesitatingly
affirm that they are, thereby assigning themselves to future martyrdom for the
glory of God.  Jesus promises them that
they’ll get it, but withholds the places of honor that are not His to grant.

It’s easy for us to wag our fingers at clueless, selfish
James and John.  However, before we get
too righteoused up, we’d better make sure we’re not making the same
mistake.  Whom do we think our lives are
about?  Do we think like Jesus did, and
give our lives over for the sake of God and others?  Or, instead, like James and John, do we claim
discipleship while really still looking out for Number One?  It’s not at all uncommon for people to think
they’re in the first group while actually being in the second.  We need to examine ourselves carefully to
make sure we aren’t.

Finally, though, we come to Jesus’ discussion of **GREATNESS IN THE KINGDOM.** Let’s look here at Mark 10:41-45.  It begins with one of the most grimly amusing
passages in the Bible.  James and John
have now acquired martyrdom for themselves, but the other disciples are as
clueless as they are.  They get upset
because they think the brothers have pulled a fast one on everybody else!

Jesus, though, explains what His kingdom is all about.  Unlike every other kingdom that has existed
before or since, the kingdom of Christ isn’t about people climbing on top of
each other, all trying to get ahead, so that once we get ahead, we can tell
everybody else what to do.  Instead, the
kingdom of Christ is about service, the humbler, the better.  We don’t show our greatness by climbing on
top of people.  We show it by getting
under people and helping them rise.  Our
example here, of course, is Jesus, who as He speaks, is on His way to the greatest
act of self-sacrifice of all time.  For
those who want to follow Him, the measure of servanthood is the cross.

Before the Lord’s Supper, let’s consider the cross and see
how we measure up.  How low Jesus was
willing to descend!  How much He was
willing to give up!  He didn’t suffer and
die because He had to.  He suffered and
died because He wanted to, because He loved us and wanted to help.  If we’re following Him, that means that our
lives need to be about loving and wanting to help.  Are they? 
Let’s examine our hearts as we partake.
