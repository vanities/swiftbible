Last week, Jonathan Aigner put up [this blog post](http://www.patheos.com/blogs/ponderanew/2016/07/05/modernized-hymns-hymns-or-contemporary-songs-with-old-words/) about the practice in some circles of singing traditional hymns in contemporary
style.  He objects because a hymn
presented performance-style still does not involve the congregation.  I’ll go even farther down that road than he
will (if you really want to involve the congregation, why not get rid of the
instruments?), but I’m in general agreement.

However, [one of the YouTube videos](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0U4AEekG7Y8) which he embedded particularly piqued my interest and
aroused my concern.  Let me start off by
acknowledging that I see a lot that I like here.  Contrary to Aigner’s thesis, the worship
leader does indeed seem to be making an effort to get the congregation singing.  I don’t know how successful he is in
this—I’ve got a suspicion that all the enthusiastically singing people in the
video are members of the choir who happen to not be wearing robes—but at least
he’s trying.



Likewise, I have no problem whatsoever with “O God, Our Help
in Ages Past”.  From my perspective, if
you’re going to use instruments, you might as well use a band as an organ.  Even the 1998 addition of a chorus to the
hymn isn’t terrible.  We’re not talking
an addition on the level of “We’re Marching to Zion”, but at least there’s
Biblical content there.  I’m never going
to object to singing about God as my shield and helper.

That introduction, though. 
Ouch.  As best as I can tell,
here’s what the worship leader is saying:

“It is great to see you this morning!  Is everybody feeling good?  We’re gonna have a lot of fun today!  Why don’t you go ahead and stand up and put
your hands together and worship the Lord? 
We’re going to start off with this old hymn that we’ve been learning
over the past few months.  It’s a ton of
fun to sing—“O God, You Are My Help in Ages Past”!  Let’s sing it together, all right?”

It’s pretty obvious what his goal is, isn’t it?  He wants to get his crowd involved so that
they will feel good and have fun.  Even
though I’m referring to him as the worship leader, it’s apparent that his
object is only secondarily worship.  It’s
primarily about making people happy.

This is a problem that can be apparent on both sides of the
worship wars.  Some people advocate for
praise-and-worship songs because they like them.  Others advocate for traditional hymns because
they like them.  I think both of those
attitudes are objectionable because they put the focus in the wrong place.

I don’t care what genre you prefer.  No worship leader should be calling the
congregation together with an injunction to have fun.  Instead, he should be saying things like,
“Come; let us kneel and bow down before the Lord our Maker.”  Worship is fundamentally not about us.  It’s about God.

If our worship is truly God-centered instead of
self-centered, that will change the kind of questions we ask and choices we
make.  If our “worship” is about
self-indulgence, musical style becomes a central consideration.  When I’m sitting at my computer in my office,
I don’t listen to music I don’t like. 
It’s all about me.  If I’m
concerned primarily about myself in worship, I will also insist on musical
styles that I don’t like, and if I don’t get what I like, that’s a powerful
incentive to go “worship” someplace else.

However, if glorifying God is my primary consideration,
suddenly my taste in music becomes vastly less important.  If I can glorify God by singing music I don’t
care for, I still ought to do that.  What
matters is not that I go home humming some catchy little ditty.  What matters is that I go home with the
knowledge that I have honored my Creator.

This is not the way that modern American society thinks, and
it’s not the way that many music ministries think.  We’ve all heard the saying, “The customer is
always right,” and many churches treat the congregant as the customer.  They adjust everything in their worship to
satisfy him, never considering that instead they ought to be teaching him that
his satisfaction is not significant.

It’s certainly true that God-centered worship will be less
popular than customer-centered worship. 
Many people are not going to be interested in seeking God’s glory
instead of their own musical preferences. 
However, these same people are also not going to be terribly interested
in seeking God’s glory instead of their own preferences in any area of life.  Their self-centered attitude toward worship
is part and parcel of a self-centered attitude toward existence (and plenty of
churches cater to this as well).  I don’t
have too much to say to people like that. 
I hope they enjoy their journey toward the wide gate, is all.

By contrast, those who pursue God-first worship will find
many unexpected joys on the way. 
One of the central paradoxes of Christianity is that the more we give,
the more we get.  When I step outside of
my comfort zone to help someone in need, I invariably find that I am the one I
am helping.  Some of the most meaningful,
rewarding relationships of my life have been with people I befriended not
because they were people I particularly wanted to be friends with, but because
I wanted to shine the love of Christ in their lives.  As somebody a lot smarter than I am once
said, “Whoever who loses his life for My sake will find it.”

The same is true of losing ourselves in worship.  I enjoy listening to music on my own, but the
joy of pouring out my heart before the Almighty is incomparably greater.  It is a soul-stirring, life-changing
experience.  No self-centered
satisfaction can possibly measure up. 
Ironically, when we pursue self-centeredness at the expense of worship
in spirit and truth, we are cheating ourselves of one of the sweetest gifts
that the Father gives His children.

I agree that we should be goal-oriented in our worship.  We should be putting the customer first.  However, we must be careful to remember who
is the Master and who is the servant.
