Last Wednesday, the largest Powerball jackpot in history was
finally claimed.  Though we don’t know
yet who they are, there were three winners/groups of winners.  The millions of other Americans who played
were, quite literally, the losers.

Much to my surprise, I’ve seen very little from the usual
suspects about the moral problems behind all of this.  Many Christians seem to believe that as long
as you weren’t using the rent money to buy tickets, playing the lottery is a
perfectly acceptable thing.



I don’t believe Jesus agrees with that analysis.  Consider His words in Luke 12:13-15 (NASB):

*Someone in the crowd
said to Him, "Teacher, tell my brother to divide the family inheritance
with me." But He said to him, "Man, who appointed Me a judge or
arbitrator over you?" Then He said to them, "Beware, and be on your
guard against every form of greed; for not even when one has an abundance does
his life consist of his possessions."*

Every form of greed, indeed. 
In times past, religious condemnation of gambling has centered on the
fact that gambling involves “games of chance”. 
To be blunt, I think that’s a terrible argument.  There’s nothing inherently spiritual or not
about risk.  In Romans 16:4, Paul
commends Priscilla and Aquila, “who for my life risked their necks”.
(NASB)  Doesn’t look like much of a
spiritual defect, does it?

The problem with gambling is not that it is a game of
chance.  It is that gambling is a game of
greed.  This analysis is complicated
somewhat by the vague nature of covetousness and greed.  If you look them up in the dictionary, you
get definitions like, “excessive or rapacious desire, especially for wealth or
possessions”.  Thanks a bunch,
dictionary!  What’s excessive?  When it comes to desire for wealth, how much
is too much?

This is a question that can only be answered by the Bible’s
foundational commandment to love.  Paul
tells us in Romans 13:8 that the one who loves his neighbor has fulfilled the
law.  Lawbreaking, then, is a failure to
love.  If I love money more than I love
my neighbor, I have surrendered to covetousness.

Covetousness can arise in any area of our lives that deals
with money, but it’s a particular problem in gambling.  Gambling is what is called a zero-sum
game.  It has a winner and a loser, and
the winner can only win to the extent that the loser loses.

This is not the case in most economic transactions.  Let’s say, for instance, that I go to
Wal-Mart and buy a pair of jeans for $20. 
That’s a positive-sum game. 
Instead of a winner and a loser, there are two winners.  I wanted the jeans more than I wanted the
$20, and I got what I wanted.  Wal-Mart
wanted the $20 more than they wanted the jeans, and they got what they wanted
too.  Everybody’s happy!

However, if I take the tag off a $10 pair of jeans, attach
it to the $20 pair of jeans, and fool the cashier, I’ve created a winner and a
loser.  I got what I wanted—jeans for
cheap—but Wal-Mart didn’t get what it wanted. 
They wanted $20 for the jeans, but they only got $10.  If I pull a stunt like that, I’ve given in to
greed.

We can think of other free-market transactions that involve
winners and losers, but gambling always does. 
Let’s look at this in the simplest setting possible.  Let’s say that my friend Brian and I are
playing poker for money, and we are playing for enough that we care about the
outcome.  One of us is going to win
(Brian), but one of us is going to lose (me; I’m a terrible poker player).  Brian cannot win unless I lose, so he cannot
possibly have my best interests at heart in sitting down to play.  Neither one of us, in fact, is loving our
neighbor as ourselves.  It is true that
my desire to take advantage of Brian has been frustrated, but I still loved
money more than I loved him.  Both of us
are guilty of sin.

A lottery has millions more players and a middleman besides,
but the essential nature of the activity does not change.  With a few strange exceptions, everybody who
buys a lottery ticket wants to win, and they want everybody else to lose.  Even the three winners of the Powerball
jackpot wish that the other two winners had lost.  That would have meant $1.5 billion for them,
instead of a mere $500 million!

It’s true that a lottery means that you are only wishing a
little harm on your neighbor for the sake of money.  So?  A
white lie is still a lie.  Drunk once a
year on New Year’s Eve is still drunk.  A
little covetousness is still covetousness. 
The perceived small scale of a sin does not make it less evil.

Even if most people
haven’t worked through this analysis, I think that most gamblers sense that
somewhere there’s something wrong with gambling.  This is evident in the nearly universal claim
among lottery-ticket purchasers that they’re going to donate at least a part of
the proceeds to charity.

Why?  I never hear
anybody who gets a new job proclaim that he’s going to donate part of his first
paycheck to charity.  People understand
that when they work for a living, they’re doing a good thing and have a right
to the money they earn.  By contrast,
they sense that the lottery winner has not done a good thing and does not have
a right to his winnings.  Underneath it
all, the promise to donate gambling winnings to charity is actually a promise
to atone for doing wrong, to play Robin Hood with money earned by the other
ticket purchasers.

The bottom line is this: 
It’s OK to gamble, so long as you are indifferent to whether you win or
lose money, or actively prefer to lose.  However,
when we gamble to enrich ourselves at the expense of others, we have fallen
prey to covetousness.  Greed is wrong,
and there should be no place for it in the heart of the disciple of Jesus.
