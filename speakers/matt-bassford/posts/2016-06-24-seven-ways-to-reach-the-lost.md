A couple weeks back, I ran across [this blog post](http://www.churchleaders.com/outreach-missions/outreach-missions-articles/163098-what-non-christians-really-think-about-christians-thom-rainer.html) by evangelism expert Thom Rainer.  Unlike
the rest of us, he’s interviewed thousands of non-Christians about their
attitudes toward Christians, and he distilled those attitudes down into seven
views that non-Christians commonly hold.

The original article is well worth a read, but I wanted to
take Rainer’s reasoning a step further. 
If we accept that these are, indeed, seven views that non-Christians
commonly hold, here are seven things that we Christians ought to do about it:



**Be joyful.** Rainer’s research reveals that many
non-Christians believe that Christianity is about being against things instead
of being for them.  If true, this shows
that many Christians either don’t understand what the gospel is about or do a
rotten job of communicating their understanding.

Sure, I spend plenty of time preaching
against sin, but fundamentally, Christianity isn’t a faith of negation.  It’s a faith of affirmation.  As Paul says in 2 Corinthians 1:20, all the
promises of God find their Yes in Christ. 
Christianity is about saying “Yes”.

It’s about saying yes to a relationship with a wonderful
Creator.  It’s about saying yes to a new
life and forgiveness of sins.  It’s about
saying yes to a community of flawed but hopeful brethren who will journey with
you.  It’s about saying yes to
discipleship, to being conformed to the image of Christ.  It’s about saying yes to the hope of eternal
life.

I simply don’t go through my days thinking about all the
“fun” stuff I don’t get to do because I’m a Christian.  I don’t want to be a non-Christian.  My faith gives my life meaning and joy it
could obtain from no other source.  Being
a Christian is good!  Others must be able
to see that in us.

**Be friendly.** Rainer reports that most non-Christians
would like a Christian friend, someone who is willing to be open about his
faith.  I suspect that this statement
could be made even more general.  Most
non-Christians would like friends, period. 
We live in an increasingly lonely, isolated world where the vast
majority of people’s friends appear only online.  Every one of us knows people who are
lonely.

Let’s reach out to them, not because we’re offering them the
gospel as a *quid pro quo* for
friendship, but because Christians are supposed to be warm, friendly, loving
people.  If we aren’t, there’s something
wrong.  And no, introverts are not exempt
from this!  [I've written](http://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2014/08/evangelism-for-introverts.html) about introverts
and evangelism before.  All of us have a role to play in making the
love of Christ real in the lives of others.

**Be
knowledgeable.** Many non-Christians
would like the opportunity to learn about the Bible from a Christian, quoth
Rainer.  If they’re brave enough, they’ll
venture into a church to do this (and I’m thankful for the brave souls who show
up at the Joliet church in search of the gospel!), but many would rather learn
the truth from an ordinary saint in a low-key setting.

Of course, we can’t teach if we don’t know.  How many Christians would feel comfortable
running a small, personal Bible study, one in which they may be called to
answer honest questions using only their knowledge of the word?  I can’t help but wonder if a lot of
Christians are afraid of evangelism because they know they’re not prepared for
the challenge.

Sitting in a pew certainly won’t get us there.  Instead, we have to put in the time
ourselves, with daily reading and study. 
My dad never worked as a preacher (and my mom certainly didn’t!), but I
can remember both of them leading studies with outsiders and new Christians
throughout my childhood.  Both of them
knew the Bible, so they weren’t afraid to share it.

**Be holy.** Rainer notes that many non-Christians
think that Christians live basically the same way that they do.  If this is true of us, it is a serious
indictment indeed!  God has called us to
be holy in all our behavior.  We’re not
simply supposed to be different from the world. 
We’re supposed to be dramatically different from the world.

This starts with the surface stuff.  We can’t talk like the world talks.  We can’t dress like the world dresses.  We can’t enjoy the sleazy entertainment that
the world enjoys.

However, being holy isn’t only about language and dress and
entertainment.  Holiness should define
our character, from the heart out. 
Holiness means that we belong to God. 
It means that we are gracious where the world is vicious, kind where the
world is uncaring, forgiving where the world is vengeful, and honest where the
world is deceitful.  All of us will
always have our struggles with sin, but if we don’t shine like stars in the
darkness around us, something has gone dreadfully wrong.

**Be helpful.** Rainer has found that many non-Christians
would like help from Christians in sorting out their messed-up families.  To many worldly people, the
white-picket-fence life that I experienced growing up and continue to enjoy
through adulthood seems like an impossible ideal.  However, it’s an ideal that anyone can claim
for themselves if only they are willing to follow Jesus.

To worldly wisdom, the godly practice of self-sacrificing
love seems like foolishness.  If I’m
constantly putting others in my family first, when am I going to get mine?  However, as any committed Christian can
attest, God’s way works because it’s better. 
The more I live for myself, the more impoverished my life becomes.  Conversely, the more I live for others, the
richer it grows.  We have the wisdom that
the world needs when it comes to marriage and the family, and we need to be
eager to share it.

**Be honest.** Rainer says that a lot of non-Christians
think that Christians try to put on a mask and pretend that they have no
problems.  I think this is a
fundamentally deceitful tendency.  [I've written](http://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2016/06/hymns-and-advertising-culture.html) about the problems it causes in our worship, and it causes similar problems in our lives.

It is better to be a Christian, but it is not better because
God puts a force-field bubble around us and protects us from trouble!  Every Christian in the Bible had to face
trials, and usually their trials were a lot worse than ours are.  When we’re going through hard times, we need
to do what they did.  We need to own the
struggle (like Paul reporting on other Christians abandoning him in 2 Timothy
4) while still glorifying the God who gets us through it.

**Be welcoming.** According to Rainer, many non-Christians
would be interested in coming to a church service if only a Christian would
invite them.  However, my experience is
that people are much more likely to show up at services out of the blue than
they are because another Christian brought them.  Most visitors are so desperate for the gospel
that they will brave a roomful of strangers in order to hear it.

That seems pretty extreme to me.  I’m not sure I’d be that brave if I were an
outsider!  However, the existence of
these people leads me to suspect that there are a lot more people out there who
aren’t self-starters but would attend if nudged.

Christians need to be willing to supply nudges.  I can’t know for sure, obviously, but I get
the sense that many Christians aren’t willing to do that.  I don’t know why that is.  Fear? 
Embarrassment?  Apathy?  I suspect that as far as the devil is
concerned, any excuse will do.

This is a shame.  As
Christians, we have something amazing.  We
have the opportunity to know Christ, and Christ is the very best thing there
is.  There are all kinds of folks out
there who would see how amazing it is too, but they need somebody to tell them
about it first.  Let’s make sure that
when people need us, we aren’t keeping silent.
