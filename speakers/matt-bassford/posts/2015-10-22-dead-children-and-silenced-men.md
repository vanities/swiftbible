Last Tuesday, I ran my first post about Macy.  I knew that October was Pregnancy Loss and
Infant Death Awareness Month (a dreadfully cumbersome title for a dreadful
subject!), so I ran a Google search for some kind of logo that I could use for
a top-of-the post image.

I found absolutely nothing that I wanted to use.  Every logo that Google pulled up looked, for
lack of a better term, girly.  There were
lots of pinks and baby blues.  Even the
designs felt feminine—ribbons, women’s-restroom silhouettes, and so forth.  I didn’t see a single image that looked like
it was approaching the death of a child from a masculine perspective.



Symbols are powerful, and sometimes, the absence of a symbol
is even more powerful.  Those
masculine-themed child-loss logos don’t exist because men don’t talk (are
expected not to talk?) about losing a child. 
This is not because men don’t care. 
Believe me; we care.  Instead, I
think it’s because the two-weeks-and-move-along code of silence under which
women suffer is even more stringent when it comes to men.  Man up and get over it, ideally before the
funeral starts.

This, combined with the less communicative nature of men in
general (studies have found that women utter around 20,000 words a day; with
men, it’s more like 7,000), leaves us tongue-tied when it comes to a subject of
consuming importance to us.  Usually, the
conversations I’ve had with other men who have lost children are train-wreck
awkward.  For instance, about a year ago,
I was leading a small-group men’s Bible study when one of the attendees, a new
Christian, revealed that about 10 years ago, one of his children had been
stillborn.

I’ve conducted lots of studies and classes over the past 15
years.  I couldn’t begin to tell you how
many off-the-wall comments and questions I’ve had to field.  However, that one took me aback, even though
I, of all people, should have been prepared to address the topic.  The best I could do was to reply with a
halting account of Macy’s death and observe, with utter inadequacy, “It wasn’t
exactly an experience I’ll treasure forever.” 
After that, we all stared at our Bibles for 10 seconds until I could
figure out a way to change the subject.

The problem here is not that men are unemotional creatures
who neither have strong emotions nor (especially) talk about them.  The problem is not that men aren’t
women.  It is that men ought to have some
culturally appropriate way of dealing with and navigating through tragedy, and
right now, we don’t seem to have one.

Make no mistake; this is a cultural rather than a
Y-chromosome issue.  The New Testament is
positively filled with instances of men weeping.  Jesus wept at the tomb of Lazarus.  Peter wept when he denied Jesus for the third
time.  The Ephesian elders wept when Paul
told them they wouldn’t see him again. 
Luke and Paul’s other companions wept when they couldn’t talk him out of
going to Jerusalem.  Paul himself wept at
the thought of former brethren who had become enemies of the cross of
Christ.  That’s just off the top of my
head; I’m sure there are other examples.

Clearly, Christians in the first century, and probably
people in the first century generally, saw no conflict between manhood and
being overcome by strong emotion.  From
somewhere, though (blame the English, perhaps?), we’ve gotten the idea that
real men choke it down and bottle it up, so that in place of emotionally
freighted conversations, we have only awkward silences.

If you’re a man, and child loss is something you’ve gone
through or are going through, you have my permission not to be silent.  Yes; it’s different for a man than for a
woman.  Losing Macy certainly didn’t take
me the same way it did Lauren.  More on
that later, maybe.

But it’s still your child, and that’s not something you can
handle by holding it inside.  Talk about
it, even if you don’t know the words, even if you feel stupid for trying.  Cry about it. 
Maybe even talk about crying about it. 
For that matter, if it feels right for you to do a Job 2:13 and sit with
a few friends for a week without saying a word, then do that.  Sometimes not talking is talking too (though
Job and his friends certainly got around to the talking part).

Whatever you do, though, don’t pretend that nothing has happened.  Nobody can solve an absence by introducing
another absence.
