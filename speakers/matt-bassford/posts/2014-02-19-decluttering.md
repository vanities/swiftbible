*Then He said to them,
"Beware, and be on your guard against every form of greed; for not even
when one has an abundance does his life consist of his possessions."*

-- Luke 12:15 (NASB)

The other day, my wife was attempting, with little success,
to get our children to pick up their toys in the living room.  She warned them that if they continued to
disobey, she would pick up all the toys for them and take them away.  Zoë and Mark ignored her.  She sent them downstairs, collected all the
toys in the toy tub, and put it in our bedroom.

So far, the effect of this on the Dynamic Duo has been. . .
nonexistent.  Zoë did pitch a fit once when she happened to glance into our bedroom and see the confiscated toys, but she forgot as soon as I closed the door.  Now, they content
themselves with books (neither my wife nor I have the heart to get rid of
something as sacred as a book), the few toys that survived the purge, and the
time-honored pastime of getting into trouble. 
Their lives continue as they had before, except with a lot less stuff.

I can’t help but wonder if we would have a similar
experience if most of our stuff vanished. 
Just as my children’s toys formed something of a secondary carpet across
the living room, our possessions form a carpet across our lives.  There are so many of them that they all blend
in with one another, and the aggregate is unremarkable.

However, we regard this accumulation not with unthinking
rejection, but with unthinking acceptance. 
Our lives are so busy (earning more money to buy more stuff?) that we
never pause to assess whether a given item is worthwhile.  Instead, we accrete more and more things,
until we start looking at moving, not because our house no longer has the right
number of bedrooms, but because it doesn’t have enough storage space for all of
our stuff.  Then, once we have filled the
new expanse of closet and garage and basement, we rent a storage unit to hold
the things that we clearly never use, but somehow can’t get rid of.

We cling to this mass of possessions until the end of our
lives, when we leave it to be sorted out by our next of kin (I’ve told my dad
on several occasions that the day after his funeral, my siblings and I are
going to rent a 50-foot dumpster, and those jars of pickles in his storage room
that were canned in 1994 are going away FOREVER).  It costs us tremendous time and expense to
accumulate, more time and expense to store, but we never ask why.

Stuff doesn’t make us happy. 
If it did, Americans would float through life on a cloud of delirious
glee.  It doesn’t add meaning to our
lives.  It certainly doesn’t bring us
closer to God.  It merely is, a
collection of objects in our homes like the collection of fat cells around our
midsections.

Perhaps it’s time to start counting our purchases the way we
count our calories.  Simply because an
item is cheap and appealing is not a sufficient reason to add it to our
lives.  We need to ask whether it will
make those lives better and more meaningful. 
If not, then we’re better off leaving it on the shelf.  Let’s pretend we bought it and donate the
money to a good cause instead.

Likewise,
we need to go on stuff diets.  There’s no
need to be an extremist here and start putting family heirlooms out on the
curb.  However, our possessions that
aren’t either useful or meaningful need to go away.

Often, we have overly high expectations for the
getting-rid-of process.  We want to have
a garage sale to sell our stuff; we want our used toys to go to imaginary needy
children who don’t have any, as though the Chinese have not buried even the
poorest household in America in cheaply produced plastic.

Those expectations become a barrier between us and a simpler
life.  Garage sales take time and effort
to organize; if we don’t have the time or the energy, our closets and basements
remain full.  It takes even more effort
to locate groups who will accept used toys. 
The only one my wife has ever found wanted to charge us for the
privilege of donating.  The toys stay
on-site too.

Instead, we need to stop worrying about accomplishing good
as we divest ourselves of stuff and regard the divesting process itself as
good.  Two bags of stuff waiting for the
garbage truck isn’t a defeat; it’s a victory. 
If it stings to throw away things for which we Paid Good Money, maybe we
should remember that the next time we are tempted to Pay Good Money for
things.



How about it?  Am I a
traitor to everything that is good about America, or am I just a Communist
who’s headed that way at a brisk clip? 
Let me know in the comments!
