Every once in a while, the government does something interesting.  In this case, I’m referring to a report
issued this month by the Bureau of Justice Statistics, which is in some way
affiliated with the Department of Justice. 
The report describes a pilot program for determining the frequency of
sexual violence on college campuses.  If
anybody else wants to read or critique it in all its 425-page glory, [here's the link.](http://www.bjs.gov/content/pub/pdf/ccsvsftr.pdf)



Sexual assault in institutions of higher learning has been
low-level in the news for a while now. 
Most sensationally, *Rolling Stone*
published a story in November 2014 called “A Rape on Campus” that later proved
to be almost entirely fabrication.  The
supposed victim made her story up.

Attention-seekers notwithstanding, pretty much everybody who
is interested in the subject has a sense that female undergraduates are
subjected to rape (which the BJS study defines as penetrative, nonconsensual
sexual contact) and sexual battery (which the study defines as non-penetrative,
nonconsensual sexual contact) with dismaying frequency.  However, a lot of the “statistics” about
campus sexual assaults are more tools of hyperbolic advocacy than they are
sober attempts to define the problem.

Though the BJS study isn’t perfect (as its authors observe,
the nine institutions surveyed weren’t randomly chosen, which means that its
conclusions should be applied cautiously elsewhere), it goes a long, long way
toward telling us what is happening on campuses.  I’m not a professional statistician or
researcher, but from my semi-educated perspective, the study appears to be
thoughtfully designed and carried out on an impressive scale.  These are solid numbers, much better than
(for instance) the data available on sexual abuse of minors.

They tell us that in the 2014-15 school year at these nine
institutions, 4.1 percent of female undergraduates were victims of at least one
rape and an additional 5.7 percent were subjected to at least one instance of sexual
battery.  We’re talking thousands of
victims here, every year.  Freshmen were at greater risk than others.  However, only
12.5 percent of the rapes were formally reported to authorities, and only 4.3
percent of the incidents of sexual battery were.  There’s not much justice being done, or even
started.

All this is hard for me to contemplate.  Every one of those victims was somebody.  Every one of them was somebody’s daughter,
just like I have a daughter.

There’s more.  In
addition to what, the study also took a good long look at where and why.  Let’s focus particularly on rape.  59 percent of rape victims believed that
their attackers were acting under the influence of drugs or alcohol.  63 percent of rape victims had themselves
used drugs or alcohol in the past 24 hours. 
More than 50 percent of them were incapacitated during the incident
and/or could not provide consent.

In the real world, here’s what these numbers translate
into.  They translate into drunken young
men raping passed-out or nearly-passed-out young women.  The much-decried campus rape culture is
revealed as the even uglier sibling of the campus partying and binge-drinking
culture.

It is hardly surprising that this problem has emerged in
that most godless corner of American society—the university.  This is where we find the people who are too
smart for God, who dismiss His existence, who deny that such a thing as universal
morality even exists, and who insist that human beings are nothing more than
animals.  Is it any surprise, then, when
university students behave like animals?

I don’t mean to imply that professors and university
administrators actively encourage and promote sexual assault.  However, it is nonetheless true that ideas
have consequences, and the unintended consequences are often as important as
those intended.  I doubt that those who
have spent their careers demolishing the foundations of faith and morality have
given as much thought to what would rise in their place.

No campus code of ethics can replace the Bible, either in
its reach or in its power over the heart. 
I hope I’m wrong, but I don’t see our secular society as having the
tools it needs to get rid of binge-drinking by college students.  Binge-drinking is what?  Illegal? 
Unwise?  Those are the kinds of
arguments that hit the foreheads of 18-year-olds and bounce off.

It follows, then, that secular society isn’t going to make
much of a dent in the problem of campus rape either.  Rather than being external to the secular
worldview, it is a logical extension of it, or at least of the secular
worldview as viewed through an alcoholic prism. 
If you can’t say, “This is capital-W Wrong,” what can you say?  Without God, how do you say it’s capital-W
Wrong?

Romans 1 describes the moral decline of the pagan nations,
from idolatry through sexual sin to a generalized decline of morality
altogether.  In the United States, we’re
finishing up Stage 2 (1:24-27) and starting in on Stage 3 (1:28-32).  As time progresses, our society is going to
encounter more things like this, more areas where a national departure from God
bears fruit that nobody wants.

This is hardly mysterious. 
Paul observed a long time ago that whatever a man sows, this he shall
also reap.  Too bad more people today
aren’t listening.
