Over the past 10 years, I’ve been devoting more and more
time to trying to figure out what makes Christians want to worship with a
hymn.  It’s one thing for a hymn to be a
rule-following English composition.  It’s
another thing for that hymn to resonate with a group of worshipers.



In fact, those two things don’t necessarily overlap at
all.  I’ve written many hymns that pass
the English-composition test.  The rhythm
bounces correctly, all the rhymes are strong, and you can put specific
Scripture references to half the content. 
These hymns appear to have all the characteristics of the great hymns of
the past that brethren have been singing for hundreds of years.

And yet, when they are put to the test, they don’t
work.  In hymnist parlance, the hymn doesn’t
“go”.  People will sing it dutifully, but
they won’t sing it joyfully, and when the author isn’t around, they won’t sing
it at all.  This is usually what happens
to the hymns I write.

By contrast, there are plenty of hymns out there that
wouldn’t earn a passing grade from a high-school English teacher.  They’re written by somebody whose familiarity
with the Bible is limited to carrying it back and forth to church.  They reveal a low level of technical
competence.  Both lyrically and
musically, the hymn may be so poorly written that the mistakes actually make it
harder to sing.

And yet, rather than sinking to the bottom of the lake of
forgotten hymns (in the words of Steve Wolfgang), some of these hymns are sung,
and sung enthusiastically.  History teaches
us that bad hymns don’t last.  All the
bad hymns from 1850 have long since been submerged, so that only the good hymns
remain.  However, for the time being,
Christians use bad hymns in worship and even (when confronted with snide
commentary from the likes of me) insist loudly that these are hymns that should
be sung.

What gives?

**“Christians Are
Ignorant!”**

The knee-jerk answer among hymn geeks is that the Christians
who like poorly written hymns are simply wrong. 
If only they were more thoughtful and better educated about worship (the
argument runs), they wouldn’t make mistakes like these.  We should keep writing broccoli hymns, and
sooner or later, the kids will get hungry enough to eat it.

There are two problems with that answer.  The first is that it won’t work.  If song leaders don’t want to lead a hymn,
they won’t lead it.  The End.

Second, I think the argument itself is illegitimate.  Not only do ordinary Christians decide what
they want to use in worship.  Ordinary
Christians should decide what they want to use in worship.  There shouldn’t be some self-appointed
worship elite that decrees from on high what hymns are to be used.  Worship should be the business of the whole
people of God.  If hymnists and hymnal
editors and skilled song leaders should work to educate the folks in the pews,
they have an equal responsibility to allow the folks in the pews to educate
them.

**Bad Hymns with Good
Music**

I probably don’t listen as closely as I should, but I think
there are three factors that drive the use of bad hymns, at least for a
while.  The first is good music.  This is not merely music that is objectively
good, but music that is good according to the tastes of the time.

Occasionally, I’ll hear people speak disparagingly of some
contemporary song because the tune sounds like it came from a Disney
musical.  Guess what?  Millions of people who have no interest in
Mozart and Beethoven will stand in line for the latest Disney movie.  A hymn tune that is culturally resonant is a
plus, not a minus.

**A Core Thought**

The second is a readily identifiable core thought.  Say what you will about “Sanctuary” (and
there are lots of things that I will say about it), but my five-year-old
daughter would probably come away from singing it understanding that she was
singing about being a sanctuary for God. 
That’s important and good.  Most
worshipers are so focused on that core thought that they don’t even recognize
the abundant goofiness in the second and third verses.

Every so often, somebody will share with me that cartoon
about the guy who identifies his occupation by saying “I write worship
choruses” seven times.  I understand the
point, but the reality is that many, perhaps even most, of the great hymns we
sing use quite a bit of repetition too. 
By the end of “He Loved Me So”, the congregation has sung the phrase in
the title twelve times.  Banal repetition
is a problem.  Repetition with nothing around it is (usually) a problem.  Thoughtful repetition is a
powerful draw.

**Non-Distracting
Content**

Since about 2004, I’ve consciously worked on using
repetition in my writing.  However, even
hymns with a core repeated thought don’t necessarily go.  This points to a third (and to my thinking,
somewhat perverse) strength of some poorly written hymns.  Because they have a strong core thought, the
fact that they don’t say anything else keeps people focused on the core
thought:  “Words words words words words
sanctua-ary for You!”  There aren’t any
foothills to obscure the view of the mountain.

Ultimately, hymns with nothing but a core thought aren’t
going to last.  Even now, I find them
objectionable on the basis of my reading of Colossians 3:16.  However, there’s a lesson here, and the
lesson is to beware of too much content.

This is hard for me to see. 
I love the Bible, so I naturally assume that more Bible = better.  That doesn’t necessarily follow.  I like steak, but that doesn’t mean that I
try to shove an entire 16-ounce ribeye in my mouth at once.  Instead, I cut it up into manageable
bite-size pieces so that I can chew, swallow, and digest it, and I will
probably end up taking about half that 16-ounce steak home from the restaurant
so that I can eat it for lunch the next day. 
Too much of a good thing is still too much.

Core thought or no, it’s entirely possible to pack a hymn so
full of content that the central thought gets buried.  This is particularly a danger in longer
hymns.  If you’ve got an eight-line hymn
with a little three-syllable repeated tag at the beginning, you can say so much
in the rest of the hymn that worshipers forget about the tag by the end of the
verse.

Hymns like this often have a focus problem.  Compare, for instance, “What a Friend We Have
in Jesus”.  It’s an eight-liner, but it
repeats some variation of “Take it to the Lord in prayer” six times.  That’s 25 percent of the total syllable count
of the hymn.  Singers are never more than
a few lines away from repeating the core thought.  You simply don’t have time to forget what
you’re singing about.  Repetition isn’t
the only way to maintain laser-like focus, but it’s the most straightforward.

However, if the writer (as I am wont to do) simply takes the
repeated line as a jumping-off point for extended spiritual reflection and
Scriptural paraphrase (double points for an exact quotation!), he can heap up
enough foothills to hide the mountain entirely. 
I’ve noticed over the past few years that my successful hymns tend to be
shorter than the ones that aren’t successful. 
I think it’s because shorter forms don’t give me as much opportunity to
bury my point.  A hymn with an obscured
core thought is no more likely to catch on than a hymn with no core thought at
all.

For those who were wondering, I have not converted.  Good hymns are still good, and bad hymns are
still bad.  However, we need to make sure
that our definition of “good” encompasses not only what Christians should sing,
but what they will sing.
