One of the primary criticisms of many praise-and-worship
songs is their lack of doctrinal content. 
They’re so filled with glowing generalities that they aren’t
particularly Christian at all, other than containing the names of God and
Jesus.  Songs in this genre sound like
they’re about a teenage girl’s latest crush (long on “love”, short on
specifics), so they have earned the descriptor of “Jesus-is-my-boyfriend”
songs.

The most dramatic way to tell whether a song is a
Jesus-is-my-boyfriend song is to replace “Father” and “Jesus” throughout the
text with “boyfriend” (parodists who are feeling trendy can substitute “bae”
for “God”).  If the song still makes
sense, it has no specifically Christian content.



This substitution makes word salad out of traditional
hymns.  They become incoherent because
the doctrinal elements in the lyrics clash with the address to a hypothetical
boyfriend.  Take, for instance, what
happens to the first verse of “My Jesus, I Love Thee”, which is certainly an emotional
hymn and was even written by a teenager:

My boyfriend, I love thee; I know thou art mine;     (makes sense so
far)

For thee all the follies of sin I resign.                        (what?)

My gracious redeemer, my savior art thou;               (not any boyfriend I’ve
ever heard of)

If ever I loved thee, my boyfriend, ‘tis now!

If I saw that without having known the hymn in the first
place, I would assume that it was precisely what it is:  a hymn into which someone had substituted “boyfriend”.  The doctrine trumps the form of address.

By contrast, consider what happens to the popular
contemporary song, “Good, Good Father”. 
In addition to the boyfriend substitution, I also changed the section
where the author veers between first-person singular and first-person plural
to completely first-person singular (which, from a compositional standpoint,
actually improves its lyrical quality).

Good, Good Boyfriend

I've heard a thousand stories of what they think you're like

But I've heard the tender whispers of love in the dead of
night     (ON THE PHONE, PEOPLE)

And you tell me that you're pleased

And that I'm never alone

You're a good good boyfriend

It's who you are, it's who you are, it's who you are

And I'm loved by you

It's who I am, it's who I am, it's who I am

I've seen many searching for answers far and wide

But I know we're all searching

For answers only you provide

'Cause you know just what I need

Before I say a word

You're a good good boyfriend

It's who you are, it's who you are, it's who you are

And I'm loved by you

It's who I am, it's who I am, it's who I am

Because you are perfect in all of your ways

You are perfect in all of your ways

You are perfect in all of your ways to me

You are…

From beginning to end, it makes perfect sense.  This is a pop song, completely innocent of
even the most basic teachings of the Christian faith.  It's not even a particularly good pop song.  If you find “boyfriend” offensive, do the
same thing with “Buddha”.  I don’t even
know if Buddhists sing hymns together, but change three words and they could
sing this one if they wanted to.

This is disastrous. 
More than a hundred years ago, R.W. Dale opined, “Let me write the hymns
of a Church and I care not who writes the theology.”  Hymns have more power over the folks in the
pews than preachers ever will.  What
happens, then, if the most important source of teaching begins to teach nothing
at all?

I fear we’re in the process of finding out.
