A few weeks back, as I’m prone to do from time to time, I
preached a sermon on evangelism.  As we
saw then, there is a wealth of passages in Scripture that tells us how we
should conduct ourselves toward outsiders. 
However, that is not the group of people with whom the writers of the
New Testament appear to be most concerned. 
Instead, they had much more to say about the way we treat other
Christians.

Over the years I’ve spent here, I’ve noticed that when some
new family of Christians moves to the area and visits us to decide whether they
want to place membership with us, the members here will go all-out to welcome
them.  I’ll see brethren literally lining
up to talk to them.  I think that’s a
good and appropriate thing.  However, we
need to make sure that we are giving equal attention to Christians who are
already members here.  Otherwise, we find
ourselves in the strange position of welcoming and inviting others in only to
neglect them once they are in.  This
morning, then, let’s consider what we should do to care for the body.



**Jesus, Our Example**

As always, the best guide to how we should consider and
treat one another is our example, Jesus. 
I could easily preach a whole sermon on the love of Christ for the
church, but a few passages will suffice for us this morning.  Let’s begin for considering the way that
Jesus cares for Christians like a **SHEPHERD.** Here, look with me at John 10:11-16.  This is a rich text, but there are two points
that Jesus makes here that I want to focus on in particular.  The first is that Jesus knows His sheep, and
His sheep know Him.  Jesus knows us
better than we know ourselves, and He reveals Himself to us both through His
word and through the experience of our daily walk with Him.  How barren our lives would be without Jesus
in them!  If Jesus is so concerned about
His relationship with every single Christian here, we should be equally
concerned.  How terrible it is when we
worship with somebody who is dear to the Lord, yet practically a stranger to
us!

Second, we see Jesus’ concern for the unity of the
flock.  He was willing to lay down His
life to keep the wolf from scattering the sheep.  We too, then, ought to be willing to devote
our lives to the same goal.  We should
never, in word or action, be the cause of any kind of division in the
church.  Similarly, we should keep an eye
out for those who seem to be drifting away from the flock and do what we can to
bring them back into the fold.

We also see the Scripture compare the relationship between
Christ and the church to the relationship between a **HUSBAND** and his wife.  Here,
consider Ephesians 5:25-27.  Once again,
there are two points in here that seem particularly relevant to us.  The first is Christ’s willingness to give
Himself up for the church.  This is an
evocative phrase.  We think of a fugitive
giving himself up when he surrenders to the police, or a wide receiver giving
up his body when he jumps to catch a pass over the middle, even though he knows
he’s about to be knocked into the next county by the linebacker a step behind
him.  In both of these cases, there is
some goal that is so important that no other consideration matters in achieving
it.  Jesus gave Himself up for us because
He was willing to pay any price for our salvation.  So too should we be willing to give ourselves
up for one another.

We see here too that Christ’s concern for the church is
particularly focused on spiritual matters. 
He is concerned with sanctifying her and cleansing her and making her
holy and blameless.  Likewise, friends,
our concern for one another should be most of all concern for one another’s
souls.  It’s too easy for the church to
become a social club, so that we come together twice a week mainly to exchange
chit-chat.  In the social-club church,
spiritual failings get ignored because it’s unpleasant to bring up such things
and talk about them.  What lies behind
that silence about sin is not concern for the feelings of another.  It’s selfishness.  It’s considering their souls less valuable
than our social comfort.  If we are like
Christ, we will reverse those priorities.

Finally, we see Jesus emphasize the importance of our
brethren in His role as **JUDGE.** Let’s examine His words in Matthew
25:34-40.  This is the measuring stick
for our conduct toward our brethren.  If
we’re treating all of them with the same love and consideration that we would
show toward Jesus if He were physically here with us, we’re behaving as we
should.  If, however, we’re treating any
of them less well than we would treat our Savior, we’re not living up to His
expectations—because the way that we’re treating them *is* the way that we’re treating Jesus.

This gives us all a lot to chew on during the Lord’s
Supper.  First of all, let’s remember all
that Jesus has done for us.  None of us
could possibly ask for more love from Him than He has already shown us.  He descended from the courts of heaven to
come to earth and die an ugly, wretched death because we needed Him to, and
nobody else could help us.  This is the
pattern for how we should love one another. 
Are we living up to it?  Let’s
ponder as we partake.

**Caring Attitudes**

Now that we’ve seen the importance of our brethren from the
example of Jesus, let’s consider four attitudes that promote caring for the
body.  The first of these is **SELFLESSNESS.** Here, let’s look at the words of Paul in
Philippians 2:1-4.  Recently, I’ve been
seeing a meme online that says, “Marriage isn’t 50/50.  Divorce is 50/50.  Marriage has to be 100/100.”  I think that’s exactly right.  If we approach our marriages like we would
some kind of contract, where the object is to make sure that our spouse isn’t
slacking and cheating us, those marriages are doomed.  The only way that we can make our marriages
work is by giving everything we have.

The same thing is true of our congregation.  All too often, Christians who have no trouble
seeing the problems with selfishness in marriage don’t recognize that their
selfishness in the church creates similar problems.  These Christians aren’t interested in what
they can give to the body.  They’re
interested in what they can get from it. 
They won’t teach a children’s Bible class if their children aren’t in
it.  If they don’t get to lead singing on
Sunday morning, they’ll place membership where they do get to.  Friends, nothing could be further from the
noble spirit of Philippians 2!  As in
marriage, selfishness in the church is ultimately self-defeating.  If we don’t regard one another as more
important than ourselves, the church will fall apart, and none of us will get
anything out of it.

Second, for the body to function at its best, the members
need to have **EQUAL RESPECT** for one
another.  Paul points this out in 1
Corinthians 12:14-24.  In worldly terms,
this makes no sense.  After all, some
people are prettier or smarter or more athletically gifted than others, so
they’re the ones who should get the respect, while the scrubs get ignored.

However, in the Lord’s church, we should be wiser than the
world.  None of us is deserving of any
special honor, because every one of us is equal in the eyes of God.  Let me give you an example.  We’ve all heard clergy in the religious world
addressed by various titles:  “Reverend,”
“Pastor,” and “Father” are all common.  However,
as with most gospel preachers, the only form of address that I will ever accept
is “Brother”.

This may sound like modesty on my part, but it’s not.  Instead, it’s a reminder of the only cause I
will ever have for boasting:  that Jesus
Christ loved me and allowed me to become His brother.  There is nothing I can ever do that can
elevate me more than being a brother of the King.

The same thing is true of every Christian in this
congregation.  There is nothing that we
can ever be or do that is more impressive than being a brother or sister of the
Lord Jesus.  All of us are on exactly the
same level, so if we start making distinctions among each other, we are
exhibiting an arrogance that our true spiritual position does not allow.

Third, along with equal respect, our behavior toward one
another should reflect **EQUAL
CONCERN.** Here, let’s continue
reading from 1 Corinthians 12:25-26.  In
my own body, I don’t think my big toe is unimportant because it’s my big toe
and not my heart.  I don’t hurt any less
because it’s my big toe and not my heart that’s hurting.  So too for us as a body of believers.  Jesus cared enough to die for every Christian
here, so we should care enough to help them on to heaven.

Too much of the time, though, the social networks of
churches look less like a circle of inclusion and more like a bull’s eye of
exclusion.  You have the in-crowd in the
middle, the ones that everybody notices and cares about, but as you move
farther and farther away from the center, you get less and less notice, until
the people on the outer ring are overlooked and ignored.  They can show up for services, they can be
absent, and nobody much cares either way.

Brethren, that’s not how we want to be here.  It’s great that we have friends here.  It’s great that we have people we feel close
to.  However, we should devote our
efforts not merely to maintaining those existing connections, but to building
connections with Christians who may feel like they’re on the outside looking
in.  We often sing, “No one is a stranger
here; everyone belongs.”  Let’s make sure
that such a song from our lips is more than an idle boast.

Finally, caring for the body requires **DILIGENCE.** Let’s give some
thought to the exhortation of the Hebrews writer in Hebrews 3:12-13.  As this text reveals, being concerned for the
spiritual welfare of one another isn’t a one-time thing.  Instead, it’s something that requires a daily
commitment from us.  In other words, if a
day goes by in which we haven’t helped another Christian on to heaven, we’ve
done something wrong.

Make no mistake, friends; this is hard!  Diligence does not come easily to most
people.  It’s why Day 200 of a diet is
far more difficult to reach than Day 2. 
One little slip-up, and you find yourself out of your routine
entirely!  Thankfully, Jesus does not
require us to lose weight, but He does require us to look out for one another
on a continual basis. Every one of us is our brother’s keeper, and we will be
until we die.  If we ever find ourselves
asking, “Should I do this for my brother,” every single day, the answer needs
to be yes.

**Caring in Action**

In the final part of our study together this morning, let’s
look at a handful of ways that our care for one another needs to express itself
in action.  The first of these is by **ASSEMBLING.**Here, let’s look, of course, at Hebrews 10:24-25.  Other than ignoring this text entirely, just
about the worst way that we can misread it is by turning it into a rule.  We look at not forsaking the assembly and
start asking how many Sundays we can miss in a row before it becomes
forsaking.  Of course, Wednesday nights
don’t count at all because we don’t see Wednesday-night assemblies in the
Bible, so if we show up for those, it’s just bonus points.

Brethren, that kind of minimum-seeking spirit has nothing to
do with the spirit of Christ!  We
shouldn’t read this text and start thinking about how often we can not be
here.  We should read it and start
thinking about the Christians who are here. 
Our whole lives belong to God, but during our assemblies, we uniquely
belong to one another.  When we choose
not to be here, we’re letting our brothers and sisters down.  They need us here.  They need our help to get to heaven—on
Wednesdays as well as Sundays—and when we choose not to be here, we’re telling
them that something else in our lives is more important than their souls
are.  This passage isn’t about
rules.  It’s about relationships.

Of course, just like any relationship does, this one has
some wiggle room in it.  For instance,
from time to time, one of the Christians here is in grad school, and in order
to graduate, they have to take a class that meets only on Wednesday night.  I think we get that.  We miss them, but we can wait until they can
assemble with us again.  What we don’t
get is when brethren similarly aren’t here for reasons that are a lot
murkier.  That’s when it gets
disappointing and discouraging.  Let’s
remember, friends, that our choice to assemble is never about us
individually.  It’s about the whole group.  With our brethren firmly in mind, let’s make
the right choice.

Second, let’s care for one another by **ASSOCIATING** with one another outside the assembly.  Consider, for instance, the command of 1
Peter 4:8-9.  A few weeks back, we
studied the importance of being hospitable to outsiders.  Here, we see a similar command to be
hospitable to one another.  From Acts 2,
we learn that the disciples of the Jerusalem church ate with one another on a
daily basis.  I’m afraid that too often,
we overlook this godly example for the sake of convenience.  Our lives are so full of work and school and
extracurricular activities that we don’t have the time or the energy to
associate with other Christians on a regular basis.

Friends, if that’s how full our lives are, our lives are too
full.  We are ignoring relationships that
have eternal significance in favor of things that literally no one is going to
care about 100 years from now.  Being on
the basketball team is not going to help little Johnny get to heaven, but
spending time with people who love the Lord sure will!  I think everybody here would proclaim that
God is the most important thing in their lives. 
Great!  Let’s make sure that the
way we actually live is in line with our values.

As we look to associate with one another, let’s work too on **INCLUDING** those who are often
overlooked.  We see Barnabas doing
precisely this in Acts 9:26-28.  It’s
certainly true that following Christ does not make for an easy road.  In addition to all the problems of life,
discipleship brings some unique problems with it.  However, one thing that a Christian should
never be is lonely.  Admittedly,
sometimes, the loneliness is the fault of the Christian in question.  Brethren keep on reaching out to them, but
they choose to go home and snivel instead. 
There’s no help for that.

It’s likely more common, though, for Christians to be lonely
because they’re overlooked by their brethren. 
When that happens, it’s a terrible shame.  Making others feel welcome isn’t a work of
the church, but emphatically should be a work of each individual Christian
here.  If there is a single soul in the
auditorium this morning whom we don’t know, our first priority should be to go
and change that.  We need to invite them
into our lives, so that they know that no matter how the outside world may
treat them, they’re not going to be ignored here.

Finally, we care for one another by **PROVIDING** for physical needs. 
Consider the point that John makes in 1 John 3:17-18.  I suspect that I’ve done enough preaching on
Biblical authority through the years that most here are familiar with the
first-century pattern.  We never see
first-century churches caring for the world’s poor.  Instead, that work is left for individual
disciples.  By contrast, we do see
churches collecting and distributing resources to help needy brethren.

However, we should not draw the conclusion from this that
because the church can help, the individual Christian doesn’t have to.  Instead, we should be open-hearted and
open-handed toward everyone, but most of all toward our brethren who are in
need.  Ideally, needy members of this
congregation should never have to seek help from the church at all, because
loving individuals have already stepped up and filled the needs they had.  As we saw in Matthew 25, the help that we
give to one another is help that we give to the Lord Jesus Himself.
