The gospel is for all, but it is nonetheless true that one of our biggest challenges in studying the Bible is that we don’t share a cultural context with any of its original audience.  This might seem like a trivial concern.  After all, don’t the words mean the same thing now they did 2000 years ago?  In reality, though, mistakes with cultural context have led to some of the most serious false doctrines around us today.  The whole “you don’t have to be baptized to be saved” thing started when Martin Luther read the book of Romans like it was written to sixteenth-century Germans instead of first-century Italians.  He took the word “works”, which Paul used to describe justification by perfectly keeping the Law of Moses, and applied it to doing absolutely anything in God’s service.

Cultural context matters, and sometimes, it’s important for us to invest ourselves in looking at a passage through first-century eyes.  To us, the account of Jesus’ triumphal entry into Jerusalem seems straightforward, but when we start comparing it to elements in the Old Testament, a whole lot of complexity starts to emerge.  Let’s look, then, at the Triumphal Entry and the Psalms.

**Songs of Ascents**

We’re going to start our journey back in time by considering three of the so-called Songs of Ascents, looking both at what they meant in the time of Christ and what they should mean to us.  The first of our trio is **PSALM 122**.  It consists of nine verses.

However, the first relevant item of information here consists of its label as a song of ascents.  We don’t naturally get that.  However, that’s because to most of us, Palestine is a two-dimensional image on a map rather than a landscape we have actually traveled.  Those maps make the region look flat, but it emphatically is not.  Jerusalem itself is built on top of a mountain.  Travelers who went the way that Jesus went on His final journey into the city came from Jericho, which sits at 850 feet below sea level, to Jerusalem, which is about 2500 feet above sea level, a net gain of about 3350 feet in 23 miles.  That, friends, is a serious hike up!  As a result, the psalms that the Jews sang as they climbed toward Jerusalem for religious festivals were known as the Songs of Ascents.

Psalm 122 is typical of the genre.  It rejoices in God’s protection, and particularly in the protection offered by the city of Jerusalem.  The psalmist praises its stability, the peace and security that exist within its walls, and the secure kingship provided by the house of David.

To the Jews who were singing this around AD 30, the difference between the psalm and reality would have been brutally apparent.  Jerusalem was still a great city, but rather than being secure from all enemies, it was occupied by Roman conquerors.  No descendant of David had reigned in Jerusalem for centuries.  Instead, the political rulership of God’s people was split between Roman governors and Idumean puppets.  In singing Psalm 122, the Jews of Jesus’ day were singing about something they had never seen but hoped to see God bring about.

Next, let’s consider **PSALM 123**.  This is the shortest of all of the psalms we’ll be considering this morning, and one of the shortest in the entire book.  In this one, though, instead of lifting up their eyes to a mountaintop destination, the singers are lifting up their eyes to the Lord.  In fact, the psalmist compares their attentiveness to the attentiveness of servants watching to see what their master is going to do.

The plea of the psalm is simple and generic.  It appeals for the mercy of God because the singers are fed up with the hostility and contempt of their arrogant oppressors.  Deliverance from them is what all of the anxious servants of God are waiting for.

Psalm 123 predates the time of Christ by centuries, but it might as well have been tailor-made for His era.  Every Jew in the Jewish nation except for the Herodians, tax collectors, and other quislings would have agreed that “proud and scornful” described the Romans to a T.  Their conception of God’s mercy consisted of His throwing out the hateful foreign invaders, ideally at the hands of a kingly son of David.  Like the servants in the psalm, thousands of them were ready—in this case, to revolt at the slightest provocation.  No wonder, then, that Judea constantly bubbled with unrest.  The very hymns the Jews sang stirred them up against the conquerors.

Finally in this section, we come to **PSALM 124**.  Unlike Psalm 123, this one is about salvation in the past tense.  No longer are the singers waiting for God to act; they’re rejoicing because He has acted.  We don’t have any trouble decoding the references here, and neither would the Jews of 2000 years ago.  This is clearly about God delivering the Israelites by parting the waters of the Red Sea so that they could escape from the army of the Egyptians.  Things looked hopeless for the runaway slaves, and for anybody who didn’t have God on their side, they were.  However, the psalmist observes, the Maker of heaven and earth is able to rescue His people from anything.

To the subjugated Jews, this would have seemed like their only hope too.  Everybody knew that you didn’t go up against the legions and win.  They were as militarily superior to anything the Jews could field as the Egyptian chariots were to the rabble who fled with Moses.  Only God could possibly tip the balance so that the Jews could be rescued from their tormentors.

Here, then, we see the religious narrative that the Jews of Jesus’ day have constructed from the Psalms.  It’s plausible.  It’s compelling.  However, as Jesus will reveal, it’s also completely wrong.  Deliverance is coming, but it’s not deliverance from the Romans at all.

**Psalm 118**

Next, let’s consider Psalm 118.  However, rather than going through it verse-by-verse, I want to try something different this morning.  Let’s begin by reading it in its entirety, then taking three different perspectives in understanding it.

The first of these perspectives is **THE MEANING FROM THE TEXT**.  In order to understand what’s going on here, I think we first need to recognize that worship in Bible times was often much more dynamic than our worship is.  We come in, sit down, and stay seated while we sing.  However, that’s not how worship necessarily went in the Old Testament; in fact, they probably didn’t sing while seated very often at all.  In addition to the Songs of Ascents themselves, we also see the Israelites singing a variation of Psalm 136 as they march out to battle in 2 Chronicles 20.  Similarly, Nehemiah’s dedication of the wall in Nehemiah 12 involves two choirs of Levites marching around the circuit of the wall while singing.

Even though we don’t have a narrative about how it was used, Psalm 118 reads like it was originally used in a religious procession too.  There’s singing about opening the gates in v. 19 because the procession is standing in front of the gates of the temple.  Then, in v. 27, there’s more singing about the altar because the procession has arrived at the altar.

Also, we should understand that Psalm 118 is not a communal hymn like the hymns we sing together are.  Instead, it bears every indication of being more like Song of Solomon, a script for what multiple groups of singers would sing.  For instance, the repetitions of “His steadfast love endures forever” in vs. 2-4 were probably sung in order by the three groups mentioned:  first Israelites, second Levites, and third God-fearing Gentiles.

I also see multiple groups in the last part of the psalm:  a singer and at least two choruses.  In v. 19, the primary singer asks to be admitted.  In v. 20, the inside chorus affirms that he ought to be admitted.  In v. 21, the singer expresses his singular appreciation that the gate has opened.  In vs. 22-25, the processional chorus members sing in plural about how wonderful these events are.  In vs. 26-27, the inside chorus welcomes the primary singer to the temple.  Finally, the text shifts back to singular in vs. 28-29 as the primary singer wraps things up.

Next, let’s consider **THE MEANING IN JESUS’ TIME**.  The original temple use of the psalm may have faded by then, but we know about at least two additional ways in which it was used.

First, it was read, recited, or chanted by households during the Passover.  Psalms 113-118 are collectively known as the Hallel, the Hebrew word for “praise”.  We even have records of Hillel and Shammai, two famous Pharisee teachers who lived before Jesus, arguing back and forth about whether Jews should recite Psalms 113-114 together or Psalm 113 alone before the Passover meal, with the remainder of the Hallel being used afterwards.  In any case, it’s nearly certain that when Mark 14:26 reports that Jesus and the disciples sang a hymn and went out afterward to the Mount of Olives, Psalm 118 was the hymn they sang.

Second, Psalm 118 was used as a sort of epilogue to the Songs of Ascents, as the pilgrims were actually entering Jerusalem.  The Jews probably recognized the processional nature of the psalm much more easily than we do, even if they weren’t still using it that way.  As they were entering the city gates of Jerusalem, they sang the gate-opening section of Psalm 118.

However, beyond those two things, there’s also **THE MEANING THEY MISSED**.  God knew, of course, that the meaning of the psalm would shift over time before He ever inspired it in the first place, and the psalm reflects that knowledge.  In a temple context, it was obvious that when the chorus cried out, “Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord” in v. 26, they were inviting him to participate in the sacrifice of the animal that was standing by the altar, ready to be bound to it, in v. 27.

In the context of the city gates of Jerusalem, though, there was no altar and no animal.  Instead, those words in that setting made a much less obvious point:  the Blessed One who was coming in the name of the Lord was also the sacrifice to be bound to the altar.

**The Triumphal Entry**

Now that we’ve gone through a thousand years’ worth of framing, let’s read all of this into the account of the Triumphal Entry itself.  The story begins with **BORROWING A COLT**.  Look at Luke 19:28-34.  At first glance, this is a weird story.  The gospels are extremely tightly written and don’t waste space on trivia.  Why, then, are we spending seven verses on the story of the disciples going to fetch this colt?  

In order to figure this out, we have to look to the Old Testament.  First, according to the Law of Moses, the Israelite king wasn’t supposed to breed horses.  This is why in the stories about David that we’ve been studying recently, the king’s sons all rode around on donkeys and mules.  In this story, we know from other accounts that this isn’t a young horse.  It’s a young donkey, an animal that is therefore associated with David and with kingship.  Similarly, there’s a prophecy in Zechariah 9 that the king will return to Jerusalem mounted on a colt, the foal of a donkey, and that he will expand his reign to the ends of the earth.

Jesus, then, in choosing to ride into town on a donkey’s colt, is doing nothing less than declaring Himself king, and the man who lends him the colt is backing His claim.  We might miss the point, but nobody 2000 years ago would have, including the enemies of Jesus.  Everybody would have seen that He was setting Himself up either to win or to die.  Nobody, though, anticipated that He might be doing both.

The second part of the story concerns **THE CROWD’S ACCLAMATION**.  Let’s read from Luke 19:35-40.  The crowds here are probably the same horde that has followed Jesus all the way up from Jericho, going to the feast themselves, and doubtless singing the Songs of Ascents as they climb.  They are primed to look at Jesus on His donkey and see “delivering King,” and that’s exactly what they do see.  They take the language of Psalm 118 about going through the gates and apply it to Jesus as King, something that God knew would happen when He inspired the psalm.

This is dangerous.  This is the-Romans-show-up-and-slaughter-everybody-especially-Jesus kind of dangerous.  The Romans had no sense of humor about subject peoples who tried to set up their own kings.  When the Pharisees try to get Jesus to shush everybody, it’s not because they’re opposed to Him.  It’s because they’re opposed to riots and bloodbaths.

Jesus’ response is interesting.  He tells the alarmed Pharisees that if the disciples shut up, the stones will cry out.  I think there are two things going on here.  First and most obviously, Jesus is the fulfillment of Zechariah 9 and Psalm 118.  He is the anointed King, and He is going to be recognized as such on His way into Zion, even if God has to make the rocks do it.

Second, though, these friendly Pharisees who hope to protect Jesus are misguided.  In less than a week, the Romans are going to kill Jesus, and they’re going to do it because He has declared Himself King.  Because Jesus must die, trying to keep the news from the Romans is pointless.  Even if no one else does, the stones will repeat the truth that will kill Him.

Finally, let’s consider **JESUS’ REACTION**.  It’s presented for us in Luke 19:41-44.  This is our second bizarre text for the reading.  Jesus has just received the cultural equivalent of a ticker-tape parade.  And yet, as He approaches the gates, even as these people are singing songs of praise about Him, He breaks down and weeps.  His words are even stranger.  His followers are looking to Him to deliver Israel, and yet He predicts that Jerusalem will be destroyed because it didn’t recognize Him for what He was—this, at the end of the parade!

The problem here is that even though the Jews have the right King, they have the wrong mission.  God is about to deliver His people through His anointed King, yes.  However, this deliverance isn’t going to be from the Romans.  It’s going to be from sin.  Jesus knows perfectly well that the Jews are so fixated on political salvation that they’re going to miss the spiritual salvation that He is offering.  In fact, they’re going to chase their dream of independence so far that it’s going to destroy them.  All the Psalms are right, but the people are reading them disastrously wrong.

**Conclusion**

I know this is a lot to process, but there are two things that I want us to draw from it.  The first is the way that even an apparently simple narrative in the gospels is usually anything but.  Instead, it's just the tip of an iceberg of history and cultural context.  The closer we get to the cross, the bigger the icebergs get.  There’s a reason why the Hebrews writer calls the day of Jesus’ death “the consummation of the ages”.  We should feel that weight when we read these things.

Second, we’re going to be partaking of the Lord’s Supper momentarily, and as we do, let’s reflect on the double irony of the Triumphal Entry.  All these crowds are cheering and singing and celebrating because they think Jesus is about to restore the kingdom to Israel.  This is a glorious day!  In reality, though, in five days, the man riding in like a king is going to be headed back out the gates carrying a cross, about to die the death of a rebellious slave.  Not so glorious.  However, two days after that, and an apparently futile death is revealed as the most meaningful thing ever to happen on the planet.  Let’s remember these amazing events as we share in the Lord’s Supper together.
