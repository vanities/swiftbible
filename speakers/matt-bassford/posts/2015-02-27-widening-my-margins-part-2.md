Now that I’ve actually received my Cambridge Wide-Margin
Reference and started using it, I have a great deal more to say on the
subject.  Normally, this is the kind of
thing that would end up in a “Preaching, Teaching, and Study” post, but if I
put it there, it would overwhelm the rest of the content.  As a result, it gets two more posts on its
own.



The first question that I think is worth exploring is, “Why
was the Cambridge Wide-Margin the right wide-margin to adopt?”  The most obvious answer here is, “Because
Cambridge sent me a review copy,” but I think it was the right choice even
beyond that.

**IN PRAISE OF
SINGLE-COLUMN WIDE-MARGINS.** This is
true even though the Wide-Margin, like the Pitt Minion, is a double-column
Bible.  Mark Bertrand has written
extensively on wide-margin Bibles over at Bible Design Blog, and every time he
revisits the subject, he’s sure to mention that he thinks all wide-margin
Bibles should be single-column.  Even
though I don’t share the same affection for single-column Bibles that Mark
does, I think he’s right about this.

It’s a matter of mechanics. 
If, in a double-column wide-margin Bible, you want to write notes next
to the inner columns of text, you’re going to be writing notes in the
gutter.  That’s hard to do.  I’m left-handed, so I have particular
troubles with the margin on the right side of the gutter, but I’m sure
right-handers have similar difficulties with the left side.

By contrast, a single-column Bible never gives the
note-taker a reason to write in the gutter. 
Instead, all the notes can be in the outer margins, where it’s easy to
write, and still be next to the text. 
Despite this, Bible publishers still produce more double-column than
single-column wide-margin Bibles.  The
only single-column wide-margins I’m aware of in non-KJV translations are
Foundation’s famous wide-margin Side-Column Reference and the Crossway
Single-Column Journaling Bible. 
Zondervan used to publish a wide-margin single-column NASB, but it’s
since gone out of print.

**SINGLE-COLUMN OR
DOUBLE-COLUMN?** However, even though
I know and believe all these things, I’ve still adopted a double-column
Bible.  For me, this is the right
choice.  First of all, it’s important to
me that the Cambridge Wide-Margin Reference pairs with the Pitt Minion.  The Pitt is one of my favorite Bible designs
ever.  Its layout is exquisite, I love
reading out of it, and it offers a full cross-reference suite in a petite
format.

Using a wide-margin Bible with the same format as the Pitt
allows me to take advantage of visual-memory cues.   I’m not a strong visual learner, but even I
will pick up on visual cues to some extent. 
It should matter to me, then, that the same words are in the same
position on the same page in the Wide-Margin as in the Pitt.  I should remember the reading and study I’ve
done in the Pitt as I look at the Wide-Margin, and it may be that I start
“seeing” the notes I’ve written in the Wide-Margin as I look at the Pitt.

Second, the Wide-Margin is a relatively light Bible for a
wide-margin.  Note that this is
emphatically relative!  I will generally
reach for Bibles that weigh about a pound. 
The Pitt Minion is Exhibit A here. 
By contrast, the Wide-Margin weighs in at 2 pounds, 11 ounces, about
three times that weight.  This is a
necessary evil.  There is no such thing
as a small wide-margin Bible with readable print.  It’s physically impossible.

That’s almost the same weight as the Crossway Single-Column
Journaling Bible, but I traditionally struggle with using Crossway’s
paragraphed single-column layouts in the pulpit.  I like using the Foundation SCR in the
pulpit, but a wide-margin ’02 tips the scales at a beefy 3 pounds, 5 ounces.  I plan to spend most of the sermon out of the
pulpit when I’m using my Wide-Margin to preach, and over the span of 45
minutes, 10 extra ounces will make a noticeable difference.

Of course, part of the reason why the SCR weighs more is
because it has larger print.  It may well
be that if the Lord allows me to live another 20 years, my close-in vision will
deteriorate enough that I’ll no longer be able to use the Cambridge
Wide-Margin.  If so, switching to the SCR
seems like a reasonable thing to do. 
Until then, I’m going to opt for the lighter, double-column Bible.

**WHICH
DOUBLE-COLUMN?** OK. . . So
double-column it is, but why this double-column Bible?  The new Zondervan NASB Note-Taker’s Bible,
for instance, uses Zondervan’s NASB Thinline setting, a setting I like very
much and have written about extensively. 
I already own a goatskin-rebound NASB Thinline, so I could rely on
visual cues in using the Zondervan set too. 
Finally, where the calf-split Cambridge Wide-Margin Reference retails
for $210, a poly-cover Zondervan Note-Taker’s NASB retails for $54.99.  I could get the latter rebound in goatskin
too and still come out ahead by about $50.

However, there’s one significant difference between the
Wide-Margin and the Note-Taker’s.  The
Note-Taker’s has a quarter-inch inner margin, which is enough to keep text from
getting lost in the gutter, but certainly not enough to write in.  Notes for the inner column would perforce end
up in the generous bottom margins of each page.

Conceptually, I struggle with that.  To me, the whole point of having a
wide-margin Bible is to have your notes written right next to the applicable
text.  It’s simple and intuitive to
glance over to the margin to see what you thought about that particular
verse.  You can circle a word and draw a
line over to your comments about it.

However, half the time in the Note-Taker’s, that isn’t
possible.  Instead, you have to glance
all the way down to the bottom of the page, which can’t have a spatial
connection to particular verses. 
Admittedly, I haven’t tried it, but to my way of thinking, if you’re
going to do that, you might as well carry a separate notebook and take your
notes in it.

Strangely enough, there aren’t very many double-column
wide-margin Bibles with space to write in the inner margins.  Most of them (that I’ve seen, at least) are Cambridge
products:  the Concord Wide-Margin KJV
and the Wide-Margin Reference in a number of modern translations.  The Crossway Wide-Margin (ESV) is another
one.

Generally, though, publishers shy away from leaving room to
write along the gutter.  Perhaps it’s a
Bible size issue.  There seems to be an
unwritten law, “Thou shalt not publish Bibles wider than six-and-a-half
inches.”  I don’t know whether that’s
because of the shelf width in Christian bookstores or because many people can’t
comfortably hold a Bible wider than that, but I’m sure there’s a reason.

However, at 7 ¼“ in width, the Wide-Margin line is well
above that upper limit.  I’m not a Bible
publisher, so I don’t have to worry about shelf width, and even though I have
relatively small hands for a man, I can hold my Wide-Margin just fine.  I’d far rather have a seven-inch wide-margin
Bible that allows me to use it in a way that makes sense to me than a six-inch
wide-margin Bible that keeps me from doing what I want.

Happily, then, logic leads me to the place where I already
am.  It’s sort of like reasoning your way
to the conclusion that you should have married the woman you already have
married.  I’m plunging into the world of
wide-margin Bibles with a Cambridge, and so far as I can tell, the Cambridge
truly is the best wide-margin for me.
