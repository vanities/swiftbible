**OVERVIEW.** If a Bible makes you re-think the way you
preach, it’s a pretty good Bible.  The
LPUT is making me re-think the way I preach.

**INTRODUCTION.** I don’t think a Bible has ever gone
through more of a workout during the two-week review period than the LPUT.  During this time, I used it to preach two
sermons, teach three Bible classes, run one men’s study, offer a two-hour
presentation at a preacher’s meeting, and deliver two 45-minute lectures.  There may also possibly have been a partridge
in a pear tree in there someplace. 
Here’s what I thought:



**PREACHING.** First things first.  By now, I’ve used and reviewed several dozen
Bibles.  The LPUT is one of the best
designs I’ve ever seen.  I know
journaling Bibles are the hot new trend in Bible marketing these days, but
there’s much to be said for taking an older format—the venerable double-column
reference Bible—and refining it about as far as it can be refined.

The LPUT was set by the Danish design firm 2K.  Generally, I think 2K does excellent work,
but they outdid themselves with this one! 
It’s clearly a descendant of the HCSB Large-Print UltraThin Bible, which
had a lot going for it but some serious flaws too.  Most notably, for reasons that escape me, the
HCSB LPUT was set in some sans-serif font. 
That made it read like a much smaller-print Bible than it actually was.

Happily, this mistake has been corrected in the CSB
LPUT.  The sans-serif whatever-it-was has
been replaced with a proprietary 2K typeface called Bible Serif.  I really like it for two reasons.  First, it “reads large”, which is very
important in Bibles.  Second, it’s not
Lexicon No. 1.  Yes, I know Lexicon is a
wonderful typeface, but I get so tired of seeing it in every Bible I
review.

The rest of the design is on par with typeface
selection.  Other settings use italicized
section headings, but the LPUT uses small-print headings that are boldfaced and
all-caps (with more important headings in larger print).  When combined with the stylistic choice to
boldface Old-Testament quotations, this adds an unusual amount of visual
interest to the design.  The layout has a
rhythm to it instead of looking like a bunch of text vomited onto the
page.  It’s more pleasing to my eye than,
for instance, the layout of the Pitt Minion (which is a *good* design!).

The way 2K handled the reference notes is equally good.  It carries over the practice from the HCSB
LPUT of keying cross-references to the page instead of the verse.  As a result, cross-references on a typical
page in the New Testament may run from *a* through
*z* and *aa* to *aj*.  If there are too many references to fit in
the center column, they spill over to the bottom of the right-hand column.

This style of cross-reference has two advantages.  First, it’s easier to use.  You don’t have to hunt for a particular verse
heading in the center column.  You just look
for *u* or whatever.  Second, omitting the verse headings permits
the use of a much narrower center column, which allows for much wider text
columns.

Footnotes are keyed to non-italicized upper-case letters, A,
B, and so forth.  They’re used for
translators’ notes (which in the CSB are quite helpful) and references to
Old-Testament quotations.  As is
appropriate for footnotes, they appear in the footer.

In addition to the typeface change, the book and chapter
contained on a particular page now appear in a running header (which is more
traditional for Bibles) instead of a running footer.  Here too, the CSB gets rid of a lot of the
weird that was in the HCSB.

So.  Really wonderful
design.  I’m an amateur rather than an
expert, but I don’t see any way that 2K could have improved on what they
did.  In fact, the design got me to
asking the age-old question of the Bible geek: 
could this be The One?  Because
large-print thinline reference Bibles are such good all-rounders, they tend to
be better candidates for The One than most, and the LPUT is a large-print
thinline reference Bible *par excellence*.

Of course, for me, there’s one big obstacle to a large-print
thinline reference Bible being The One. 
For the past couple of years, I’ve been committed to using wide-margin
Bibles for expository sermons.  However,
wide-margin Bibles don’t work for topical sermons at all, and even for
expository sermons, I find that these days, I’m actually not referring much to
the notes in the Bible.  Besides, I like
the freedom that comes from setting my Bible down on the pulpit and wandering
off with nothing in my hands at all.  In
short, the days of my insistence on Bible-writing may be coming to an end.

If I’m not trying to write in it, the LPUT is an excellent
choice for a preaching Bible.  It feels *amazing* in the hand (I’ve never used a
Bible with a cover this lovable), it’s not very heavy, and the verse numbers
are easy to locate (an advantage that double-column Bibles have over
single-column Bibles).

It is true that when I first started using it, I had trouble
flipping pages.  I couldn’t get them to
turn as quickly as I wanted them to. 
However, after a couple of weeks of heavy use, the  problem has disappeared, so that I’m reaching
the verse I want almost as soon as I finish telling the congregation what the citation
is.  It could be that I’ve adapted to
pages of that size and weight; it could simply be that with more use, the Bible
has broken in more and the pages aren’t sticking together anymore.  Regardless, I don’t think this is an issue.

**TEACHING.** I have a great fondness for text Bibles,
but Revelation is where text Bibles go to die, and I’m teaching a class on
Revelation.  To do the book justice, I
need a full reference suite, and the LPUT has one.  What’s more, the CSB cross-references seem
useful.  I don’t know how many there are
in the translation, but the number seems comparable to the number in the ESV or
NASB, and even with some of the usual citing-passages-two-chapters-over stuff,
generally, they’ll send me where I want to go.

In terms of size, the LPUT is fine.  It’s just as delightful in the hand when I’m
teaching as when I’m preaching, and it weighs less than two pounds.  I never found myself deterred from holding it
by its weight.

The only issue I had in using it in Bible class was
translation-based.  In my experience, the
CSB generally ends up meaning the same thing as the NASB, ESV, and so on, but
it gets there using different words and simpler sentences.  As a result, because the CSB read differently
than the formal-equivalent translations in my head, I didn’t have the same confidence
in the translation that I would in one that was more familiar.  I said, “My translation says. . .” a
lot.  However, the differences in
translation didn’t lead to actual problems in understanding and interpreting
the text.  They just unsettled me.

**STUDY.** In my office, the LPUT did what I wanted
it to do.  I used the cross-references
quite a bit in preparing for my Revelation classes, and the size, though
slightly larger than I prefer, was never a problem.  I did miss a verse citation, but I think this
one was on me rather than on the LPUT. 
Unlike the various Crossway layouts, the verse numbers in the LPUT are a
different size than the quotation marks. 
There’s not much possibility for mistake, but apparently I can still
make one when I’m not paying attention.

Also, this shouldn’t matter, but I enjoy using the LPUT in
the office simply because the Bible is so much fun to hold.  I don’t think I’ve ever used a Bible more
lovable than this one.

**CONCLUSION.** On a pragmatic level, Bible design is
about sales.  Designers want to do such a
good job of setting a Bible that purchasers will buy the Bible simply because
of the setting.  The setting sells the
translation.  Crossway has been winning
this battle for years.  My congregation
has probably shifted from a majority-NASB group to a majority-ESV group because
Crossway Bible designs are so good.

I think the design of the LPUT rises to this height of
excellence.  I’ve been debating for a
month whether I want to switch to the CSB as my primary translation.  To be honest, I don’t think I would even be
having this conversation if the LPUT weren’t such a wonderfully designed
Bible.  It even has me reconsidering how
much I care about writing in Bibles!

When it comes to Bibles, I’m pretty jaded these days.  If the LPUT can get me so excited that I’m
debating whether it’s The One, this is a *good*
Bible.  I can’t give it anything else
here but a 10/10.
