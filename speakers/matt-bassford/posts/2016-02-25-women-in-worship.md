A few weeks ago, I blogged about the emerging practice among
some churches of Christ of putting women in teaching and even preaching
positions in the assembly.  To me, this
seems to be a clear violation of 1 Timothy 3:11-12.  In response to this post, I received a number
of questions about other issues related to women and authority.  Because of my particular interest in worship,
I’ve chosen to address two of those questions, whether women can sing in the
assembly and whether an assembly can sing a hymn written by a woman.



**Should women
participate in congregational singing, or does the Scripture require them to
remain silent?**

The key text here is 1 Corinthians 14:34-35, which reads, “The
women are to keep silent in the churches; for they are not permitted to speak,
but are to subject themselves, just as the Law also says. If they desire to
learn anything, let them ask their own husbands at home; for it is improper for
a woman to speak in church.” (NASB)  The
Greek word translated here as “silent” is *sigaō*,
which does indeed mean, “to keep silence, hold one’s peace”.

Note too that throughout this context, Paul is careful to
limit the scope of his instruction.  In 1
Corinthians 14:23, he emphasizes that he is addressing the time when “the whole
church assembles together.”  Likewise,
14:26 frames the discussion in terms of what should happen “when you
assemble”.

This language is the same as the “when you meet together”
language in the discussion of the Lord’s Supper in 1 Corinthians 11.  In both cases, the Greek word in question is
synerchomai.  These texts aren’t merely
about times when more than one Christian happens to be the same place; they’re
about what we would call a worship service, when every member of the
congregation who is able to attend is expected to be there.  When we read 1 Corinthians 14:34 in this
light, it may well be that “women are to keep silent in the churches” would be
better translated as “women are to keep silent in assemblies.”

On its face, then, 1 Corinthians 14:34-35 seems to be
teaching that women shouldn’t make any noise during assemblies.  This reading appears to contradict the
commands of Ephesians 5:19 and Colossians 3:16, in which all Christians are
instructed to sing.

As always, we should accept the facial reading of a passage
unless there is a compelling Scriptural reason not to.  However, I believe that such a Scriptural
reason exists with respect to 1 Corinthians 14:34-35.

This analysis begins with the question, “Silent as opposed
to what?”  Our immediate reaction is
“Silent as opposed to making noise,” but that’s not the only way to read the
text.  It’s equally logical to answer the
question by saying “Silent as opposed to being the speaker,” and indeed,
throughout the context, Paul is concerned with how speakers in an assembly
should conduct themselves, rather than with how the others assembling should
conduct themselves.

Tongue-speakers should only speak if they have an
interpreter, and then one at a time, two or three at most in one assembly.  If there is no interpreter, the
tongue-speaker must keep silent (14:27-28). 
Prophets should speak one at a time, with two or three at most speaking
in one assembly.  If another prophet
receives a revelation, the first prophet is to keep silent (14:29-30).  Women are never to speak at all, but are to
keep silent (14:34-35).  Unless we want
to argue that this text prohibits tongue-speakers and prophets from singing in
the assembly (in the situations where Paul instructs them to keep silent), we
can’t argue that his injunction should keep women from singing in the assembly
either.

This is the best textual reading, and it is powerfully
supported by Paul’s teaching on worship elsewhere.  In particular, look at Romans 15:5-6, which
reads, “Now may the God who gives perseverance and encouragement grant you to
be of the same mind with one another according to Christ Jesus, so that with
one accord you may with one voice glorify the God and Father of our Lord Jesus
Christ.”

Even though the verse does not mention singing, it is
clearly about singing.  In what other
worship activity do Christians glorify God with one voice?  This text emphasizes the importance of inward
and outward unity in our song worship. 
We’re not supposed to be silently listening to a soloist.  We are to be glorifying God together.

Because of the extended list of greetings in Romans 16, we
have a much better idea about the identity of the audience of this Pauline
epistle than we do of most others (the letters to Timothy, Titus, and Philemon
being obvious exceptions).  Of the people
to whom Paul was writing, at least eight were women:  Priscilla (16:3), Mary (16:6), Tryphaena,
Tryphosa, and Persis (16:12), Rufus’s mother (16:13), and Julia and Nereus’s
sister (16:15).

The number may be higher, but in either case, the point is
clear.  Women were unquestionably among
those Christians whom Paul expected to glorify God with one accord and one
voice.  When Christian women sing in our
assemblies today, it is no violation of God’s expectations.  It is a powerful assertion of our unity in
Christ.

**Can a congregation
sing hymns written by women, or should those hymns be removed from our
repertoire?**

The key passage in answering this question is 1 Timothy
2:11-12, which reads, “A woman must quietly receive instruction with entire
submissiveness. But I do not allow a woman to teach or exercise authority over
a man, but to remain quiet.” (NASB)  The
text appears to consider two possible situations:  one in which a man is teaching/exercising authority
over a woman, and another in which a woman is teaching/exercising authority
over a man.  Of the two, only the first
is acceptable.  Women are not to be
teachers or leaders in the church (as per 1 Timothy 3:15).  Men are.

This does not mean that a woman can never say or do anything
in a Bible class or even an assembly from which a man might learn.  After all, it’s quite possible to teach even without
saying a word.  Consider, for instance,
the woman of God who brings her unbelieving husband to Wednesday night Bible
class.  Even if she never makes a comment
throughout the entire session, she has still taught an important spiritual
lesson to her husband and to others who are paying attention.  According to 1 Peter 3:1-2, she has done
exactly what she’s supposed to be doing. 
The only way to prevent Christian women from teaching lessons like that
in our assemblies and Bible classes is to bar them from attending altogether.

The problem, then, can’t be that a woman has taught
something.  It can only be that a woman
has become the teacher.  She has become
the one exercising authority.

Though it may not be obvious to anybody but a hymnist, this
point is enough to answer our question. 
As I and any other hymn writer will attest, a hymnist, whether male or
female, is not in a position of teaching or authority.  As a result, there is no Scriptural bar to
the use of a hymn written by a woman.

When I preach a sermon, I am in a position of teaching and
authority.  I decide what the topic is
going to be, and I decide how it is going to be presented.  What I decide is going to happen in the
assembly is what happens.  The brethren
learn what I want to teach them.  I am,
quite literally, the man.

Likewise, when I lead singing, I am once again the teacher
and authority figure.  I decide what the
brethren ought to be using to teach and admonish one another in song, and I
prepare my song list accordingly.  It’s
my call to make.  Worship will proceed
according to my decisions.  I’m still the
man.

By contrast, when I write a hymn, I have no authority
whatsoever to decide what happens to that hymn. 
I can pray about it.  I can have
hopes that the hymn will be used.

However, those decisions are entirely up to somebody else
(unless I lead my own hymns, which I greatly dislike doing).  If song leaders, who are the real
teaching/authority figures, decide to use my hymn, it will be used.  If they don’t, it won’t.  This utter lack of authority is actually one
of the most frustrating things about being a hymnist!

For a song leader to lead a hymn written by a woman, then,
is no different from a preacher quoting the words of a woman from the pulpit (as
I myself have been known to quote my mother). 
In either case, she is not the authority figure.  He is, and the decision to teach using her
words is his.
