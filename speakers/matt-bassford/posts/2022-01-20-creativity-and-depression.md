On my post about antidepressants last Friday, my dear friend
Andy Diestelkamp made a comment that concluded, “I do wonder, however, if a
sunnier Matt Bassford would have deprived us of songs like ‘Exalted’, ‘Evensong’,
and especially ‘A Foretaste of Your Rest’ or ‘This Time I Will Praise the Lord’.”  In this, Andy is referring to the widely
recognized connection between creativity and depression.  The depressive artist is a stereotype, and,
if my hymnist friends are any evidence, it’s more than that.

However, I think that the interplay between the two is more
complicated than depression causing creativity. 
If anything, it’s the opposite. 
Creativity causes depression. 



Obviously, I can speak only for myself here, but I perceive
creativity as an inner sensitivity and awareness.  I write by intuition, not analysis (I’m not
conventionally analytical at all), and when I’m working hard on something, I
turn deeply inward and. . . listen for the right answer.  The house could be burning down around me,
and I wouldn’t notice.  I’m lost in my
writing.

I think a tendency toward depression is the dark side of
this ability to self-focus.  I’m always
aware (unusually aware, I think) of what I’m thinking and feeling, but when I’m
feeling out of kilter, both thoughts and feelings get stuck in a negative
feedback loop.  I’m used to relying on my
intuition, but it gets distorted in such times, and I can no longer trust that
what I sincerely feel is valid.  It can
take me years to claw my way back out.

For me, writing has always been a coping strategy, and as
coping strategies go, it’s a pretty good one. 
It allows me to bleed off excess emotional energy, and instead of ending
up with a drug habit or similar, I end up with something that hopefully will
help others.  Win!

However, the coping strategy has its limits.  When I’m down at the bottom of the well, I don’t
write much, and what I do write generally isn’t very good.  To verify my impression here, I looked at the
dates of composition for my most widely known hymns.  With one exception (2000), the years in my
life when I was most depressed also were mostly barren of artistic achievement.  What I did write I generally achieved with
help, like a tune in the case of “Maranatha” (though I revised it extensively
for years afterward) or a tune, idea, and first verse in the case of “Servant
Song”. 

In short, it seems to me that creativity, like most traits,
has two sides.  That which allows me to
write also leaves me vulnerable to depression. 
An everyday amount of negative emotional energy puts an edge on my
writing and makes me better; an unusual amount shuts me down and makes me
miserable.  Antidepressants might have
changed the things I wrote and when I wrote them, but I don’t think they would
have kept me from writing or writing well. 

I regard my creativity as a gift from God (to the point
where I’m uneasy writing this because it seems me-glorifying instead of
Him-glorifying), but I think that depression is unequivocally born of the
devil, like all the other things in this fallen world that cause misery and
pain.  To the extent that medications,
counseling, etc., make his work harder, so much the better!
