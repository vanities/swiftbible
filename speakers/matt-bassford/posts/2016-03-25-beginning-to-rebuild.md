It’s a fact of life that everything on this earth runs
down.  Whatever we’ve established, be it
professional, personal, or spiritual, it runs down over time.  Even though it’s nice to catch the decay in
its early stages, there are times in all of our lives when we have to start
over.

This, of course, is not a new problem.  After the Babylonian captivity, the Jewish
nation found itself in the position of having to restore a whole land full of
ruined cities, including Jerusalem itself. 
Let’s see what lessons we can learn today from their process of
beginning to rebuild.



**Nehemiah in Susa**

Strange though it may seem to us, the rebuilding of the
walls of Jerusalem actually began thousands of miles away, during the time that
a man named Nehemiah spent in Susa, the capital of the Persian Empire.  It started with Nehemiah **MOURNING** about the state of the city of his fathers.  Look at Nehemiah 1:1-4.  Even though he is a Jewish exile, Nehemiah
has done quite well for himself.  He has
an important position as a palace official in Persia.  However, his faith is such that the bad news
about Jerusalem and the returned exiles upsets him deeply.  This isn’t his personal problem, but his love
for the Lord and His people makes it his personal problem.

In the same way, no spiritual problem, be it in our own
lives or in the church, is ever going to get solved unless we care about the
problem.  It’s easy to be
indifferent.  Our lives are going well,
so we shrug about some sin we allow to continue, some weak Christian in danger
of falling away, or some friend or co-worker who hasn’t heard the gospel.  Doesn’t bother us any!

Brethren, we must understand that apathy is a wrecker of
churches and a destroyer of souls.  If we
want the devil to win, all we have to do is nothing, and unless spiritual
problems upset us, nothing is all we’re going to do.  Let’s make God’s priorities our priorities
and mourn accordingly.

Nehemiah is upset, then, but before he does anything else,
he uses **PRAYER** to bring the problem
before God.  This appears in Nehemiah
1:5-11.  The first thing that stands out
about the prayer is Nehemiah’s humility. 
He wasn’t one of the people who worshiped idols and brought the
Babylonians down on the old kingdom of Judah, but he still says “We have
sinned.”  This isn’t God’s fault.  This is his fault and his people’s
fault.  Second, Nehemiah asks God to act
in accordance with the promises He has already made.  Third, Nehemiah doesn’t ask God to solve the
problem while Nehemiah does nothing. 
Instead, he asks God to help him as he works.

Like Nehemiah, we too need to own our spiritual
problems.  Our spiritual failings aren’t
due to circumstances beyond our control. 
They’re there because we chose to be evil.  Similarly, if our congregation has problems,
we’re responsible for those too because we’re part of the congregation.  If we’re looking to solve some spiritual
problem, we’re almost certainly covered by some promise that God has made, so
we don’t have to worry about that. 
However, we do need to recognize the disconnect between praying for
God’s help while doing nothing about the problem ourselves.  If we’re going to pray, let’s pray for God’s
help as we act to do His will.

Once he has prayed, we see Nehemiah demonstrating great **BOLDNESS** in pursuit of God’s
goals.  The story unfolds in Nehemiah
2:1-4.  This might not seem like any big
deal to us, but we need to remember that Nehemiah isn’t working for some middle
manager in a cube farm.  He is working
for the king of Persia, who was an absolute monarch, and if you did anything to
make the king’s day less than wonderful, you were in immense trouble.  If Nehemiah chooses the wrong words here, he
could very well end up with his head separated from his shoulders.  Nonetheless, he forges ahead in presenting
his problem and with God’s help, earns the king’s favor.

They say that the journey of a thousand miles begins with a
single step, and when we’re confronting some spiritual problem, that first step
is often the hardest.  Presumably, none
of us are going to be faced with death for trying to do God’s will, but we are
likely to have to speak truth to somebody who doesn’t want to hear it.  That’s when we too need to be bold.  If we paper over problems for the sake of peace,
the problems are only going to get worse.

Finally, we see that Nehemiah has **A PLAN** for what he wants to do to solve the problem.  Here, consider Nehemiah 2:5-8.  This is obviously not some spur-of-the-moment
blurt.  Instead, even before the king
invites Nehemiah to request help, Nehemiah has already figured out what he
wants to say.  If he hadn’t done this, he
would have embarrassed himself and lost the opportunity.

Today, friends, we need to be wary of falling into the trap
of admiring the problem.  Admiring the
problem is when you lament what a terrible, terrible problem it is, and how
this solution and this solution won’t work, but you never actually point out a
solution that you think will work.  As
spiritual traits go, admiring the problem is entirely useless.  God doesn’t need problem-pointer-outers.  God doesn’t need people who moan and say,
“Somebody should do something about that.” 
God needs people who say, “We need to do something about this, and
here’s how we’re going to do it.”  As the
saying goes, if we aren’t part of the solution, we’re part of the problem.

**Nehemiah in Jerusalem**

The second part of the story that we’re going to consider
this morning concerns what happens to Nehemiah once he reaches the vicinity of
Jerusalem.  First, we see Nehemiah taking
steps to **COMPREHEND THE PROBLEM.** Here, consider Nehemiah 2:9-16.  As the text explains, there are two main
kinds of problems that Nehemiah is facing. 
Most obvious are the physical obstacles that the Jews will encounter in
trying to rebuild the wall.  When the
Babylonians sacked the city in 583, they did a thorough job of it, so that the
wall was entirely destroyed, and Nehemiah’s survey reveals just how much work
there is to be done.

Second, we see two men mentioned for the first time:  Sanballat the Horonite and Tobiah the
Ammonite.  These men are going to be the
main human opponents of the rebuilding of the wall, and they will prove to be
just as much an obstacle as the ruin the Babylonians left behind them.  One gets the impression that Nehemiah has
already marked them out as troublemakers.

Note, though, that Nehemiah is keeping his own counsel about
all of this.  He doesn’t show up and
announce to the Jews that they’re going to be rebuilding the wall without
having studied the problem himself.  He
doesn’t ask the crowd for information about who his enemies are going to
be.  Instead, he gathers all of his
information privately before acting publicly.

In the same way, friends, when we’re working for the Lord,
we need to get a good handle on the scope of the problem before we try to go
solving it.  Some people are
impulsive.  Instead of “Ready, aim,
fire”, they prefer “Ready, fire, aim”.  That
kind of reckless behavior is likely to do as much damage to the Lord’s church
as inaction would.

Similarly, let’s use our own judgment to at least figure out
the broad outlines of what we should do before we go consulting others.  As the book of Nehemiah goes on to reveal,
Tobiah and Sanballat had plenty of allies among the Jews, and they could have
frustrated Nehemiah if he hadn’t already had enough information to act
decisively.  Likewise, if we point out
problems to brethren before we have a handle on the solution, that will often
lead to inaction.

Next, we see Nehemiah deftly **MANAGING THE REACTION** to his wall-building proposal once he goes
public.  Look at Nehemiah 2:17-20.  First, it’s obvious that he knows how to
handle his fellow Jews.  When he presents
what he wants to do, why he wants to do it, and why he thinks they can do it,
they straightforwardly accept his plan and get to work.  Second, Nehemiah has obviously anticipated
the criticism that he’s going to get from the likes of Tobiah, Sanballat, and
Geshem.  Rather than allowing them to
discourage him or the others who are working, he replies with a stinging rebuke
that permits the building to begin.

From this, friends, we should learn that any time we are
addressing a spiritual problem, unless it is entirely between our ears, solving
that problem is going to mean figuring out how to work with others.  Nehemiah’s a leader, not a tyrant.  He doesn’t trample over the Jews to do what
he wants.  Instead, he gets the buy-in he
needs to be successful.  We too will be
most effective when we are able to persuade others to help us wholeheartedly.

Also, we should be prepared to deal with people who don’t
want to help.  Sometimes, they’re being
honest in their opposition, and we simply need to devote time and patience to
winning them over.  At other times,
though, we are going to run into Tobiahs and Sanballats, both inside and
outside the church.  We can’t let people
who are determined to be obstructionist derail us either.

Finally, the text tells us how the Jews **BEGIN TO BUILD.** We could
read the entirety of Chapter 3 here, but for a sample of it, let’s just read
Nehemiah 3:1-2.  The whole chapter is
like this:  a list of names of people who
worked on the wall, most of whom never show up anywhere else in the whole
Bible, and the section of the wall that they built.

This isn’t the most poetic passage in the entire Bible, but
it does reveal two crucial things. 
First, Nehemiah and the other Jewish leaders didn’t rah-rah about how
great it would be to rebuild the wall and then go out and do nothing.  Instead, they got right to work.  Second, this wasn’t Nehemiah and his servants
out trying to build the whole wall themselves. 
This was a broad-based effort by the whole Jewish community.

Today, we should learn from both of these attributes.  Too often, when we’re addressing a problem,
whether it be inside ourselves, in our families, or in our congregation, we
come to complete agreement that something must be done, but then nobody
actually does anything.  At this point,
we need to answer the question, “How do we make sure that we are actually going
to begin doing this and follow through on what we have decided?”  Otherwise, nothing will happen.

Second, we have to resist the temptation to be a one-man or
one-woman band.  Sure, we can make
meaningful changes in our own lives by ourselves, but we can’t make meaningful
changes for a group by ourselves.  This
is where the buy-in from the previous point becomes so important.  We need to get people involved not only in
agreeing that a solution is important, but in carrying it out.
