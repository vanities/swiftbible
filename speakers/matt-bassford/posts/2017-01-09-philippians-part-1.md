It seems like I’ve been preaching nothing but
answering-questions sermons for several months now.  I think that’s important and good.  Even though the Bible is thousands of years
old, it’s relevant to all of us, and part of my job is to show everyone how
it’s relevant.

However, even things that are important and good can lead us
to overlook other things that are equally important and good.  When I preach a sermon on some topic, unless
there happens to be a whole context of Scripture that addresses precisely that
topic, the only way to cover the subject adequately is to pull passages from
several different parts of the Bible.

I think there’s truth in even a single verse of the word of
God; I wouldn’t quote from the Bible like that if I didn’t.  However, the fact remains that with the
exception of some parts of the Old Testament, the Bible wasn’t constructed to
be a reference work.  Instead, it’s a
collection of smaller works, each one of which has its own purpose and its own
meaning.  If we spend all our time
pulling verses out of context, we will never see the meaning that God intended.

It’s important, then, to study not only topics, but
texts.  With this in mind, let’s turn
this morning to the first part of the book of Philippians to see what lessons
we can learn from it.



**Paul’s Situation**

When we consider the first chunk of the book of Philippians,
we see that it has a lot to say about Paul’s situation.  Like most Pauline epistles, this one opens
with an **INTRODUCTION.** Let’s look at it in Philippians
1:1-2.  Within this text, there are a
couple of things worth noting.  The first
is that the recipient of this letter is the church in Philippi.  As with many of the churches Paul
established, the founding of this one was marked by persecution, but at this
point, we can tell that the church in Philippi is prospering.

Paul addresses the letter not only to the saints who make it
up, but also to the overseers and deacons there.  As we know all too well, establishing and
maintaining an eldership in a local congregation is hardly an easy task, but
the Philippians are getting the job done. 
Of all of Paul’s letters addressed to churches, this is the only one
also addressed to church leaders.

This address is also important for what it reveals about the
structure of an early congregation.  By
the second century, churches began to appoint bishops in the modern sense:  a singular leader who governs a modern
church.  Under the bishop were elders and
deacons.  Here, though, there is no
evidence of an earthly head for the church. 
It’s not part of the pattern.

From here, Paul moves into a segment of **THANKSGIVING.** Let’s read
here from Philippians 1:3-8.  We can’t
read this without being struck by the warmth of Paul’s tone.  The book of Acts reveals that by the time of
Paul’s imprisonment in Rome, when he writes this epistle, he’s already visited
Philippi several times, and it’s obvious that these are Christians whom he
really loves.

In particular, Paul is especially thankful for their
devotion to him.  The Philippians have
shown Paul their affection in the most meaningful way possible:  by helping him when he needed help.  From the second missionary journey, right
after he founded the church, all the way through to his imprisonment in Rome,
the Philippians have been helping him financially, and Paul understands that
their willingness to help reveals their love for him.

All this is important because it shows us that support of
preachers is spiritually significant. 
Paul isn’t excited and joyful here because he’s gotten all this
money.  As he reveals later, he knows how
to get by with next to nothing.  Instead,
he’s excited because the Philippians’ willingness to give shows that they’re on
the right spiritual track.  Their behavior
reveals that Jesus is at work in them, and Paul is confident that the Lord will
finish what He started.  Even today, we
must learn to see our support of preachers as a spiritual investment, to look
on it not with the eyes of a banker, but with the eyes of Christ.  Helping and rewarding godly men in their work
is good for us too!

Next, we see **PAUL’S
PRAYER** for the Philippians.  It
appears in Philippians 1:9-11.  We’re
familiar with the “add to your faith virtue” passage in 2 Peter 1, but this
prayer reveals a different spiritual progression.  Rather than ending with love, it begins with
love, the love that the Philippians have already revealed in their support of
Paul.  Paul asks that their love might
lead to knowledge and discernment, which in turn will lead them to approve the
excellent.  This then should lead to blameless
behavior, which will yield the fruit of righteousness, which will glorify
God.

This tells us, friends, that the foundation of understanding
and obeying God’s will must be love, the self-sacrificing love that desires the
good of others as the Philippians desired the good of Paul.  We aren’t going to be able to tell up from
down spiritually unless we read the word through eyes that have learned to look
with love on our brother.  Without love,
our understanding of the Scripture will be flawed, our ability to recognize
good and evil in the world will be equally flawed, and our attempts to serve
God will be flawed too.  Ultimately, He
cannot be glorified by loveless Christians. 
As Jesus says, everything depends on love.  If we get that right, a whole lot of other
things will fall into place.  Let’s
practice this ourselves and pray that others will too.

**Paul and Conflict**

The next section of the text of Philippians concerns Paul’s
perspective on the conflict that was such a part of his life and will
inevitably be part of the life of any genuine follower of Christ.  This begins with Paul’s description of **HIS IMPRISONMENT.** Look here at Philippians 1:12-18.  There may be no better passage in the Bible
to illustrate what a difference our attitude can make in our lives.  Here, we see that Paul has been imprisoned by
the Roman authorities for no greater crime than preaching the gospel.  Most of us in that situation would be as glum
as we could be, but Paul’s attitude is one of great joy.  So what if he’s imprisoned?  He’s still going to keep on preaching
Christ!  As a result, even his captors
know all about the gospel.  What’s more,
his example has emboldened the other Christians in Rome to proclaim Christ too.

However, there are problems even with this.  Some of these substitute preachers are using
the gospel as a way to promote themselves and try to make Paul look bad.  They’re hoping that every time Paul hears
about them, it’s going to twist the knife a little more.

Here too, though, Paul’s joyful attitude isn’t even
dented.  Who cares if these people are
preaching Christ under false pretenses? 
They’re still preaching Christ! 
Paul’s gospel has never been about himself.  It’s always been about the Lord, and as long
as He’s glorified, Paul is pleased.

I don’t know about you, but when I read that, the troubles
in my life start seeming pretty minor, and my reaction to them starts seeming
pretty sulky.  We whine a whole lot more
than Paul does with a whole lot less excuse! 
Instead, we need to learn from him to glorify God in every situation of
our lives.  If that’s our goal, we’re
certain to achieve it and find joy in the achievement.

Paul continues this discussion by explaining **HIS EXPECTATIONS.** They appear in Philippians 1:18-20.  Paul isn’t merely rejoicing in the present;
he’s rejoicing in the future too, and his reasons for future rejoicing are
twofold.  First, he rejoices because of
his conviction that the prayers of the faithful, including the Philippians,
will lead to his deliverance.  Prison is
a whole lot more bearable if you can see the light at the end of the tunnel!

Second, though, and more interestingly, Paul rejoices
because he believes that he will not be put to shame, but that Jesus will
always be exalted in him, if he lives or even if he dies.  This is an odd thing for him to say.  In earthly terms, being executed by the
authorities is about as shameful as it gets. 
Nobody brags about how their kid is on Death Row.

And yet, in Paul’s eyes, even that level of shame is still
glorious because of the way it lifts up Jesus. 
The death of every martyr says, “The Lord is important enough that I was
willing to die for Him.”  In the same
way, friends, we need not to be so concerned about our earthly position, our
earthly relationships, or our earthly reputation.  We need to be concerned about whether Jesus
is glorified in the way we live and even the way we die.  If we get that, we’ve got what’s important.

Next, we see Paul explain his thinking further as he talks
about the results of **LIFE AND DEATH** for
him.  Read on in Philippians
1:21-26.  Paul summarizes his point in
the famous epigram, “To live is Christ and to die is gain.”  Really, though, he means that to live is
Christ and to die is Christ.  If he
continues to live, he will continue to walk in the footsteps of Jesus, helping
others on to heaven like the Lord did during His ministry.  If he dies, he gets the reward of being with
the Lord forever, and he can’t imagine anything better than that.  He believes, though, that he’s going to
remain on earth so that he can be useful to the Philippians and others.

Here, I think we see the importance of a life founded on
Jesus.  If we build our lives on anything
else, we can fail.  The love of our life
can abandon us.  Our children can turn
out to be worthless.  Our businesses can
go belly-up.  There is no certainty in
anything under the sun.

However, nobody who puts their trust in Jesus will be
disappointed.  He will never fail us nor
forsake us, and even if all of these other disasters happen to us, when we put
Jesus first, we will always have what’s most important to us.  We get to serve and follow Him in this life,
and in the life to come, we get to be with Him. 
No force on heaven or on earth can stop it.

Finally, we see Paul encouraging the Philippians to **SHARE IN CONFLICT.** Look at Philippians 1:27-30.  Here, we plainly see how opposed the wisdom
of God is to the wisdom of the world. 
The wisdom of the world says that conflict is a bad thing, and that if
we find ourselves out of step with what most people around us believe, we
should go along to get along.  Similarly,
suffering is a bad thing.  Sensible
people avoid suffering at all costs.

Paul, though, describes conflict as a sign of salvation and
suffering as a blessing.  Of course, this
only applies when the conflict and the suffering come about as a result of the
gospel.  However, if we are faithful to
the gospel, we can be certain that the conflict and the suffering will come
too.  The world doesn’t like the truth of
the Bible, and if we’re noisy about that truth, it will oppose us.  If we still don’t shut up, we may lose
friendships and jobs over it.

However, when we find ourselves involved in conflict and
suffering because of our faith, we also find ourselves in good company:  the company of the Philippians, of Paul, and
of the Lord Himself.  There is no way to
follow Jesus and avoid these things.  The
only way to avoid them is to sign on with the world as yet another enemy of
God.  If we see trouble on the horizon
because of Christ, we don’t dare dodge it. 
We have to treat it, as the Philippians did, as a sign of salvation.

**The Imitation of Christ**

In the final part of this reading from Philippians, Paul
primarily concerns himself with the imitation of Christ.  This begins with **HIS CALL TO UNITY**.  We find
it in Philippians 2:1-4.  The content of
this reading links back to some of the things we saw at the end of chapter
1.  Notice that there, Paul tells the
Philippians that they have to conduct themselves in a manner worthy of the
gospel by standing firm in one spirit and striving together with one mind.  This is how we get there.

According to Paul, it begins with our motivation.  Rather than focusing on the things that the
devil puts in our way to make us bitter and divided, we’re supposed to look to
the encouragement of Christ, the fellowship of the spirit, and the affection
and compassion we have for one another. 
These things will inform our determination to be united.

Once we share in this will to unity, it will change the way
we behave.  Instead of acting from
selfishness and pride like people in the world, we will be more concerned about
our brethren than about ourselves.  We
won’t strive to get our own way.  We’ll
work for the best interests of others.

This concept is simple enough for a child to understand, but
it is one of the hardest things for any group of people, be it a family or a
church, to master.  In both situations,
strife is always the result of at least one person forgetting to put others
ahead of themselves.  We can have this
text memorized and still blow it. 
Instead, we need to examine ourselves and our hearts to make sure that
we are truly regarding one another as more important than ourselves.

Paul illustrates this principle by pointing to **THE MIND OF CHRIST.** He explains in Philippians 2:5-11.  Jesus could have insisted on His heavenly
equality with God, but instead, He emptied Himself to become one of us.  As though it were not enough to become a man,
He humbled Himself to the point of death on the cross, one of the most
humiliating, shameful deaths that a human can experience.  God then rewarded His perfect humility with
ultimate authority.

Brethren, if the humility of Jesus as recorded in this
passage doesn’t humble us, we aren’t paying attention.  We think we have to stand up for ourselves
because we have so much to protect.  If
we don’t look out for Number One, who will? 
And yet, Jesus gave up more than we can imagine without a single
complaint.  We have these resentments
that we cling to because someone offended our precious dignity, and yet Jesus
didn’t even cling to His own life.  This
is a text that should make it hard for us to look ourselves in the mirror
sometimes, and it should constantly call us on to be more like Him.  Our service to God is defined by the way that
God served us.

Paul then continues to present **THREE APPLICATIONS** we should take from Jesus’ humility and
authority.  They appear in Philippians
2:12-18.  The first of these is that the
Philippians, and indeed we ourselves, should work out our salvation with fear
and trembling.  This is both
humility-based and authority-based.  The
authority side is easy.  We should
tremble before the One who has the name above every name.  This makes sense for humility too,
though.  It’s impossible to say for sure,
of course, but I’ve known Christians before who seemed to have a problem with
pride.  To the extent that they were
working out their salvation at all, they were doing it with smug certainty
rather than fear and trembling.  They
forgot that their life belonged to God, not them.

Second, humble people who submit to the authority of Jesus
won’t grumble or dispute.  They know that
Jesus doesn’t want them to, and they’re too lowly-minded to want to insist on
their own way anyway.  Because grumbling
and disputing are so common among the worldly, when we live a life that is free
from those things, we’ll shine with Christ wherever we go.

Finally, Paul urges all Christians to rejoice.  The Philippians are supposed to rejoice even
if they hear that Paul is being martyred. 
Why?  Because the joy that we have
in Christ is immune to circumstances.  We
don’t need to fight over the scraps that matter so much to the worldly.  We have the hope of eternal life, and when
somebody has that hope, death isn’t the worst thing that can happen to
them.  It’s the best.
