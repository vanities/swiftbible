I’m now about two months into teaching the preschool class
for the Joliet church.  This is not the
result of some ghastly accident; instead, I volunteered to do it.  We’d been having trouble rounding up teachers
for the bottom two classes recently.  I
considered the problem and figured I might as well do my part.  Besides, I couldn’t very well encourage
others to do something I wasn’t willing to do myself!  Onto the signup sheet went my name.

I suspect my commitment raised more than a few eyebrows
among the members at Joliet, but I figured I could do it.  I live with a four-year-old and a
five-year-old.  I understand how they
think.  Besides, I’ve always enjoyed
teaching, and teaching is teaching, right?



Before the quarter started, I prepared by sitting in on one
class session (conveniently taught by my wife) and teaching another class
myself with her (unnecessarily) in attendance. 
I also resolved that I was going to act out all the stories using Lego
minifigures.  Due to my son’s interest in
Star Wars Legos, many of the characters have borne a strange resemblance to
Imperial stormtroopers.  White stormtroopers make great angels!

Before the quarter began, and even just after it started, I
collected a few you’re-a-better-man-than-I-am comments from the other men at
Joliet.  However, to be quite honest,
I’ve had very little trouble and a great deal of fun.  I believe there are several reasons for that.

First, it has helped considerably that I simply continued
the class structure I watched my wife use. 
It’s clear to me that the kids like the predictable, almost ritualistic
aspect of it.  Now we sing the attendance
song, now we sing other songs, now we sing the Bible songs, etc.  Everybody likes knowing what’s coming next.

For that matter, it’s useful that I enjoy singing, even if I
miss the melody for “I and II Chronicles” in the books-of-the-Old-Testament
song every time.

Third, I think I benefit in some ways from being a man.  This runs counter to the received wisdom in
churches of Christ.  Almost universally,
women teach lower-division Bible classes because of the presumption that they
are more nurturing, more caring, and better equipped to relate to the children.  It is certainly true that women have a unique
role in training children, but the same thing is true of men.  In fact, it is fathers rather than mothers
who are commanded to bring their children up in the discipline and instruction
of the Lord.  A man might teach a
children’s class differently than a woman would, but some of those differences
are bound to be helpful.

For instance, I haven’t had any real struggles keeping the
class under control.  In most families
with Christian parents, it is the man rather than the woman who is the authority
figure.  As a result, children are more
likely to take a male teacher’s words as authoritative.  On a very few occasions when the students
continued to act up, I deployed my Daddy Voice, which elicited startled jumps,
wide eyes, and complete obedience.

Similarly, I have reason to believe that male students
relate better to male teachers.  Like
most small children, my four-year-old son has a particularly close relationship
with his mother.  If you ask him whom he
wants to snuggle him or tuck him in, it’s going to be Mama every time.  However, Marky has been insistent the whole
quarter that he’d rather have Daddy than Mama teaching his Bible class.  I’m less likely to sweat the small stuff, and
besides, Daddy acts out Bible stories with Lego storm troopers!

I’ve felt this to be true even beyond my own son.  The other semi-regular male student in the
class is a boy whom his grandmother brings to church sometimes.  From the first class I taught with him in it,
he took a shine to me.  I get the sense
that it matters to him, and matters to him a lot, that a man is teaching his
Bible class.  In the mind of a
four-year-old boy, if a man does it, it must be important.

Of course, there are ways in which teaching a children’s
Bible class is more difficult for a man. 
Most notably, men have to deal with the fear that any man who wants to
teach children is a sexual predator looking for opportunities to prey upon the
students.

Nor is this fear unfounded. 
I suspect children are much more likely to be molested by a teacher,
even a Bible-class teacher, than they are by some stranger in a trench coat who
jumps out of the bushes.  Congregations
ought to be vigilant.  This applies not
only in the case of men who want to teach lower-division classes, but in the
case of men who want to teach more traditional middle-school Bible classes too
(where the scrutiny is often much less).

A man teaching children, then, has to have regard not only
for what is honorable in the sight of God, but also for what is honorable in
the sight of others.  I suspect that most
female Bible-class teachers will take a four-year-old boy to the restroom
without a second thought.  A man should
never take a four-year-old girl to the restroom unless she is his own
daughter.

This gave me some trouble in the very first class I
taught.  I had meant to get cell-phone
numbers from the mothers of my students so that I could text them if one of the
students needed a potty break.  I didn’t,
so naturally, right in the middle of class, one of the students had to use the
facilities.  I had to march her upstairs
and hand her off to her mother, which disrupted the flow of the class pretty
thoroughly.  I promptly got cell-phone
numbers for everybody and predictably haven’t had to use them since.

As for other concerns, it helps that the interior walls of
our classrooms don’t block sound very well, so just as I have a pretty good
idea what the guy next door is up to, he pretty much knows what I’m doing too
(and doubtless winces every time I sing “I and II Chronicles” wrong).  He and I have been friends for years, but I
know he wouldn’t hesitate to confront me if he thought I was up to no good.

However, men of God shouldn’t allow the specter of sexual
predation to keep them from being useful. 
Teaching a lower-division class isn’t for every man, but it’s probably
for more men than most think.  Aptitude
for teaching translates downhill just fine, and men have some things to offer
students that women don’t.

Nor do all the benefits flow one way.  I think that teaching a gaggle of
preschoolers has made me a better preacher. 
It keeps me focused on the importance of telling a good story, and in
wanting a good story, adults aren’t all that different from children.  Besides, even for old souls like me, revisiting
childhood with actual children can be a lot of fun.  Lego stormtroopers for the glory of
God—what’s not to love?
