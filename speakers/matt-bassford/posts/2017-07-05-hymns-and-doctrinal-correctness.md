It should be no secret to anyone who reads my blog that I
desperately want hymns to Say Something. 
Teaching and admonishing with a content-free hymn is about like eating
with no food.  You can go through the
motions, but you aren’t going to accomplish much.



**False Doctrine**

Interestingly, though, most brotherhood criticism of hymn
content hasn’t been about hymns that say nothing.  It’s been about hymns that say the wrong
thing.  On one level, this makes
sense.  We certainly don’t want to import
false doctrine into our assemblies, and there are hymns that teach false
doctrine.  Most of us would not be
interested in singing the original couplet from Fanny J. Crosby’s “To God Be
the Glory” that reads,

The vilest offender who truly believes

That moment from Jesus a pardon receives.

Singing that line would undermine every sermon on New-Testament
baptism ever preached!  As a result, for
the past 50 years, the editors of brotherhood hymnals have used an altered
version of the couplet instead.  That may
not be the best answer (some believe that respect for authorial intent should
lead the editor simply to omit the verse), but it is one that has been
generally acceptable to churches of Christ.

**Doctrinal Witch Hunts**

At the other extreme, though, is the determination not only
to address cases of clear error, but to ferret out every use of language that
might be erroneous.  This impulse, though
still alive and well today, found its highest expression in Ellis Crum’s *Sacred Selections*, which I will forever know
as *Sacred Corrections.*

Perhaps my favorite example of Crum’s editing style is in
the hymn originally titled “When We All Get to Heaven”.  In his hands, it became “When the Saved Get
to Heaven”.  He may originally have been
concerned that the hymn taught universalism (as though “we all” cannot apply to
the contents of a church instead of the contents of a planet).

Instead, though, I prefer to imagine Sister Church Dragon surveying
the congregation with a cold, disapproving eye, concluding that some of *those* folks aren’t going to measure up,
and singing “when **the saved**
get to heaven” in a harsh, flat alto with emphatic intent.  I’m teaching and admonishing *you*, slackers!  Hint, hint.

However, as much amusement as I gain from Sister Church
Dragon (and I’ve known women in the church who absolutely would have thought
and done this), I’d rather worship with the version of the hymn that allows me
to be optimistic about my fellow congregants.

**For Hymnists**

Clearly, there’s a difference between lyrics that we must
not sing without altering and lyrics that are only a problem when viewed
through jaundiced eyes.  I think, though,
that the line between the two varies based on situation.

For instance, the position of hymnists is different than the
position of anybody else.  We are the
ones with authorial rights in our own work. 
We can tweak and edit endlessly if we so desire.

However, we are also the ones with the least control over
whether our hymns are sung.  When the
song leader calls out the number of our hymn in an assembly someplace, we don’t
get to stand up and give a little speech about what we really meant.  As a result, if there’s an apparent doctrinal
issue in something we’ve written (whether there actually is nor not), if the
song leader leads it even once, he’s going to get pulled aside and probably
won’t lead it again.  There are too many
hymns that don’t cause trouble for congregations to use the hymns that do.  Ironically, I think some churches prefer
hymns that say nothing because such hymns necessarily also say nothing
objectionable.

In short, if you want your work sung, learn to give the
brotherhood no occasion for reproach.

**For Elders**

By contrast, members of local congregations don’t have power
to alter a hymn, but they do have the power to determine whether it will be
sung or not.  In some ways, elders have
the most difficult job here, even if they aren’t actually leading singing.  On the one hand, they have a responsibility
to shepherd the flock, but on the other hand, they are also responsible for not
lording it over other brethren.

I’ve heard of elderships that developed lists of hymns in
the church hymnal that were not to be sung (or actually marked out those hymns
in the physical hymnals with the Red Stamp of Death).  I think this runs the risk of being perceived
as high-handed.  I won’t say that an
eldership should never put hymns on such a list, but I think they should regard
it as a last resort, only to be employed if they are convinced that there is no
way a particular hymn can be sung in spirit and in truth.  Generally, the egregious stuff has been
filtered out by the hymnal editor already. 
Besides, if the elders don’t trust a song leader to select sound hymns,
why are they letting him lead singing in the first place?

**For Song Leaders**

Instead, much of this local responsibility falls on the
shoulders of the song leader.  When we
sing together, we are all teachers, but it is the song leader who determines
the content of the lesson.  It’s probably
true that a man we would not trust to teach an adult Bible class should not be
leading singing on Sunday morning either (though I think it’s wise to give
younger and less experienced men the opportunity to develop their skills—with
guidance from others—during less prominent worship occasions).

Song leaders need to thoughtfully consider their hymn lists,
ask whether each hymn on the list is a good vehicle for spiritual instruction,
and determine whether the brethren will be able to sing each hymn on the list
in good conscience.  Here, I think song
leaders are wise to err on the side of caution. 
If a hymn might cause trouble, why lead it?

**For Worshipers**

Finally, we come to worshipers in the pew.  From some, the advice is “If you don’t like
it, don’t sing it.”  I myself, though, am
extremely reluctant to refuse to sing.  I
have a responsibility to encourage my brethren, and when others notice my
silence, the result can only be discouraging. 
Nor do I care for the notion that my refusal to sing will be instructive
for the song leader.  That strikes me as
awfully passive-aggressive.  If I’ve got
that much of a problem with a hymn he leads, I should go talk to him about it.

I will only exercise the nuclear option when I encounter a
hymn that is not merely bad but that is actively untrue.  I might wish that the song leader had given
me a better tool for teaching and admonition, but I’m going to use the one I’ve
been handed to teach and admonish unless I find myself teaching something I
believe to be false.  I can’t sing such
hymns in good conscience.

For the rest, though (which makes up the vast majority of
the church’s repertoire), I think it’s my responsibility to teach and admonish
as best as I can.  If I’m given a choice
between understanding a hymn in a truthful way (“Nor if I’ll walk the vale with
Him” is about the valley of death in Psalm 23) and understanding it in an
untruthful way (“Nor if I’ll walk the vale with Him” is about premillennialism),
I’m going to choose to mean it in the truthful way rather than refusing to sing
it.  I will do this even when the author
may originally have intended to teach false doctrine (as is probably the case
with“I Know Whom I Have Believed”).  The
phraseology of the hymn still gives me the opportunity to use it to teach
truth.

Generally, the original intent of the hymnist is less
important than what we can legitimately make a hymn mean.  Of course, reasonable brethren can differ
about whether a particular line can be re-construed or is so blatantly false
that there’s no intellectually honest way to understand it as truth.  However, it’s true that 99.9 percent of the
time, there is some correct way to understand the hymns in our hymnals.  We need to be generous enough to look for it.

**Conclusion**

It’s good to pay attention to the content of our hymns.  Indeed, we are commanded to.  However, it is neither helpful nor wise to
pick at every little detail in a hymn that might be false doctrine.  Typically, we offer problematic worship not
because we are singing hymns that are false. 
Instead, we offer it because we are singing hymns that are meaningless
or singing meaningful hymns in a thoughtless way.  We ought to be concerned about the rare
problem too, but we should not allow it to distract us from the common one.
