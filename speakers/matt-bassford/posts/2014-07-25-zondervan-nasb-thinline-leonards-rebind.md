As much as I like the text setting of this Thinline, it
simply wasn’t a Bible I wanted to make part of my regular rotation in its
straight-from-the-publisher form.  I
spend enough time with my Bibles that I knew I would wear out a polyurethane or
bonded-leather binding.  A nice supple
genuine-leather cover like Foundation puts on many of its Bibles would have
been acceptable, but Zondervan doesn’t publish a GL Thinline.

In consequence, I decided that I wanted to have the Bible
rebound by Leonard’s Books.  I’d heard
good things about them both from the Internet and from people I knew who had
used their services, so I decided I wanted to see for myself.  I also decided I wanted to review the Bible
and have it be a blind review.  I paid
for the rebind just as an ordinary customer would and didn’t tell the Leonard’s
staff I was planning on reviewing the results until they had already put the
Bible in the mail.  I admit I feel a
little guilty about not being completely forthright with the Leonard’s staff,
but in the end, I decided a review of the Leonard’s experience from the
perspective of Nobody Special would prove valuable to others.



Even though they had no special reason to treat me well, my
experience with Leonard’s customer service was excellent from beginning to
end.  I interacted with three different
Leonard’s representatives, Margie, Kelsi, and Valerie, and all three of them
were unfailingly courteous, friendly, and helpful.  They responded quickly to all of my e-mails,
invited me to supply every detail of my preferences, and, when I sent them my
Bible, quoted me a price that was exactly what their price list online had led
me to expect.

This certainty was very important to me.  I looked at some other rebinders online, but
a couple of them wanted me to fill out a price-quote form before they would even
give me an idea of how much my rebind might cost.  In fact, Leonard’s was the only rebinder I could find at the time (this has since changed) who supplied an up-front estimate for a goatskin rebind.  This seems like an obvious way for all rebinders to improve their customer service.

The text block I sent Leonard’s is not possessed of any
sterling qualities besides its setting. 
The paper is probably under 20 gsm; the edges are silvered rather than
gilded.  Nonetheless, I decided I wanted
it rebound in goatskin.  As long as I’m
going to the trouble and expense of sending the thing off in the first place,
why not go whole hog. . . um. . . goat? 
I know it’s not a terribly traditional look, but to go with the
silvering, I opted for Leonard’s chocolate soft-tanned goatskin with twin
navy-blue ribbons.  Leonard’s told me to
expect the Bible back in four weeks, and indeed, four weeks to the day after we
settled on the details, they e-mailed me to let me know the Bible was en route.

I’ve never owned nor even handled a hand-bound Bible before,
so once I shucked my rebind out of its packaging (supplying my kids with weeks’
worth of bubble wrap in the process), the sensation was foreign to my
experience.  Indeed, the first word that
came to mind (after, “Wow, that’s soft!”, at least) was “overengineered”.

As I’ve discussed already, the original poly cover of the
Thinline did not impress.  It felt like
somebody on the Zondervan design team had decided to stretch that polyurethane
out as much as they thought they could get away with, all to save a few pennies
from the cost of production.  The
high-end natural-grain goatskin that Leonard’s used for the rebind is MUCH
thicker, despite being a much pricier material. 
In fact, the goatskin is so thick that, where the goatskin is doubled on
the edges, the ends of the spine flare out. 
The same is true of the top and bottom of the cover itself when I look
at it spine-on.

The result is a cover that feels almost decadently soft and
thick.  When it is combined with the
simple silver stamping on the spine, the Bible looks and feels like it came
from 1854 instead of 2014.  This makes me
happy.  I’m enough of a Luddite that I
love handmade goods.  My house is filled
with handmade furniture, rugs, and quilts, all of which have that same
gloriously inefficient feel.  It’s a
delight to have a Bible that doesn’t handle like it was designed by a
mathematical equation.

Of course, part of enjoying handmade goods is accepting and
even appreciating their imperfections.  I
don’t set my handmade desk on the curb because one of the drawers is a little
temperamental.  I don’t toss a handmade
quilt in the garbage because some of the stitching is uneven.   No
human hand, no matter how careful, will consistently match the precision of a
machine.

My rebind has its
imperfections too.  The top and bottom
margin between the endpaper and the edge of the cover varies by about a
sixteenth of an inch from edge to spine. 
I suppose that means that the Bible is set crookedly in the cover by
some infinitesimal amount.  I don’t
care.  Margie was very clear that I
should talk to her if I had some problem with the results, and I haven’t felt
the need to bring it up with her.

Instead, I mention this only so that if someone else gets a
Bible rebound by Leonard’s, and there’s some tiny flaw with it, they won’t
freak out.  Handmade goods can be
imperfect and yet still be quality work, and that is certainly true of my
rebind.  When I show the rebound Bible to
other Christians after services, nobody says, “Ohhh, that cover’s a teeny bit
crooked!”  Instead, they say it’s the
finest Bible binding they’ve ever seen. 
I’m sure it is.  It’s certainly
the finest binding I’ve ever seen.

In short, I could not be more pleased with the results of my
rebinding experiment, blind review and all. 
I really liked the Zondervan text setting, but I wasn’t willing to
invest myself in a Bible with such a cheap-feeling cover.  Now, the same Bible is a joy to hold.

Fridays on this blog are dedicated to the proposition that
Christians should use different Bibles for different purposes.  However, I like my rebound Thinline so well
that I actually tried to make it my one-and-only, using it everywhere from the
pulpit to the glove box on vacation.  Ultimately, that didn't work out; the Thinline can't compete with dedicated full-size Bibles as a preaching tool.  Nonetheless, it says a great deal about the overall quality of the Bible that I even made the attempt.
