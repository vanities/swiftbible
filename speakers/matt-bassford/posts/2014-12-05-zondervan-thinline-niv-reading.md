I spent the usual amount of time doing my daily reading from
the Thinline NIV, even though I didn’t do it in the usual place.  The Bible accompanied me to a hymnwriting
seminar I attend every year, which meant that it got used as much in my dad’s
living room as in my office at home.  I
admit I did cheat a couple of times and read from my dad’s Foundation SCR
instead of retrieving the Thinline NIV from the car (50 feet is a long way!),
but I still feel like I used it enough to get a good impression of its
quality.  Here’s what I thought:

**SIZE.** The Thinline NIV is. . . a thinline.  I generally like the size of thinlines for
reading, and this Bible was no exception. 
Even though I carried out my readings in a number of different
environments (we also stopped to visit my sister for a night), in which I am
traditionally more distracted, I still didn’t really notice the size of the
Thinline NIV.  In my opinion, a paperback
book is about the ideal size for reading from hard copy, and even if the
Thinline NIV doesn’t have that form exactly, it’s still within spitting
distance.  No reason to complain about
that!



**LAYOUT.** As I’ve noticed before, the Thinline NIV
appears to be inspired by Crossway’s Thinline (ESV), and the same features that
make the Crossway such a useful reading Bible are here too.  Like the Thinline ESV, the Thinline NIV is a
double-column Bible, sans center reference column.  What references exist are tucked discreetly
at the bottom of the page where they interfere with nothing.  The Zondervan is a skoche narrower than the
Crossway, about an eighth of an inch or so, but this doesn’t appear to have
narrowed its text columns much.  Each one
of them is hair wider than two inches.

As a result of these things, the Thinline NIV carries a
notable number of characters per line.  I
make it out to be in the neighborhood of 44 cpl.  That is excellent, as high a number I’ve seen
out of any double-column Bible.  It works
well with prose, of course—you have to be down around 30 cpl before a
double-column Bible starts causing trouble in the prose sections of the
Scripture.  In theory, it should also
fare well in the poetically formatted portions of the word.

In practice, however, that isn’t quite how things work out,
at least from me.  As I’ve already noted,
the Thinline NIV carries over many of the best design features of the Thinline
ESV.  Indeed, I would say that in some
areas, it even improves on that design. 
For instance, I like the Zondervan’s Utopia better as a typeface than
the Crossway’s Lexicon No. 1.  Utopia is
lighter-weight than Lexicon No. 1, so the contrast between boldfaced heading
and normal text is greater.

However, in some places, the Thinline NIV kept features from
the Thinline ESV that I don’t like.  In
particular, I don’t care for the way that both format the Psalms.  As far as I can tell, throughout all of its
editions of the Bible, Crossway has developed a policy of indenting second
poetic lines in a verse, and of double-indenting portions of lines that run on
to the next line.

In Crossway’s single-column Bibles, this looks
beautiful.  I’ve never seen a Bible with
a lovelier layout than the Crossway Legacy in, for instance, the book of
Lamentations.  It’s the way that poetry
should be formatted.

However, it doesn’t work nearly as well in the poetry
sections of double-column Bibles. 
Because there are so many double-indented run-on lines, the whole column
takes on an almost-centered look, which isn’t clean or pretty.  Both the Thinline ESV and Thinline NIV have
this unfortunate design feature.  I
prefer instead the layout of Zondervan’s older Thinline NASB, in which second
lines aren’t indented at all and overhangs are only single-indented.  Among other things, I think my eye has an
easier time locating the overhangs when they aren’t indented nearly to the
middle of the column.

Unfortunately, my reading plan had taken me to Psalms during
the time of the Thinline NIV, so I was confronted with this design flaw
repeatedly.  Other than that, though, the
Zondervan’s layout was superb for reading: 
paragraphed, no obtrusive reference columns.

**READABILITY.** This is about what you would expect for a
thinline.  It has a lot of quirks that
don’t bother me but are likely to bother others.  I’m fine with the Thinline NIV’s 8-point
type; many are not.  It isn’t a
line-matched Bible, and that annoys some people.  Because it’s a thinline, the paper isn’t very
opaque, so there’s plenty of ghosting.  I
more or less ignored all of these things, but if they’re likely to bother you,
turn your attentions elsewhere.

However, there was one feature of the Thinline NIV that made
me grind my teeth a little bit.  Unlike
my Thinline ESV (which is the Value Edition), it’s a words-of-Christ-in-red
Bible.  I have yet to see a mass-market
Bible that does a really good job of handling the words of Christ in red, and
the Thinline NIV conforms to the sad rule. 
Its red text isn’t red, really; it’s more orangey.  It may be that the light weight of the
typeface used does the Bible no favors here.

As I’ve found elsewhere, light “red” text backed up by black
text on the other side of the page takes a moderate ghosting problem and makes
it worse.  Unless the line-matching
coincidentally happens to be perfect, even I will start noticing the images
from beyond.  As always, I cannot help
but hope that someday, Bible publishers will abandon the use of words of Christ
in red.  However, I fear that this is
unlikely.  As long as most mass-market
Bibles are red-letter, consumers will continue to expect red-letter Bibles, and
the cycle of frustration will continue.

**CONCLUSION.** The Zondervan Thinline NIV is basically
the Crossway Thinline.  It’s a little
different in a few ways, but the impact of those changes is neutral.  I therefore assign it the same score as a
reading Bible as I assigned the Crossway: 
4.5/5.
