A few weeks back, I brought my Tyndale Select over to my
photographer’s house so that we could compare it to his Cambridge Clarion.  As I’ve mentioned before, he’s the one of the
two of us who really knows typography and design.  He’s the one who used to carry a copy of
Bringhurst around like a second Bible. 
After we got through checking out leading, text figures, small-cap
fonts, and other minutiae, he opined, “This is the way the Bible would look if
I got to design it.”

I don’t see any reason to dispute his judgment.  The setting of the Select (and of its sibling
the Schuyler Caxton) is magnificently designed.



This magnificent design, however, comes from an unusual
source.  Whenever I review a Bible that
strikes me as being really well laid out, I generally assume that it’s the work
of an outside typography firm, such as 2K or Blue Heron.  Bibles designed in-house usually don’t wow
me.  I was surprised, then, to turn to
the copyright page of the Select and not see another design company’s name
there.  A little later, when I was
chatting with evangelicalbible.com’s Beth Rhodes, she confirmed that the Select
was indeed a Tyndale design.

Based on what else I’ve seen from Tyndale, though, this
isn’t as surprising as it might be if it came from some other publishers.  First of all, the formatting of the NLT
itself shows some savvy.  Some design
decisions are general to a translation rather than to a particular Bible, and
the NLT design decisions are solid.

From what I’ve seen, all NLT’s have dialogue in Scripture broken out by speaker, as it would be in a novel.  All NLT’s have the couplets in the back half of Proverbs separated by hard returns so that they read as discrete logical units.  All NLT’s have Paul’s handwritten postscripts to his letters (such as the one in Romans 16:21) set in small caps to emphasize the difference between Paul’s handwriting and his scribe’s handwriting.  This points to the larger truth that most/all of the Pauline epistles were dictated, not written.  These are the kind of design decisions that make me say, “That was thoughtful.  I’m glad they did that,” rather than “Why in the world did they do that?!”

The individual Tyndale NLT’s I’ve examined show similar
thoughtfulness.  For instance, even
though the Tyndale Premium-Value Large-Print is a bottom-tier budget Bible, I
appreciate many of the design choices in it. 
I like the choice of Meta Serif as a typeface and the wide, generous
columns.  It’s also the only low-end
Bible I’ve ever seen that uses text figures rather than titling figures for
numbers in the body text.  In fact, the
only design choice in it that I really don’t like is the half-tone book-title
bars.  They look smudgy, and they don’t
really fit with the classic quality of the rest of the layout.

By contrast, the closest thing that I come to not liking in
the Tyndale Select text block is the choice to set it in Lexicon No. 1.  This is more a matter of context than
anything.  Lexicon No. 1 is a great typeface
for Bibles.  Its strong serifs lend
themselves well to small print.

The problem is that because it’s such a great typeface for
Bibles, lots of Bible publishers already use it.  Pretty much all new Cambridge and Crossway
designs are in Lexicon No. 1 (the one exception I can think of is the ESV
Reader’s Gospels, which is set in 12-point Trinité,
another Bram de Goes typeface).  As such,
when I open another Bible set in Lexicon No. 1, it tends to blend into the
crowd, and the design of the Select deserves better than that.

In fairness, though, if the biggest complaint I have to
offer is “The typeface in this Bible is so great that a lot of other Bibles
already use it,” that’s not much to complain about!

Other than that, I love ev-e-ry-thing about the design of
the Select.  Like the Premium-Value
Large-Print, it uses text figures.  For
those of you who haven’t read Bringhurst, text figures are “lower-case” numbers.  “Upper-case” numbers are called titling
figures, and they’re undesirable in body text for the same reasons that it’s
undesirable to set text in ALL CAPS. 
They stand out from the rest of the page, and they make readers feel
like the book is shouting at them.  Text
figures are a classy, subtle alternative.

Speaking of caps, the Select uses a dedicated small-caps
typeface for its small-caps sections. 
This is important, and it’s different from what happens when we select
“Small Caps” in Microsoft Word.  Word
(and most publishers) creates small caps by borrowing upper-case letters from
the normal typeface and shrinking them down so that they are the same height as
lower-case letters.

However, this is only an imperfect solution.  Letters don’t only have height and width;
they have weight--the thickness of the strokes that make up a letter.  Generally, all letters in the same font
(Times New Roman is a typeface; Times New Roman 11 is a font) have the same
weight.  As a result, when you take an
11-point capital N and shrink it down so that it’s eight points high, it’s
going to have a lighter weight than the 11-point letters around it.

This is particularly conspicuous in words that begin with a
normal capital but have the rest of their letters set in small caps, such as Lord in the Old Testaments of most
Bibles.  For instance, in my NASB Pitt
Minion, it’s obvious that the designers didn’t use a dedicated small-cap
typeface in the Old Testament.  The L’s
of “Lord” are darker than the ord’s. 
Oddly, the small-cap quotations in the New Testament of the NASB Pitt
are set in the appropriate small-cap typeface. 
By contrast, the Select uses the small-cap typeface throughout, which
lends itself to a harmonious layout.

There is, however, one place where the Select’s design isn’t
harmonious, and I didn’t want it to be. 
The verse numbers in the select are in a different typeface than the
text, and they’re very light.  This makes
them easy to ignore when I’m reading, but paradoxically, also allows me to
locate them when I’m scanning for a particular verse.  I don’t know whether the Tyndale designers
intended this result, but in my experience, they achieved it.

By contrast, Crossway’s verse numbers, though also in a
sans-serif typeface, are bolded.  In
theory, this should make them easier to spot. 
In practice, it makes them blend in with Lexicon No. 1 quotation marks
because they’re about the same weight.  I’ve
missed my share of verse citations in ESV’s because of that.

Between lines, I love the leading of the Select.  With my eyeball, I guesstimated the leading in the Select at 11 points (with 8.75-point text).  Mark Bertrand actually measured it and concluded that the leading was 11.5 points.  I’ll go with Mark here.  Either way, that’s a lot of leading!  When combined with line-matching, it makes the text extremely easy to read.

As a result, the block of text on a given page has the “long” feeling I associate with single-column layouts that work for me, rather than the “squat” feeling I associate with single-column layouts that don’t.  The Tyndale designers weren’t stressing about page count.  Instead, they wanted pages that worked.

One last feature of the design bears mentioning:  the gutter. 
Even though our mothers all admonished us to keep our minds out of the
gutter, gutters, and the inner margins which define them, have a significant
impact on readability.  Bibles with
inadequate inner margins have a problem with text sliding into the gutter,
which makes that text much harder to read.

The Select, however, is the first Bible I’ve ever seen that
has inner margins much larger than the outer margins.  I make the former out to be ½”, and the
latter only ¼”.  This is brilliant.  Because the Select has nearly 2100 pages,
it’s going to be a thick Bible, and the thicker the Bible, the wider the inner
margins need to be to keep the text out of the gutter.  Rather than opting for ½” margins all the way
around, which would probably have made the Bible either wider or longer than
they wanted, they put the extra margin space where it was needed—in the gutter.

I could go on.  I
could talk about the simple, classic book titles (none of that “The Epistle to
the. . .” stuff for Tyndale), or the segregation of cross-references to the
reference column and translators’ notes to the bottom of the page.

However, by now, the point should be sufficiently
clear.  In designing this Bible, the
folks at Tyndale seem to have done everything right.  If anything, they’ve succeeded too well.  I’ve talked to several people already who
have expressed the wish that Tyndale would publish an NASB Select!  I wouldn’t hold my breath on that one, but we
all should appreciate what the Select setting has done for the NLT.  It gives people another reason to use the
translation, because the setting is so good.

Hat tip, y’all.  You
deserve it.
