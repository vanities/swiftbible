One of the more interesting trends in the hymn world (at least, in my corner of the hymn world) over the past 50 years has been the increased use of folk tunes as hymn tunes.  Generally, I think this is a promising development.  Any tune that people have been singing for hundreds (thousands?) of years is good enough to sing today, and folk tunes are generally simple enough to adapt easily to a congregational context.

A tune that has made the transition is O WALY WALY.  Despite the name, it has nothing to do with robots from Pixar, instead being best known as the tune to "The Water Is Wide".  In *Psalms, Hymns, and Spiritual Songs*, it appears with the Fred Pratt Green hymn "O Christ, The Healer" as #403.

Even though I like and agree with many of the editorial decisions in *PHASS*, I'm not a big fan of this match.  I don't think "O Christ, The Healer" is among the stronger Pratt Green texts in the hymnal, and it has particular problems when paired with O WALY WALY.  So far as I know, Pratt Green didn't write the lyrics particularly for the tune, which meant that he had no reason to be particularly consistent with internal line breaks.  As long as he got eight syllables together that ended on a rhyme half the time, he was content.

However, O WALY WALY is a tune that demands strong internal line breaks.  The dotted quarter note four syllables into each line is a musical punctuation mark that requires a lyrical pause almost as pronounced as the pause at the end of each line.  As a result, a multi-syllable word that stretches across the pause inevitably interferes with comprehension.  The congregation that sings "O Christ, the Healer" to O WALY WALY will find itself emphasizing interior syllables such as "ail", "clam", and "mu", along with a couple iterations of "that".  This diminishes the focus and effectiveness of worship.

I don't think it's correct to conclude, though, that because O WALY WALY isn't suitable for "O Christ, the Healer", it isn't suitable for worship generally.  It will work quite well with a hymn with good internal line breaks, like "When I Survey the Wondrous Cross".  Isaac Watts wasn't writing to O WALY WALY either, but I suspect he was such a logical persnicket that he wrote with strong line breaks as a matter of course.  The internal breaks in the first verse, for instance, fall on "survey", "Prince", "gain", and "contempt".

However, I am reasonably sure that trying to get the people in my religious tradition to sing "When I Survey" to anything but HAMBURG is a fool's errand.  In consequence, I decided to rework one of my own texts to have the stronger line breaks that the tune requires.  The results, if perhaps not as strong as one of the greatest hymns ever written in the English language, appear above.  I adopted Matt Harber's arrangement of O WALY WALY from *PHASS* because it has the great virtue of simplicity and was not copyrighted, though if this ever goes anywhere, I'll certainly talk to Matt about using it.

Finally, of course, if anybody has any comments, critiques, or suggestions for improvement, I'd be happy to hear 'em!
