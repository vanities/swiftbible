Every once in a while, you run across something on the
Internet that makes you want to grit your teeth, roll your eyes, and sigh in
exasperation all at once.  For me, ["Upgrading Religion for the 21st Century"](http://www.salon.com/2016/05/22/upgrading_religion_for_the_21st_century_christianity_is_forcibly_evolving_to_cope_with_science_and_progress/) was one such piece.  In case you don’t
feel like trudging through the article, it argues that if Christianity wants to
survive in the 21st century, it is going to have to abandon the
Bible to cope with a world in which cultural relativism and the scientific
method reign supreme.  To be honest, I’m
not sure which bothers me more:  the
author’s smug certainty about his conclusions or the shoddy argumentation with
which he reaches them.



**Christianity in
Decline?**

Let’s take it from the top.  
The first issue I have is in the first paragraph.  He says, “Everywhere around us, traditional
Christian theology and its culture is breaking down in hopes of remaining
relevant.”   I find this assertion
particularly rich coming from someone who spends several paragraphs worshiping
at the shrine of cultural relativism.  It
reflects a nearsighted focus on the post-industrial West while ignoring
everywhere else on the planet.

It is true, sort of, that Christianity in America is in
decline.  It is not at all true that Christianity
outside of America and Europe is declining. 
Christianity is thriving in South America, Africa, and Asia, in the
places where the vast majority of the 2 billion Christians today live.

However, the same cannot be said for the atheism that the
author regards as the future of humankind. 
According to the widely respected Pew Research Center, by 2050, atheists
are [projected to decline](http://www.pewforum.org/2015/04/02/religious-projections-2010-2050/) from 16 percent to 13 percent of the world’s
population.  Perhaps rather than worrying about how to
upgrade Christianity, the author should be worrying about how to upgrade
atheism.

**Christianity Unable
to Adapt?**

After this promising start, he continues on to argue that
Christianity is fundamentally incapable of adapting to a world with many
different, equally important cultures. 
This is a head-scratcher.  So far
as I know, none of the Christians alive on earth today are first-century
Palestinian Jews, yet Christianity began as a sect of first-century Palestinian
Jews.

Every single one of those two billion Christians out there,
then, is a testimony to the vast cross-cultural appeal of the gospel.  In one form or another, Christianity has been
around for two thousand years, thriving in places and times as different as the
third-century Roman Empire and today’s sub-Saharan Africa.  If you want to, you can find YouTubes of
“Amazing Grace” being sung in Japanese, Tamil, and Swahili.  I’m not exactly getting a “doesn’t adapt
well” vibe here.

Furthermore, the adaptive strategy that the author urges on
Christians—abandoning the black-and-white rules of the Bible—is actually a
strategy for suicide.  The Pew Center’s [mammoth 2015 report on religion in America](http://www.pewforum.org/2015/05/12/americas-changing-religious-landscape/) reveals a fascinating correlation between
commitment to the Bible and health of a religious group.  Generally speaking, the more liberal an
American denomination is, the faster it is declining.  Groups like the Episcopalians, who have
jettisoned all that tiresome stuff about the virgin birth and sin in favor of
saving the planet, are practically dying in the pews.

By comparison, the much more conservative and Bible-centric
evangelicals, though declining slightly as a percentage of the American
population, are holding steady numerically. 
In other words, the best way for Christianity to adapt to the changes of
the modern world is not to adapt.  There
can be no substitute for the Bible.

**The Scientific Method**

Next up:  the
scientific method.  The author is a big
fan of the scientific method.  He
believes that it is, if not the only method that mankind can use to uncover
truth, at least the best one.  Can’t
prove that Jesus died for the sins of humankind using the scientific
method?  Well, you shouldn’t believe it,
then!

Where to begin?  Let’s
start with the notion that the scientific method is somehow more “humble” than
Christianity, because the latter relies on the guidance of an omnipotent
God.  To paraphrase *The Princess Bride*, I do not think that word means what he thinks
it means.  Which is the more humble
position, proclaiming, “We can figure it all out ourselves!” or saying along
with Jeremiah 10:23 that it is not in man who walks to direct his steps?

Certainly, science as it is currently practiced could
benefit from more humility.  For
instance, [a 2015 study](http://www.readcube.com/articles/10.1002/scin.2015.188007006) of 97 published psychology papers found
that only 36 percent of the results could be replicated.  The other 64 percent?  Junk science.

Similar problems exist in other scientific disciplines.  In real life, scientists are people too, and
like other people, they often decide what they want to believe and then massage
the facts until they support that belief. 
Until science is carried out by robots, it doesn’t seem robust enough to
sustain inquiry into the fundamental questions of existence.

However, there’s a deeper issue with the author’s faith in
scientific inquiry than that.  He
believes that any truth worth finding can be uncovered using the scientific
method.  Sadly for him, his belief itself
cannot be proven using the scientific method. 
What possible experiment could be designed to prove that nothing exists
beyond what we can sense and measure?  It
would be like me declaring that pottery doesn’t exist because I can’t find potsherds
with my metal detector.  Ironically, the
very standard he proposes declares that he is mistaken.

**Transhumanism**

Throughout his essay, the author proclaims his interest in
transhumanism, the striving of humanity to become like gods.  He seems to think that proponents of
traditional religion should be really bothered by transhumanism and think it
blasphemous.  To be honest, I’m not
particularly bothered at all.  It’s kind
of like when my four-year-old son points a stick at me and says “Bang!”  In both cases, there is, shall we say, a
slight mismatch between intention and capability.

The author wants to become God?  I wish him well.  However, if his article is representative of
the quality of his thought, he’s got some work ahead of him.
