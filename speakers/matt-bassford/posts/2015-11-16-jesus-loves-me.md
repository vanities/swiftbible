As is true for most preachers, sometimes, I find that what I
say from the pulpit is influenced by the other things that happen in my
life.  In this case, what I decided to
preach on was dictated by my blog.  I’m still
going strong, as I have been for the past couple of years, at
hisexcellentword.blogspot.com.  These
days, most of what I say Tuesday through Friday is original content, written
expressly for the blog, which has been quite well received.

Recently, I’ve been blogging about God’s standards for the
hymns we sing and what makes a good hymn. 
Wednesday, I’m planning a post about 1 Corinthians 14:15 and singing
with understanding.  When I apply this
text, my favorite example isn’t a hymn we think of as a grownup hymn at
all.  Instead, it’s “Jesus Loves Me!”,
inarguably the greatest children’s Bible-class song ever written.  However, despite its target audience, “Jesus
Loves Me!” has the structure and Biblical content to be useful to adults
too.  I think it’s worth some time to
invest some thought in a spiritual song we don’t usually think much about.  This evening, let’s consider “Jesus Loves
Me!”

**Verses 1-2**

There are more versions of “Jesus Loves Me” than there are
fleas on a stray dog, but in the sermon today I’m going to focus on the
five-verse version in our hymnals.  Let’s
begin, naturally, with **THE FIRST
VERSE.** In many ways, this is the
gold standard for the opening couplet of a hymn.  It is extraordinarily simple, it is
extraordinarily clear, but it says something extraordinarily important.  Where would any of us be if we didn’t have
the Bible to tell us that Jesus loved us? 
The love of Jesus expressed in the word is the cornerstone of
Christianity.

Consider, for instance, the Lord’s own expression of this,
in John 15:9.  We’re used to this idea,
but we should never move beyond its power to amaze us.  Jesus is fundamentally different from us.  We are all created beings, but He created all
things.  Our understanding is limited;
His encompasses universes.  We are frail
and vulnerable, but He is omnipotent.

What a strange scene it is, then, for Jesus to lower
Himself, to become a human being like we are, to be walking toward the Mount of
Olives on a spring night 2000 years ago, and to tell a bunch of schlubs like us
that He loves them!  That’s
incomprehensible.  It’s like us going out
to our backyard, bending down to an anthill, and telling the ants that we love
them.  You know, on one level, I get the
deists.  It makes sense that a God
powerful enough to create the universe would have no interest in the likes of
us.  However, that’s not the way it
is.  We know that He does love us, and we
know it because the Bible tells us so.

Next, we learn about the “me” whom Jesus loves.  It’s identified in the third line here as
“little ones”, but this is true in both a literal and a metaphorical
sense.  Here, let’s read from Matthew
18:1-6.  Obviously, the child himself
belongs to Jesus, but so too does everyone who humbles himself like a child to
come to Jesus.  Notice that in this
reading, even Jesus Himself refers to those who believe in Him as “little
ones”.  Like children, we acknowledge
that we can’t make it through life on our own, that we need help from someone
who is stronger than we are.

As a result, whenever we sing this song, we’re singing it in
a double sense.  We’re singing it about
those who are literally children, but we’re also singing it about ourselves,
and to the extent that we’re humble enough to group ourselves with the “little
ones”, its message is equally relevant to us.

From here, we can turn our attention to **THE SECOND VERSE.** As with
many of the verses that we’ll be looking at, it’s not at all hard to tell where
the author got this from.  Turn with me
to Mark 10:13-16.  Even though Mark is not
as concerned with strict chronology as Luke, for instance, is, it still appears
that this is occurring very late in the ministry of Jesus, as He is making His
way to Jerusalem to be crucified.  At
this point, Jesus’ popularity is at an all-time high, so His life is filled
with distractions, and He’s got a lot on His mind besides.  His disciples know this, so when a crowd of
parents show up because they want Jesus to bless their children, the disciples
shoo them off.  Jesus, however, is not OK
with this.  Jesus is mad.  He insists on having the children brought to
Him and laying His hands on them.

It’s hard to imagine any other great leader behaving this
way.  They all have people who take care
of the riff-raff.  However, Jesus doesn’t
have people who take care of the riff-raff. 
He is people who take care of the riff-raff.  The disciples didn’t much care about the
children, but they were precious to Jesus. 
This should be powerfully reassuring to us, whether we are literal or
metaphorical “little ones”.  Even when we
feel like nobody else cares about us, we can be sure that Jesus does.

**The Third Verse**

Next, before the Lord’s Supper, let’s look at **THE THIRD VERSE.** Here too, we meet two ideas that don’t go
together much outside of a Christian context. 
Love is the greatest joy of our earthly existence.  Death is the greatest sorrow.  What do the two have to do with each other?

Jesus explains in John 15:12-13.  Even though death is such a bad thing, it can
still be something we choose to embrace for the sake of somebody we love.  Indeed, being willing to face death for a
friend is the highest expression of love. 
After you give your life, you’ve got nothing left to give.  That, of course, is the ultimate sign of
Jesus’ love for us.  He didn’t talk a
good game about loving us and then abandon us when we needed Him.  Instead, He stayed and died.  If it’s incomprehensible that Jesus would
love us in the first place, it’s beyond incomprehensible that He would be
willing to lay down His life for us.

However, the results of Jesus’ death were as good as the
event was grim.  Consider what the
Hebrews writer tells us about it in Hebrews 10:19-22.  As much as I would love to preach yet another
Hebrews 10 sermon for y’all, now is not the time, but it’s worth noting even so
that the first half of the chapter is all about our need for spiritual
cleansing, the inability of the sacrifices of the Law of Moses to provide it,
and Jesus dying to provide one sacrifice for sin for all time.

In the third and fourth lines of this verse, it looks like
the author has lost focus a little bit. 
What does washing away our sin have to do with being able to come in
through the gates of heaven?  However,
that’s exactly the connection that the Hebrews writer makes here.  Because our hearts have been sprinkled clean
from an evil conscience and our bodies washed with pure water, through the good
offices of Jesus, we are able to approach the throne of God in heaven.  This is true in a couple of different senses.  We draw near that throne every time we pray
or sing, but we will finally come into the dwelling place of God literally and
fully when we are accounted righteous on the Day of Judgment, not because of
our own worthiness, but because Jesus loved us and died for us.

The applications of everything we’ve said to our celebration
of the Lord’s Supper are obvious. 
There’s something that’s true about every snapshot from the life of
Jesus that we’ve looked at, whether He was using a child as a sermon illustration,
inviting other children to come to Him, or teaching His disciples on the night
of His betrayal.  In every one of these
instances, there was a thought in the back of Jesus’ mind, and the thought was,
“I’m going to have to die on the cross.” 
That weighed on Him and weighed on Him, like it would on us.  He knew what would happen when He went to
Jerusalem, but He went anyway, and when He got there, He freely offered His
blood on the cross for our salvation. 
“Jesus loves me!  This I know, for
the Bible tells me so.”  If we have the
love of Jesus, we have everything we need. 
Let’s remember Him as we partake.

**Verses 4-5**

From consideration of the greatest thing that Jesus has done
for those whom He loves, we move on to what He continues to do in **THE FOURTH VERSE.** He sanctified us by the offering of His
body once for all, but He continues to watch over us daily, even when it seems
that we have been abandoned by everyone else. 
Consider, for instance, the experience that Paul describes in 2 Timothy
4:16-18.  Here, he’s describing a dark
time in his life from the perspective of another dark time.  Both of these events take place after the end
of the book of Acts.  Paul was in Rome
for at least two years after his arrival, but at some point after that, he was
summoned to plead his case before the emperor.

This is a good news-bad news thing.  The good news is that Paul is innocent of any
crime.  The bad news is that the emperor
hearing his case is Nero, who is crazy, evil, and the first guy to institute
persecution of Christians by the Roman Empire.  Trial before Nero is not a cheerful
prospect!  Not surprisingly, all of
Paul’s friends find somewhere else to be when he’s on trial, all except one.  However, the one who stuck with Paul was
enough, because He was Jesus.  Through
Jesus, Paul was acquitted and was able to proclaim the gospel widely.

Now, however, history is repeating itself.  Once again, Paul is in prison.  Once again, all of his friends have abandoned
him, all except one, and that One is still Jesus.  Just as “Jesus Loves Me!” does, Paul expresses
his confidence that he Lord will keep him safe.

In context, this is a particularly interesting word for Paul
to choose.  Only a few verses up, we see
Paul offering a valedictory statement about his life.  He says that he has fought the good fight,
finished the race, and kept the faith. 
He knows, whether by the Holy Spirit or by common sense, that the next
trial before Nero is going to mean his death. 
And yet, he still says that Jesus is going to keep him safe.  He obviously doesn’t mean this in the sense
of earthly security.  Instead, he’s
expressing his confidence that Jesus is going to keep safe the part of him that
matters.

So too for us.  The
safety of Jesus does not mean that He is going to infallibly guard us from
every bad thing that might happen to us in this life.  In fact, we know that the opposite is
true.  If the world continues long
enough, every one of us will die.  What
it does mean, though, is that Jesus is going to protect us from the evil one
and deliver us safely to heaven.

The hymn concludes with shifting the focus from Jesus to us
in **THE FIFTH VERSE.** The first two lines here express a desire
for Jesus to purify and claim our hearts. 
In both directions, this is a big deal! 
It seems like in America, we’re always trying to escape the past.  Everybody wants to meet with a psychologist
so they can finally work out their issues with their mothers.  Politicians provide heroic biographies that
may or may not line up with the findings of independent investigation.  They’d rather have people talking about how
they invented the Internet than what they actually did.  The reality is, though, that regardless of
what we tell somebody else, we can’t escape the truth about the past within
ourselves.  With the right trigger, a
shameful memory from 15 years ago will come rushing back, and there’s nothing
we can do to move past it.

Nothing, that is, except turning to Jesus.  Look at what the Hebrews writer points out
about what Jesus can do in Hebrews 9:13-14. 
There are things that the Jews did back then to cleanse the body, and
we’re still interested in bodily cleansing today.  It’s not at all unusual to hear someone speak
of wanting to take a shower after some particularly unpleasant interaction.  However, only Jesus can cleanse the conscience,
and having cleansed, only He can dedicate us to God.  If we want a fresh start, if we want a new
beginning, we have to seek that new beginning through the Lord.

Finally, the writer of “Jesus Loves Me!” employs a powerful
parallel that is often present in the New Testament.  Because Jesus died for us, we are under
obligation to live for Him.  Consider the
way Paul puts this in Galatians 2:20. 
Paul was a guy who understood guilt. 
Imagine that awful moment when he realized that in persecuting the
church, he was murdering innocent people who were right about the Messiah!  The shame must have been unbearable,
unendurable, but Jesus freed him from it. 
In speaking of his old self as crucified with Christ, we get a sense of
how much Paul welcomed the spiritual death of that old self.  Because Jesus had bought him and paid for
him, now it was Jesus who lived in Paul in every meaningful sense.  Jesus’ goals were now Paul’s goals.  Jesus’ self-sacrificing love for others was
the love that Paul strove to show.

Paul got it.  We need
to get it.  The Lord Himself observed
that he who is forgiven little, loves little. 
The issue here, of course, is not the littleness of our sins.  Even if we had only sinned once and were
forgiven of that one sin, that forgiveness would still be massive!  Instead, the littleness is in the way we
perceive our sins.  We think we were
pretty good before we were forgiven, so Jesus didn’t do all that much for us,
so we don’t have to do that much for Him.



Brethren, that’s quite frankly ludicrous!  The measure of the guilt of our sins is not
our subjective, Satan-deceived perceptions. 
Instead, the measure of our guilt is that Jesus had to die to have it
erased.  He would never have offered so
great a sacrifice to solve a minor problem. 
He didn’t rescue us from an inconvenience.  He redeemed our souls from hell.  Because He has done so much for us, there is
nothing that we have the right to deny Him.
