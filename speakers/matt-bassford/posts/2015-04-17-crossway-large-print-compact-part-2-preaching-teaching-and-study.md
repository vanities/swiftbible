**OVERVIEW.** The Large-Print Compact performed well in
the pulpit, and its small size made it a delight to use while teaching Bible
classes.  I don’t recall having had any
trouble with it during study either.  I
hold only two things against it.  It
isn’t a wide-margin Bible (duh), so I couldn’t copy expository sermon outlines
into it, and its extremely thin paper occasionally gave me trouble as I was
trying to turn to a passage.

\*             \*             \*

The LPC got a respectable workout as a preacher’s
Bible.  Because of my schedule, I ended
up using it for three different sermons. 
During one of those, I only read passages from the projection screen
behind me, so I literally did not use the LPC for anything.  The other two, however, were topical sermons
that had me turning in the LPC for every passage I cited.  I wielded the LPC through five auditorium
Bible classes, and I also relied on it in my sermon preparation and general
study for three weeks.  Here’s how it
performed:

**PREACHING.** The LPC’s rotation coincided with yet
another evolution in my pulpit practice. 
Sometime in January, I read an article that argued that people have an
easier time remembering the things they write by hand rather than the things
they type.  Apparently, typing isn’t
effortful enough to make much of an impression on us.

I resolved, then, to make hand-writing an outline part of my
sermon preparation.  Expository outlines
now go next to the relevant text in my Cambridge Wide-Margin NASB, but topical
outlines go into a fancy little notebook that my then-girlfriend, now-wife,
bought me when she visited Venice a dozen years ago.  As a result, the LPC’s ability to hold a
half-sheet or quarter-sheet outline is no longer relevant.

However, the LPC does look awfully spiffy when stacked on
top of my sermon notebook, almost like half of a matched set.  I’m sorely tempted to get it rebound, in some
lustrous goatskin that would also match the notebook, and make the twosome my
regular topical-preaching pair.  However,
the very last thing I need is another Bible competing for pulpit space against
all the Bibles I review and the Pitt Minion I have already committed to use
through the end of 2015!  Clearly, the
situation calls for two Sundays a week instead of just one. . .

As I would expect from a double-column paragraphed Bible,
the LPC didn’t lead me to miss any verse citations, either in rehearsal or when
I was actually preaching.  I would guess
that the verse numbers are about 5-point, and despite all the aspersions that I
cast on Crossway’s “quotation-mark verse numbers” last year, I never had any
trouble making them out in this Bible. 
That’s a good thing.



On the less-good side of the ledger, I did have some trouble
(during one rehearsal and one actual sermon) with quickly turning to the
passage I wanted.  Part of this may be my
own fumble-fingeredness, but I suspect that at part of the issue is the
extremely thin paper in the LPC.  It’s
relatively easy to miss separating a single page, even for somebody who is as
used to Bibles as I am.  This wasn’t a
big deal (it maybe cost me an extra five seconds on a couple of occasions), but
I did notice it, and as always, Noticing Is Bad.

**TEACHING.** As far as I’m concerned, the LPC is very
near the ideal size for teaching a Bible class. 
The Bible doesn’t weigh a whole lot more than a class workbook does, and
I felt equally comfortable strolling around the front of the auditorium with
either.  I like Bibles that I don’t have
to think about, and I didn’t have to think about the LPC when I was teaching
from it.

I’ve mentioned before that the cross-references I care most
about are the tweener cross-references, the kind that Crossway and Zondervan
will put along the bottom margin of a “text” thinline.  It’s not a big deal to me when a cross-reference
points me to a verse that addresses a similar topic, but when Paul quotes from
the Old Testament, I very much want the citation to the passage he’s
quoting.  This is important enough to me
that I went through my Zondervan NASB Thinline, which does not have any
cross-references at all, and wrote in all the cross-references for quotations
from the Old Testament.

The LPC, however, is a true tweener Bible.  It has those cross-references, along with
references to parallel passages in the Synoptic gospels and probably a few
others I haven’t noticed.  That’s a good
and important feature in a text Bible, and it really doesn’t take up that much
space.  The tweener references were
there, and I used them.  For instance,
when we were studying Romans 9, they took me from Romans 9:12 to Genesis 25:23.
 It’s true that I would have been able to
thrash for a few seconds and find the story of the birth of Jacob and Esau
anyway, but it was nice not to have to thrash. 
In short, the LPC gave me the references I wanted, and I never wanted a
reference that it failed to supply.

**STUDY.** The LPC did just fine here.  Recently, I’ve been splitting my study time
between my office at the church building and my desk at home, so it was nice to
have a handy Bible that I could tote around with me without much thought.  The text was easily readable, and I could set
the Bible pretty much anywhere I wanted to as I was simultaneously reading and
typing.  I’m beginning to develop the
suspicion that, readability issues aside, smaller Bibles actually work better
for study.

So far as I’ve been able to tell, I didn’t miss a single
citation in any of the study materials I prepared while using the LPC.  Well; that’s not entirely true.  I did miss one citation, but that was because
I read the correct number but wrote it down wrong.  Sadly, the LPC isn’t going to save me from
myself any more than any other Bible will.

Of course, the LPC doesn’t offer me one thing that I’ve come
to appreciate in an office Bible—wide margins.  Like all the members
of its semi-compact tribe, the LPC has narrow margins, only slightly more than
a quarter-inch.  If you wanted to put a
sermon outline in the margins of the LPC, you’d have to hire one of those guys
who does paintings on single grains of rice! 
Of course, this is hardly the LPC’s fault.  Blaming a Bible the size of the LPC for not
having wide margins is like blaming fire for not being cold.  You’re not actually upset with the Bible;
you’re upset with the laws of physics. 
However, it is nonetheless true that I prefer a wide-margin Bible for
preaching these days, and the LPC doesn’t give them to me.

**CONCLUSION.** Even though the LPC probably wasn’t
designed with pulpit work in mind, it doesn’t perform badly there.  If I haaaad to pick one Bible to use for
everything, there are many worse choices than this one.  For one thing, if I had to pick a
one-and-only, it wouldn’t be a wide-margin; I don’t like reading out of Bibles
I’ve written in.  It is true, though,
that the lack of wide margins and the uncooperative paper make this a less than
perfect preaching Bible.  I award it an
8/10.
