The Foundation served the usual two-week tour of duty
sitting next to my computer in my office at home.  That’s where I do my Bible readings every
morning.  Here’s what I thought of it:

**SIZE.** I prefer my reading Bibles to be as
paperback-like as possible.  Clearly, the
GPR is not.  It’s not as large and heavy
as a study Bible or something, but it’s definitely larger than I care to
one-hand for a reading of several chapters. 
I spent a lot of time reading it by balancing it against my diaphragm,
and even then, I used my forearm to keep the right side from flopping down too
much.  I was never able to forget about
holding the GPR.  I always knew it was
there.



**LAYOUT.** I didn’t have a terrible experience with
the GPR’s layout, but I wasn’t enthralled with it either.  My Bible-reading plan has me entirely in
prose at the moment, and in Leviticus and Mark, the GPR’s double-column layout
does pretty well for itself.  Generally,
the line length wasn’t a distraction.

There was one moderately frequent exception to this,
though.  The GPR has a fairly short line
length, even for a double-column Bible. 
I count about 32 characters per line. 
However, despite this short line length, the Bible is still justified in
alignment.  This means that some lines
have to stretch out a few words to fill an entire line, leaving unnaturally
large spaces between the words.

For instance, the last two full lines of Leviticus 26:6 only
have 28 characters each, so that each word appears to have two spaces after it
instead of one.  It looks about like
somebody took a shotgun and shot holes in the text.  I don’t want to pay attention to format when
I’m reading, but the shotgun holes make me notice.

Also, the GPR is verse-by-verse, not paragraphed.  As a result, many verses end on a partial
line, and when even a full line only contains 32 characters, those partial
lines aren’t going to contain very much information.  They slow down reading the same way that the
“widows” at the ends of overly long lines of poetry in double-column Bibles do.

Speaking of poetry, the GPR mangles it pretty
thoroughly.  Few poetic lines in the
Bible are going to be shorter than 32 characters, so just about every line is
broken.  When I read a psalm in the GPR,
the layout of the text gives me almost no sense of how the poetic parallels are
supposed to work.  Honestly, the
designers of the Bible might have been just as well served to put all the
poetry in prose.  In its current
chopped-up form, it doesn’t read like poetry anyway.

It is also true that because the font in the GPR was so
large, each page contains comparatively few words.  Excluding a couple of hundred pages of
concordance and so forth, the Bible text alone in the GPR runs to 1950 pages,
which is nearly double the number of pages in a typical thinline.  As a result, I felt like I had to turn the
page a lot in a typical reading, which, in dealing with the bedsheet-like pages
of the GPR, is no small endeavor.

Finally, it is true that the GPR is a cross-referenced
Bible, but that didn’t really bother me. 
The references are at the end of each verse instead of in a big,
obtrusive column, and they’re in a font small enough (I would guesstimate it at
about 9 points) that I wonder whether the intended audience of the Bible would
be able to use them.  For my part,
though, I was able to cheerfully ignore them while reading.

**READABILITY.** Here, of course, is where the GPR stakes
its claim to utility as a reader.  Its
text is in 14-point font.  You go much
larger than that, and you’ll have to print your Bible on a billboard.  For Christians with poor close-in eyesight
who want to use the NASB, the GPR is far
and away the best choice.  Indeed, I recommend it more than I recommend any other Bible, for precisely that reason.

Even here, though, all is not ideal.  Although the GPR contains something like 2150
pages, it’s not a very thick Bible.  This
does not mean that Foundation has discovered a magical formula for Bible
format.  It means that in order to keep
weight and thickness down, they printed the GPR on fairly thin paper.  It strikes me as being about the same weight
as thinline paper, probably about 20-25 gsm.

Because Foundation hasn’t discovered a magical formula for
thin but opaque paper either, the Bible has a significant amount of
ghosting.  I can see not only the text on
the reverse of a page, but the text facing the page reverse, the text on the
reverse of the facing page, and sometimes the text on the facing page of
that.  Four levels of ghosting is pretty
dramatic.  For those who have trouble
reading heavily-ghosted Bibles, the ghosting in this one may take away what the
giant font gives.

**CONCLUSION.** Even though I have spent a lot of time
sniping at various flaws in the GPR, it is actually not a terribly bad reading
Bible.  I wouldn’t be overjoyed about
doing my daily readings out of it, but I wouldn’t hate the experience
either.  14-point font and double column
go a long way to making my life easier. 
As a reader, I rate the Bible at 3.5/5.
