Among my other vices, I enjoy playing collectible card
games:  Magic the Gathering, Hearthstone,
etc.  They appeal to me in part because,
unlike solo computer games, you’re playing a human opponent, which means that
even online, psychology is very important. 
You can’t bluff a computer or hound it into a misplay, but you can do
those things to a person.

I also enjoy reading articles about CCG theory, which often
focus on psychology.  All serious players
know that winning requires a certain state of mind, so CCG writers have spent a
lot of pixels advocating a particular lifestyle even when you’re not
playing.  Eat well!  Sleep enough! 
Get lots of exercise! 
Meditate!  Only when the body and
the soul are at their best can the mind and the will be strong (note that they offer such advice with a complete lack of irony).



Recently, though, I read a piece that took an entirely
different tack.  I won’t link it because
of its vulgar nature (I would not have started reading it had I known how bad it
would be), but despite its crudity, it proved to be insightful.  Basically, the author argued that sometimes
people lose card games because they need to lose.  Somewhere deep down inside, they are
convinced of their own unworthiness, and so in a vital part of the game, they
will contrive a way to play badly.  They
don’t get the win they said they wanted, and they will probably blame the
outcome on factors beyond their control, but they will get what they actually do
want:  a validation of their own sense of
inferiority.

The author observed, and I think correctly, that this
problem can and will show itself in far more serious ways, many of which have a
significant spiritual effect.  You start
an affair or a drug habit, you give in to your temper or your laziness (knowing
perfectly well that it’s a bad idea), because the resultant disaster will
validate your negative vision of yourself. 
You need to lose, and so you do.

I believe the principle can be broadened even beyond
that.  Unless we are uniformly successful,
there is likely to be a common thread behind our failures, and it is likely to
be within us rather than outside of us.

I must confess that this is true of me.  My problem is not that I need to lose.  I generally don’t seek out self-destructive behavior
or sabotage myself in the clutch. 
Instead, my fatal flaw (one I share with my mother, incidentally) is a
stubborn focus on what ought to be instead of what is.  I would rather fail than retreat from my
sense of what is “right”.

In some cases, of course, this is a good thing.  If God expects me to live in a certain way, I
have to do that regardless of consequences. 
However, when my sense of right comes from me rather than from God and I
follow it to the point of failure, that stops looking like Daniel 3:16-18 and
starts looking like Ecclesiastes 7:16. 
Excessive loyalty to our human ideals finds no favor with God.

However, my overall point is neither that we should avoid
self-sabotage nor that we should beware excessive righteousness.  Instead, it is that we should know
ourselves.  By nature, I’m an
introspective guy, and I like to think that I know my own thought processes
fairly well, but it took an unexpected nudge from a card-game theory article
for me to identify a serious blind spot. 
The heart is desperately deceitful, after all!

Every one of us, I think, will benefit from consideration of
our failures, whether spiritual or otherwise (and usually the otherwise will
bleed into the spiritual).  One of my
favorite memes from despair.com has always been “The common element in all your
failed relationships is you,” which, like much great humor, is also insightful.  There is probably a common element to our
failures generally, some piece of mental furniture that keeps us from
fulfilling our potential in life or in our service to God.

Our search for that common element isn’t likely to be
pleasant.  Like the failed CCG player,
we’d rather attribute our failure to circumstances beyond our control.  However, if we want to serve a God who
desires truth in the innermost being, unflinching self-honesty is the only way
to get there.
