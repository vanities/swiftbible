The other day, I happened to be looking at the attendance
sheet for the month of March, and I noticed an interesting correlation.  For Sunday worship, our attendance numbers
for the month were 77, 94, 96, and 84, which represents a significant increase
over where we were a couple of years ago. 
For Sunday Bible classes, those numbers were 57, 69, 69, and 71, while
for Wednesday Bible classes, they were 42, 49, 43, and 42, with one week not
recorded.

That’s quite a difference, brethren, and it points to a
troubling trend.  As a rule, like
attendance at one service a week is a sign of spiritual interest, attendance at
multiple services a week is a sign of spiritual maturity.  There are exceptions; I know that some here
are prevented from attending by work schedules, inability to drive at night,
and things like that.  However, I worry
that others have gotten into the habit of pleading impossibility when it’s
actually a matter of priorities and convenience.  I’m not here to shame anybody about this;
instead, I want us to reflect this morning on the benefits of faithful
attendance.  Let’s consider why we should
come to church more.



**People Reasons**

The first reasons I want us to consider are those having to
do with people.  Of these, the most
obvious is **TO ENCOURAGE.**  Because you simply can’t have a sermon on
church attendance without it, let’s go to Hebrews 10:23-25.  Usually, when we consider this text, we treat
it as a binary situation.  Either you’re
forsaking the assembly, or you’re not, and the question arises of how many
services you can miss before crossing over that dreaded line into
forsaking.

However, I think this is a sloppy reading that misses the
point.  The Hebrews writer’s main concern
isn’t whether Christians are showing up at church or not.  It’s whether they’re encouraging one another
or not, and lack of attendance is a problem because it prevents encouraging.

Encouragement, of course, is not a binary matter.  Instead, it’s on a spectrum.  Some Christians are not at all encouraging,
some are slightly encouraging, and some are very encouraging, and one of the
ways we encourage is by simple attendance. 
It matters to all of us when a new Christian makes a point of assembling
faithfully, or when someone who hasn’t been reliable in the past makes an
obvious effort to attend more, or when those who face sickness or other
difficulties fight through those things to be with us.

On the other hand, it is positively discouraging when a new
Christian stops attending.  It’s also
discouraging when a faithful member’s attendance starts to slip, when somebody
who was reliably there every time the doors were opened now isn’t.  At first, it’s surprising.  It hurts. 
But then, as time goes on, we come to the dispiriting realization that
we can’t count on that brother anymore.  In
short, we should never fall into the trap of thinking that our presence or
absence on any given Sunday or Wednesday doesn’t matter.  It does, very much.

Conversely, we need to assemble in order **TO BE ENCOURAGED**.  Look, for instance, at what Paul says about
his expectations for his planned visit to Rome in Romans 1:11-12.  Even Paul needed the encouragement that came
from being around other Christians, and if somebody like that needed the strength
that came from associating with brethren, how much more do we!  The world we live in is a difficult and
hostile place.  We’re constantly
surrounded by ungodly, unloving people and unpleasant situations that wear away
our will to go on.

It is for exactly this reason that God in His wisdom
established the church.  He knew that
none of us are strong enough to keep going on our own.  Instead, we need one another.  What’s more, we need to be around one
another.  We need to be around people who
are welcoming instead of hostile, loving instead of self-centered.  We need to be reminded that no matter how we
might feel sometimes, that we’re not going through life alone, that instead, we
belong to a spiritual family of people who love us and stand ready to help us.  When there’s so much sorrow in the world,
what more potent antidote can there be than to sing joyfully together with
other Christians and to study together the precious and magnificent promises of
God?

So often, I see Christians who are going through a hard time
drawing back from the church too, and it hurts to watch that, because I know
they’re making exactly the wrong decision. 
Our assemblies aren’t a drain on our resources.  They add to them.  If we simply gather our strength and come, we
will get so much more out of it than we had to invest.

Third, we need to be here **TO BE INFLUENCED.** Consider
Paul’s advice in 2 Timothy 2:22.  Why is
it that Timothy needs to flee youthful lusts by associating with godly
people?  Because the associating with
God’s people will help him to overcome the youthful lusts!

I know there are churches out there where the attendees have
as much in common as the shoppers in a local Wal-Mart.  They’re coincidentally there at the same
time, but other than that, they have nothing to do with each other.  That’s not how the church is supposed to be,
brethren!  Instead, we’re supposed to be
involved in one another’s lives outside the assembly, and that starts with our
interactions before and after as well as during the assembly.

All of us are social creatures, and we simply can’t help
being influenced by the people we’re around. 
The influence of the ungodly people of the world will always be
there.  They will always be pulling us to
become more like them.  One of the best
ways for us to counteract that is by surrounding ourselves with godly people
whenever we can.  They’ll draw us to be
more like them.

Sadly, many brethren don’t see the importance of this.  They have that Wal-Mart-shopper relationship
with the other Christians in the pews, but their real friends are outside.  Brethren, that’s something we need to think
long and hard about.  It is most likely
true that wherever they go, we will spend eternity with our friends.  By coming here, we associate ourselves with
those who will help us on to where we want to go.

Fourth and last, we should assemble **FOR OUR CHILDREN.** Here,
let’s reflect on the moving scene of Acts 21:3-6.  Here, the church is seeing Paul off on the
final leg of his fateful journey toward Jerusalem.  They gather to pray with him on the beach,
but it’s not only the adult men of the congregation who are there.  It’s their wives and children too.  What an impression this must have made!  One can easily imagine those youngsters
telling their grandchildren 70 years later about the day they knelt down on the
beach to pray with the great apostle Paul.

Today, our assemblies are a little short on apostles, but we
must not underestimate the impact they have on our children.  I’m sure that all of us who are raising
children tell them about how important God is to us, but they can’t merely hear
that from us.  They need to see it
too.

Kids are hardly stupid. 
What’s more, they’re uniquely placed to see who we really are.  When somebody doesn’t show up for services, I
really don’t know why, but if they have kids, those kids know.  If we tell our children that God is first in
our lives, but they see us regularly choosing to put other things ahead of God,
what message are we actually communicating?

We live in an increasingly wicked world.  In such an environment, we need to take
advantage of every opportunity we can to teach our children to serve the
Lord.  Our times together are a really
obvious opportunity.  Let’s make sure
that we’re making the fullest use of them we can.

**God Reasons**

However, we aren’t here only for one another.  We’re here for reasons having to do with God
too.  The first of these is **TO LEARN.**Consider the attitude described by Peter in 1 Peter 2:1-3.  Like anyone who has ever been the parent of a
newborn, I know exactly what this is like! 
Yes, babies are cute and snuggly and wonderful, but they’re also
incredibly obnoxious, and they’re obnoxious because they don’t ever shut up
about wanting to eat.  They don’t care if
it’s 2:30 in the morning; if they’re hungry, they are guaranteed to let you
know all about it.  That’s how God made
them, because if they weren’t like that, they wouldn’t get the nutrition they
need to grow.

This, brethren, is Peter’s picture of what our attitude
toward the word is supposed to be like. 
We are supposed to constantly demand feeding just like they do.  What’s more, just as a baby won’t grow
without proper nourishment, we won’t grow without proper spiritual nourishment.

Admittedly, nowhere in any of this have I said the word
“church”, but its application to our assemblies should be obvious.  Of all the places where any of us might go in
our lives, this is the one where we come into closest contact with the
word.  Sure, I guess we can
hypothetically whip out our smartphones on our lunch breaks and do some Bible
study, but the point of our workplaces isn’t to teach us about God.  The point of church is.  There are people here who are eager to feed
us, and in real life, if Christians aren’t getting nourishment here, they’re
not getting it anywhere.

Second, we assemble **TO
CONNECT** with God.  Look at what Paul
reveals in Ephesians 2:19-22.  Our church
building isn’t a temple of God.  It’s not
the place where He dwells.  However,
whenever we come together, whether in our building or elsewhere, that assembly
is God’s spiritual dwelling place.  God
is everywhere, but when we assemble, He is particularly here with us.

This is why it’s such nonsense when people talk about how
they’re spiritual but not religious, which usually means that they believe in
God but don’t belong to a church.  They
think that when they go out into the forest or the mountains, that they’re closest
to God out there, but the reality is that there is nowhere closer to God than
right here.  We can’t have a relationship
with God without also having a relationship with His people.  We come to God together, not as a
disconnected collection of spiritual Lone Rangers.

As a result, if people love God, this is where they will
be.  They yearn for His presence, so they
assemble where He is present. 
Conversely, those who aren’t interested in assembling here show that
they don’t love God, or at least that they don’t believe what He tells them in
Ephesians 2.  People like this often
complain that they feel something is missing from their lives.  What they’re missing is the presence of God
in the assembly of His people.

Third, we assemble **TO
WORSHIP**.  I can’t describe this
action any better than the psalmist describes it in Psalm 95:1-7.  We get some sense of the importance of
worship when we call our assemblies “worship services”.  Fundamentally, we are here to glorify God,
and that’s true not only of our coming together in this building, but of our
very existence.

Worship serves at least two critical functions.  First, it gives God what He deserves.  He is our Creator.  We exist because of Him, and He gives us
every good thing.  He gave us His Son to
save us, and we owe Him a massive debt. 
The least we can do is offer the sacrifice of praise in response.  If we don’t do that, we’re as ungrateful as
we could possibly be.

Second, worship is an essential form of mental
calibration.  We’ve all heard the saying,
“He thinks the world revolves around him.” 
The reality is that there are an awful lot of people who really do think
the world revolves around them.  They put
themselves right at the center of it all, and there’s an impulse inside each
one of us to do exactly the same.  However,
when we worship, rather than putting ourselves at the center of the universe,
we put God there.  We remind ourselves
that He is more important than we are, and this realization calls us to live
accordingly.

The duty to worship is a responsibility we have even if we
find ourselves assembling with a church as dead as Sardis.  However, that isn’t the case here.  Our worship, particularly our song worship,
is stirring and powerful, thanks to the wholehearted involvement of song
leaders and congregation alike.  Why
shouldn’t we be here to glorify God every change we get?

**Sharing in the Lord’s
Supper**

During our final segment this morning, because we’ve already
spent so much time considering relationships, let’s ponder the sharing that occurs
during the Lord’s Supper.  Most
obviously, this includes our sharing **WITH
CHRIST.** Look at what the Lord says
in Luke 22:14-16.  Rarely does Jesus say
something that more clearly reveals His thinking and emotions than His words
here do.  It reminds me of a scene in an
action movie, where the hero is about to go off to face the bad guy, but before
he does, he goes home and watches his sleeping children for a few moments.  He’s reminding himself, “This is why I’m doing
what I’m doing.”

I think exactly the same thought process is going through
Jesus’ mind here.  He knows without a
shadow of a doubt that He is about to be arrested, humiliated, beaten, and
killed in a slow and agonizing way.  He
doesn’t have a wife and kids, and even His own earthly brothers have rejected
Him.  His disciples, this motley band of
fishermen, tax collectors, and rebels, these people are His real family.  They don’t know it, but their eternal
salvation depends on His willingness to die for them.  And so Jesus wants to eat with them one last
time, with all of their cluelessness, bravado, and ridiculous concerns, because
He loves them, and they are the reason why He is about to die.

Fundamentally, we share in the Lord’s Supper to commune with
Jesus.  We remember Him in the knowledge
that He has never forgotten us.  Even
now, He sees us in our foolishness and imperfection, no better than the
disciples, and loves us enough that He died for us too.

Second, the Lord’s Supper is a sharing **WITH ONE ANOTHER.** Consider
Paul’s words in 1 Corinthians 10:16-17.  As
with our singing, God intends for our sharing in the Lord’s Supper to be an
expression of our spiritual unity.  With
His body and His blood, Jesus purchased everyone who is a part of this local
body of Christians.  We probably aren’t
people who would have much to do with each other if we didn’t worship
together.  We differ from one another in
race, background, education, age, political views, and position in life.  According to the thinking of our age, all of
us should scatter off to our own little tribes of people who are like us, but
that’s not what Jesus wants.

Instead, He wants us to understand that all of our
differences are outweighed by one central fact, the fact that He died for each
one of us.  In so doing, the Son of God has
shown us that every Christian here has an unimaginable worth.  That, first and foremost, is what we should
see when we look at one another: 
somebody who was bought with the precious blood of Jesus.  Next to that, all of the distinctions that
matter so much to the world become insignificant.

Our responsibility, then, is to allow that value to inform
the way that we treat, speak to, and think of one another.  We are to be one, as Jesus and His Father are
one.  We are to love and forgive one
another as Christ has loved and forgiven us. 
When we partake of the Lord’s Supper, we are pledging ourselves to this
sacred goal.

Finally, in the Lord’s Supper, we share **WITH PAST AND FUTURE.** Flipping
forward a chapter, let’s examine what Paul writes in 1 Corinthians
11:23-26.  Philosophically, it’s very
important to us to imitate the first-century church.  This is a practice that connects us back to
them.  I don’t think they partook of the
Lord’s Supper the same way that we do.  I
doubt they had shiny silver trays, and they certainly didn’t hand them down the
pews in their church buildings!  However,
the essence of the practice remains the same. 
We use the same unleavened bread and fruit of the vine they did, and we
too remember the death of the Lord Jesus and commune with one another.

We are united in faith with them as we are with our brethren
here today.

Of course, there’s a forward-looking element to our
experience too.  They shared in the
Lord’s Supper 2000 years ago, we share in it today, and next week, if there is
a next week, we will share in it once again. 
The story of the dying love of Jesus will be repeated, week by week,
until the time when He returns to claim for His own everyone who has ever loved
Him.  Our feast is not merely a memorial
one.  It is anticipatory too.

Before the Lord’s Supper, then, which lies at the heart of
our Sunday worship, let’s reflect on these things.  Rather than being distracted by whatever
thought comes flitting through our brains, let’s concentrate on our communion
with Christ, with one another, and with all His people everywhere, as together
we participate in showing forth His death until He comes.
