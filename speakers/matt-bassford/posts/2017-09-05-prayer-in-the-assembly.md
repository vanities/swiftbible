As New-Testament Christians, our view of religious hierarchy
is much simpler than most.  We take our
cue from the teaching of Jesus in Matthew 23, where He observes that there is
one Father and we all are brothers. 
There is no Scriptural basis for all those complicated organizational
flow charts in the denominational world. 
All of us have equal access to God.

There is, of course, one significant exception to the
rule—the Lord Himself.  Every Christian
is a priest, but He is our high priest. 
We have the right to approach God in heaven on our own, but we have that
right because of Him.  Let’s prepare our
minds for the Lord’s Supper this morning, then, by considering the Hebrews
writer’s thoughts on Jesus, our high priest.



This discussion begins with the writer’s commentary on **DRAWING NEAR.** Look at Hebrews 4:14-16.  In this context, the writer tells us to do
two surprising things.  The first is that
we are to draw near with confidence to the throne of grace.  Imagine that! 
Compared to almighty God, all of us are a bunch of insects.  By rights, we should have no business thinking
that we even should attract the attention of One so much greater than we
are.  And yet, the writer tells us not
only that we should draw near, but that we should draw near boldly:  “Here I am, God!  It’s me, the insect!”

Contextually, the writer’s insistence that we should hold
fast to our confession is equally surprising. 
Remember:  Hebrews is written to
people who are in the middle of being persecuted.  The writer tells them that if they continue,
some of them are going to pay for their faith with their lives.  Even so, they need to continue anyway.  What in the world is going on with that?

The answer to both questions is the same.  They, and we, should hold fast and draw near
because we have Jesus as our high priest. 
He gets us.  Even though He is
God, He understands perfectly well what it’s like to be human because He became
human too.  He knows what it’s like to
keep serving God, even when it’s hard. 
He sympathizes so much, in fact, that He gives us the right to bring our
little problems to the Creator whenever we want.  How amazing He is!

The writer then explains the work of Christ further by
identifying the traits of **EARTHLY
PRIESTS.** Look at Hebrews 5:1-4.  In particular, there are four priestly
attributes that the writer zeroes in on. 
The first is that the earthly priests acted as go-betweens for men to
God.  The Israelites understood how
perilous it was to approach the Holy One. 
A priest would stand in their stead and make sure that they had the
connection to God that they needed but were afraid to form.

Second, because of his own human weaknesses, the earthly
priest dealt kindly with the sinners who came to him for help.  It wasn’t like he had some right to get up on
his pedestal and wag his finger at them. 
He was imperfect too!  This, by
the way, is something that all of us should remember.  Before we get angry and self-righteous about
somebody else’s sin, whether they’re Christians or not, we should consider our
own sins.  We aren’t holy because we’re
perfect; we’re holy because we’re perfected, and we should be gentle with those
who are imperfect too.

Third, the earthly priest offered sacrifices for sin.  This was necessary.  The Israelites had to do something to atone
for all the times they violated God’s law. 
If they didn’t want to die themselves, something else had to die.  Before he could offer sacrifices on behalf of
the people, though, the priest had to offer sacrifices for himself.  He was sinful too.

Finally, the priest wasn’t a priest because one day he up
and decided to become a priest.  Instead,
he had to be called by God.  In fact,
this call came to Aaron, first of all of the Israelite high priests, and only
those who were descended from him had the right to officiate.

In comparison to all of this, the writer next considers **CHRIST.**Let’s read on in Hebrews 5:5-10. 
Notice first of all that this is a chiasm.  In other words, the writer is going to talk
about the same things he talked about in v. 1-4, except it’s going to come in
reverse order.  It’s called a chiasm
because it’s shaped like the Greek letter chi, which looks like an X, and Christ
is at the center of it.

First, like the earthly priests, Christ was appointed.  He wasn’t a priest by virtue of His descent
from Aaron; in fact, Jesus came from the tribe of Judah, not Levi.  Instead, He was a priest because of the
decree of God, who prophetically announced Him in Psalms 2 and 110.

Second, like the earthly priest, Jesus made offerings while
He was on earth, and He even made them on His own behalf.  However, these offerings weren’t dead animals
burned on an altar, and they weren’t because Christ had sins that He needed to
atone for.  Instead, the offerings of
Jesus were His supplications and prayers for God to rescue Him from physical
death.  Because of Jesus’ perfect
faithfulness, God heard Him and raised Him.

Third, Christ’s personal behavior fitted Him to serve as a
priest.  However, unlike the earthly
priest who learned compassion for the sinner from his own encounters with sin,
Christ learned what obedience meant through His sufferings.  He didn’t sin, but He came to understand why
we might.

Finally, like the earthly priest, Christ acts for men to
repair their relationship with God. 
However, He doesn’t do this by means of external gifts and
sacrifices.  Instead, He Himself, by
virtue of His perfect life and perfect death, is the source of our eternal
salvation.

There are many benefits that we receive as a result of the
high priesthood of Jesus, but one of the most significant of them all is the
right to pray.  The more we mature as
Christians, the more we understand the power and importance of prayer.  I don’t think that there’s anything too big
or too small for us to pray about.  If we
can’t find our keys, there’s not a thing in the world wrong with asking God to
help us find them.  However, some prayers
are better offered individually than corporately.  There are probably better uses for our time
here than praying for everybody’s lost keys! 
This morning, then, let’s consider the nature of prayer in the assembly.

**How We Should Pray**

Let’s begin by considering what the Scripture has to say
about how we should pray when we pray together. 
First, God wants us to understand that we should pray **FROM HOLINESS.** Consider Paul’s instructions in 1 Timothy
2:8.  When first we read this passage,
it’s tempting to read it as an instruction for our physical posture in prayer,
that we’re supposed to literally stretch out our hands like we see people doing
in those old paintings sometimes. 
However, I don’t think that’s the point at all.  In Isaiah 1, for instance, God actually
condemns His people for spreading out their hands before Him.  The problem was that those hands were covered
in blood.  If we come to God in our
unrepentant sin, it doesn’t matter what physical position we assume!

Back in 1 Timothy 2, then, the focus of Paul’s commandment
isn’t on the lifting up.  It’s on the
presence of holiness and the absence of wrath and quarrelling.  The prayers that we lead in the assembly are
in some ways distinct from any other areas of our lives, but in other ways,
they’re a continuation of those lives. 
We can’t be hypocritical and wicked all week, then come trotting into
the church building with our pious face pasted on and call on the name of the
Lord like we’re the most obedient disciples who ever were!  Maybe nobody else in the congregation knows
what we’re up to, but God certainly does, and He is sickened by our insincerity
and corruption.

The same thing is true of anger and quarrelling.  How dare we constantly get into it with our
friends, our families, and especially our brethren, then come before God in the
name of the Prince of Peace!  If we want
peace with Him, we’d better be working on peace with one another too.

Second, our prayers should be combined **WITH THANKSGIVING.** Consider
Philippians 4:6.  I don’t think this
verse is aimed exclusively at our prayers in the assembly, but I think it’s no
less important there than everywhere.  There
are certainly so-called Christian groups out there that have an attitude toward
prayer that is quite frankly greedy. 
This spirit can only produce prayers that look like Christmas lists and
are much more reflective of covetousness than of reverence.

In our prayers, rather than being greedy, we should be
grateful.  Before we make our requests
known to God, we should thank Him for all that He has already done.  For every single one of us, the list of the
ways that God has already blessed us is immense!

Mingling our petitions with thanksgiving serves two
important functions.  First, it helps us
to pray with humility rather than with arrogance.  Some people actually have the nerve to get
angry with God when He doesn’t answer their prayers the way they want.  That kind of behavior reminds me of when we
give our kids donuts for breakfast as a special treat and they start pitching
fits because they don’t get the kind of donut they wanted.  I mean, really?  Rather than focusing on everything that has
just gone right, they’re focusing on the little bit that hasn’t.  As long as we’re in Christ, we will always
have more to be thankful for than to complain about.

Second, remembering the past helps us to pray in faith.  After all, we’ve already seen God’s
graciousness in so many ways.  We can be
sure from that that He will bless us again in future.

Third, we should pray **IN
FAITH.** Here, look at Mark
11:24.  I think this is a passage that a
lot of Christians struggle with.  After
all, we all know that there have been times when we prayed for something and
didn’t get what we asked for.

However, I think once we sort out the context, it becomes
clear what Jesus is talking about.  The
immediate context of this text is the story of the withered fig tree, where
Jesus curses a fig tree because it didn’t have any fruit on it, and the tree
dies.  However, the other context of this
story is of events like Jesus driving the moneychangers out of the temple and
confronting the chief priests.  Jesus
didn’t curse the fig tree because He up and decided to get mad at a plant for
no good reason.  Instead, He cursed it as
a parable, an illustration of what was going to happen to God’s people because
they rejected Him.  The timing of God’s
judgment against the rebellious Jewish nation was in part going to be
determined by the prayers of the persecuted church, so what Jesus is really
saying is, “God will fulfill His promise to rescue you and judge the wicked,
even though the enemies of the gospel look powerful and untouchable, and you are
few and powerless.”

This offers us a way to understand what it means for us to
pray in faith—faith that God will keep His promises.  He kept His word to the first-century church
by destroying its enemies, and He will surely keep His promises to us too.  When we pray for anything He has promised,
like wisdom or forgiveness of sins, we can be sure we will get it.

Finally, we should pray **STEADFASTLY.** This idea appears in Colossians 4:2.  Both as individuals and as a group, it’s easy
for us sometimes to give up on prayer. 
This reminds me of one of my hobby horses, when Christians borrow
language from Jesus’ prayer in the garden and say, “Not my will, but Yours be
done.”  As I’ve said before, in those
circumstances, Jesus knew something that none of the rest of us can ever know,
that God was certainly not going to give Him what He asked for.  We never have the same reason for resignation
to the divine will.

Besides, it goes without saying.  God’s will is always going to be done.  We pray because we want to change His will,
and the more steadfastly we pray, the more likely we are to change it.  How many times did Elijah pray for rain in 1
Kings 18 before rain finally showed up? 
If, after the first prayer, He had said, “Not my will, but yours be
done,” and given up, he wouldn’t have gotten what he wanted.  I think the same rule applies to us.  Seeing that God carries out His will is His
concern, not ours.  Our concern is to be
what God wants us to be—a people persistent in prayer.

**Whom We Should Pray
For**

Just as the Scriptures tell us how we should pray, so they
reveal whom we should pray for, particularly in the assembly.  This is, of course, not an exhaustive list,
but I think all of these groups should make regular appearances in our
congregational prayers.  First, we ought
to pray for **OUR RULERS.** Consider here 1 Timothy 2:1-3.  It’s true that we live under a different
system of government than did Christians in the first century.  Unlike them, we select our own rulers.  However, I believe that even for us, our most
important political action is still prayer.

Note that Paul urges us to pray for all who are in high
positions—not just the ones we like or agree with, but all of them.  Just like God blesses all men, we should pray
for all of our rulers, and in much the same spirit of love.  We should ask God’s blessing on the guy we
voted for and are happy to see in office, but we should be equally constant in
asking God’s blessing on the guy we didn’t vote for and are unhappy with.  Arguably, in fact, we should be even more
sincere and fervent in praying for the latter. 
After all, he obviously needs the help!

I think it’s appropriate to ask God help leaders make wise
and godly decisions, but Paul here is particularly concerned with our freedom
to worship.  We have this right
currently, but I believe it would be wise for us to pray fervently that it will
continue.  I do not think that even 10
years from now, our nation will be as friendly toward our faith as it is now.  We constantly need to ask that God will allow
us to remain undisturbed.

Second, we should pray for **PREACHERS.** In fact, this is
a subject to which Paul frequently returns, among other places, in 2
Thessalonians 3:1-2.  As is often the
case, he’s requesting a prayer with two parts: 
an appeal that he might be bold, and an appeal for protection from those
he offends when he is bold.

Certainly, every preacher will appreciate both of those
prayers being offered on his behalf.  I’m
a pretty forthright guy, but I still struggle with the temptation to soft-pedal
the truth when I’m afraid that somebody won’t want to hear it.  Boldness is no less important to preachers
today!

However, I think there’s an even more fundamental prayer
lurking underneath both of those.  Paul
was an inspired apostle.  He always knew
what the truth was; the question was whether he would be determined enough to
proclaim it fully.

Today, though, it’s easy for preachers to be mistaken about
what the truth is.  Nobody’s going to get
it right 100 percent of the time.  In
addition to boldness, then, my preaching brethren and I need wisdom,
understanding, and the ability to discern the right before we proclaim it.  The more the church prays for us in these
things, the happier and more effective we will be!

Finally, when we pray together, we should pray for **THE SICK.**James makes this point in James 5:14-16.  This is a text that is directed primarily
toward elders and secondarily to the rest of us.  Whatever we think about elders anointing the
sick with oil, it’s really a moot point unless we happen to be elders!

What is not a moot point, though, is the identity of those
for whom we’re supposed to be praying. 
When we see “the sick”, we tend to think in terms of prayers for
physical illness, and there’s nothing wrong with offering such prayers, but I
don’t think that’s what James is talking about. 
Instead, I think he’s talking about those who are spiritually ill.

There are a couple of things that lead me to this
conclusion.  First, elders are not
doctors.  Even though I believe their prayers
are powerful on any occasion, their particular expertise is with problems that
are spiritual, not physical.

Second, James makes promises that these prayers will be
effective.  The one who calls for the
elders will be raised up.  The one who is
prayed for will be healed.  Those things
are not true in a physical context. 
We’ve all prayed for people who went on to die in physical terms.  However, they are true spiritually.  If we come to God seeking spiritual help, as
long as we pray in faith, He will certainly bless us with the help we
seek.  It’s good for us to be concerned
about the physical needs of our brethren, but it’s particularly important for
us to be concerned about their spiritual need, and to reflect that concern in
our prayers.
