A couple weeks ago, I fielded one of the more unusual
requests I’ve received as a preacher.  A
sister who is a member at Joliet and her non-Christian son asked if I would be
willing to pray with them after services because his father had just died.  As I talked with them for a few minutes
before praying, it quickly became evident what was behind their request.  There hadn’t been any funeral for the
deceased, so they felt like they needed some kind of event to give them
closure.  This was the idea on which they
had settled.

Strange though it may seem to many of us, skipping the
funeral has actually become a trend.  A
funeral director I talked to a couple years back (if you’re a preacher, you get
to talk shop with undertakers) opined that the future for his occupation looked
bleak.  More and more people are unwilling
to pay for a funeral because they don’t see a point in having one.



This, I think, is a significant straw in the wind.  It shows the direction that American society
is headed.  Generally speaking, funerals
are two things.  They are an expression
of religious belief and an expression of community.  A decline in the rate of funerals, then,
speaks to a decline in those two things.

I’ve already talked at length about the decline of religion
in America, but the (closely linked) decline of community is equally
interesting.  Since the beginning, most
people in most places have lived communal lives.  You defined yourself first as part of a
group, and to be on your own was to be an outcast like the wretches described
in Job 30:1-8.

Over the past 50 years, however, it has become possible for
Americans to live an increasingly insular existence.  Money can purchase and government programs
can provide many of the benefits that used to come from the community.  When your house gets flooded, you get your
help from FEMA rather than your neighbors. 
Also, mass media and the Internet appear to offer the benefits of social
interaction with a community, but without its many frustrations.  You have to put up with the annoying guy at
church, but you can exile the annoying guy on TV with a click of your remote.

However, if it man now can be an island, he is at best an
unhappy island.  The modern replacement
of community is more apparent than real. 
FEMA might rebuild your house, but they won’t come to your potluck.  The people on TV won’t annoy you, but they
won’t attend your funeral either.  You
can exist without a community, but you can’t truly live, and many Americans
have made a career of trying to fill that hole in their lives with everything
but what will actually work.

This is tragic for them, but it is a golden opportunity for
Christians.  The gospel of Christ is
first and foremost a gospel of community. 
While we rightly emphasize the plan of salvation, we often forget that
salvation is a means, not an end.  Forgiveness
of sins is meaningless if it doesn’t affect our relationships with God and with
others.  It is transformative precisely
because it transforms those relationships. 
As John puts it in 1 John 1:3, “What we have seen and heard we proclaim
to you also, so that you too may have fellowship with us; and indeed our
fellowship is with the Father, and with His Son Jesus Christ.” (NASB)

The first-century pattern for the church aligns closely with
this goal.  It doesn’t offer the rock
concerts and coffee shops that appeal to the consumerist, individualist mindset
of the modern American, but it is optimally designed to build community.  2000 years ago, it melded people from every
demographic group imaginable into a unified whole, and it does the same thing
today.

Congregational a-cappella singing is a community-building
exercise.  [A recent study](http://rsos.royalsocietypublishing.org/content/2/10/150221) showed that singing together is a marvelous way for adults to connect with one
another.  Communal prayer has a similar
function.  The Lord’s Supper is
explicitly about horizontal and vertical fellowship.  Sermons and Bible classes define the beliefs
and practices of the group.  Mix that in
with an outside-the-assembly lifestyle of shared meals and self-sacrificing
love, and you’ve established a community with extraordinary emotional power.

I suspect that many preachers have noticed, as I have, that
the Bible doesn’t have a whole lot to say about how ordinary Christians should
share the gospel with the lost.  However,
there are whole books of the New Testament that instruct ordinary Christians in
how to get along with other Christians—in other words, in how to preserve and
enhance community.  Perhaps this is
because community was how ordinary Christians in the first century appealed to
the lost.

Even today, in a nominally majority-Christian country, most
people don’t buy in to the morality of the Bible.  As a result, the plan of salvation is
answering a question they aren’t asking. 
Why would they care that they are violating the norms of a group they
don’t belong to?  Still less would
first-century pagans have been concerned about sinning against the God of
Israel!

The only way to get the answer to make sense is to get
people to ask the question, and at least a very good way to get them asking the
question is to get them to buy into the norms of the group.  Why would they want to do that?  Because it ought to be evident that the Lord’s
church is a wonderful group to belong to! 
They see how Christians treat one another, and they say, “I want that
too.”

Maybe people aren’t asking “What must I do to be
saved?”  However, there are millions who
are asking, “How can I fill this hole in my life?”  We’ve got a great answer to that question.  In fact, I believe we have the very best
possible answer.  A church isn’t a mere
social club.  It is the working out in a
particular place and time of the timeless love of Christ.  That is what we're supposed to be inviting people into--not merely a personal relationship with God, but a communal relationship with God and with His people too.
