It is true that I haven’t used the Legacy at the church
building for any prolonged period of time. 
I don’t know whether the cover is going to start falling off after three
years of use.  However, I did put it
through a two-week tour of duty, and that time was enough to give me an idea of
what kind of a preaching, teaching, and study Bible it is.

**PREACHING.** First, the size of the Legacy works well
when I’m preaching.  I’ve already
commented on the odd heaviness of the volume for its size, and one of the
consequences of that is that it rests solidly in the hand.  At 6” x 9”, it is more than large enough to
hold a sermon outline during the journey to the pulpit.

 Sadly, the layout of the Legacy is less than ideal for
preaching.  In its favor, the inner
margins are generous, and the text is moderately sized, on unusually opaque
paper, and line-matched.  This makes the
Bible easy to read in the pulpit.

However, problems arise from its format.  I prefer single-column, verse-by-verse Bibles
for preaching.  The Legacy is certainly
single-column, but it’s paragraphed.  As
a result, instead of being conspicuously aligned along the left-hand margin, all
the verse numbers are tucked away in the paragraphs.

This problem is made worse by the font that Crossway chose
for the verse numbers.  They’re small,
they’re, superscript, they’re sans serif, and they’re boldface.  In consequence, they’re easy to overlook, and
if you do happen to notice them, they look a lot like quotation marks.  At my desk, when I have time to squint at the
text to make sure I got the right reference, that’s not as big a deal.  When I’m in the pulpit, trying to locate
verses *quickly* above all else, it
becomes important.

I preached two sermons using the Legacy, both of which were
heavily Bible-reliant.  The Legacy didn’t
give me any trouble during the first sermon, but I missed a citation during the
second, and I did so because of the format of the Legacy.

The text in question was Hebrews 2:5-8.  In our versification system, v. 8 is a split
verse.  The first half of it concludes a
poetic quotation from Psalm 8; the second contains the Hebrews writer’s prose
commentary on the quotation.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgSf6cx-5RGPbrvsTy2SdCeT_zr1PkwuKTQJoFRbljPDupOtUdytIOnXni_B9k29gzfbvb8BFvMI0AfW9p1AKdxP7Fm1141A1323BvWrGM32jV2rVYqA3iwNOKCTyGREZ-KRM449fXizw/s1600/PTS3.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgSf6cx-5RGPbrvsTy2SdCeT_zr1PkwuKTQJoFRbljPDupOtUdytIOnXni_B9k29gzfbvb8BFvMI0AfW9p1AKdxP7Fm1141A1323BvWrGM32jV2rVYqA3iwNOKCTyGREZ-KRM449fXizw/s1600/PTS3.jpg)



The Legacy separates the poetic quotation from the second
half of the verse with two hard returns. 
When I was reading the passage during my sermon, I saw the line break,
assumed the verse had come to an end, and did not drop down below the line
break to read the rest of the verse.  I
may well have noticed the absence of a number for v. 9 if I were using a
setting that didn’t have the line break and made verse numbers more prominent.

This was all the more awkward because my discussion of the
text relied in part on the content of the latter part of v. 8.  I had a vague something-isn’t-right-here
feeling even as I was preaching, but I didn’t realize my mistake until I resumed
the reading during the next point of my sermon. 
This alone would probably be enough to deter me from using the Legacy as
a pulpit Bible.

**TEACHING.** I didn’t have the chance to teach a Bible
class with the Legacy during its tour, but its size and format don’t really
commend themselves to that use either. 
The same strange heft that makes the Legacy satisfying to hold when I’m
preaching (and then set down on the pulpit with an authoritative thunk) would
give me problems during a 45-minute Bible class.  The Bible weighs nearly three pounds, and
that’s too heavy to carry without thought.

In terms of cross-references, the Legacy is a tweener
Bible.  It doesn’t have the 95,000
cross-references of a Foundation SCR, but it does contain translation notes,
Old-Testament prophecy citations, Synoptic citations, and the like at the
bottom of the page.  Generally, those
things are the only cross-references I use anyway, but those who want a more
robust cross-referencing system would be well advised to look elsewhere.

**STUDY.** In the office, the Legacy was so-so
too.  I was fine with the size, I didn’t
hate reading a lot of verses in it, but once again, I was tripped up by the
verse numbers.  In this particular case,
I was preparing a section of a sermon on John 6:53-69.  I decided to include the reaction of the Jews
in v. 52, but the teeny-tiny verse numbers (the notation for v. 52 in the
Legacy looks an awful lot like v. 53) obscured the fact that I was indeed
including another verse.  I caught the
error during my preparation routine, but I’d still rather have a Bible that
helped me avoid errors in the first place.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEiQhE15c3o00JOTepgHDLhv3jzmPemQAvfn5u9JglF8zNrEupWtiX47rRAZs9OT05BbvsS-5JQ6VydCC5g_b9xWlSa-KWrEypIK3NhbkHdzeDrmwtKo9VxfZUGrQttpv6aGIp1L2gwRMA/s1600/PTS5.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEiQhE15c3o00JOTepgHDLhv3jzmPemQAvfn5u9JglF8zNrEupWtiX47rRAZs9OT05BbvsS-5JQ6VydCC5g_b9xWlSa-KWrEypIK3NhbkHdzeDrmwtKo9VxfZUGrQttpv6aGIp1L2gwRMA/s1600/PTS5.jpg)

**CONCLUSION.** The Legacy is a lovely Bible, no doubt
about that.  When I left it sitting in
the pulpit after I was finished one week, a member of the congregation
commented on how nice the layout looked simply to look at.  Sadly, that gorgeous form doesn’t lend itself
to gorgeous function, at least not for me in my preaching, teaching, and
study.  In particular, the hard-to-locate
verse numbers and the missed citations they cause are a problem.  As a result, I can’t rate the Legacy any higher
in this category than 3.5/5.
