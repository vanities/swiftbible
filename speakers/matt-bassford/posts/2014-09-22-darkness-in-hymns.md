*If indeed many brethren write particularly dark hymns, the darkness may come from the Bible itself.*

If you spend enough time looking at hymns, you start
noticing patterns.  One such pattern is
that writers from a particular time period will tend to write from a particular
perspective and focus on a particular set of topics.  In the repertoire of the Lord’s church, the
most conspicuous example of this is found in the Stamps-Baxter-style hymns from
the radio era, say 1920 to 1960.  More
than anything else, writers from that time loooved to write about heaven.  It is likely true that there are more Stamps-Baxter
hymns about heaven than about all other possible topics put together, and they
all employ the same imagery of homes above, golden strands, meeting to part no
more, etc.



After the most recent Hymninar, one of the attendees made a
similar observation about writers in the church today—that our shared outlook
appears to be a dark one.  She certainly
had justification for making such a claim. 
During our devotions throughout the week, various song leaders often
selected hymns by the Lutheran writers of the sixteenth and seventeenth
centuries.

Of all the different eras represented in *Psalms, Hymns, and Spiritual Songs*, that
time is the grimmest.  Its hymnists wrote
against the backdrop of cheerful events like the Thirty Years’ War, during
which a third of the population of Germany was killed.  The quality of the Lutheran hymns is superb,
but they do tend to start at dreary and go downhill from there.  A sister I know who was raised Lutheran calls
them “Lutheran funeral music”.

A similar darkness also appeared, though, in the hymns we
ourselves were/are writing.  Probably the
most commonly chosen topic was the crucifixion of the Lord.  Other popular topics included enduring
through trial and various forms of dying (the deaths of the martyrs, dying to
self, and our own literal deaths). 
Thankfully, we aren’t enduring our own Thirty Years’ War (and may such a
disaster never overtake us or our children!), so what’s with all the gloom?

In truth, I think the gloom is borrowed rather than our
own.  It begins with our traditional
search for objectivity in spiritual matters. 
The rallying cry of the Lord’s church has always been, “It is
written!”  We are unpersuaded by others’
subjective experiences of salvation, speaking in tongues, working miracles, or
anything else.  The scope of our faith is
bounded by book, chapter, and verse.

So. . . what are hymnists from such a religious tradition
going to write about?  Are they going to
write, like Fanny J. Crosby, about “the pure delight of a single hour that before
Thy throne I spend”?  We’re fine with
singing such a thing, which entered the repertoire long before any of us were
alive, but we’re probably not going to write it.  None of us would stand up in the middle of a
Bible class and proclaim our pure delight in this single hour, even if we felt
it strongly.  It would seem boastful and
not very relevant.

Writers in the church, then,  are going to return to that which frames the
rest of their religious experience: 
book, chapter, and verse.  We put
more Bible in our hymns than *anybody.* I’ve seen brethren format hymns with
Scripture citations above each phrase, just in case the singer would otherwise
miss the point.  Even our first-person
pronouns are often borrowed from the Bible. 
The “my” in “The Lord Is My Light” isn’t Charli Couchman’s.  It’s David’s. 
As a result, hymnists in the church often produce hymns in which their
individual perspective cannot be discerned.

Instead, what often shines through is the perspective of the
Scripture, and let’s face it—the Bible generally and the New Testament
particularly is a pretty gloomy book. 
The central event of the gospel is God dying on a cross.  First-century Christians lived in and wrote
about a hostile world filled with temptation, persecution, and suffering.  The miracles of Jesus were commonly greeted
not with applause, but with fear.  Even
the brightest parts of the New Testament are a study in contrasts:  salvation despite sin, joy despite difficult
circumstances, peace despite conflict, and life despite death.  Like our pronouns, our gloom is borrowed from
the first century.

This is not the way that most self-professed Christians in
modern America are inclined to see things. 
They find the gospel of a Joel Osteen more congenial.  The national setting predisposes them, and
indeed us, to believe that God wants us all to be rich and happy.  This conviction finds its way into
contemporary praise songs, many of which somehow manage to express love for
Jesus without mentioning dirty words like “sin”.  Perhaps the authors love Jesus because they
think He helps them with their car payment.

The Bible, however, warns us that though such a setting may
be more pleasant, it is no less perilous. 
The hope of riches is an empty one, and it is easier for a camel to pass
through the eye of a needle than for the rich to inherit the kingdom of
God.  Worldly pleasure is nothing more
than a mask over the ugly reality that even the righteous are scarcely
saved.

We know that our hymns are supposed to teach and admonish,
and an admonition is a warning.  Don’t we
need warnings about the illusions of our culture most of all?  The moral of the story may well be that
Christian hymnists ought to be writing dark hymns, not because our society
predisposes us to darkness, but precisely because it doesn’t.
