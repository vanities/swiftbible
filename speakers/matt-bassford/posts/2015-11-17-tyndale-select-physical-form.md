**OVERVIEW.** This is a high-end premium Bible.  It has everything you would expect from a
high-end premium Bible, including the price tag.

I got my copy of the Tyndale Select by requesting it from
the Tyndale marketing department.  They
sent it to me with no strings attached, other than my promise to review
it.  Here’s what I think of its
appearance and construction.



**VITAL STATISTICS**

Translation:  New
Living Translation (2015)

Cover:  Goatskin

Binding:  Sewn

Trim Size:  5 ⅜” x 8 ¼”

Text Block Width:  1 ½
”

Weight:  2 pounds, 4
ounces

Column:  Single

Layout:  Paragraphed

Print Size:  8.75/11.5
Lexicon No. 1

Words of Christ: 
Black

Cross-References:  Yes

Ribbon Markers:  2

**SIZE.** The Select is larger than I expected it
to be.  When I read about it online
(single-column reference format, 8.75-point font, etc.), I came away from the
description with the impression that this would be a Clarion-sized Bible.  It isn’t. 
It’s not a personal-sized Bible at all. 
Instead, it’s a fairly portly mid-sized Bible.  In my Bible collection (which keeps expanding
despite my best efforts), the closest analogue to the Select is my old-school
Nelson Teacher’s Edition ASV.  It’s not
going to fit in your glove box.  It’s not
going to fit in your purse.  This is a
primary Bible rather than a carry Bible.

**COVER.** The Select is printed and bound by
Jongbloed, which is the same printer that Cambridge uses, so it’s hardly
surprising that the cover is strongly reminiscent of a top-tier Clarion or
Wide-Margin Reference.  If you’ve handled
one of those, you know how the Select feels.

Like the Clarion and WM, the cover of the Select is
edge-lined goatskin.  Because of its
softness and durability, goatskin is generally considered. the best possible material
for a Bible cover (though devotees of Abba calfskin may disagree).

“Edge-lined” means that the cover of the Select is attached
to the book block using a different and more expensive method than that used in
mass-market Bibles.  All mass-market
Bibles have a paste-down binding.  This
means that the book block is attached to the cover by the endpapers, which are
glued to a piece of book board underneath the cover material itself.  This results in a stiffer cover that puts
more stress on the inside hinge of the cover, by the spine.

By contrast, edge-lined Bibles don’t have any bookboard at
all, nor do they have endpapers. 
Instead, the cover material is backed by a piece of similarly flexible
material (in the case of the Select, polyurethane).  A tab of that material is glued to the book
block, and a piece of leatherette is glued on top of it.  Thus, the Select has a poly hinge rather than
a paper or leatherette hinge, and because the cover is so flexible, less stress
is placed on the hinge when the Bible is opened (unless you make your Bibles do
Bible yoga like Mark Bertrand does, which you shouldn’t).  In the case of the Select, the goatskin cover
is attached to the polyurethane backing by perimeter sewing.

All of this makes for an extraordinarily durable Bible.  As part of my Bible-evaluation process, I try
to figure out how a Bible will fail.  I
flex the cover and examine the seams, endpapers, and hinges, looking for a weak
spot.  With the Select, I don’t have the
foggiest idea how or even if it will fall apart.  Even the hinge doesn’t seem to be a problem
because it never has to flex all the way open when I’m holding the Bible in my
hand.  When it comes to prophecies of
doom, I’m drawing a blank.  My Select
will perish because one of my kids drops it in the toilet, maybe?  All the Selects come with a lifetime
warranty, but I don’t see Tyndale having to replace too many of these.  The Bible is meant to be used for decades.

Also, I find the Select to be as lovable as it is
durable.  As a goatskin Bible should, it
has that delicious leathery smell. 
There’s a gilt line around the perimeter of the lining that adds a
classy touch.  On the edge of the cover
away from the spine, the Select has a semi-yapp.  In other words, the cover extends ⅜” beyond the edge of the
book block instead of the usual ¼”, and it curves inward a little bit.  This doesn’t interfere with the use of the
Bible, but it does look cool.

I even like the aesthetic of the imprinting on the
cover.  No repeating the publisher’s name
or the translation half a dozen times here. 
Just “Tyndale”, “Holy Bible”, and “New Living Translation”.  The spine doesn’t have any ribs on it; there
is nothing on the front cover.  The whole
thing conveys a sense of understated class, which, to be honest, has me
considering the NLT in a way I hadn’t before. 
This is the kind of cover you put on a Bible that’s meant to be taken
seriously.

In a high-premium Bible, you’d better do everything about
the cover right.  Tyndale did.

**TEXT BLOCK**.  The Select is sewn.  This comes as no surprise.  I have trouble imagining a Bible publisher
dumb enough to produce an edge-lined goatskin Bible and then glue the text
block.  As a result, Select owners will
be spared the frustration of watching their Bibles shed pages.

A couple of other features are worth talking about
here.  The first is that the goatskin
versions of the select are art-gilded. 
This means that red dye is applied to the edges of the pages before they
are gilded.  As a result, the page edges
appear reddish gold when the Bible is closed and red when the Bible is
opened.  Maybe it’s just my copy, but the
art-gilding in the Select looks a little darker than in comparable Cambridge
Bibles.

This takes us to my only complaint about the construction of
the Select:  the ribbons.  Normally, I roll my eyes at Bible geeks who
get all excited/frustrated about ribbons. 
Who cares?  It’s a ribbon!  You use it to mark your place!  However, the ribbons in the Select are the
only part of its construction that appears to be less than top quality.  They’re made out of some kind of thin black
fabric, they’re not quite as long as I would like them to be, and almost as
soon as I opened the Bible, they began fraying. 
I had to trim the ends and apply clear nail polish to keep them from
unraveling.  That’s not a big deal, but
it does keep the Select from achieving the Mary Poppins ideal of being
practically perfect in every respect.

**VALUE.** Of course, if you want a Bible with all
these physical attributes, you’re gonna have to pay for them.  Right now, the sticker price on the Select is
$290.  I don’t care who you are; that’s a
lot of money to drop on a Bible.  Even
the retail price on a goatskin Cambridge Clarion is “only” $250.  However, if you want a premium NLT, your
choices are either the Select or its relative the Schuyler Caxton, which is the
Select’s book block in a Schuyler-designed cover.  I think the choice between the two is largely
a matter of taste.  Some people are
Select people.  Some people are Caxton
people.

I wouldn’t recommend the Select to anybody as their first
NLT (unless you’re just a Bible collector and never intend to use the
thing).  However, if you know you like
the translation, intend to use it, and love fine Bibles, this is an excellent
choice.  It’s hardly a steal, even at the
deeply discounted online prices, but it offers solid value for money.  I think the Select is deserving here of an
8.5/10.
