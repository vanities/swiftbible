Because every one of us is a unique person, each one of us struggles with unique spiritual difficulties.  Our dear departed brother Dennis Clark fought against alcoholism all his life, to the point where, 20 years after he took his last drink, he still described himself as a recovering alcoholic.  By contrast, I’ve never drunk a drop of alcohol in my life, and I don’t understand why people would want to drink or become drunk.  And yet, Dennis was an amiable soul who would surely have been baffled by my struggles with contentiousness.  That doesn’t mean that he was better than I am, or vice versa.  It just means that we’re different.

Despite these differences, though, there are some sins that are truly universal, and perhaps first on the list are the sins of the tongue.  All of us, at some point in our lives, have said something evil.  What’s more, evil speech is one of the most difficult evils to eradicate from our lives.  It’s an area where we all need help.  Let’s consider this morning, then, what the Scripture says about governing our speech.


**James on Speech**

The best place to begin this study is with James’ exploration of the issue, probably the longest single section of Scripture to address the problems generated by our tongues.  It begins with a warning to TEACHERS.  Look at James 3:1.  For many Christians, this is a difficult text to master because it runs counter to the views they hold of heaven and hell.  We are so used to distinguishing between the saved and the lost that we assume that all the saved have the same experience in eternity, as do all the lost.

However, this binary view of the afterlife doesn’t really match up with Scripture.  For instance, Jesus makes clear in Luke 12 that the ignorant sinner will face a comparatively light punishment, the willful sinner will face a comparatively more severe punishment, and the Christian who misuses a position of responsibility in the church will face the most severe punishment of all.  Now, we could debate endlessly about just what makes these punishments different, but the fact of their difference isn’t really up for dispute.

It’s in this light that we need to read James 3:1.  Teachers are in a position of responsibility in the church, and they need to take that responsibility seriously.  For better or for worse, their words can have an eternal effect not just on their own souls, but on the souls of their hearers.

God’s overriding command is simple:  teach the truth.  In the word, that truth is accessible to every person of moderate understanding.  As teachers, we don’t dare be lazy students of the word who overlook the truth through carelessness, nor foolish students who ignore the truth because of the persuasive words of men, and certainly not wicked students who distort the word for our own gain.  There will be a reckoning for all of those errors, and the only way to avoid that reckoning is by faithfulness to the Scripture.

Of course, our words matter even when we aren’t teaching a Bible class, which is why James next warns us of THE POWER OF THE TONGUE.  Let’s read here from James 3:2-5.  According to the apostle, this power manifests itself in two main ways.  First, the power of the tongue is apparent in how difficult it is for us to control it.  As James dryly observes, anyone who has mastered the tongue has got the rest of his spiritual life squared away too.  If we’re going to sin anywhere, then—and we all do sin—we’re going to sin with the tongue.  As long as we’re around people, we’re going to be talking, and every time we open our mouths, it’s another opportunity to sin.  We’re talking with a co-worker, and before we realize it, we find ourselves gossiping about somebody else in the office.  Our kids are frustrating us, and so we say something scathing and sarcastic to them.  Somebody asks us an awkward question, and almost without thinking about it, we find ourselves lying.  It’s like trying to keep a hyperactive dog in a fenced yard.  The slightest gap or hole, and that dog is off to the next county!

The problem is, though, that sins of the tongue can easily have an impact out of proportion to the effort we put into them.  I’ve often heard adulterous women described as home-wreckers, but it’s likely that the true home-wreckers are our tongues.  Ain’t none of us married to Jesus, so every one of us who is married has an imperfect spouse.  Without working on it very hard, even without thinking about it very hard, what we say to them can become nothing more than a bitter catalogue of those imperfections.  They didn’t do something we wanted; we let them hear about it.  They tried to do something we wanted; we tell them how they did it wrong.  They do something right; we nag them about all the other things they didn’t do.  And so on.  Every single marriage that has ever been, including ours, can be destroyed by nothing more than words.

This is what James is trying to get us to understand when he warns us against THE DANGER OF THE TONGUE.  Consider James 3:6-8.  This is one of the strongest condemnations of anything in the entire Bible.  Just imagine how much we would cite a passage that warned against immodest dress or instrumental music in these terms!  That would be something we would stay a mile away from, right?  Except, of course, James isn’t talking about immodest dress or instrumental music.  He’s talking about talking, something every single one of us does every single day.

Hanging on a wall in her house, my sister has a picture she’s had since she was in college.  It’s a photograph from the 1930’s of a dozen construction workers sitting on a steel beam, eating their lunches, about 70 floors off the ground.  As many of you know, I hate heights.  I can’t look at that picture without my stomach doing flip-flops!  However, these men are so used to working 500 feet or more off the ground that being in a place where a single misstep means disaster doesn’t bother them in the slightest.

In a similar way, James wants to remind us that simply because we are used to the dangers of the tongue doesn’t mean that we should forget about those dangers.  If somebody drew a gun on me and forced me out onto that steel beam, I’d probably go on my belly, hugging that thing for dear life!  Probably all of us would have better lives if we exercised similar caution in the things we said.

Finally, James takes a look at THE CONTRADICTORY TONGUE.  This appears in James 3:9-12.  James points out that even the tongues of Christians can often find themselves blessing God on Sunday but cursing other people on Monday.  He then shows that this apparent ability to be contradictory is unique in the creation.  No grapevine bears figs.  No spring can produce both salt and fresh water.  In doing so, he implies that the apparent contradictions in what we say aren’t actually contradictions.  It is from the fullness of our hearts that we speak, and if our speech is all sweetness and light when it comes to God but filled with meanness when it comes to others, that suggests that what we really have in our hearts is meanness, and all the sweetness and light is fake.  That fake praise may fool others, but it won’t fool God.

In saying this, James shows what the real problem with the tongue is.  The tongue is a muscle.  It doesn’t do anything more than we tell it to.  What we say, or, for that matter, what we type or text, reveals who we are.  All of the problems with our speech, then, arise from problems in our hearts, problems that we either don’t recognize or don’t control.  Evil speech reveals the evil in us.

**Jesus and Our Speech**

Next, we’re going to turn our attention to what the Lord Himself had to say about our speech.  This begins with his statement about THE MOUTH AND THE HEART in Matthew 12:33-35.  I certainly believe that everything in the Bible is true, but there are times when God’s word blows me away with how true and how profound it is.  This is one of those times:  “The mouth speaks out of that which fills the heart.”  Jesus dispels all of the delusions we may have about ourselves and others with this one simple observation.  People aren’t different from the things they say.  Instead, the things they say reveal who they truly are.  If you want to know who somebody is, just listen to them.  They’ll tell you soon enough.

This was certainly true of the Pharisees.  They wanted to present themselves as all holy and righteous, but they showed their true colors by attributing the miracles of Jesus to the power of the devil.  They didn’t really love God or the truth.  Instead, they loved their power and position, and so, even though Jesus was clearly from God and offered them the way to God, they opposed Him because He threatened them.

So too for us.  We may cherish delusions about the goodness of others, even though their speech proves them to be evil.  This is perhaps most often the case in a romantic situation, where we want to believe better about someone we’ve fallen in love with than they actually deserve.

However, our delusions are most often about ourselves.  Very few people are willing to acknowledge the evil in their hearts to themselves.  While we need to listen to others, we need to listen to ourselves most of all.  We may believe that we are gentle and kind in our interactions with our families, yet our words are full of harshness and contempt.  We may believe that we put God first in our lives, yet our Facebook and Twitter feeds are full of meaningless chatter.  We may believe that we love our friends and neighbors, yet we say nothing to them about the gospel.  Let me tell you, friends—I often do not want to admit that my words tell the truth about who I am, but they do.  They may paint a harsh portrait of me, but it is an accurate one.

This is a sobering thought, and it’s all the more sobering because we will GIVE AN ACCOUNT for our speech.  Look at how Jesus continues in Matthew 12:36-37.  This, friends, is a truly ominous passage.  Jesus doesn’t say that we will give an account for our evil words.  He says that we will give an account for our careless words!  Christians will go to hell and be eternally lost because they were careless in what they said.

I don’t know about you, but that makes me go a little wide-eyed.  Part of me wants to protest, “But I didn’t mean to do any harm!  I just wasn’t paying attention!”  Well, let me put it to you like this.  Let’s say that after services, when everybody is standing around talking, that I were to put a blindfold on, pick up my trusty battle axe, and wade through those conversation groups, swinging my axe around wildly.  When my day in court came, could I get away with protesting that I never meant to hurt anybody?  Of course not!  If I cause great harm simply because I am being reckless rather than evil, it does not excuse the harm.

So it is with our tongues.  You might say that comparing the tongue to a battle axe isn’t a fair comparison, and it’s not.  Throughout human history, the tongue has done far more damage than battle axes ever have or will.  I might be able to chop somebody’s head off with a battle axe, if I actually owned one, but I can drive somebody away from Christ with my tongue.  Sadly, there are all too many Christians who would never think of physically murdering someone, but who have caused the loss of souls by what they said.  They didn’t mean to, but they did, because they were careless.  Unless they recognize what they have done and repent, the Lord will demand an accounting from them, and they will be condemned by their careless words.  That could be us, and unless we acknowledge the eternal danger our speech poses, it will be us.

We particularly need to watch out for careless speech when it comes to MINDLESS WORSHIP.  Consider the words of the Lord in Matthew 6:7-8.  When we take the awesome step of approaching the throne of God in heaven, it needs to be mindful and meaningful, rather than mindless and meaningless.  It’s very easy to do the opposite.  It’s tempting when we’re praying to dip into the bag of prayer clichés, something that we can rattle off smoothly without having to think about it.  Of course, this is not to say that we can assume that someone else’s cliché-ridden prayer is thoughtless.  None of us can judge hearts.  However, we must take care that we aren’t using clichés so that we don’t have to think.

Mindless worship can also be a problem in our singing.  Believe me, I am as attuned to the beauty of our congregational singing as anybody, and God means for it to be beautiful and moving, but the music isn’t the point.  Instead, it is supposed to be a vehicle for thoughts and words.  If our song worship is thoughtless and mindless, it does not please God.  This is important first of all for song leaders.  One of the things that I love about our worship here is how thoughtful our song leaders are.  It’s clear to me that they pick out hymns not for how they sound, but for what they say.  Long may they continue to do so!

Second, though, every worshiper in the pews has a responsibility to be mindful too.  A few weeks back, I was joking with Josh about how there are some hymns that we sing from the brain stem.  We have sung them so many times that we would probably still be able to sing them even with late-stage Alzheimer’s.  However, we must take care that familiarity does not lead to thoughtlessness.  When we rattle off those familiar hymns without thinking about their meaning, without attempting to teach others and glorify God, we are engaging in worship that alienates Him instead.  Honestly, I struggle with this.  It’s easy for my mind to turn down some bunny trail instead of thinking about what I’m singing.  However, God expects mindfulness.

Before the Lord’s Supper, then, let’s carefully consider both the life of our Lord and His words.  While He was on earth, He had a mission, and He lived like it.  Never in Scripture do we ever see Jesus do or even say anything careless.  As a result, He was perfectly suited to be our sacrifice and the source of our eternal salvation.  As always, His expectation is that we live like He did, and that we speak like He did.  When we fall into the trap of careless, harmful speech, or even of worship that is from the lips but not the heart, we have ceased to follow His example.  Let’s reflect on our responsibilities here as we partake.

**Paul on Speech**

In the final portion of our study together this morning, we’re going to consider warnings from the apostle Paul about other kinds of ungodly speech.  Let’s begin with what he tells us about LYING SPEECH.  Look at Ephesians 4:25.  In context, this, and the next couple of passages we’re going to look at, are part of Paul’s discussion of the transformation that is supposed to take place in our lives once we put on Christ.  Lying is a sin that the world loves, but it should never be something for disciples of Jesus.

Of all of the commandments of the Lord, this one may generate more eye-rolling and rejection than any other.  I rarely hear non-Christians talk about “little white lies” anymore, because it’s assumed in modern-day America that people are going to lie whenever they believe it’s to their own advantage.  Most of the people around us think of lying less as a sin and more as a “Get Out of Jail Free” card.

In only a few words, Paul sketches out the problem with this.  We need to put away falsehood with our neighbor, but most of all, we need to put away falsehood with our brother, because we are members of one another.  Do the different parts of our physical bodies ever send lying signals to one another?  When our leg is perfectly healthy, does it send a wave of pain to our brain as though it has been broken?  When we take a mouthful of sour milk, does our tongue report that the milk is perfectly wholesome?  Any body in which the members lie to one another is at war with itself, and that certainly applies to the church!  It applies to our workplaces, our schools, and our homes too.  Lying inevitably creates confusion and erodes trust, and the more lies are told, the worse the problem gets.  Truth-speaking is a prerequisite for health.

Second, we are responsible for putting off UNWHOLESOME SPEECH.  Paul reveals this in Ephesians 4:29.  In many ways, this is a difficult idea to understand.  I haven’t been able to find a translation, even the dynamic-equivalent translation, that I think does a good job of capturing the image here.  “Corrupt speech” in the old KJV might be the closest.  As I understand the text, the speech that Paul is talking about is speech that is corrosive, speech that is contaminating, speech that eats away at the one who hears it.

There are a couple of different kinds of speech that fall into this category.  Yelling at and verbally abusing somebody certainly does.  However, corrosive speech doesn’t have to be that dramatic to be corrosive.  How about passive-aggressive family members, friends, and co-workers, like Mother Gothel in the movie *Tangled*?  These people don’t yell.  They don’t raise their voices, but everything they say is subtly demeaning and subtly hurtful.  Perhaps worst of all, this kind of subtly corrosive speech is deniable.  Usually, when we try to confront somebody like this, they respond with wide-eyed innocence and imply that our overly sensitive pride is where the problem actually lies.  Because they slide away from a straightforward discussion of their behavior, it often continues unchecked for years.  It’s terrible when we’re on the receiving end of this.  It sucks all the hope and joy out of life.  Worse still is when we are the ones doing it.

In addition, we need to appreciate the corrosive, corrupting effect of gossip.  Unlike abusive speech, whether straightforward or passive-aggressive, gossip actually makes us feel good, whether we share it or hear it.  However, gossip is damaging too.  It damages the person we’re gossiping about, obviously, but it also damages us.  In essence, when we gossip, we enter into a contract of shared self-righteousness, in which we and our fellow gossipers agree that we’re pretty good folks, and certainly better than So-and-So over there!  Friends, that feeling of self-righteousness and contempt is utterly delusional, and utterly evil.  None of us has any right to boast in our moral superiority, and when we forget this, we also forget Christ.

Third, we must put off FILTHY SPEECH.  Paul raises this issue in Ephesians 5:3-5.  There are really three kinds of speech that are at issue here.  The first is filthiness, which is any speech that is crass or crude.  Paul here is talking about straightforward cuss words.  The second kind is silly talk.  This doesn’t mean silliness like my kids are silly sometimes.  Instead, it carries with it the sense of innuendo, double meanings, and slyly sexual content.  Finally, we come to coarse jesting, which is just plain ole dirty jokes.

Paul identifies two main problems with this kind of speech.  The first is that it isn’t proper for saints.  It’s simply not appropriate for one who aspires to be godly, and if we engage in filthy talk, it will erode our influence.  Take me, for example.  I’m a preacher of the gospel, and the gospel is true no matter who preaches it.  However, people expect, and rightly so, that a preacher’s life will reflect his message.  If you were talking to me one day, and I launched into a dirty joke, how would that affect the way you listened to the next sermon I preached?  What if I had a potty mouth and responded to every frustration and disappointment with a string of profanities?  If that were the kind of man I was, would you even want me in the pulpit?

Brethren, what’s true for preachers is true for every Christian.  All of us are ambassadors for Christ.  That means all of us have to safeguard our influence by safeguarding our speech.  Only if our words are gracious rather than foul will others respect us enough to hear what we have to say about the gospel.

Second, Paul points out the connection between immoral speech and immoral behavior.  When we talk about things that should be kept private, we call those things to mind and create an awareness of them.  That’s particularly dangerous in situations where an attraction already exists.  There’s a reason why innuendo is a weapon in the arsenal of every seducer.  Speaking lightly of a sin brings it closer to actually happening.
