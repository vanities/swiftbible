Over the past 50 years, a tremendous change in worship
services has overtaken most denominational churches.  At the beginning of this period, most
churches relied on the same pianos and organs and choirs, along with
congregational singing, that had typified denominational worship since the
Victorian era.  Today, though, those
traditional trappings, along with traditional hymns, have fallen by the
wayside.  They’ve been replaced by rock
bands singing an unfamiliar repertoire that isn’t conducive to congregational
singing.

All this is very intentional.  In his church-growth handbook *The Purpose-Driven Church*, Rick Warren
observes that when visitors come to an assembly and feel like they’re expected
to sing, the expectation makes them uncomfortable.  The way to make visitors comfortable, then,
this line of thinking goes, is to have an assembly in which none of the
congregation sings, in which churchgoers have been converted from active
participants to passive audience members. 
As a result, the “worship experience” of most believers now is akin to
attending a spiritually themed concert.

This makes sense to the wisdom of the world, but it runs
counter to the wisdom of the word.  Let’s
look at Scripture, then, to see why song worship matters.



First, it matters because it is **A SACRIFICE.** We find this
description in Hebrews 13:15.  In order
to understand what’s going on here, we first have to reject the connotations of
the English word “sacrifice”.  In
English, “sacrifice” normally carries with it negative overtones.  You’re giving up something you want for the
sake of a greater good.

However, that’s not true of Biblical sacrifice.  In Scripture, “sacrifice” is simply something
that is offered to God.  It could be a
sacrifice of thanksgiving or, as here, a sacrifice of praise.  It is an act of worship to God.  Just as we give a scholarship to a scholar,
so we give worship to the One who is worthy. 
We freely acknowledge that He deserves to be praised.

It’s worth noting, though, that worship and sacrifice are
active, not passive.  I have
denominational friends who will report after Sunday services, “I really
worshiped today!”  By that, they don’t
mean that they actually sang.  Instead,
they mean that they sat there and listened as the praise band wailed away, and
the beautiful music gave them the feels.

I suppose that’s enjoyable for the audience, but it’s a far
cry from the Hebrews writer’s “the fruit of lips that give thanks to His name”.  If we’re just sitting there doing nothing
while others are singing, friends, we are not sacrificing, worshiping, or
pleasing God.

Now, obviously, not all of us are created equal in our
abilities as singers.  That’s not
important.  What is important is that we
use all we have to praise God from the heart.

Second, song worship is important as **AN EXPRESSION OF JOY.** Here,
look at James 5:13.  Note that even
though this certainly applies in the assembly, it applies outside of it as
well.  Whenever we are cheerful, wherever
we are cheerful, we are supposed to sing praise.

I think this is important for at least two reasons.  First, when we stop to praise God whenever we
are glad, it reminds us of the ultimate reason why we are glad.  Satan can give momentary pleasure, but joy is
outside of his power to bestow.  The best
things in life, the truly good and perfect gifts, all come from the hand of
God.

When we are cheerful, then, because life seems to be going
our way, or simply because it’s a nice day outside and the sun is shining and
the birds are singing, those moments are a reminder of the blessing and
presence of God in our lives.  When we
give Him the glory rather than claiming it for ourselves or attributing it to
chance, we are giving Him what He deserves.

Also, praising God when we are glad, and indeed praising God
with gladness, is a powerful antidote for the devil’s claim that serving God is
miserable and boring and no fun.  Sadly,
there are all kinds of Christians who fall for this.  Even if they don’t actually fall away, they
stare out at the sinful world with all the fervent longing of a dog staring at
two pounds of cheese.

Brethren, that’s just dumb. 
In real life, there is nothing that the world offers that will leave us
better off if we accept it.  It takes all
the devil’s salesmanship to convince us that we would be better off.  However, when we rejoice in the Lord, it
exposes that salesmanship for the lie it is.

Third, song worship matters because it is **AN OUTPOURING OF THE WORD.** Consider Colossians 3:16.  According to Paul here, we should sing
because we feel driven to sing, because we have all of this Bible truth bottled
up within us looking for an outlet to express itself.  When it does express itself, the outcome is
naturally that others are taught and admonished.

From this text, we should learn that the hymns we sing are
important for what they say and not only how we sing them.  I’m afraid that there are brethren out there
who think that as long as we are singing a hymn without instrumental help,
we’re fulfilling this commandment.

Nothing could be further from the truth!  In reality, some hymns do a much better job
of expressing the truths of Scripture than others.  They are filled with profound Biblical insights.  Other hymns, well, the nicest thing you can
say about them is that they don’t teach false doctrine, but it’s because they
don’t really say anything above the level of the teaching in a toddler class.

Brethren, that’s not how things should be, and the
responsibility here falls largely on the shoulders of song leaders.  A song leader is a teacher, and we need to
take our teaching role seriously when we lead. 
We should select hymns not for how they sound, but what they say.  This content-first approach will ensure that
every hymn we sing is fit to express a rich indwelling of the word.

In addition, song worship is important as **A SYMBOL OF AUTHORITY.** Here, look at Colossians 3:17, just one
verse down from what we just read.  I
suppose that we can argue back and forth about what activities Paul has in mind
when he says “Do everything in the name of the Lord,” but there’s not much of
an argument that he means to exclude the discussion one verse up.  If this text is about anything, it’s about
song worship.

Of course, doing everything in the name of the Lord doesn’t
mean going around saying, “Jesus, Jesus, Jesus” while you’re doing it.  It means submitting to the authority of the
Lord in everything, particularly when it comes to the way we worship Him.

When come together on Sunday morning and sing hymns, it
could not be clearer that we are acting under the authority of Jesus.  Once again, one verse up, we see the Lord’s
chosen apostle Paul instructing us to sing. 
We know that when we do this, we’re obeying the authority of Christ.

However, the same can’t be said for any other form of music.  Nowhere in the New Testament do we see a
command to use musical instruments, nor an example of Christians doing so, nor
a passage that leads us to the conclusion that we should.  Here, I can’t help but be reminded of a
comment that I saw a brotherhood preacher make online.  He said he became convinced that the
instrument was acceptable in worship because in the parable of the prodigal
son, when the prodigal returns, they welcome him with music and dancing, as
though fictional people in a secular celebration provide some kind of guide for
what we should do in our assemblies!  In
reality, those who claim Bible authority for using the instrument in worship
made up their minds to use the instrument before they ever opened their Bibles
and went looking for justification.

Next, let’s consider song worship as **A SIGN OF THE SPIRIT.** This
idea is presented in Ephesians 5:18-19. 
I know that some Christians tense up whenever anybody suggests that we
have anything to do with the Holy Spirit today, but I think it’s important for
us to look at what the text is telling us rather than reading right through v.
18 to get to the part we care about.  The
key to understanding the text is paying attention to Paul’s comparison.  On the one hand, we’re not supposed to become
drunk with wine.  However, we are
supposed to be filled with the Spirit.

Let’s start with the drunken half first.  We all know that drunk people behave
differently than sober people do.  We speak
of them as being “under the influence” of alcohol.  Their personality changes.  Their speech changes.  They say and do things that they would never
have considered while sober.  They become
different people.

Let me suggest that being filled with the Spirit means just
about exactly the same thing.  Somebody
who is filled with the Spirit is under the influence too, except that rather
than being under the influence of alcohol, they’re under the influence of the
Spirit of God.  Their personality
changes.  They constantly become less
like their old selves and more like Jesus. 
Their speech changes.  It’s
different from the way their worldly selves used to talk.  They say and do things that they never even
would have considered when they were still living a life of sin.  Much like the drunk, they too have become
different people, except they have changed for the better.

Paul tells us that the first symptom of being filled with
the Spirit is speaking to one another in psalms, hymns and spiritual songs, and
making melody in our hearts to the Lord. 
Worldly people don’t do that.  Why
would they?  They don’t have any
reverence for God.  They don’t have any
concern for helping others on to heaven. 
They might not even like to sing. 
However, if we have truly been regenerated by the Spirit, song worship
is something that we will love.

Finally, song worship is **A DISPLAY OF UNITY.** Consider
here the exhortation of Romans 15:5-7. 
Admittedly, this passage doesn’t use the magic word “sing”.  However, it’s clear from its content that
singing is what Paul has in mind.  He
notes that the purpose of his instruction is so that the Christians in Rome can
glorify God with one voice.  All of them
are going to be speaking simultaneously. 
When else in our worship does that happen?  The Lord’s Supper and the collection are
essentially silent acts.  When we pray,
one person leads and everyone else follows along.  The same is true of our sermons and even our
Bible classes.  After all, if more than
one person is speaking at once, the result is not edification, but chaos!  Singing is all that’s left.

However, precisely because we are speaking together, our
singing reveals, or at least ought to reveal, our spiritual unity.  It reveals first of all our agreement on mode
of worship.  After all, if I found myself
in a denominational worship service, I wouldn’t sing.  I couldn’t sing.  It would violate my conscience.  When I sing here, it shows that I believe
we’re worshiping in spirit and truth.

Second, singing shows, or should show, that we are of one
mind.  It’s all too possible for people
who are singing to check out mentally, but we should be thinking about the
things we are singing, and by allowing those sentiments to come from our lips,
we’re expressing our agreement.

Finally, singing should show our harmony with one
another.  Paul tells us here that our
singing isn’t supposed to come from habit. 
It’s supposed to be the result of our living peacefully with one another
and our welcoming one another.  God
intends for our unified singing to be an expression of our fellowship with one
another.  If, by contrast, we sing
together while remaining at odds with one another, we’re like the spring in
James 3 that is sending out both fresh and bitter water.  We need to make a change.
