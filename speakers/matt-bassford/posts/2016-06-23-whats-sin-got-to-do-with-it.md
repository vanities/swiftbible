It’s grimly amusing to watch American society, as it tears
down all the old taboos, erect new taboos in its place.  Modern culture isn’t more tolerant; it’s
simply tolerant of different things.  One
of the things it will not tolerate is discussion of sin.  In a world in which all sorts of four-letter
words are freely used (Apparently most parents these days commonly swear in
front of their children.  Who knew?), sin
has become a three-letter word that is not used.  Nobody wants to hear about the awfulness of
sin and the righteous wrath of God.



This is true even in many churches.  For instance, the Townend/Getty hymn “In
Christ Alone” contains the couplet, “But on that cross as Jesus died/The wrath
of God was satisfied.”  Some hymnal
editors were so bothered by discussion of the wrath of God that they asked
Townend and Getty if they could rewrite the couplet to read, “But on that cross
as Jesus died/The love of God was magnified.” 
Townend and Getty said no, but the editors printed the revised hymn
anyway.

Even though I don’t see eye to eye with Townend and Getty on
a lot of things, I think they’re dead on about the importance of discussion of
wrath and sin.  Nobody likes to talk
about sin; I don’t even like to think about my own!  However, when we indulge our tendency to
ignore an unpleasant topic, Christianity itself becomes logically
incoherent.  Conversely, a Biblical
appreciation and discussion of sin accomplishes four necessary goals:

**It accentuates the
holiness of God.** If sin isn’t a big
deal, then neither is God’s holiness.  If
sin is insignificant, then so are the barriers to our having a relationship
with Him.  He accepts us just the way we are,
and our transgressions are on a par with a toddler getting into Grandma’s
cookies.  He might slap our wrists, but
we could never imagine that He might reject us. 
A weak view of sin lulls the sinner into a false sense of security.

However, once we begin to see sin for what is, we also grasp
what it means for our relationship with the Holy One of Israel.  God’s eyes are too pure to approve evil, and
if we practice evil, He is too pure to approve us.  God doesn’t greet sin with an, “Aww; isn’t
that cute!”  Instead, everything in His
righteous nature is disgusted by it. 
Only when we understand that can we also understand the seriousness of
our situation apart from Christ.

**It emphasizes the
grace of God.** As is true with
holiness, a weak view of sin inevitably leads to a weak view of divine
grace.  If we don’t see sin for what it is,
the grace of God is less rescuing us from a horrible death and more rescuing us
by fixing our flat tire.  Sure; it’s nice
that God is gracious, but we really could have made it on our own if we needed
to.  Rather than a necessity, grace is a
convenience, and there are millions of people who treat it as a convenience.

However, where sin abounds, grace abounds all the more.  Far from being an inconvenience, sin is a
soul-corrupting, life-destroying, eternally damning disaster.  Without the grace of God, every one of us is
on a set of greased rails that leads straight to hell.

The grace of God,
then, doesn’t get me over a spiritual speed bump.  It lifts me out of a Grand Canyon of
despair.  It sets my feet on a firm place
to which I never could have climbed on my own. 
The grace of God doesn’t merit an absent-minded thank-you.  Instead, it inspires awe and entitles God to
my eternal gratitude and praise.

**It highlights the
sacrifice of Christ.**   This is perhaps the most important point of
all.  If sin is unimportant, why did
Jesus have to die?  Like God, I’ve only
got one son.  I can’t imagine the
circumstance that would lead me to send him to his death for the sake of
others, but I can imagine lots of circumstances that wouldn’t lead me to do
that.  Unless it is a situation of
absolute, direst need, forget it.  I keep
Marky, and you sort out your own problems.

If God sent Jesus to die for the sake of a minor issue,
why’d He do that?  Did He not love Jesus
as I love my own son, and so was willing to sacrifice Him for a matter of
convenience?  Is the Creator of heaven
and earth an idiot who didn’t recognize that the death of His only begotten Son
was way overkill?  For that matter, was
Jesus Himself a lunatic who whimsically endured suffering and death to solve a
non-problem?  None of these answers
satisfy.

However, once we appreciate the depravity of sin, everything
else comes into focus.  God gave His Son
to die because the problem was so serious that no lesser sacrifice would
do.  He was willing to watch His Beloved
die because there was no other way to redeem us.  Similarly, Jesus set His face to Jerusalem,
displaying awesome love and courage, because He knew that nothing but His blood
could cleanse our souls.  Without a
strong view of sin, none of this makes sense. 
With such a view, it makes perfect, devastating sense.

**It guards against
apostasy.** Without sin, Christianity
is a religion looking for a reason to exist. 
If I think I’m probably going to heaven even if I never set foot inside
a church building, you’re going to have to come up with something else to get
me to show up.

This might seem like an impossible challenge, but plenty of
false teachers have risen to the occasion. 
Generally, they substitute carnal appeals for the gospel of salvation.  Be a Christian so only good things will
happen in your life.  Be a Christian so
God will make you rich.  Be a Christian
so you can be happy.

These things are, of course, a gross misrepresentation of
Biblical truth, and that’s bad enough by itself.  Even beyond that, though, they tend to
undermine faith.  If I become a Christian
so that God will supply my earthly needs and wants, I’m going to expect Him to
do that.  In exchange for my worship and
service, He’d better hold up His end of the bargain!  Of course, life doesn’t work like that, so my
expectations will inevitably be disappointed. 
Every loss and tragedy I suffer will embitter me against God or lead me
to stop believing in Him at all.

By contrast, “Because He will save me from the eternal death
my sins have earned” is a wonderfully sufficient answer to the question, “Why
should I follow Jesus?”  The free gift of
eternal life is priceless, and next to it, the gospel of health and wealth
looks tawdry.  The biggest mansion on
earth pales in comparison to a dwelling place in my Father’s house.

Furthermore, as long as we fix our eyes on eternal life, we
will never be disappointed.  So what if I
suffer trial and hardship in this life? 
Those things are not even worthy to be compared to the glory to
come.  If God never blesses me again in
this life but will receive me in the next, I can ask for nothing more.

There are plenty of churches today that are convinced they
need to bring in outsiders by softpedaling sin. 
In addition to being a distortion of the gospel, I think that’s
ultimately ineffective.  Unless you point
out what people are being saved from, you haven’t given them a reason to be
saved.  It might not be pleasant to talk
about sin, but it’s a necessary first step to the joys of salvation.
