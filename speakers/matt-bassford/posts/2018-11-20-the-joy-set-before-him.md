Most people have favorite colors.  I, like other language nerds, have favorite
words.  Perhaps my most favorite word of
all is the word “joy”.  I like the
concept, of course,  Who doesn’t?  I like how short and simple it is:  three letters, one syllable.  The sound of the word when we say it is
appropriately bright and cheerful.  I
even like the way it makes our mouths move. 
If you pay attention, you’ll notice that by the end of saying “joy”, the
corners of your mouth have drawn up like you’re about to smile.  “Joy” is just a neat word.

Even better is the way that “joy” is used in Scripture.  Many of my favorite passages are about joy
and rejoicing, but one of the most powerful uses of the word appears in a
context that isn’t very joyful.  Look
with me at Hebrews 12:1-2.  Here, the
Hebrews writer is sternly encouraging Christians to remain faithful to the Lord
even through persecution, and he points to Jesus as our example.  He endured the cross, He despised the shame
of His mockery and trial, and He did it all, the Hebrews writer tells us, for
the sake of joy.  What was this joy?  What can we learn from it?  These are questions that are worth answering,
so let’s spend our time this evening considering the joy set before Him.



**The Joy Set Before
Jesus**

The great hymn “He Loved Me So” asks the question, “Why did
my Savior come to earth?”  Certainly, as
the hymn notes, love is part of the answer to that question, but we see from
Hebrews 12 that joy was part of it too, not the joy of being crucified, but the
joy of what would come after being crucified. 
The first component of Jesus’ joy was in **RETURNING TO THE FATHER.** Jesus
certainly knew that He was headed back to heaven, as we learn from John
16:28.  Indeed, all the way through
Jesus’ farewell discourse in John, His delight in the Father is clearly
apparent.

This, friends, is something that we need to think about more
than we do.  Probably most of us have
wondered what heaven will be like. 
Probably most of us have wondered if being in heaven forever won’t be,
well, heavenly.  Won’t we get bored
spending eternity up there with nothing to do?

If we’re concerned about that, we can’t do any better than
looking at the attitude of the one human being who actually has spent time in
heaven with God.  Jesus never seems concerned
about the downside of going back to heaven. 
Indeed, He’s positively eager to do so.

All the way through His ministry, in fact, Jesus’ longing
for God is clear.  That’s why He spends
so much time in prayer:  because He
misses His Father and longs to be reunited with Him.  Prayer is as close as He can get for the time
being, so He prays, much like when we’re physically separated from our
families, we’ll spend as much time with them on the phone as we can.

Jesus, who knew better than anyone else, didn’t think
spending eternity with God would be boring. 
Instead, He regarded being in God’s presence as an eternal source of
joy, a joy so great He was willing to go through being crucified to get back
there.

Likewise, Jesus set His face toward Calvary for the joy of **SPENDING ETERNITY WITH US.** Look here at John 14:1-3.  This is one of the most famous texts in the
entire Bible, and justifiably so. 
However, many Christians don’t know that Jesus’ words here track the Jewish
marriage customs of His day.

Back then, before the bride and groom got married, they held
a formal betrothal ceremony about a year beforehand.  In that ceremony, the groom-to-be would give
his father-in-law the agreed-upon brideprice and the man and the woman would
pledge themselves to one another.  Then,
the groom would return to his own father’s house and spend the next year
preparing a place for his future wife to come and live with him.

Jesus’ words here, then, parallel an experience that many of
His disciples had lived through and that all of them were familiar with.  He’s telling them, and they would have
understood, that He longed to spend eternity with them as deeply as a betrothed
husband-to-be longed to share his life with his bride.  In fact, all the way through the gospels,
Jesus repeatedly compares Himself to a bridegroom and us to His bride, with
heaven as an eternal, shared wedding feast.

I don’t imagine that bridegrooms back then enjoyed waiting a
year before savoring the full joys of marriage. 
Lauren and I were only engaged for about four months, and even that was
hard!  However, they endured the wait
because the outcome was worth it.  So
too, our Lord endured the cross because we were worth it.

Finally, Jesus endured the cross and despised the shame
because of the joy of **GLORIFYING
GOD.** Consider John 13:31-32.  I think this text is particularly important
in helping us understand what the Hebrews writer is talking about when he says
that Jesus “despised the shame”.  We all
know what shame is.  None of us like
being ashamed.  The idea of despising
shame is much less common, though.  The
writer is telling us that all of that humiliation and mockery, all of His
enemies standing around the foot of the cross hurling abuse at Him, all of that
was beneath Jesus’ contempt.  He didn’t reply
to it because it wasn’t worth replying to.

Jesus put up with such hostility and refused to be diverted
from His course even a little bit because He had His eyes fixed on God’s
glory.  God is certainly glorified by the
physical creation.  The universe is a
monument to His power.  However, His
greatest glory appears in the spiritual realm, where He is glorified by the
eternal purpose that redeemed us from sin.

Think about it.  Who
could have imagined that God would entrust us with free will, watch as we were
seduced from His side by Satan, and yet, the whole time, have a plan in mind
that could reconcile us to Him?  Who
could have imagined that the centerpiece of this plan would be Him sending His
perfect Son to suffer and die for sinners? 
The death of Jesus on the cross is God proclaiming for all time, “This
is My nature.  This is who I am.  I am mercy. 
I am love.”  To see the Father
glorified like that, Jesus was willing even to die.

**The Joy Set Before Us**

The joy set before Jesus is the same joy that is set before
us.  We too can anticipate the joy of
spending eternity with God.  Jesus knew
what He was missing, we don’t know and can’t know until we experience it, but
it will be better than we can imagine. 
We too can look forward to our place at that table of that great wedding
feast of union between the Lord and His people. 
We too can turn our eyes toward the time when we will become part of an
eternal monument to the glory of God’s grace.

The joy set before us is great, and because it is great, we
can do two things.  First, **WE CAN ENDURE.** This is the Hebrew writer’s point in
Hebrews 12:3.  Here, the writer is not
merely calling us to consider the example of Jesus.  He is calling us to consider the joy of
Jesus.  Yes, following Christ is not
always easy, but nobody who crosses that finish line will ever say it wasn’t
worth it.

We know what it’s like to endure for the sake of earthly
rewards.  I suffered through three years
of the most difficult, boring classes imaginable in order to get a law degree
that I never ended up using.  I get up
six days a week and work out even though I know perfectly well that my body is
going to fall apart sooner or later no matter what I do.

Brethren, if we’re willing to go through so much for so
little here, then why wouldn’t we also be willing to go through so little here
for so much there?  I’ll freely admit
that serving God is not always fun.  It
will demand a lifetime of endurance from each of us.  So what? 
It’s still worth it!

Finally, because of the joy set before us, we can imitate
Jesus and **DESPISE SHAME.** Consider Peter’s take on this in 1 Peter
4:3-5.  I’ve said over and over again
that one of the greatest spiritual obstacles in our path is fear, and one of
the most prevalent fears that Christians confront is being looked down on
because of their faith.

This is the fear that can lead us into sin because we don’t
want to look bad in front of friends or a love interest.  This is the fear that can keep us from doing
what we know is right so that others don’t think we’re weird.  This is the fear that keeps us silent about
the gospel even when we spend every day surrounded by the lost.

Peter wants us to remember that there is only one thing to
be afraid of, and that is falling short on the day of judgment.  If people malign us because we are disciples
of Jesus, who cares?  Even if they make
fun of us for the rest of our lives, that’s still only what, 50 years at
most?  50 years is nothing compared to an
eternity spent in the love of God.  The
shame that we face here is shame that we have every reason to despise.
