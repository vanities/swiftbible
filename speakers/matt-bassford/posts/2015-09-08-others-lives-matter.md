My Facebook news feed these days is filled with assertions
that various kinds of lives matter.  I’ve
seen lots of retweets with the hashtag #blacklivesmatter.  In some kind of rebuttal, lots of
(predominantly white) folks have been replying, “All lives matter.”  After the recent spate of politically
motivated shootings of police officers, there have been several photos of
officers in uniform holding up placards reading “My life matters.”



In theory, I suppose all of this should make me happy.  I’m pro-life in every sense of the word.  I try to devote my life to the proposition
that even the most insignificant life matters, whether it belongs to a police
officer, an African American, or some undifferentiated representative of
humankind.

However, what’s underneath all of these assertions of
important lives is something else that I don’t like as well.  In their own way, each one of those
statements is asserting, “My life matters, and so do the lives of people like
me.  I need to speak up because I am
afraid of those who are not like me.”

I see two problems with this.  The first is that it encourages an
unspiritual mindset.  In Matthew 5:46-47,
Jesus observes, “For if you love those who love you, what reward do you
have?  Do not even the tax collectors do
the same?  If you greet only your
brothers, what more are you doing than others? 
Do not even the Gentiles do the same?” 
If we are concerned only with the plight of those who are like us, if we
are indifferent to the concerns of those who are different, our lives do not
reveal the influence of Christ.

Second, this kind of thinking invariably turns inward.  It focuses on the self and on the group,
particularly on the fears of the self and the fears of the group.  When it comes to fear, I’m with Yoda.  Fear is definitely a door of opportunity for
the Dark Side.  Very rarely does anything
good come out of fear (excepting, of course, the fear of the Lord, which this
ain’t), and all sorts of evil things do. 
A dialogue driven by fear isn’t a dialogue at all, but rather a shouting
match, and the longer it continues, the more likely fearful words are to become
fearful, evil actions.  It spawns a
vicious cycle of precisely the sort that Jesus was warning us against.

Instead of shouting, we need to try listening instead, of
paying attention to what the other fears and why he fears it.  In this situation, it seems to me that just
about everybody has some kind of a point. 
It’s not my job, though, to assert the correctness of my side.  It’s my job to figure out where the other
side is coming from and acknowledge truth where truth is spoken.

For instance, it is quite clear to me that in America in
2015, racism is still definitely a thing. 
Nor are the effects of previous forms of racism dispelled simply because
those forms are no longer practiced. 
Instead, the consequences linger on like poison in the bloodstream of
the body politic.  White privilege is
definitely a thing, and most of the people who assert that they are self-made
(so why isn’t everybody else as successful as they are?) need to own all the
ways in which they are emphatically not self-made.

I don’t have to think very long to come up with a list of a
number of ways in which I was born into advantage.  I am 100 percent Caucasian.  Some of my ancestors probably owned
slaves.  Certainly, none of them were
slaves.  My parents and grandparents
didn’t have to face segregation and redlining. 
When I bought my house, I didn’t have to deal with unscrupulous subprime
mortgage lenders who would have jacked up the interest rate on me if I had been
black.  My interactions with police
officers have been generally positive. 
When they’ve written me tickets, I’ve deserved it, and I’ve never had to
worry about being stopped and interrogated because a crime had been committed
in the vicinity.

I was raised by my two biological parents, who were happily
married to each other.  All of my needs
and most of my wants were provided for. 
I lived in safe areas with good schools because my parents could afford
it.  They gave me a strong moral and
religious grounding that has served me well all my life.  They paid my way through college and even
through law school, fat lot of good that did anybody.

They also gave me entrée into the extremely potent social
network of my religious tradition.  Every
job I’ve ever had above the McDonald’s and Wal-Mart level, I got because a
Christian put in a good word for me. 
Anywhere I might go in the country, I’ll find a community of faith that
will welcome me with open arms.  In fact,
I’ll probably know somebody there, or at least have a friend in common.  If I suffer some financial disaster, I know
that the brethren will step up and support me, to the tune of thousands or even
hundreds of thousands of dollars.

All that is exactly what privilege looks like.  Sure; I haven’t blown it.  I haven’t taken those advantages and flushed
them down the toilet, like all too many people do.  However, I haven’t exactly risen above my
station either.  At best, I’ve held
serve.

As a result, I can’t claim any right to wag my finger at
some single mom in the inner city who’s on welfare (and we all know she’s
black, right?) and say, “Why isn’t she successful like me?”  The problem, of course, is precisely that she
isn’t like me.  She didn’t get the white-picket-fence
upbringing or the powerful social network. 
She did get the legacy of institutional racism.

If I had been born in her position, maybe I would have
succeeded where she “failed”.  More
likely, I would have held serve in that situation too.  I would have followed my parents again—this
time to become part of a sneered-at urban underclass.  It is probably true that there but for the
grace of God go I.

I say this not to promote a sense of white guilt.  I don’t feel guilty about all I was given;
instead, I am humbled by it.  Who am I,
that this is the life that was handed to me?

Nor am I advocating some sort of massive government
intervention.  First of all, I’m simply
not a political animal.  Second, it seems
to me that government action in racial matters has had a tendency to make the
situation worse, not better, even when the government was trying to do
good.  Cabrini Green and all the other
housing projects, anybody?  There are
problems that are beyond the power of any human institution to solve.  I think racism is one of them.

Instead, I say it in the hope that anybody who reads it will
take a step back from themselves.  I
wouldn’t be who I am if I weren’t what I am, and that’s true for all of
us.  We can’t possibly understand the who
until we understand the different whats. 
Once we understand, hopefully we can learn to be a little less fearful
and a little more compassionate.  It’s
not our responsibility to assert that our lives matter, whoever “our” might be.  Instead, we’re here to prove that others’
lives do.
