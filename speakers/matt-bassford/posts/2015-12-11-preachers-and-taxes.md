As a result of several situations I’ve encountered recently,
I’ve concluded that many Christians and churches may not have a full
understanding of the peculiarities of the tax code as they apply to
preachers.  The subject has been an
interest of mine for several years (which is what happens when a lawyer becomes
a preacher), so I figured it would be useful to briefly explain what I believe
preachers and churches ought to be doing in this area.



**What Form to File**

Many brotherhood preachers insist that they are not
employees of the church.  Regardless of
what our beliefs may be, though, the IRS believes (and will litigate) that a
minister is an employee.  They take this
position on the basis of the common-law tests for distinguishing an employee
from a contractor.  As a result, when a
preacher’s “home church” reports his income at the end of the year, it should
file a Form W-2 rather than a Form 1099. 
If you’re so determined that you’re not a church employee that you want
to go to court with the IRS, fine.  For
myself, I would rather render unto Caesar the piece of paper due Caesar.

Because the preacher is an employee rather than a
contractor, he can’t report his income and his expenses on Schedule C.  However, when a preacher engages in
appointment preaching, preaches a meeting, conducts a wedding, etc., that is
considered independent-contractor income and should be reported on Schedule
C.  A church that pays a preacher more
than $500 in the course of a year for such independent work is required to file
a Form 1099.

**What Taxes to Pay**

Even though a preacher is considered an employee of the
church, he is still subject to self-employment tax.  This counter-intuitive result is spelled out
in 26 U.S.C §§ 1402(c)(4) and 3121(b)(8). 
As a result, where most employees pay only half of FICA and related
taxes, the preacher is on the hook for both halves.  After deductions, this means that 14.1
percent of the preacher’s gross income goes to Uncle Sam, in addition to any
income taxes he may owe.  Boo!

There is, however, an exception to this rule.  Ministers may file Form 4361, requesting to
be exempted from self-employment taxes. 
This requires the minister to affirm that he has a conscientious
objection to public insurance.  I know
that paying both halves of Social Security is a pretty big whack, and I
sympathize with all who don’t want to pay it. 
However, before you Try Really Hard to develop a conscientious objection
to public insurance, I would suggest that you reconsider.

The big issue here is not losing out on Social
Security.  The Social Security payout is
predictable, and a preacher who is careful to save for retirement can easily
replace it, especially given that he’s not paying thousands of dollars into
Social Security every year.  Instead, the
big issue is not being covered by Medicare. 
Health care gets EXPENSIVE when you get older, and the exempt preacher
could easily find himself on the hook for hundreds of thousands or even
millions of dollars in medical expenses.

In short, if you’re afraid you’ll go to hell if you receive
public insurance, fill out Form 4361.  If
not, don’t.

**How to Avoid Taxes**

Tax evasion is a crime. 
Tax avoidance, taking advantage of legal means to reduce one’s tax
obligation, is one of my favorite hobbies. 
I’m perfectly willing to pay what the law obliges me to pay, but not a
dime more.  Because the preacher’s tax
situation is so complicated, the tax code as it stands today offers several
useful opportunities.  Of these
opportunities, the most valuable are those that move money out of the reach of
self-employment tax.  Two avenues in
particular commend themselves here.

**Saving for Retirement**

In times past, preachers were paid so poorly by brethren
that they often did not have opportunity to save for retirement.  Thankfully, we live in an era where preachers
are often more generously compensated, which makes retirement savings
possible.

The very best way for a preacher to save for retirement is
via elective deferral of income into a 403(b) plan.  Here’s the way this works.  The preacher asks the church to establish a
403(b) retirement account for him.  Vanguard,
Fidelity, etc., will all offer this option. 
Once this is done, at the end of the year, the preacher asks for some
amount of his income for the next year ($10,000, say) to be deferred into this
account.

This offers two advantages. 
First, elective deferrals into a 403(b) are exempt from taxation unless
you’ve set up your 403(b) as a Roth IRA (which I wouldn’t advise).  It’s basically like saving in a normal
401(k), in that it is subject neither to income nor self-employment taxes.

Second, because the preacher can prove that all the money in
his 403(b) came from ministerial income, when he retires, he can ask the church
to designate distributions as housing allowance.  We’ll talk more about the ministerial housing
allowance later, but the point of it is that if you use housing-allowance money
on house-related expenses, that money is not subject to income tax.  As a result of this, some of the money that
the preacher puts in his 403(b) will *never*be taxed, which is incredibly useful! 
I don’t know why a preacher would use any other retirement-savings
vehicle.

**Expenses**

Like any other employee, a preacher will accumulate business
expenses:  mileage, business meals,
etc.  The best way to handle these
expenses is by setting up fixed-limit reimbursement according to an accountable
plan.  Here is not the place to explain
the intricacies of an accountable plan. 
Those who want more information should consult IRS Publication 463, which,
like most IRS publications, is surprisingly readable and will guide you unto
all truth.

In practice, here’s the way this works.  At year’s end, the church designates some
portion (say, $8000) of the financial resources available for the preacher as
his expense allowance for the next year. 
Throughout the year, as the preacher incurs expenses, he reports them in
timely fashion to the church treasurer (jumping through all the IRS hoops), who
then reimburses him, up to that $8000 limit. 
Reimbursements are not subject to income or self-employment tax.

This might not seem like a big deal, but in reality, the
IRS-approved reimbursement is often larger than the actual expense
occurred.  For instance, I drive a
paid-for Toyota Corolla.  In 2015, the
IRS allows me to expense 57.5 cents per mile. 
It does not cost me 57.5 cents to drive that car for a mile, but I
faithfully log all my business mileage (visiting shut-ins, trips to meetings,
Bible studies, etc.) and regularly submit the log to our treasurer for
reimbursement.  If I come out ahead in the
exchange, that’s hardly my fault!

**Housing Allowance**

Sadly, not all of the tax-avoidance opportunities available
to the preacher are so promising as retirement and expenses, but they’re still
worth pursuing.  In this latter category,
we include the ministerial housing allowance.

Here’s the way this works. 
According to 26 U.S.C. § 107, a preacher does not report the part of his
compensation that he spends on housing as income.  Once again, there are all kinds of rules for
how you determine what you spend on housing. 
They appear in IRS Publication 517 (which is also surprisingly useful
and readable).  The money that meets the
IRS standards here is subject to self-employment tax (Boo!) but not subject to
income tax.  Because most of us spend a
lot of money on our house, utilities, and the like, the housing allowance means
that the preacher’s reported income is often reduced by $20,000 or more, with
tax consequences that may be significant.

**Health Insurance and
Obamacare**

Sometimes, the indirect consequences of the housing
allowance are more significant than the direct consequences.  This is particularly true when it comes to
the purchase of health insurance.  Many
churches still provide employer-based health insurance for preachers.  However, it’s usually more cost-effective for
the church to discontinue that coverage, increase the preacher’s compensation
by the amount previously used to purchase insurance, and have him purchase
individual insurance on the relevant exchange.

This is the result of the method used by the government to
calculate Obamacare subsidies (both premium and deductible assistance).  Uncle Sam looks at a modified version of
adjusted gross income.  Preachers,
because of the housing allowance, have an adjusted gross income much lower than
they otherwise would.  Uncle Sam then
concludes that preachers are poorer than they actually are and gives them a
much more generous subsidy.  There are
other wrinkles here (Do not, whatever you do, report an income below the
poverty level if you live in a state that didn’t expand Medicaid!), but the
overall result is quite beneficial.  To
be frank, I don’t see a reason why preachers should be so favored, but as long
as we are so favored, I don’t see a reason to ignore the benefit.

everybody else, happy taxing!
