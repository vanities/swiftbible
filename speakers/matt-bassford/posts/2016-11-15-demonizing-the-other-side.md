Well, now that the election is over, we’ve all been able to
get back to business as usual, right? 
That might be true for my Uncle Bob, who has never bothered to get
hooked up to the Internet, but the online hordes know differently.  I would say, in fact, that the tone of my
Facebook feed is even more rancorous than it was two weeks ago.



On the one hand, my liberal friends (of whom I wish I had
more) are frightened by all the awful things that Trump supporters have supposedly
been doing since the election.  The KKK
has been displaying banners on overpasses, Muslim-American women have been
assaulted, Hispanic children have been bullied at school, and so on.

On the other hand, my conservative friends (of whom I have
plenty, though I wouldn’t mind having more) are displaying a strange mixture of
self-righteousness and paranoia.  “We won
the election; deal with it!” (with lots of articles about the function of the
Electoral College)  is mixed with lurid
reports about the continuing protests, with riots, windows smashed, cars set on
fire, and George Soros busing rent-a-protestors from city to city.  Oh, and Trump actually won the popular vote,
too!

There are two things worth observing about these competing
sets of claims.  The first is that they
are largely untrue.  As [this article](http://reason.com/blog/2016/11/11/election-night-hijab-attack-false) points out, a lot of the stories about bigotry in motion have gotten Snopesed
(apparently, the people who run Snopes have decided that they don’t want to be
truth-hating Communists anymore).  I’m
sure there has been a bigot or two, but that’s simply the law of large numbers
operating in a country of 320 million people. 
It’s a statistical certainty that there is at least one Trump voter dumb
enough and mean enough to do literally anything.

Similarly, as far as I can tell, the worst thing that the
vast, vast majority of left-wing protestors did was block traffic.  There have been occasional fires (doubtless
leading to widespread panic among dumpsters), but the Watts riots, this ain’t.

However, these stories have gotten wide currency among their
respective audiences because each base wants the stories to be true.  Liberals want to believe that the average
Trump voter really is racist, sexist, homophobic, etc., even though experience leads me to believe that the average Trump voter was a person of
good will faced with an unpalatable choice.

Likewise, conservatives want to believe that Clinton
supporters are lawless hoodlums who must be brought to heel before they ruin
the country.  They’re the mob that the
Electoral College protects us from! 
However, more limited anecdotal evidence leads me to suspect that the
average Clinton voter was also a person of good will faced with an unpalatable
choice, doing what they thought best.

The problem is that there’s no moral high ground to be
gained in crediting folks on the other side with good will.  If what they want is basically to be left
alone, there’s no reason to fear them and no need to call for urgent political
action.  What’s more, there’s no longer a
significant difference between Us and Them. 
They cease being malevolent, conniving, and hostile and start looking a
lot more like Us, ordinary, decent people with their share of concern about an
uncertain future.

I’m hardly a political expert.  When I consider America’s problems, Ecclesiastes
1:15 comes irresistibly to mind!  I do,
however, have considerable interest in the human heart, and this I know:  Once you start telling yourself bad stories
about somebody else, once you start listening to and seeking out and believing
bad stories, it cannot but corrupt you. 
This is true on an individual level, where we can talk ourselves into
believing that an acquaintance is the next best thing to the devil, all on the
tiniest shreds of evidence considered in the most hostile light.  We can do it with groups, too, with people we
don’t even know, and teach ourselves that they are threatening simply because
they’re different.

Sadly, we never use (and indeed never can use) this kind of
storytelling to talk ourselves into doing good. 
We don’t say, “He’s a threat to my way of life, so I’d better go out of
my way to be nice to him!”  Instead, we
use it to justify every kind of evil: 
“He’s a threat to my way of life, so I’d better strike back at him (or
strike first at him) every chance I get!”

As we act out of fear, we give the other a reason to fear us
and also be motivated by that fear.  Just
as perfect love casts out fear, so too perfect fear casts out love, and if
there’s anything that people ought to be able to agree on, it is that casting
out love is something of which we truly should be afraid.

In a season like this, I think we would all be best advised
to give up on wholesale and go retail. 
All those lurid Facebook-shared articles and memes are peddling a
version of reality that helps the authors (clicks = $$$) but hurts us.  It’s a diet of spiritual junk food.

Instead, every one of us should be making an effort to reach
out to those who are not like us.  Talk to
people you know don’t agree with you. 
Care about them, their perspectives, and their lives.  Try to understand.

Oddly enough, people tend to shrink once you start talking
to them.  The vague Other is very
threatening.  The middle-aged white man
(or black college student, or. . .) across the coffee-shop table from you, with
all of his dreams and confusions and insecurities, isn’t, not so much.  There are demagogues out there, on both sides
of the aisle, but generally, I think we mean well for one another, and
face-to-face, that comes across.

It’s easy for all of us to feed our fears.  Let’s work on feeding our love instead.
