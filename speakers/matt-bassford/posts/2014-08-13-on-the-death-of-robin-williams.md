*Since you have in
obedience to the truth purified your souls for a sincere love of the brethren,
fervently love one another from the heart.*

-- 1 Peter 1:22 (NASB)

Despite all the wars and rumors of wars that fill the globe,
the foremost news story these days is of the passing and apparent suicide of
Robin Williams.  Some find this
objectionable.  Why should it be, they
ask, that we mourn the death of a solitary man when hundreds, perhaps
thousands, are killed in Iraq and Syria?



The question, though reasonable, is not mindful of our
frame.  We have not seen the faces and do
not know the names of those countless victims of terror.  As a result, our compassion for them can
never be more than an abstraction. 
That’s not our fault.  That’s how
human beings are.

Williams, by contrast, was a name, a face, and a voice
too.  We’ve all seen him in any number of
movies, even those of us who are as disconnected from pop culture as I am.  Though our bond with him is an illusory one
(we didn’t really know him, and he certainly didn’t know us), the illusion is
compelling nonetheless.

The story of his death is equally compelling, both as tragedy
and as a call to wisdom.  In worldly
terms, Williams had it all:  talent,
money, fame, and every pleasure of the flesh he wanted.  According to the wisdom of the world, this
should have made him the happiest man on the planet.  After all, don’t millions spend their lives
struggling to attain a tithe of what he had?

And yet, in having talent, money, fame, and pleasure,
Williams found that he had nothing.  A
life filled with these things was so unbearably empty that he could no longer
stand to continue in it.  I’ve long
suspected that the worst thing that can happen to a citizen of the world is for
all his dreams to come true, and Williams’ sad fate only confirms my suspicion.  We must realize that when we look to build
our lives on any of these things, we are building on sand.  Their end is not contentment, but despair.

I also grieve for the loneliness that must have marked
Williams’ last few days.  Apparently, he
didn’t have anybody he trusted enough to reach out to and confide in.  The best psychiatrists in the world are no
substitute for a single true friend. 
That’s not a road I walk.  In
worldly terms, I may not have much, but I have hundreds, perhaps thousands, of
people who love me and care about me. 
There are few things on earth more precious to me than the community of
the Lord’s people, and in a world filled with so much pain, they are for me an
enduring source of consolation.

Of course, I know perfectly well that many will read this
with cynicism and resentment.  In other
Christians, they have found not solace and support, but disappointment and
betrayal.  I mourn that too.  God’s plan for His church is only as
effective as its execution, and I recognize that all too often, brethren who
should have reached out have turned away instead.  I recognize that all too often, I have been
the one who turned away.

None of us will ever do perfectly, but we can all do
better.  We must allow the passing of a
famous man whom we never knew to remind us of the needs of the overlooked
people in our lives, people whom we do know and can help.  We must make sure that our claims to be a
Christian family, and indeed our claims to be disciples of Christ, are more
than lip service.

Conversely, to those who are on the outside looking in, I
say this:  Be willing to be
vulnerable.  Be willing to risk, to dare,
to be open, even to be hurt.  I
understand the fear of rejection—quite well, in fact—but the possibility of
failure is far better than the certainty of loneliness.

I understand depression too, and even though it may have
chemical and hereditary roots, it flourishes in an environment of
alienation.  As the hymn says, I don’t
know a thing in this whole wide world that’s worse than being alone.  It is often the case that God expresses His
love through the love of His people, and when we are cut off from them, we
usually end up cut off from Him too, caught in a cycle of misery that turns
inward ever more tightly.  Don’t turn
in.  Reach out.  You may well receive more than you ever
thought possible.

Suicide is dismayingly common.  In the United States, it is something like
ten times as common as homicide.  The
true tragedy of Williams’ suicide is not that we have lost a great actor or a
great comic, but that we have lost a human being.  Our value does not lie in what we do.  It lies in who we are.  Everyone who is made in God’s image is a
creature of infinite worth.  As
appropriate as it is for us to remember Robin Williams, it is even more
appropriate for us to remember that.
