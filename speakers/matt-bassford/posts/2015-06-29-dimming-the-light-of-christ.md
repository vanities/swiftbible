Back in the 1980s, the baby boomers were dubbed “the me
generation”.  To many observers, their
selfishness and greed seemed like a dramatic departure from the higher ideals
that had characterized previous generations. 
Since that time, of course, successive generations have earned the same
title by their self-centered behavior. 
Millions of people around us know no higher God than themselves.  They are concerned only with what makes them
happy.

Whether or not this is a new problem in the United States is
debatable, but it is certainly not a new problem for mankind.  Indeed, much of the New Testament was written
to address the problem of pride.  It
contains many texts that are applicable to our me-first society, but we’re
going to look at only one of them:  a
context from James that warns us against dimming the light of Christ.

 **Through Quarreling**

James first points to the danger of pride causing
quarreling.  He begins by identifying **THE TRUE PROOF OF WISDOM.** Let’s read here from James 3:13-14.  Here, brethren, we find a loaded question if
ever there was one!  I’ve certainly known
brethren, who, if they were being candid, would identify themselves as
possessing wisdom and understanding. 
However, these same brethren often have a habit of stirring up trouble
in the congregation.  Because they
believe themselves to be wise and understanding, they tend to want to share
their superior wisdom and understanding with others, and when others don’t see
it their way, they’re apt to get angry about it:  “How dare these morons reject what I have to
say when I am so clearly right!”

James observes, though, that the true mark of superior
wisdom is not having the right answer for everything, or at least believing and
arguing that we do.  Instead, genuine
wisdom expresses itself through good deeds and gentleness.  The first place we ought to be turning our
wisdom is on ourselves, not on others. 
Of course, getting our own spiritual houses in order is a lot harder
than shouting our think-so’s to the world, but only thus do we truly reveal
Christ in us.

By contrast, if we’ve got all these good ideas for everybody
else, but none for ourselves because we’re already perfect, we should at least
be honest with ourselves about what’s driving us.  It’s not wisdom.  It’s jealousy and ambition.  We’re doing what we’re doing because we want
to be the ones who are running everybody else, and we resent those who already
seem to be in charge.  There’s a
tremendous amount of self-righteousness associated with this.  We feel so certain of ourselves, so sure that
we’re right about it.  Genuine
righteousness, however, is nowhere to be seen.

James continues on to attribute this disposition to **EARTHLY WISDOM.** Look at James 3:15-16.  Here, as elsewhere in the Scripture, we
encounter the idea that there are actually two possible sources of wisdom.  There is the wisdom that is taught by God on
the one hand, and the wisdom that is natural and earthly on the other.  This is the wisdom that teaches that we
should follow the law of the jungle, that all that matters is getting our way
and what we want, and if somebody else gets offended or hurt in the process,
well, that’s too bad!

From the worldly perspective, this makes perfect sense.  If you want to be happy, you have to pursue
the things that make you happy and not let anybody else stand between you and
happiness.  There are thousands of
articles on the Internet urging precisely this kind of wisdom.  If I were feeling mischievous, I might insert
some quotations from Joel Osteen here, but I won’t.

Sadly, a great irony attends those who walk the path of
earthly wisdom.  The more we pursue our
self-centered vision of happiness, the more true happiness recedes from
us.  We end up with lives filled with
disorder and evil.  Anytime a group of
people follows the wisdom of the world, sooner or later, that group is going to
blow up like a stick of dynamite.

It happens in families, it happens in friendships, and it
happens in churches.  There is no more
tragic sight than a congregation that is supposed to have learned wisdom from
God demonstrating that it has learned the world’s wisdom instead.  These churches cost us our young people,
drive away the weak, and cause the name of Christ to be blasphemed among
outsiders.  It all starts with Christians
who are so focused on “I” that they have forgotten about “us”.

James’ solution to the problem is **THE WISDOM FROM ABOVE.** He
explores it and its effects in James 3:17-18. 
The wisdom from above doesn’t start quarrels.  It resolves them.  It doesn’t open wounds.  It heals them.  It is able to do all of these things because
it puts the other before the self.  To
the world, this is nonsense.  We all want
to be happy, don’t we?  How can we be
happy if we don’t grab our happiness with both hands?  How can we be happy if we constantly let
others around us have their way instead of insisting on our way?  How can we be happy if we constantly serve
others rather than ourselves?  To the
world, that’s about as logical as strapping lead weights to your horse before
the Kentucky Derby.  It’s not a way to
win; it’s a way to lose.

In reality, friends, happiness does not come from heaping up
possessions.  It does not come from being
right.  It does not come from getting our
way.  Instead, true joy, true contentment
can only arise from the richness of our relationships with God and one
another.  When we nurture those
relationships through humility, mercy, and willingness to yield, when we prize
peace with others more than the tyranny of the self, that is when we are able
to savor the best that life has to offer.

What is more, when we live a life that is genuinely good and
loving is when those around us are able to see Christ in us.  The most Christlike Christians I know aren’t the
loud ones, the bossy ones, the self-insistent ones.  Instead, they are the quiet ones, the humble
ones, the ones who are first in line to serve. 
Ironically, it is the Christians like these who actually have the
influence over others that the ungodly crave. 
Because people see their good works and trust their wisdom, they can
accomplish more with a whisper than the selfishly ambitious can gain with a
shout.  The wisdom from above, then,
doesn’t merely satisfy us.  It also
glorifies God.

**Through Double-Mindedness**

From here, James continues on to point out that we also dim
the light of Christ through double-mindedness. 
He begins by identifying **THE
SOURCE OF CONFLICT** in many Christians’ lives.  Consider James 4:1-3.  This is an ugly description, but it becomes
even worse when we realize that James is addressing brethren.  I don’t think the point here is that these
disciples are actually murdering one another. 
Instead, as he does in James 2, he cites a shocking sin as an example to
make us realize that in the eyes of God, our own sins are no less extreme.

Once we recognize that, the picture really begins to come
into focus.  This text is about people
who are both-and Christians instead of either-or Christians.  God presents them with the choice between Christ
and the world, and they try to choose both. 
Even though they claim to be led by the spirit, they are walking
according to the flesh.  Their search for
pleasure isn’t making them happy, but neither is their search for God, because
they’re looking for God for the wrong reasons.

Sadly, this pattern of behavior is all too evident today,
both outside and inside the church.  This
is what the prosperity-gospel movement is about.  With varying degrees of subtlety, prosperity-gospel
preachers assert that if you seek God, He will give you the earthly blessings
that are what you really want, so that godliness becomes a means of gain.  The people in James 4:3 certainly think that
godliness should be a means of gain, but they’re upset because the vending
machine seems to be broken.  Even among
our own number, I’ve known plenty of brethren who have just enough religion to
make them miserable.  Friends, pursuing
both God and the world at the same time doesn’t give us both.  It gives us neither.  The flesh and the spirit short-circuit each
other.

Such efforts are doomed to fail in part because they provoke
**ENMITY WITH GOD.** Here, read with me from James 4:4-5.  The thing that always gets me about the
prosperity-gospel folks is that they assume that God is an idiot.  They think that if they make a big show of
loving Him, even though what they really want is the earthly goodies He can
give them, He’ll be fooled by the display and give them what they truly
love.  Come on, now.  Don’t you think that the Creator of heaven and
earth might be at least a little bit smarter than that?

However, when we ourselves limp between two opinions,
surrendering part of our lives to God, but keeping the other part for
ourselves, we are making exactly the same mistake.  After all, He has commanded us to love Him
with all of our hearts and all of our minds and all of our souls and all of our
strength.  If we don’t, and live like we
don’t, He’ll see right through us.

Of course, it’s even worse than that.  When our lives are part-godly, part-sinful,
generally the part where we obey God is the part where we aren’t particularly
tempted to sin.  I’ve never wanted to
murder anybody, so I’ll give God that. 
I’ve never wanted to get drunk, so I’ll give God that.  But these things over here, the sins that
actually appeal to me, I’m going to keep those for myself!

It reminds me of Saul and the people in 1 Samuel 15.  Even though God had commanded them to destroy
all the livestock of the Amalekites, they spared the best of the animals,
destroyed only the ones that they thought were worthless, and claimed that they
had obeyed the commandment of the Lord. 
Not so.  They did exactly what
they wanted to and put a coat of religious whitewash on it.  Brethren, if the only sins that we give up
for God are the sins we don’t like, we’re giving up nothing, and we will earn
His wrath as our reward.

Instead, we must seek **EXALTATION
THROUGH HUMILITY.** This is where
James heads in James 4:6-10.  Here, he
points out that the root problem behind double-mindedness is pride.  It’s not that we don’t believe in God.  It’s not that we’re ignorant of His
will.  The problem is simply that we
refuse to carry it out.  We read His
commandment and say, “So what?  This is
too important to me to give up for You.” 
The Lord has no use for that kind of arrogance, and the outsiders around
us will be quick to recognize its hypocrisy. 
The light of Christ cannot shine in the double-minded.  Only the devil dwells in a heart like that.

Instead, the only way we can draw near to God is humbly, on
His terms, not ours.  God does not have a
drop of mercy for the proud rebel, but He has an ocean of it for the penitent
sinner.  When we proudly exalt ourselves
and insist on having our own way, God is going to hammer us down flat.  On the other hand, when we come to Him on our
knees, filled with reverent fear for His judgment and sorrow at our sin, He
will lift us up.  He will set us higher
than we ever could have climbed on our own. 
Just as it does in our relationships with others, the pursuit of pride
in our walk with God will bring us low. 
Only humility, repentance, and single-minded obedience will exalt us.

In just a few moments, we’ll be sharing in the Lord’s
Supper.  As we do, let’s pause to
evaluate our own walk with God.  We know
what perfect humility and perfect submission look like.  We see them in the Lord Jesus, who humbled
Himself to the point of death, even death on a cross, Let’s remember Him now
and ask whether His mind is evident in us.

**Through
Presumptuousness**

Finally, James points out the problems with pride when it
manifests itself as presumptuousness.  The
first instance of presumptuousness that he cites is **JUDGING OUR BROTHER.** Let’s
read from James 4:11-12.  Here, James is
returning to a theme he’d already explored some in James 2, the theme of
setting oneself up as a judge.  In this
case, though, the would-be judges are setting themselves up not only as judges
of others, but of God’s law itself.

We violate this commandment when we condemn where the
Scriptures leave a matter up to individual judgment.  We’re so convinced that an activity is evil
and wrong that we blast it even though the only Scriptural support for our
outrage lies in the Book of Second Opinions. 
In that case, our judgment is truly our own.  We’re certainly not getting it from God!  Similarly, we are sitting in judgment on the
law by implying that it is incomplete and inadequate.

This should be a warning to all of us, brethren, about the
limits of our spiritual discernment. 
Certainly, God expects us to use our wisdom to avoid potential spiritual
dangers.  For instance, even though
nothing in Scripture commands it, I decided a long time ago that I was never
going to study the Bible alone with a woman who was not my wife.  It’s fine for me to follow that rule; indeed,
I can even recommend it to other preachers. 
However, I cannot flatly say that a man who has violated my rule has
sinned.  I can say that I believe his
decision is unwise, but I cannot condemn it, for if I do, I have become his
judge, the judge of the law of God, and the judge of God Himself.

This kind of condemnation is extremely damaging to the cause
of Christ, particularly when it comes to dealing with young people.  If we insist that the Bible should be the
guide to their lives, they had better be able to see that everything we teach
comes from the Bible.  Teenagers are very
quick to spot inconsistency, and once we start inserting our own think-so’s and
labeling them as Scripture, they will accuse us of not being true to our own
ideals and reject those ideals altogether.

The next example of presumptuousness in this context lies in
**ASSUMING A FUTURE.** Here, consider James 4:13-14.  A little lower down, James describes this
conduct as arrogance, and that’s a precisely correct definition.  We often use “arrogance” as a synonym for
“pride”, but the word actually has a shade of meaning beyond that.  We are arrogant when we claim something for
ourselves that we don’t have a right to. 
We arrogate it to ourselves.  In
this case, what we are arrogating is nothing less than control over our own
lives.  We make all these confident
predictions about what’s going to happen tomorrow or next year, and we never
stop to think that by tomorrow, we might already be cooling our heels in the
coroner’s office.  That is arrogance.

Furthermore, this arrogance has a corrosive effect on
everything in our spiritual lives.  If I
can make it to tomorrow or next year just fine on my own, then I don’t need God
for that, and there are probably a lot of other areas where I don’t need God
either.  I can be righteous on my own,
without His help and His grace.  I don’t
need the strength that comes from assembling with His people on Wednesdays or
Sundays, because I’m plenty strong on my own. 
I don’t need to give back generously to the Lord’s work, because I
earned this money on my own.  If
self-reliance is our thing, we will self-reliance ourselves and everybody who
is following us right out of the church.

In addition to leading us into the condemnation of the
devil, this presumption about our future is utterly unfounded.  Human beings aren’t powerful and
self-reliant.  In this life, we are easy
prey for blood clots, cancer cells, drunk drivers and our own
carelessness.  We might think we’re
eternal, but our lives are as transitory as the steam in our breath on a cold
morning.

In consequence, James encourages us to think of the future
only **“IF THE LORD WILLS”**. Look at
James 4:15-17.  Of course, these are not
magic words.  We can’t go around saying
“Lord willing” and expect God to be pleased as we make our arrogant way through
life, any more than we can sing, “I Need Thee Every Hour” and only pay
attention to Him for four hours a week.

Instead, “If the Lord wills” has to be something that we
mean.  We have to admit in our hearts that
every breath we take, we take because of His mercy, that every dime we’ve ever
earned was a gift from Him, that He decides where we will be tomorrow or next
year, and not we ourselves.  As
self-reliance has spiritual consequences, so too does reliance on God.  The more we recognize our need for Him, the
more we will seek Him.  The more we
acknowledge His power, the more we will entrust our lives to Him, so that we
live not only if the Lord wills, but as the Lord wills.

In typical Jacobean fashion, though, James ends this section
on a somber note.  He warns us that sin
is not merely when we do what we know we shouldn’t, but when we fail to do what
we know we should.  In this case, he’s
been instructing us that we should seek the effacement of ourselves:  in our relationships with others, in our moral
decision-making, in our judgments, and in our assumptions about the
future.  The less of ourselves that we
allow to remain, the more we will reveal of Christ.
