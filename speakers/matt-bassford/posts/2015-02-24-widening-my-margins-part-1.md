Over the past year of blogging about Bibles, I’ve gotten to
know a number of fellow Bible geeks online. 
This has given me some unexpected insights into a topic I’ve never
thought much about:  the way that other
people use their Bibles.  I naturally
tend to be a small-Bible fancier.  I have
good eyes, so smaller-print Bibles are fine with me.  I have relatively small hands for a man, so I
don’t particularly enjoy holding Bibles the size of an unabridged dictionary
(do they still make those?).  Also, I
live in fear of destroying further large Bibles the way I destroyed a 2002
Foundation Side-Column Reference during a vacation a few years back, so I
prefer Bibles small enough to be stowed in a car’s glove box, safely out of the
way.



This has led me to consume a fairly steady diet of smaller
Bibles.  Pitt Minions, large-print
compacts, and thinlines are pretty much what I favor.  However, I see others in the Bibliophile
community do differently.  Some of them
seek out options I don’t feel any real need to pursue.  Though I sympathize with those with aging
eyes, I don’t need large-print Bibles like they do.  However, there are other options that have
always intrigued me, and chief among them is the option of using a wide-margin
Bible.

When I think of writing in Bibles, the first image that
comes to mind is that of my mother’s ’02 SCR. 
I inherited it when she passed on a few years back, and it is *heavily* written in.  To be specific, it is heavily
underlined.  I suppose that my mother believed
that you ought to underline important things when you read, and in the word of
God, everything is important, so. . . 
I’m pretty sure there are whole chapters, possibly even whole books, in
that Bible in which every word is underlined except for the articles!

Now, far be it from me to deny my mother’s sincerity or
devotion, but that’s never made a lick of sense to me.  Yes, every word of the Scripture is
God-breathed, but if you underline everything, that has the same logical effect
as underlining nothing.  As a result,
even when I wielded an ’02 SCR too, my Bible was note- and underline-free.  Bible-writing simply didn’t resonate.

However, I’ve since seen other Bible-fanciers doing something
that does resonate:  copying sermon notes
and outlines into wide-margin Bibles and then preaching from them.  My sermon-preparation process is fairly
involved.  First, I scratch-outline my
main points with supporting passages. 
Then, I type up a four-page full-sentence manuscript.  After that, in the few days before preaching,
I read the manuscript out loud in full three times.  One of those will be a full-blown dress
rehearsal, complete with Scripture readings and PowerPoint run-through.  Finally, I condense the manuscript to a
single-sheet outline, which I will then preach from on Sunday morning.

Of all of that process, the part I really can’t stand is the
manuscript-condensing part.  By that
point in time, I’m fairly familiar with the sermon, so typing out a shorter
restatement doesn’t hold much to interest me. 
Honestly, it’s starting to become a problem.  I should be typing my preaching outline on
Friday morning, but since I adopted this preparation format, outline time has
slid from Friday to Saturday night to Sunday morning.  I dislike it so much that I don’t do it until
I absolutely have to, which is unfortunate on several different levels.

Over the past several months, I’ve begun toying with the
idea that the cure for my disease is copying my outline into a wide-margin
Bible instead.  Intellectually, I find
that much more appealing than a typed restatement of a typed manuscript.  For one thing, I discard all of those
outlines as soon as I use them.  They’re
disposable and therefore insignificant.

However, I obviously wouldn’t discard my wide-margin every
Sunday!  Instead, it would become a
physical repository of all of my sermons, a repository of my distilled thinking
about the Scripture.  That’s not
insignificant.  It’s powerfully
meaningful.  Also, the physical process
of writing in a Bible is more appealing than pounding on a keyboard.  I type as naturally as I breathe, but the
process of translating paragraphs into careful phrases and circling and, yes,
underlining must be a thoughtful one.

Finally, Bible-annotating may help me with the primary goal
of my sermon-prep process, which is achieving the “ready recollection” so
beloved of prayer leaders in my religious tradition.  I want to do the best job I can every time I
step behind the pulpit, which for me means rehearsing the roughness out of my
delivery.  I can tell that the process of
outline-formulation helps me remember, but [this article](http://www.businessinsider.com/handwriting-helps-you-learn-2014-12) suggests that writing my sermon notes by hand would help even more.

There I was:  interested
in adopting a wide-margin Bible, but not quite ready to take the plunge.  Then, I got to talking with Marcus Hinds at
Cambridge about what Cambridge Bible I would like to review.  The thought crossed my mind that it might be
best for me to review a newer Cambridge product, so I flipped through the most
recent Cambridge catalog and noted that they’ve only recently come out with a
calf-split NASB Wide-Margin Reference.

I said to myself, “Self, that would be a cool Bible to
review!”  First of all, I already own a
calf-split NASB Pitt Minion, which would make for a neat-looking matched set
with the Wide-Margin.  I could even
review the two Bibles as a unit, and although all the Bible aficionados know
that Cambridge Wide-Margins share settings with their little-brother Pitt
Minions, I’ve never seen anybody write about the two together like that.  Third, my NASB Pitt is the subject of my “One
Bible, One Year” vow, so I could still do all of my study and prep out of it,
while using the identically laid-out Wide-Margin as a sermon notebook of
sorts.  That would give me the
opportunity to determine how much visual verse memory carries over from a Pitt
to a Wide-Margin.

I pitched the idea to Marcus, and soon a calf-split NASB
Wide-Margin appeared on my doorstep.  So
begins my wide-margin adventure.  We’ll
see how it goes.
