The older I get, the more I realize the truth of Solomon’s
statement that there is nothing new under the sun.  When I was a kid, I can remember the adults
back then joking that if some article of clothing went out of fashion, all you
had to do was put it in the closet for 20 years, and it would be stylish
again.  I had no idea what they were
talking about, but now I see high-school students wearing things I remember from
when I was in high school.  I guess that
when Zoë is that age, she’ll probably be wearing bell bottoms or something.

Fashion trends may come and go and come again, but human
nature doesn’t even change that much.  No
matter what people today are wearing, inside, they’re no different from those
who lived thousands of years ago.  We all
wrestle with the same fears, the same desires, and the same yearning for
eternity that God set within our hearts. 
Because of this similarity, we have much to learn from what God has said
even to those who lived under another covenant. 
In illustration of this, let’s look at some moral instruction from 3000
years ago:  the first three psalms.



**Psalm 1**

Naturally, if this is going to be our course of study, we
must begin with Psalm 1.  It begins by
describing **THE BLESSED MAN.** Let’s read here from Psalm 1:1-2.  This is a subtle text, and it’s subtle
because it isn’t really about what this man is doing.  It’s about what he’s listening to.  The psalmist doesn’t say, “How blessed is the
man who doesn’t do wicked things, or sin, or scoff.”  Instead, he’s blessed because he doesn’t walk
in the counsel of the wicked or stand in the path of sinners or sit in the seat
of scoffers.  In other words, he’s
looking at the things that evil men say and do and saying, “I don’t want any
part of that.”  He’s choosing to reject
the bad conduct of others.

Instead, he’s getting his instruction, his counsel, and his
path from the law of the Lord.  What’s
particularly important here, though, is what the psalmist reveals about his
attitude toward the law of the Lord. 
It’s not spiritual broccoli to him. 
It’s not that he’s reading and meditating on the law because he knows
it’s good for him, but he doesn’t really like it.  Instead, his delight is in the law of the
Lord, and he meditates on it day and night the same way that worldly men might
meditate on women or making money or getting what they want.  He thinks about it because he loves it.

Each one of us should ask ourselves this morning whether our
delight is in the law of the Lord.  It’s
good that we’re here, obviously. 
However, we need to examine our hearts to make sure that we’re doing the
right thing for the right reason:  not
because somebody made us come here, not because we’re eating our spiritual
broccoli, but because this is what we love. 
If we don’t have that love for the word, we need to learn it, both by
spending time with it ourselves and by associating with those who love it.  Only then will we be blessed.

Next, the psalmist contrasts **THE RIGHTEOUS AND THE WICKED.** Here,
let’s consider Psalm 1:3-4.  If we were
to sum up v. 3 here in a word, that word would be “stable”.  The righteous man leads a stable
existence.  He’s rooted, he’s secure,
he’s predictably useful, and he continues to prosper.  By contrast, the wicked don’t have any of
that stability.  The psalmist describes
them as chaff, that light outer shell of wheat when it gets threshed.  Chaff is first of all unstable.  It gets blown wherever the wind wants to take
it.  Second, though, chaff is
impermanent.  It’s not like a tumbleweed
that retains its shape.  Once the wind
takes it, it’s gone.

This is a vivid illustration of the importance of
righteousness, both in this life and in the life to come.  Obviously, there are no guarantees on
earth.  Solomon observes in Ecclesiastes
8 that sometimes, it happens to the righteous according to the deeds of the
wicked, and to the wicked according to the deeds of the righteous.  However, these things are an exception, and
we should not allow the exception to swallow the rule.  The rule is that if we choose to be
righteous, we will benefit from being righteous.  If we are diligent about imitating the love
of Christ in our marriages and families, they will be stable and strong.  If we offer service to our employers as to
the Lord, our employers will appreciate that and reward us accordingly.  If we are consistently kind, generous, and
humble in our dealings with others, our lives will be rich with love and
friends.

For the wicked, on the other hand, it is not so.  The wisdom of the world says that if we want
to get ahead, we constantly have to fight to dominate, to get our own way in
every area of our lives.  The problem is
that selfishness and arrogance have exactly the opposite result.  They wreck families.  They poison careers.  They alienate friends.  Rather than giving us people and things in
our lives that we can depend on, the pursuit of sin leaves us alone and
helpless.

Finally, the psalmist explains what will happen to each in **THE JUDGMENT.** Consider here Psalm 1:5-6.  Even though it is generally true that
righteousness benefits us in this life, that isn’t the reason why any of us
should be righteous.  This is.  Life on earth is messy.  Sometimes people don’t get what they deserve,
in both directions.  However, on the Day
of Judgment, all the mess disappears. 
God is the ultimate Judge.  His
knowledge and His wisdom are both perfect, and He will use them to perfectly
discern between the righteous and the wicked.

This, friends, is why we have to keep on in serving
God.  Maybe it happens to us according to
the deeds of the wicked, even if we aren’t. 
I know some Christians who seem to spend their lives getting dumped
on.  However, even if we never get a
single benefit out of being righteous in this life, when God says to us, “Well
done, good and faithful servant,” not a one of us is going to complain!  As Paul says in 2 Corinthians 4, the glory to
come is incomparably better.

**Psalm 2**

Next, of course, we come to Psalm 2.  It’s about twice as long as the first psalm,
and its first section concerns **THE KINGS
OF THE EARTH.** Here, let’s read from
Psalm 2:1-3.  As we saw a couple of weeks
back, this is first and foremost a messianic psalm.  Although the kings of the nations doubtless
conspired against David, Solomon, and the other kings of God’s people, this
passage was truly fulfilled only when the Jewish leaders and Pilate came
together to condemn Jesus to crucifixion. 
As the psalm makes clear, they were driven by pride.  They couldn’t tolerate His claims of Godhood
and kingship, they didn’t want to submit to Him, and so they had to kill Him.

Today, the authority of Christ still poses a problem even
for people who aren’t rulers.  Countless
millions of people in our country today reject New Testament Christianity
because they don’t want to do what the Bible says.  Sure, they might come up with these
long-winded arguments about how they don’t believe in God, or about how their
religious tradition is really better. 
When you get right down to it, though, they wanna do what they wanna do,
and if God through His word tells them to do anything else, they’re going to
throw off His yoke.

After this, though, we see how **GOD REACTS** to rebellion by high and low alike.  Look here at Psalm 2:4-6.  One of the things that strikes me about the
presentation of God in Scripture is that He has kind of a dark sense of
humor.  Whenever the Scripture presents
Him as laughing, it’s never gentle, lighthearted laughter.  It’s always got an edge to it.  So it is here.  The kings of the earth are conspiring against
Jesus, but God is watching the whole scene and laughing.  It reminds me of when my kids come up with
some scheme to trick me into giving them what they want.  They think they’re being all sneaky and sly,
but I, of course, see right through it and roll my eyes at their
shenanigans.  So it is with God.  Next to Him, all these powerful, clever
rulers are less than children, and no matter what they come up with, His decree
of Jesus as King will still stand.

The same is true for us today.  No matter how determined we are to rebel
against Jesus, our rejection of His authority does not diminish that
authority.  He is far too powerful for
any of us to overthrow, and behind Him stands the Father, who is more powerful
still.  People in this country can live
their lives however they want.  They can
make whatever laws they want, declaring evil good and good evil.  Their rebellion is just as doomed as the
rebellion of the chief priests against Jesus. 
God is still watching all of this from His throne in heaven, and He is
laughing them to scorn too.

In these things, we need to remember **THE LORD’S DECREE.** Consider
Psalm 2:7-9.  This is important for two
reasons.  First, it’s a significant piece
of internal evidence for the authenticity of Scripture.  If we step back and think about it, Jesus’
claim to be the Son of God is a pretty extravagant claim.  Normally, when people say such things, we
lock them up in asylums.  Why do we
believe Jesus?  Psalm 2 is part of the
reason.  It’s easier to accept Jesus as
the Son of God because Psalm 2 predicts that there would be One who would be
the Son of God.

Second, this text emphasizes the authority of Jesus.  God made Him King over everybody,
period.  The other day, Alfredo was
telling me about somebody he knows who insists that the Bible is nothing more
than a history book, that he doesn’t feel compelled to follow it.  It is certainly true that the Bible contains
a great deal of history, but the New Testament also describes itself as the law
of Christ, and if we reject that law, we also reject the Lawgiver.  We reject the One who has been given
authority to rule by God Himself.  That
is the worst decision we can possibly make.

The psalm concludes, though, with **A WARNING.**  Look at Psalm
2:10-12.  There are many things that we can
learn from the Scripture, but one of its primary lessons is that rebellion
against God’s authority never ends well. 
Adam and Eve, the Israelites in the wilderness, the nations of Israel
and Judah, and the chief priests and elders all tried to reject God.  The Jewish leaders even nailed His Anointed
to a cross and killed Him.  In the end,
without fail, all of them perished.

Today, 2000 years later, we face the same choice.  Are we going to do homage to the Son, or are
we going to try to reject Him too?  As we
saw earlier, God will unfailingly reward those who obey Him.  However, He will also unfailingly punish
those who disobey.

Before the Lord’s Supper, then, let’s remember that this is
the *Lord’s* Supper that we are
celebrating.  Jesus died to serve us,
yes, but He also rose to rule over us. 
If we submit to Him, we will know the blessings of His reign, but if we
refuse to serve, He will destroy us as He has destroyed all the other rebels.  Let’s reflect, then, on our submission to His
authority as we partake.

**Psalm 3**

The third psalm in our series this morning, Psalm 3, doesn’t
get as much play as the first two. 
However, for reasons we’re going to explore, it’s every bit as worthy of
our study.  It begins by describing the
psalmist’s problems with **ADVERSARIES.** Look at Psalm 3:1-2.  If the ascription to the psalm is correct,
David wrote this as he was fleeing from Absalom when Absalom rebelled against
him.  At that point in time, David certainly
had enemies to spare!  His situation
looks so bad, in fact, that some of them are claiming he’s even beyond God’s
power to help.

Hopefully, none of us feel beset by enemies to that extent,
but it may well be that we feel similarly oppressed by other problems.  If that’s the case, we need to learn from
David’s example to be as up-front about our problems as he is.  This is often a challenge for us.  From somewhere, we’ve gotten the idea that
being a child of God means that we’re supposed to be happy happy happy all the
time, to the point where, no matter how we’re doing, if someone asks us how
things are going, we paste on a smile and say, “Fine.”

The weird thing is that none of the people of faith in
Scripture actually acted like that.  The
psalms are filled with outpourings of grief and despair.  Paul spends the whole book of 2 Corinthians
talking about his sorrow and suffering in the service of Christ.  Even Jesus Himself revealed in the garden of
Gethsemane that His soul was distressed to the point of death.  Do we think that if we keep a stiff upper
lip, it will make us holier than Jesus? 
Just because He died for our sins doesn’t mean that our lives are
perfect, and we don’t help anybody by pretending otherwise.

Next, though, David focuses on **THE LORD’S CHARACTER.** Read
with me from Psalm 3:3-6.  Here, I don’t
think David is describing what he sees God doing.  I think he’s describing what God has always
done.  His enemies are insisting that
even God can’t help him, but David looks back at God’s past works and sees that
God clearly can.

This, friends, is where God should truly be found in the
life of the Christian.  Maybe what’s
behind the fake-smile version of Christianity is the belief that we should act
as though God has already solved our problems. 
Once again, I don’t know where we’re getting that, but we aren’t getting
that from the Bible.  Exodus 33 tells us
that God spoke to Moses as a man speaks to his friend.  Clearly, Moses was close to God, yet he put
up with 40 years’ worth of problems in the wilderness and died before he ever
set foot in the land of Canaan.

Instead, true faith is revealed not in the claim that God
has solved our problems, but in the conviction that He will.  Maybe it won’t happen quickly, maybe it won’t
even happen in this lifetime, but sooner or later, God will deliver His people.  Look at David’s reaction here.  After reflecting on God’s goodness, he
doesn’t come out of it saying, “I’m not going to be unhappy.”  Instead, he says, “I’m not going to be
afraid.”  Unhappiness is about the
present, but fear is about the future, and David knew that somehow or other,
God was going to work it all out.

The psalm concludes with **DAVID’S PRAYER** for deliverance. 
Here, consider Psalm 3:7-8.  Even
though David is no longer afraid, he still appeals to God for help.  In fact, his prayer is actually an outgrowth
of his confidence.  If David didn’t trust
God, why ask Him for deliverance?

In the same way, friends, our faith in God should reveal
itself in our prayers.  Although
obviously I can’t know for certain, I get the impression that some Christians
think that private prayer should be like the public prayers we offer in the
assembly.  They should be formal, they
should be structured, they should be eloquent, and they should be relatively
dispassionate, like we’re making a speech to God.  My private prayers, though, are never like
that at all.  They’re short, they’re
spur-of-the-moment, they’re not particularly well thought-out, and their
emotional content is often more important than their intellectual content.  If that’s what suits our needs, I’m confident
that it’s what God wants to hear.  If we
call upon Him as Father, our prayers should be as simple, direct, and unfeigned
as when our children come to us.
