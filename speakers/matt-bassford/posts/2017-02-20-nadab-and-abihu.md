In the Lord’s church, we make a big deal out of
authority.  We talk a lot about being
careful to follow the pattern of the first-century church in our work and
worship together.  There are a number of
different reasons for this approach.  One
of the first is our love for God.  We
want to please Him, so rather than doing our own thing, we do the things we
know He likes.

However, we are equally motivated by our reverence for
God.  Even though God is good, He is not
safe.  The Bible is filled with stories
about both His kindness toward the righteous and His wrath toward the
wicked.  If we want to be properly
respectful of God’s power and authority, we have to pay attention to what all
of these stories reveal about Him.  With
this in mind, let’s consider a grim tale from the Old Testament, the story of
Nadab and Abihu.



**The Brothers’ Death**

Let’s begin our study of this story this morning by looking
at the events leading up to the brothers’ death.  That story itself is found in a few verses in
Leviticus 10, but I think that to properly appreciate it, we have to understand
it in the context of the preceding two chapters.

In those chapters, we see something very different.  We see everything being done **ACCORDING TO THE COMMANDMENT.** In context, what’s going on here is that
Aaron and his sons are being ordained as priests according to a lengthy process
that God has spelled out.  I want to read
a string of passages that shows how Aaron and his sons obey:  Leviticus 8:4, 8:9, 8:13, 8:17, 8:21, 8:29,
8:36, 9:5, 9:10, 9:16, and 9:21. 
Together, these passages tell us that they assembled the people
according to the commandment, clothed Aaron according to the commandment,
clothed his sons according to the commandment, offered a sin offering according
to the commandment, offered a burnt offering according to the commandment,
offered the ram of ordination according to the commandment, waited for a week
in front of the tent of meeting according to the commandment, brought more
offerings according to the commandment, offered a second sin offering according
to the commandment, offered a second burnt offering according to the
commandment, and offered a wave offering according to the commandment.

Brethren, that’s tedious to say.  Just imagine how much more tedious it would
have been to do!  All told, this process
took more than a week, and there are all kinds of sub-steps that we didn’t even
talk about.  However, Aaron and his sons
carried out all of those steps faithfully. 
God had commanded them to do it that way, and God was holy, so they were
going to do it right.

In the closing section of Leviticus 9, we see **THE RESULT OF OBEDIENCE.** It appears in Leviticus 9:22-24.  Because Moses, Aaron, and Aaron’s sons have
gone through this complicated ritual correctly, God reveals His glory to the
assembled people of Israel, and He demonstrates His approval of all that has
happened by sending fire from before Him to consume the sacrifices that Aaron
and his sons have left on the altar.

When I read this story, one of the questions that comes to
my mind is why God did it this way.  Why
have this whole process that Moses and Aaron have to go through?  Why can’t Moses just shake Aaron’s hand and
say, “Congratulations!  You’re in!”  I can come up with several possible answers
to that question.  Maybe God wanted to
demonstrate His greatness.  Maybe He
wanted to reveal His holiness and the difficulty of approaching Him.  Ultimately, though, the why doesn’t
matter.  If God says “Do it,” and you
want to honor God, then you do it.

We live 3500 years after this time, but the same principle
holds true.  There are commandments that
make a whole lot of sense to me, but there are others that I don’t understand
as well.  However, whether we are allowed
to understand the why or not, that doesn’t change our responsibilities toward
the what.  If we honor God, we will do
what He says, and only what He says. 
After everything we do in His name, we must be able to say, as Moses and
Aaron could, that it was done according to the commandment.  Then, and only then, will God approve our
actions.

Sadly, however, Nadab and Abihu depart from this pattern by
offering **UNAUTHORIZED FIRE.** This incident is reported in Leviticus
10:1.  Especially in the context of the
last two chapters, this text comes as a shock. 
Over and over again, we’ve seen that some religious action was done
according to the commandment of the Lord. 
By my count, there are 11 variations of that phrase in the preceding
text.  Here, though, that pattern comes
to an abrupt halt.  The fire that Nadab
and Abihu are offering is not fire that the Lord has commanded.  There’s a key distinction here.  It’s not that the Lord said, “Don’t offer
this fire.”  Instead, it’s fire that the
Lord did not affirmatively command them to offer.

Because this is not fire that the Lord commanded, there’s an
adjective that Moses attaches to it. 
Most translations call this “strange fire”, and if you go to a Hebrew
interlinear and look it up, that’s what you’ll find.  However, the ESV took a more interpretive
tack, which is why I’m using the ESV this morning.  Rather than calling it “strange fire”, the
ESV calls it “unauthorized fire”.

I think that translation really gets the point across.  After all, this isn’t fire that is strange in
its appearance or nature.  It looks like
fire in a firepan normally does. 
Instead, what makes it strange is that it is foreign to God’s
commandments.  Anything that is foreign
to God’s commandments, whether fire or anything else, is unauthorized, and that
is no less true today than it was then.

We clearly see how God feels about this in **HIS RESPONSE.** It appears in Leviticus 10:2-3.  Again, this is an account that we should read
in parallel with Leviticus 8-9.  Earlier,
Moses and Aaron did according to the commandments of the Lord, so fire came
down from heaven and consumed the sacrifices they offered.  Here, Nadab and Abihu did not do according to
the commandments of the Lord, so fire came down from heaven and consumed
them.  The care with which Moses and
Aaron had obeyed wasn’t pointless fussiness. 
Instead, it made the difference between life and death.

This is a familiar story to many of us here, but we must
appreciate how shocking it is.  After
all, Nadab and Abihu weren’t worshiping idols. 
They were trying to worship God, and there’s no evidence that they were
being insincere about it.  After more
than a week of doing everything exactly right, they make one little slip-up,
and God incinerates them for it.  How
unfair is that???

However, we must look at this not though our eyes, but
through God’s eyes.  We might see an
understandable mistake, but God sees dishonor and a failure to treat Him as
holy.  Created beings do not get to
decide how to worship their Creator. 
Instead, it is the Creator who instructs His creations.  Even now, we do not dare presume to sit in
judgment on the commandments of the Lord and decide that we like our way
better.  Only if we obey do we honor Him
and live.

**The Aftermath**

In the next part of our study, I want us to consider the
aftermath of Nadab and Abihu’s foolish action. 
It begins with a story that emphasizes **THE IMPORTANCE OF HOLINESS.** Let’s
continue reading in Leviticus 10:4-9.  In
what must have been a spectacularly awful moment, the two brothers were burned
up for their sin, but after God was through with them, their charred,
smoldering bodies were still there in front of the altar.  Two of Moses and Aaron’s cousins have to come
in and carry them away.

However, even after this gruesome spectacle, Moses tells
Aaron and his two surviving sons that they are not to mourn their dead
relatives.  They can’t even leave the
area around the tent of meeting.  If they
do either one of those things, God is going to kill them too.  Their consecration as priests has already
been finished, and they cannot defile themselves to mourn even when they must
have felt a nearly unbearable desire to mourn. 
They must preserve their holiness.

Today, every Christian here has been consecrated as a priest
before God.  We are His holy nation, and
we owe our holiness not to the blood of bulls and goats, but to the precious
blood of Christ.  We constantly stand
before God, and we must preserve our holiness, no matter the provocation.  In Christ, our sins are forgivable, but that
does not ever mean that sin is acceptable. 
The world will excuse nearly any sin, but God expects us to do His will
no matter what.

Next, we encounter an interesting passage about **ALCOHOL AND DISCERNMENT.** Consider Leviticus 10:8-11.  At first glance, this text appears to have
nothing to do with what came before it and indeed what comes after it, but in
reality, it adds an important dimension to our understanding of the story.  There’s no reason for God to warn Aaron about
wine and strong drink in the middle of this tragedy unless wine and strong
drink had something to do with creating the tragedy.  The text doesn’t say so outright, but this
strongly implies that Nadab and Abihu offered the unauthorized fire because
they had been drinking, and the alcohol interfered with their judgment.

I believe there’s a powerful lesson for us here.  There are many passages in the New Testament
that condemn drunkenness, but I’m not aware of any similar passage that
outright says, “Thou shalt not drink alcohol.” 
However, I think texts like this one give more than adequate reason for
Christians in our time and place never to go near the stuff.

We all know that you don’t have to have a BAC of .08% before
alcohol starts affecting your judgment.  To
put things another way, alcohol consumption interferes with our ability to
distinguish between the holy and the common. 
When we drink, we aren’t as good at discerning what God wants us to do.  Brethren, I have plenty of trouble with
discerning and doing what’s right even when I’m stone-cold sober.  I shudder to contemplate the field day the
devil would have with me after I’d had a few! 
Unless we’re dominating in our striving against sin, drinking is a risk
we don’t dare take.  Otherwise, we might
end up like Nadab and Abihu did.

Now, it’s certainly true that everything we’ve studied so
far is in the Old Testament, and there’s this pop-religious concept floating
around that the Old-Testament God was a God of anger and wrath, but the New-Testament
God is a God of mercy and love.  If we
follow that line of thinking, we might imagine that we can get away with a lot
more now because God is nicer.

However, before we go too far in that direction, we need to
consider what is actually **THE NEW-TESTAMENT
PERSPECTIVE**.  Look, for instance, at
Hebrews 12:25-29.  The Israelites served
God under a fleshly covenant, and we serve Him under a spiritual covenant.  However, that doesn’t make the penalties for
disobedience less severe.  It makes them
more severe.  If God did things like that
to people who disregarded the commandments of Moses, what will He do to those
who disregard the commandments of Jesus? 
God has not changed, not at all.

The final two verses of this reading fit the story of Nadab
and Abihu so well that I can’t help but wonder if the Hebrews writer was
thinking of it as he was writing. 
Because the God we serve is a holy God who demands that we treat Him as
holy, we must offer to Him the worship that we know He will accept.  We must serve Him according to the pattern He
has laid down.  We must always approach
His holy throne in a spirit of reverence and awe.  Why? 
Because as Nadab and Abihu learned to their everlasting sorrow, our God
is a consuming fire.

**Acceptable Worship**

As we’ve seen, careful adherence to the commandments of the
Lord is a New-Testament principle as much as it is an Old-Testament
principle.  Let’s apply what we’ve
learned to determine what acceptable worship looks like in the context of the
Lord’s Supper.  The first thing that we
must determine about the Lord’s Supper is **THE
FREQUENCY** with which we should observe it. 
The most helpful passage here is Acts 20:7.  It’s not surprising that the observance of
the Lord’s Supper is linked Scripturally with the first day of the week.  Jesus rose from the dead on the first day of
the week.  The Jews observed Pentecost on
the first day of the week, so that’s when the first gospel sermon was preached
too.  Paul urged the Corinthian church in
1 Corinthians 16 to take up a collection for the needy saints on the first day
of the week.  No other day was as
significant to early Christians as that day, to the point where John refers to
it in Revelation as “the Lord’s day”. 
This is the day when we would expect Christians to meet to partake of
the Lord’s Supper, and that’s exactly what we see in Acts 20.  When we imitate their example, we are
following God’s pattern.

At this point, some enterprising soul might say, “But what
about Saturday?”  It’s true that there is
no commandment in Scripture saying, “Thou shalt not partake of the Lord’s
Supper on Saturday.”  However, more to
the point, there is no commandment of the Lord that permits us to celebrate the
Lord’s Supper on Saturday, and if we do anyway, we are walking in the footsteps
of Nadab and Abihu.  What God commands,
we do.  Nothing else.  It’s that simple.

Similarly, we obey the commandment of the Lord when it comes
to **THE BREAD AND CUP** in which we
share.  Paul explains in 1 Corinthians
11:23-26.  Here, we see that the commandment
comes straight from the Lord.  Jesus told
us what to do:  have a memorial meal to
remember Him.  He also told us how to do
it:  by using bread and the cup, which we
know from Luke’s account must contain the fruit of the vine.

This is different from, for instance, the command to
sing.  There, God provides the
what—teaching and admonishing one another in psalms and hymns and spiritual
songs—but He doesn’t tell us how. 
Anything that involves Christians singing to one another will do.  The particular how we’ve chosen is four-part
harmony directed by a song leader.  We
are obeying the commandment.

However, we would not be obeying the commandment if we
substituted a different means for the one that God has provided.  For instance, there are brethren out there
who have turned the Lord’s Supper into a common meal.  They put on a whole spread, and it’s like a
standard potluck, except there’s a jug of grape juice and a basket of crackers
in the middle of the table someplace. 
Never mind that Paul tells us we have houses to eat and drink in; this
is how they want to do it.

In response, I can only say that God would call such a
gathering an unauthorized Lord’s Supper just as surely as He called Nadab and
Abihu’s incense unauthorized fire.  It is
not the observance that the Lord has commanded, so it cannot please Him.

Finally, we learn that there is an internal element to the
Lord’s Supper too.  As we eat, we must **DISCERN THE BODY.** Look at 1 Corinthians 11:27-29.  This is an important reminder that obedience
to the Lord’s commandments isn’t about sleepwalking our way through an outward
ritual.  Even in the Old Testament, God
was always concerned about the heart of His worshipers, and that concern
continues today.

When we partake of the Lord’s Supper, it’s not enough for us
to be eating the right food and drinking the right beverage on the right day of
the week.  Instead, our thoughts must
also be on the Lord Jesus and on ourselves, as we reflect on the body He gave
for us and examine our lives to determine whether we have been faithful to
Him.  If we don’t, we are bringing
judgment on ourselves no less than Nadab and Abihu brought judgment on
themselves.

In fact, it is likely that once we allow our inward
commitment to collapse, it won’t be long before the outward form of obedience
decays too.  We treat God as holy by the
way we behave, but our reverence for Him begins in the heart.  If we no longer fear and honor Him, our
hypocritical observance will inevitably turn into open disobedience, with
equally inevitable consequences.

Before the Lord’s Supper, then, let’s remember that we are
approaching God together, just as Nadab and Abihu once approached God.  He is a holy God, and one way or another, His
holiness will be revealed in us, whether by our obedience or by our
punishment.  The meal in which we are
about to share is a solemn thing.  Let’s
eat and drink according to the commandment we have been given, so that both
with our actions and our thoughts, our God is pleased.
