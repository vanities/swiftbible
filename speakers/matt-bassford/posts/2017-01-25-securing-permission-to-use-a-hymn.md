Over the past couple of weeks, I’ve gotten a couple of
different requests for a blog post on how to get permission to use a hymn.  I’m hardly an expert here; I’ve never
compiled a hymnal nor even a supplement, so 
I simply haven’t had to do this very much.  However, I have been on the other end of the
permission process a fair amount, and I did go to law school once upon a
time.  Consider all this accordingly.



For those who are interested in compiling a supplement or
adding to a slide deck, though, seeking permission beforehand is
important.  This is true even for
unfamiliar hymns that don’t include a copyright notice or the names of the
author or composer.  Most likely, those
hymns did not spring, Athena-like, fully formed from the head of Zeus.  Instead, they were probably written by
somebody whose name and copyright notice were removed by a helpful
hymn-distributor.

This has certainly happened to me.  A few years back, I attended a singing at
which one of my hymns was projected on the screen sans any identifying
language.  After the singing ended, I was
talking to the organizer, who apologized for not including any of my work.  “Oh, but you did,” I told him.  It turned out that he had seen the hymn,
still without identification, at yet another singing and asked for a copy.

Believe me; I completely sympathize with those who love to "sing a new song" before the Lord!  This brother is a good man who acted in good faith from a good heart (indeed, once he figured out what had happened, he apologized profusely).  However, it still concerns me that things like this happen.

My concern about copyright has nothing to do with
money.  I’ve never seen a dime as a
result of 20 years of hymn writing, and I never expect to (though if some congregation
wanted to hire me full-time to produce two hymns a week—almost certainly two
bad hymns a week—I’d be OK with that). 
Instead, I’m primarily interested in preserving the integrity of my
work.  I estimate that to take a hymn
from idea to final, publishable form takes my composers and me about 100
hours.  That’s a direct cost.  It doesn’t take into account all the time and
effort I sink into hymns that prove not to be good enough.

After all that time, everything in the hymn is exactly as I
want it to be.  I’ve stared at the text
for hours.  I’ve run it through several
layers of editors.  I’ve prayed about the
hymn, sometimes praying for particular words in the hymn.  My goal is for the lyrics to sound
effortless, but they are anything but.

As a result, my heart shrivels at the thought of some
supplement editor studying the hymn for two minutes, concluding that they know
Just How to improve it, and proceeding to implement their improvements in
another 15 minutes.  From my perspective,
that’s about like using sandpaper to scrub down somebody else’s ’65
Mustang.  I copyright my hymns in the
hope that it will deter would-be improvers, or at least that they will ask me
for permission before improving.  I know
plenty of other hymnists who feel exactly the same way.

As a result, it’s important for compilers to get permission
before compiling.  These days, most
people should start by getting a CCLI license and checking to see if the
desired hymn is in the CCLI catalog (you can determine this quite easily by going
to [ccli.org](http://ccli.org/)).  The well-known
contemporary praise songs are going to be in there, as well as older hymns, such
as “Great Is Thy Faithfulness”, that happened to be written on this side of the
great copyright divide (as a general rule, hymns published before 1923 are in
the public domain, and hymns published 1923 or thereafter are under
copyright).  Getting a license and paying
the yearly fee spares you the pain of tracking down all those individual
copyright holders and making arrangements with each one of them.

However, most songwriters among the churches of Christ don’t
use CCLI (big names like Keith Lancaster and Ken Young are exceptions
here).  For myself, I have two primary reasons
for abstaining.  The first is that I
suspect that signing on with CCLI would primarily enrich CCLI and not me, and what's the point in letting them charge people money so I can get my measly twenty-five cents a year in royalties?

Second, CCLI talks a good game about protecting songs from
unauthorized alteration in its ["For Songwriters" FAQ](https://us.ccli.com/what-we-provide/faq/#songwriters, but in the), but in the“What We Provide” section of their site, they claim to offer “broad coverage for. . . instrumental/vocal arrangements”.  This is murky, and my suspicion is that
it would empower licensees to do whatever they want with hymns and feel like
they had permission for doing so (non-lawyers tend not to pay much attention to
asterisks and fine print).  As a result,
most hymnists in the brotherhood go elsewhere for help with distributing our hymns.

In my case, the most prominent elsewhere is [songsofthechurch.org](http://songsofthechurch.org/).  This is a
moderately sized site that hosts a number of hymns written by brethren.  They charge a small fee (now typically about
$2.50 per hymn) for downloading a clean copy of a hymn, PowerPoint slides, and
the right to use all of the above.  No
writers see any of that money.  It goes
to pay for the site hosting, and any excess goes to the adoption-funding
nonprofit Sacred Selections.  If I were
putting together a supplement, I would check songsofthechurch second.

Finally, if you want permission to use a hymn that doesn’t
show up in either of those places, I would ask around online.  Many hymnists (I among them) maintain a
significant Internet presence, and generally, we’re a pretty tightly knit
bunch.  Once you find one of us, you have
probably found all of us.  If you’re
looking for permission to use somebody else’s hymn, I’ll happily point you in
the right direction.  If you want
permission to use one of my hymns, I will happily give it to you *gratis*, provided that you don’t intend
to go all Ellis Crum on it.  I might send
you to the relevant composer for a clean copy, though.

All this is kind of a pain. 
However, it’s a respectful pain. 
It’s respectful first of all to hymnists.  I know I’m not a great writer, nor will I
ever be one, but my work is my work.  If
I had wanted to give somebody else the final word on revising it, I would have
done so.  Copyrighting my hymns protects
my expression, and that’s important to me.

Second, it’s respectful to the law.  It’s illegal to violate copyright
protections, so honoring them is rendering unto Caesar what is Caesar’s.  Sure, it would be easier if we could just
assemble a supplement with half an hour spent at the photocopier, but being
law-abiding is often more difficult. 
When we do the difficult thing because it is right, God is pleased.
