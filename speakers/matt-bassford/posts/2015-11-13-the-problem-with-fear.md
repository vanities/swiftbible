I’ve been following the recent racial unrest at the
University of Missouri with more interest than most.  I grew up in Columbia and went to college at
Mizzou.  I still have many friends in
town and on campus, and the university will always hold a special place in my
heart.  It’s been hard to watch, then, as
people associated with an institution I love have behaved badly and become the
focus of criticism from left, right, and center.  Nor has the controversy brought out, in
Lincoln’s words, “The better angels of our nature.”  Instead, the various posters in comments
sections and on message boards are enough to make me despair of the human race.

This has been all the more difficult because in my
estimation, at least, both sides have a point. 
I believe that racism continues to be a serious problem in the United
States today, and I have it on good authority that the situation at Mizzou is
no different.  At the same time, though,
I sympathize with those who are baffled when a system president is pressured
into resignation essentially for not condemning racism loudly enough.  I was saddened by the hostility that the
protesters showed earlier week toward journalists who were simply trying to do
their jobs, not least for the protesters. 
It would be a shame if their message were dismissed because of bad
optics.

If everybody has a point, then why have events collapsed
into an adversarial stalemate that is giving opponents of the university all
the ammunition they will ever need?



I think the answer boils down to fear.  So far as I can tell, everybody on both sides
has behaved as they have because they were afraid and felt threatened.  The protestors felt threatened by what they
perceived to be a racially hostile climate on campus.  Former MU President Tim Wolfe, in what was
probably the defining moment of his presidency, didn’t get out of his car and
talk to protesters during the MU Homecoming parade.  I’d guess he was taken off guard and didn’t
know what to do.  He was afraid.

The MU Board of Curators accepted Wolfe’s resignation and
forced Bowen Loftin out because they feared that the situation would spiral out
of control even more than it had.  The protesters
(along with communications professor Melissa Click) drove photographers away
from the Concerned Student 1950 campsite because they were afraid of the
hostile reporting that increased media scrutiny might bring.  For that matter, the conservatives howling
for Click’s head are driven by fear of an academic-led liberal totalitarianism
in which their opinions will no longer be tolerated.

Ironically, all of
the people acting out of fear have mostly succeeded only in producing the thing
they feared.  Mizzou is more racially
polarized now than it was six months ago. 
The rate of racist incidents has increased dramatically.  Wolfe is now out of a job.  Despite the curators’ housecleaning, the
controversy at Mizzou continues unabated. 
The hostility to reporters brought CS 1950 more unwelcome media
attention than they had ever dreamed possible. 
It, rather than their agenda, will be what people remember about their
protest.  Finally, the conservative
backlash, both on campus and online (has it really become acceptable again to
proclaim oneself a racist???), will doubtless only increase the protestors’ original
sense of being under threat, quite possibly leading to more extreme behavior.

The problem with fear is that when we are afraid, we usually
project a fear-based caricature on those who disagree with us.  They’re ordinary, complicated people who are
probably a lot like us, but instead, we see them and begin to treat them like
two-dimensional villains straight from central casting.  In doing so, our hostility molds them into the
very thing we fear.  They weren’t before,
but they are now, thanks to us.

The Click vignette illustrates this pattern perfectly.  I’ve heard that she’s a good communications
professor, well-liked by her students. 
She doesn’t go around assaulting reporters or encouraging others to
assault them.  On one Monday, though, she
saw a reporter as a threat and did both. 
Now, she has been converted to a symbol of foamy-mouthed liberal
intolerance by those who are afraid of exactly that.  If she still has her job a year from now, I
will be very much surprised.  She, in
turn, will become a martyr to those who agree with her, a symbol of the threatening
intolerance of the racist right.

It is commonly observed that the Bible repeats variations on
“Do not be troubled” and “Do not be afraid” hundreds of times.  Normally, this is construed as a call to the
faithful to be bold:  “Don’t give
up!  Stand strong!”

However, I think it is also (particularly in the New
Testament) a call to the boldness of leaving ourselves open and
defenseless.  We’ve seen what happens
when we project our fears on others. 
Something very different happens when we project our belief in the
goodwill of others, our hopes for a better future, and above all, our
conviction that the other can love and is worthy of love.

This isn’t easy. 
“Love your enemies,” (read:  “Love
those whom you perceive as your enemies.”) has never been the most popular
commandment in Scripture.  Even when
embraced, it’s hard to practice.  Enemies
are quick to notice slights and injuries; they’re prone to explain away acts of
service and kindness.  Hating our enemies
is satisfying, but loving our enemies is often frustrating.

However, short of exterminating our enemies, only love
offers a resolution to conflict.  Only
love can make, if not allies, at least friends. 
My advice, then, to anybody who is upset by any part of the Mizzou
uproar, is this:  pick the people you
least agree with, and find something worthy of love in them.  Appreciate the protesters’ yearning to be
treated the same as anybody else. 
Remember Wolfe for his sincere concern for the MU system, not for his
refusal to get out of a car.  Acknowledge
that Click did what she did for people she cared about.  If they have made mistakes, well, haven’t we all?  I myself often have been fearful and stupid
too.

Admittedly, it’s tough to leave behind the
self-righteousness that comes from believing that “we” are the good guys and
“they” are the bad guys.  It’s satisfying
to be part of a group that reaffirms our own prejudices while mocking those who
do not share them.  However, if we’re
willing instead to search for something better in those who disagree with us,
what we ultimately will find is something better in ourselves.
