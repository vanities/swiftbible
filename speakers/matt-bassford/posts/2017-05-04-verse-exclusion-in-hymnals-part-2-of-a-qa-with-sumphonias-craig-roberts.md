*A couple weeks back, I posted [the first half](http://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2017/04/verse-inclusion-in-hymnals-q-with.html) of an e-mail discussion with Craig Roberts, one of the editors of the hymnal* Psalms, Hymns, and Spiritual Songs*, about the editors' decision to include more verses of some hymns in* PHASS *than is typical.  This is the second half of our discussion, and it focuses on the editors' decision to remove some verses that are commonly included.*

**MB:  However, even if the general policy of the *PHASS* editors was to include, there are
obvious exceptions to the rule in the hymnal. 
Why do there need to be such exceptions?**

CR:  To clarify, the
general policy was not to include more stanzas. 
It was to include more stanzas of superior quality.

The editorial process was based
more on criteria and less on rules + exceptions.  Each stanza was handled like a journal
manuscript.  If it met the criteria, it
was published.  If it did not meet the
criteria, it was “released to the authors.”

**MB:  How can doctrinal considerations
affect the decision to include or omit a verse?**

CR:  Surely we agree
that doctrinal accuracy is the number one consideration when writing, singing,
or publishing a hymn.  Our hymns are to
praise God.  They are to teach.  As a result, they cannot contain falsehood.

We need to remember that hymns are written by authors whose
doctrinal positions often differ from those of the readership.  And some of their stanzas would be recognized
as false doctrine.  How should these
stanzas be handled in the publication of a hymnal?  Obviously, they must be omitted or published
in revised form.

A case in point is the 2nd stanza of “To God Be
the Glory,” which originally read,

“The vilest offender who truly
believes,

That moment from Jesus a pardon receives.”

This stanza was omitted in *Praise for the Lord* (Wiegand, 1992), which was a good
decision.  In other hymnals, however, the
stanza was revised and published.  By the
time PHASS was in production, the revised stanza had become widely circulated and
even popular among our brethren. 
Therefore, PHASS published the revision instead of omitting the original.

Sometimes a stanza doesn’t necessarily express an erroneous
view, but it sort of sounds like it does, at least to a significant number of
our readership.  This occurs when a
stanza addresses a controversial doctrine. 
A stanza may describe the Holy Spirit with language that resembles charismatic
leanings.  It may contain overtones of
Calvinism or millennialism.  In these and
similar cases, publishing stanzas in PHASS would have done more harm than good.  So they were generally omitted, although some
undoubtedly slipped through the filter.

**MB:  Can concerns about literary quality lead to
the omission of a verse?  If so, how do
you balance these concerns against respect for the intent of an author?**

CR:  It is not enough
for a stanza to be scriptural.  It needs
poetic quality.  If a stanza lacks poetic
quality, it can annoy our brethren and even be parodied, regardless of its
scriptural accuracy.  To quote the late
L.A. Mott, “many of the songs we sing are offensive,” and “not because they are
unscriptural.”

The hymns we sing are supposed to be pretty, with or without
the music.  To be effective, they need to
be written in a way that attracts buy-in. 
To please the Lord, they should offer our best sacrifices.

It is commonplace for editors to negotiate with authors
regarding the language used in manuscripts. 
In PHASS, several of these negotiations occurred with authors who were
still living.  An example of this
occurred with “Christ Is Alive” (253 in PHASS), as editors had email and phone
conversations with Brian Wren.  (See the
author’s comment following the copyright notice of that hymn.)

In cases where the author is no longer living, the editors should
seek the balance you mention—strive to capture the original intent of that
author while “fixing” the stanza.  It
would have been nice to have edited the first stanza of “Lord, We Come before
Thee Now.”  It was not modified in PHASS,
as it is well known, but it could have been edited to represent the author
better and improve the quality of the publication.  The first stanza uses the term “suit” to
denote “plea.”  With the majority of our
current readership, “suit” connotes clothing. 
So to them, the stanza sounds like “We’re here to worship You Lord;
please don’t criticize our sport coats.” 
This is not the intent of the author. 
In other old hymns, “suit” has been changed to “plea.”  See for example, John Newton’s “Come My Soul,
Your Suit Prepare.”

**MB:  Isn’t literary quality subjective?  If so, who decides if a hymn or stanza has
adequate literary quality?  Why would
they be qualified to make these decisions?**

In edited publications, the best decisions occur by
consensus of trained editors who understand the readership and think
independently.  From what I can tell,
though perhaps I am in too close for objectivity, the decision editors of PHASS
fit the description of trained editors. 
They represented 100 years of combined experience in critiquing the
literary quality of English hymns.  That’s
a pretty good amount of expertise for decision level editing on every stanza.  Also, they fit the description of being independent,
to the point of head-butting at times.  Even
with agreeable people, independent dialogue is 95% pleasant and 5% unpleasant,
but the unpleasantness is necessary to reduce groupthink and prevent any single
editor from successfully imposing his/her exclusive taste on the
publication.  Also, these particular editors
came from diverse geographic backgrounds, which increases awareness of general tendencies
of many congregations (a.k.a. “the readership”) across a broad landscape.

**MB:  How were these decisions made?  Was there a set of criteria or a process?**

Regarding literary quality, decisions were made by
evaluating each stanza according to standard criteria taught in composition
classes and seen in successful hymns.  These
criteria include structure, content, focus, clarity, redundancy, word choice, and
other traits.  Each stanza was also checked
for consistency of rhythm, rhyme, imagery, and meter.  Criteria of literary quality can be found in
the excellent writings of New English Renaissance authors, such as Ruth Duck,
Timothy Dudley-Smith, and Brian Wren.

There are pragmatic reasons for insisting on literary
quality, which is best explained from the negative perspective.  When a stanza uses inconsistent meter, it
causes the congregation to stutter then double-check the staff to make sure the
music is being sung correctly.  When a
stanza ends a phrased with a forced rhyme, it strikes a noisy gong or a
clanging cymbal.  When a stanza changes
imagery, it prevents word pictures from forming in the brain.  These and other negative traits interrupt the
thought process and therefore the worship, which is a mental activity.  As a friend once told me, “Anything that gets
in the way of the message” gets in the way of worship.

If a stanza violates known criteria of literary quality, the
hymn probably needed review before circulation or publication.  Should a stanza be omitted in this case, the
author is partly responsible.

**MB:  What about the Christians who say, “You left
my favorite verse/a verse of my favorite hymn out!”?**

CR:  This is no doubt
true and a comment that each of us could say. 
But in a publication like this, the group is more important than the
individual.  And “my favorite” stanza is
not necessarily “our favorite” stanza.

Since PHASS was published in 2012, the editors have received
many a “thank you” for what is not in the hymnal.  We have noticed that a stanza championed by
one person is often criticized by many people, and rightly so in most
cases.

When a stanza appeals to one or two people but annoys the
majority, publishing it causes polarization in a group.  It also reflects badly on the publication.

I would encourage people to critique the stanzas they regret
not seeing in PHASS or any other hymnal. 
Ask the tough questions.  Is the
stanza written well, or is it clunky?  Is
it redundant?  Does it present doctrine
with consensus wording, or does it touch hot buttons with questionable
language?
