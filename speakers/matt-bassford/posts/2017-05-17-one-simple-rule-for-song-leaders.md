I am, unabashedly, a hymn geek.  One of my life goals is to write a great hymn
(a goal that, to be sure, is impossible without the providential help of
God).  As a result, I spend a lot of time
thinking and writing about the intricacies of what makes hymns work.  In this, I have three groups in mind as my
primary audience.  The first is other
hymnists.  The second is those who have
influence on hymn repertoire, whether at the congregational or brotherhood
level.  Third, I write for other hymn
geeks, people who enjoy contemplating the subtleties of such things
just-because.

The members of all of these groups either have or should
have an appreciation for the nuances of what makes a sacred song useful in the
congregation.  Hymnists who don’t
understand their craft will only write effectively by accident.  Hymn-introducers who don’t understand what
they’re introducing are likely to waste a lot of time and effort on songs that
will prove to be congregational duds.



However, most Christians don’t have to operate at that
level.  You don’t have to be able to tell
a strong rhyme from a weak one to go to heaven! 
This is true not only of the ordinary worshipers in the pews, but even
of most song leaders.  In my experience,
most congregations only have one or two men who actively introduce new
songs.  Everybody else generally leads
from the repertoire they grew up singing while occasionally bringing in a newer
hymn that has already been introduced.

This is not a problem. 
In order to drive a car, you don’t have to know how to build an
engine.  However, I’m certainly aware
that ordinary song leaders can be intimidated when I start poking around under
the hood.  Really, though, everything I
say about hymns can be boiled down to one simple rule for song leaders.  It’s a rule that I believe every song leader
can and should follow.

It is this:  When you
are preparing a hymn list, figure out what you want to say first, then find a
hymn that says it.  Don’t just start
flipping through the hymnal.  Ask, “What
do I want the congregation to say to God and to itself at this point in the
service?”  Do I want an opening hymn that
expresses our joy at being able to come before the throne of God?  Do I want one that is more reverent and
dwells on God’s greatness and our insignificance?  And so on.

When the song leader is dealing with a theme that he means
to carry through multiple hymns (whether the theme of the sermon or a theme of
his own devising), he needs to consider what he wants to say with each one of
those hymns.  Leading three hymns with
the same message back-to-back-to-back is like repeating the same sentence three
times in a row.  Leading three hymns with
the same message back-to-back-to-back is like repeating the same sentence three
times in a row.  Leading three hymns with
the same message back-to-back-to-back is like repeating the same sentence three
times in a row.  It’s not good
communication.

Instead, in order for a themed service to be effective, the
song leader must figure out what aspects of the theme he wants to address.  A service about the death of Christ could
reasonably explore the events of His last few hours, the spiritual significance
of those events, and our mixed emotions at what took place.  Once he has those subtopics figured out, the
song leader can look for hymns that fit.

A song leader who prepares his song list in this way will
select and lead 99 percent good hymns. 
Asking the question, “What do I want to say?” shifts the focus to
content, and a hymn in the repertoire with good content is a good hymn.  He doesn’t have to worry about all the
technical discussions of hymn quality; he will naturally choose a familiar,
emotionally resonant hymn anyway.  Nobody
chooses to lead hymns he doesn’t know (at least at the level of minimal
comfortableness) and doesn’t like unless somebody else puts him up to it.

The barest of bare-bones outlines will help considerably
here.  In my experience, no matter who
the song leader is and no matter how much thought he’s put into the service,
the opening hymn, the Lord’s Supper hymn, and the invitation hymn will
generally be good.  Even looking for
content at the extremely generic level of “It should prepare our minds for the
Lord’s Supper,” will screen out hymns with nothing to say.

As a rule, bad hymns emerge in the 3-5 slots of the worship
service that aren’t labeled.  That’s when
the song leader turns to hymnal-flipping, and “It’s fun to sing,” “My daughter
likes it,” and “I remember singing this with Mama,” begin to emerge as justifications
for leading a particular song.  This
process does not necessarily lead to the selection of a subpar hymn (your
daughter may have a good hymn as her favorite), but it’s not guaranteed to
produce a good hymn either.

Writing hymns is enormously complicated, but leading them
doesn’t have to be.  Congregational
singing is fundamentally about communication, and as long as the song leader
has a message in mind for each hymn slot, he will choose hymns that communicate.  It’s when we aren’t thinking about saying
anything at all that we run the risk of saying nothing.
