As surely as the swallows return to Capistrano in the spring, I am guaranteed never to write or preach a holiday-related sermon.  Mother’s Day, Labor Day, Thanksgiving—I pass them all by.  This is not because I am opposed to holiday-themed sermons.  It is simply that I spend my days in a genial fog and forget.

However, there is one exception to this dismal pattern—New Year’s Day.  I remember it because the calendar forces me to.  I have to put a different year next to my name underneath the sermon title.  The first Sunday of each year, then, is certain to have me writing about New Year’s resolutions or something similar.

This year is no different.  However, rather than constructing a topical sermon about some worthy spiritual goal, I want us to consider a context of Scripture that calls us to globally re-evaluate ourselves.  Let’s look this morning, then at what Paul has to say in Romans 12-13 about renewing our minds.

**Our Bodies, Christ’s Body**
The first of three divisions that I see in the text concerns the subject of the body, both our own bodies and the body of Christ.  Within this, the first couple of verses address spiritual TRANSFORMATION.  Look at Romans 12:1-2.  In context, this passage marks the single most important thematic shift in the entire book of Romans.  To this point, the first 11 chapters of Romans have dealt with doctrinal matters.  Paul has been establishing the whys and wherefores for the gospel plan of salvation.  Now, though, he’s reached the so-what.  Given that God has done so much, here’s what we ought to do about it.  In fact, we can view these two verses as a summary of the rest of Romans.  In them, Paul tells us that we need to give two things to God:  our bodies and our minds.  Let’s start with our bodies first.  The phrase “living sacrifice” here is certainly a striking one, but I’m not sure it leads us in the right direction.  “Sacrifice” makes us think of bulls and goats and altars, but somehow, after we’ve been sacrificed, we go on living.

However, the Greek here can be just as legitimately translated as “offering”, which doesn’t have all of those sacrifice-y connotations.  Think instead of the gold and silver that the Israelites offered for the construction of the temple in the time of David.  Some of that gold and silver was fashioned into the holy vessels used in the temple service, and once those censers and basins and snuffers were consecrated, they couldn’t be put to common uses anymore.  You didn’t take a temple basin home to wash the baby in.

In the same way, friends, Paul is urging us to consecrate our bodies to God’s work.  We are to be holy vessels just like the utensils of the temple.  We’re not supposed to give those holy vessels over to immorality and wickedness any more than the Israelites were supposed to use the temple furnishings for the worship of idols.  Instead, our bodies are to belong to God, dedicated to His purposes and His glory.

As our bodies are to be holy, our minds are to be holy too.  Paul is writing to Christians here, yet he still urges us to be transformed by the renewing of those minds.  The Greek word here is metamorphoo, from which we get our English “metamorphosis”.  When a caterpillar makes a cocoon, inside that cocoon, it is renewed and transformed into a butterfly.  In the same way, friends, throughout our lives, we are to constantly renew our minds, so that we will be transformed into the people God wants us to be.  Once we become who we’re supposed to be, we can do what we’re supposed to do—the perfect will of God.

Next, Paul points out one of the main ways we carry out God’s will, by serving in THE BODY OF CHRIST.  Let’s read from Romans 12:3-8.  The apostle’s instruction here boils down to two main points:  figure out what you’re supposed to do, and then do it.  First of all, we need to have some self-awareness.  I’ve certainly known Christians who have the problem Paul warns against here.  They think they’re experts at everything and good at everything.  This can be a big problem, particularly when people who don’t know enough Bible to teach effectively hold themselves out as authorities on the Scripture.  Nobody’s good at everything, and we need to recognize both what we’re good at and what we aren’t.

However, even though nobody’s good at every aspect of serving the Lord, everybody’s good at something.  We are responsible for figuring out what our somethings are and then doing what we’re good at.  That way, when we’re all working together, our different strengths mean that everything gets done.  Not everybody’s good at serving, but some are.  Not everybody’s good at teaching, but some are.  Not everybody here has the financial resources to be a powerhouse giver, or the vision to be a good leader, or the compassion to be especially merciful, but at least one person here has every one of those gifts.  We need to figure out where we fit in God’s plan for this congregation, and then devote ourselves to that role.

Even outside of our particular gifts, though, we are all still responsible for LOVING ONE ANOTHER.  Paul explains our responsibilities here in Romans 12:9-10.  Every Christian is responsible for loving his brethren, and here’s what love looks like.  Love isn’t hypocritical.  We can’t be all nicey-nicey to our brother’s face and then turn around and badmouth him behind his back.  We can’t condone sin in our brother, but when we see good in him, we should encourage it and promote it.  We must regard one another with tender affection.  In 1 Thessalonians 2, Paul compares his affection for the Thessalonians to the affection of a nursing mother for her child.  That’s how mindful of one another we ought to be, and how gentle to one another we ought to be.  Finally, we ought to celebrate the service of others rather than our own service.  It’s not my role to go around talking about what a great preacher I am.  Instead, my speech should honor the other brethren here, for the thousand and one ways that they effectively serve God.

Just as we are all responsible for loving one another, we are also responsible for WORKING FOR THE LORD.  Consider Romans 12:11-13.  As I look over these two verses, there’s one idea that jumps out at me, the idea of steadfastness.  Being a Christian means keeping going.  It means that we don’t try serving the Lord for two weeks and then quit.  Instead, we find our role and stick to it, both when it’s easy and when it’s not.  Whenever life gets difficult, we don’t quit on God.  Instead, we cling more closely to Him.  Even in our prayers, we are diligent.  We don’t just bow our heads during services.  Instead, we spend our days, weeks, months, and years constantly bringing our thanksgiving and cares before our heavenly Father.

Paul also has quite a bit to say here about our attitude.  First, we’re supposed to be fervent.  God doesn’t appreciate lazy, apathetic Christians who are bored with it all.  Second, we are supposed to be joyful, not because of our earthly blessings, even though those may be numerous, but because of the hope we have of eternity with God.  Finally, we’re supposed to be hospitable.  It needs to be our practice, indeed, our habit, to invite people we don’t know into our homes so that we can get to know them better.  It doesn’t take any talent to be enthusiastic, joyful, or hospitable.  It just takes a sincere devotion to the Lord.

**Relationships with Others**
Next, Paul delves deeper into the subject of godliness in our relationships with others.  First, he encourages GODLY REACTIONS.  In this regard, consider Romans 12:14-15.  The contrast to worldly behavior here could not be greater.  Worldly people, when faced with those who persecute them, strike back.  They return a harsh word for a harsh word.  Likewise, the worldly react enviously toward the blessings of others.  When their co-worker gets the promotion and they don’t, they aren’t happy.  They’re mad that they got overlooked.  Similarly, the worldly are unmoved by the trials and suffering of those around them.  They say to themselves, “So what if they’re going through a rough spot?  It’s none of my concern!”

Christians, though, are to be the opposite of all these things.  When others oppose and oppress us, we don’t swear at them or pray for their destruction.  Instead, we bless them and pray for their souls.  Similarly, when we see others doing well, particularly when we see our brethren doing well, we’re happy for them.  If another brother gets a fine new house or a fine new car, great!  We feel their blessing like we would our own.  Finally, we’re not to be detached from the sorrows of others.  When we see them hurting, we’re supposed to hurt too.  We’re supposed to stand out for our righteous lifestyles, but we should stand out just as much for our sympathy and compassion.  It’s not easy to live in this way, but it is Christlike.

Next, Paul warns against PRIDE.  Look at Romans 12:16.  It’s interesting that self-centeredness is a theme not just here, but in the previous point too.  The self-centered curse their persecutors, envy the success of others, and are unconcerned with the trials of others.  Likewise here, the self-centered only want to associate with the high-class folks and don’t have any interest in the janitors of mankind.  They also have a high opinion of themselves, and they can’t imagine that anybody who disagrees with them could have anything worth saying or listening to.  I fear we’ve all known people who are just like this.

Christians, by contrast, are supposed to have a very different standard for assessing people.  The ungodly look at things like beauty, wealth, and earthly accomplishments, but we’re supposed to be concerned with others’ character.  Most of the brethren here don’t have advanced degrees, but I’d still pick every one of you over 99 percent of the people I went to law school with, because you are decent people who want to do right, and generally speaking, my classmates weren’t.  There are many things about ourselves that we can’t change, but every one of us can choose to serve God, and that’s the most important thing.

Likewise, it’s critical that we learn to listen to others.  As I think I’ve said before, this is a struggle for me.  People in my family generally come into the world thinking that we know everything about everything, and for me, life has pretty much been a long, hard lesson that maybe I don’t know so much as I thought I did.  None of us can learn by flapping our mouths.  We can only learn by listening, and it’s only when we start really paying attention to what others are saying that we might start picking up a little wisdom.

Next, Paul promotes PEACEMAKING.  Read with me from Romans 12:17-18.  Once again, Paul clearly spells out what the godly and ungodly alternatives are here.  I can’t help but read this passage without thinking of my children.  Zoë and Marky are experts at returning evil for evil to one another.  Zoë snatches a toy away from Marky.  Marky shoves her.  She hits him over the head with a toy container.  He howls and comes running to me, complaining bitterly about his sister’s behavior.  At this point in my parenting, I have a rule that any time one of my children comes complaining to me about the other one, both of them get punished, because I know that both of them are guilty, and that even Solomon couldn’t sort it all out.

I admit that there’s some comedy to all that, but there is absolutely no comedy when adults start behaving like three-year-olds!  Some of the stories that I collect from my wife and some of the others here about their workplace experiences are enough to make me roll my eyes—bickering and petty feuds that sometimes last for years.  The only thing more pathetic is when Christians, who ought to know better, engage in the same ridiculous behavior.  We need to be better than that.  Instead of striving for one-upsmanship, we should strive for peace.  Sometimes peace isn’t possible.  Sometimes people are determined to make our lives miserable no matter what we do.  However, when peace is possible, it’s our responsibility to pursue it.

Finally in this section, Paul warns us against REVENGE.  In this regard, look at Romans 12:19-21.  I think that when it comes to this text, there’s an easy application and a hard application.  The easy one is obvious and straightforward.  We’re supposed to take Jesus as our example, not the Hatfields and McCoys.  We aren’t supposed to take the law into our own hands; instead, we are to trust the justice of God.  Like I said, that’s easy.  It might not be so easy for drug dealers in Englewood, but for most American citizens, taking that kind of personal vengeance isn’t really an option.

Instead, our struggles arise with much more subtle temptations to return evil for evil, in our relationships with our families and in our relationships with one another.  I’m probably not going to buy a gun and go out hunting somebody I think has done me wrong, but how about refusing to watch the kids for my wife some night because I’m mad about an imagined slight?  How about gossiping about one of the men here because I didn’t like what he said in the business meeting?  That kind of thing won’t bring me to the attention of the police, but it’s no less returning evil for evil than slashing somebody’s tires would be.

The point is, friends, that we have to decide whom we trust to take care of us.  On the one hand, we can put our trust in ourselves, believing that if we don’t stand up for ourselves, nobody will.  On the other hand, we can put our trust in God, believing that no matter what anybody does to us, He will still protect us.  I don’t have to stick it to my wife.  I don’t have to stick it to my brother.  God’s got my back.

What’s more, the consequences of returning good for evil rather than returning evil are profound.  When we return evil for evil, we almost guarantee that the recipient is going to return evil right back to us.  That’s a great way to wreck a marriage or a friendship over nothing.  However, when we return good for evil, we stop the cycle of retribution, we allow the relationship to heal, and we glorify God.

**Godly Living**
In the final section of Romans that we’ll be looking at this morning, Paul begins with a discussion of GOVERNMENT.  It appears in Romans 13:1-4.  These days, it’s fashionable all over the country to complain about the government, and I suppose that it always has been.  For some reason, it’s much easier for us to focus on the perceived foolishness, incompetence, and corruption in government than on all the very significant good things that it does for us.  Back in the days of the Old Testament, God’s people had to worry continually about bands of Arameans, Moabites, Philistines, or just plain ole thugs sneaking up on them, killing them, and taking their stuff.  Because our government is much stronger than the government of the Israelites ever was, we don’t have to fear such things.  A hostile army hasn’t come through the Chicago area in 200 years, and that’s a span of peace without precedent in human history.  We should be thankful for that.

Government, then, is a good thing in our lives, and like all good things, it comes from God.  As you know, I try not to get involved in politics, but whenever I hear a Christian repeat the line about taking his gun when you pry it from his cold dead hands, I cringe.  The only time we may, and indeed must, disobey the government is when a human law contradicts the law of God, and despite what some may think, the Second Amendment is not actually in the Bible.  Short of that, our responsibility as Christians is to obey the government, and if we choose to do otherwise, we are actually disobeying God.  Simply because we think the government is bad is no excuse for disobedience.  Remember; Paul is writing this at a time when the head of his government is the Emperor Nero, and if Nero was no excuse for rebellion, we don’t have one either.

Paul then continues to point out specific responsibilities in the relationship between THE CHRISTIAN AND GOVERNMENT.  In this regard, let’s look at Romans 13:5-7.  This boils down to two main responsibilities:  tax and custom on the one hand, and fear and honor on the other.  The first of these two is relatively straightforward:  Christians are supposed to pay their taxes.  Now, I believe that if there’s some lawful, legitimate way for us to reduce our tax burden, such as, for instance, keeping a record of business expenses, it’s perfectly acceptable for Christians to take advantage of that.  However, we can’t understate our income, run cash-only businesses to avoid leaving a paper trail, or engage in a host of other illegal exploits.  That kind of behavior is not only unlawful but also ungodly.

Just as we have financial duties toward our government, though, we also have duties of loyalty and respect.  Maybe we don’t agree with the governor.  Maybe we don’t agree with the president.  That’s fine; our country gives us the right to do so, and even to speak openly against their policies.  However, we must take care that our disagreement always remains respectful.  Even if the United States gives us the right to mock and insult elected officials, God does not.  As the saying goes, we have to debate the issue, not the man.

Next, Paul emphasizes the importance of LOVE in all we do.  Read with me from Romans 13:8-10.  This is an interesting counterpoint to what we just read.  Paul tells us that we need to fulfill our obligations to the government, but there is one obligation we can never fulfill, and that is the obligation to love.  None of us as Christians can ever reach the point where we say, “I’m checking out.  I’ve loved enough.”  Instead, we are indebted to Jesus for the rest of our lives, and one of the conditions of our indebtedness is that we love others, particularly that we love other Christians as He loved them.

That all sounds wonderful and uplifting and easy to ignore, but the applications here are about as concrete as Jimmy Hoffa’s overshoes.  Paul tells us that every commandment in the Scripture is in some way an expression of love, be it love for God or love for our neighbor.  We are being loving only when we keep the commandments, not just the thou-shalt-not’s, but the thou-shalts.

We’ve already looked at some of the thou-shalt’s this morning, and my goodness, but they’re hard!  If we have an ounce of self-awareness, every one of us is able to look back over the past year and see a hundred different ways we failed to love.  We didn’t tell our neighbor about the gospel.  We didn’t visit shut-ins on that free Saturday afternoon.  We didn’t welcome that visitor.  We didn’t take food to our co-worker when he was sick.  We didn’t sign up to teach that extra class on the teaching matrix in back.  We didn’t love enough, and so we didn’t do what we knew was right.  Certainly I have my litany of failures too.  We need grace for all the things we do that we shouldn’t, but most of all, we need grace for the things we don’t do that we should, because the obligation to love is an obligation that never, ever goes away.  We’re never going to get there, but in this coming year, let’s make the effort to come closer than we ever have before.

Finally, Paul urges us to put on THE ARMOR OF LIGHT.  Consider Romans 13:11-14.  Here, Paul returns from offense to defense.  Being loving is necessary, but doing all kinds of nice things for people doesn’t do us any good if we live lives filled with immorality.  As Paul points out, salvation is nearer to us than when we first believed.  Every day we live brings us one day closer to the day when the Lord will return or we will die, whichever comes first.  We don’t have any idea how long that span of time will be, and it wouldn’t do for us to be caught sneaking around doing wicked stuff when the sun rises.  The only way we can make sure that our behavior is proper is by putting on Christ.  We need to live our lives like He would live them if He were put in our position.  We need to be filled with His rejection of every sin, from drunkenness to fornication to strife.  Whatever it may be, we need to respond as resolutely to temptation as He would.

Before the Lord’s Supper, then, let’s reflect on these things.  Let’s remember first of all the perfect sinlessness and perfect love of Jesus.  Indeed, He was sinless because He loved so perfectly.  Second, let’s consider ourselves and the year ahead, and ask what we can do to imitate Him.
