Because I
kept this Bible at the church building for five years, it was the only Bible I
used for preaching, teaching, and study throughout that time.  I definitely have some opinions about
it.  Many of these may well be unique to
me; however, I think they do raise issues that are worth talking about.

**PREACHING.** The PSR is considerably
smaller than the largest Bible I would use in the pulpit, with a trim size of 7
¾” x 5 ¼”.  In fact, for my tastes, it’s
actually a little too small.  It didn’t
do a very good job of concealing the edges of my sermon notes when I was en
route to the pulpit.  Not a big deal,
certainly, but irksome nonetheless.

The type in
the PSR is 7.4-point, which is certainly on the small side (for the newer PRB,
Crossway bumped that up to 8-point). 
However, though my distance vision is rotten, I can see up close just
fine, so that never bothered me much.

There were
two things about the setting of the PSR that I didn’t care for in the pulpit,
though.  First, it is set in paragraphed rather
than verse-by-verse form, which makes a particular verse more difficult to
locate.  Similarly, the verse numbers in
the PSR are superscript, which means that they’re easy to overlook when reading
but hard to find when locating a passage.

In sum, the
PSR isn’t an awful pulpit Bible by any means. 
After all, I used it successfully in that capacity for five years.  However, there are probably other Bibles that
are better suited to that function, at least for me.

**TEACHING.** On the other hand, the PSR
performs very differently for me when I use it to teach a Bible class.  Here, the PSR shines.  It’s smaller size makes it handy and easy to
hold for long periods of time.

Conversely, I
don’t particularly mind the paragraphed nature of the PSR when I’m teaching a
class.  Sure, the split-second delay in
locating passages is still present, and a verse-by-verse Bible might be a
little better, but I’m less worried about polish in my teaching than in my
preaching, and the delays don’t trouble me.

Also, I
appreciate the cross-references in the PSR. 
The PSR (along with the PRB) tucks its cross-references unobtrusively
along the inner margin, and they look to be in about 5-point type, but they’re
there when you need them.  Other than the
Pitt Minion, I’m not aware of a reference Bible that is smaller than the PSR,
so you get a lot of oomph in a small package.

**STUDY.** Overall, the PSR is a so-so
study Bible.  Its small size isn’t
particularly beneficial when I’m not holding it for 45 minutes at a stretch,
but that smaller size does inevitably lead to thinner paper and smaller
font.  That smaller font doesn’t really
catch up to me when I’m reading occasional passages while preaching or
teaching, but it can get irksome after a full day at the office.  Also, unlike the PRB, the PSR isn’t
line-matched, which doesn’t help either.

In five
years, I might have used the PSR’s cross-references once or twice while I was
sitting in my office, to idly look up an Old-Testament quotation in Hebrews or
something, but I never used them for anything more serious than that.  They may as well not have been there.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEiFMeKzIJWuL6fxOhGcP9qT0qghblvJoCeKFlF_-ZtWtvtj5ZPGlIkQZdH9kFVV1wIqt6OYAG22A6PVKey04LDnjCM9ysIl99_s7W8QmMOmwOebVvMUMQEBxSz5nLZ7Cvh2rWoJ1k_gFA/s1600/PTS4+Verse+Numbers.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEiFMeKzIJWuL6fxOhGcP9qT0qghblvJoCeKFlF_-ZtWtvtj5ZPGlIkQZdH9kFVV1wIqt6OYAG22A6PVKey04LDnjCM9ysIl99_s7W8QmMOmwOebVvMUMQEBxSz5nLZ7Cvh2rWoJ1k_gFA/s1600/PTS4+Verse+Numbers.jpg)

This takes us
to the PSR’s greatest shortcoming as an office Bible—its paragraphed format.  The verse numbers in the PSR are superscript,
itty-bitty, hard to see, and easy to overlook. 
In fact, I spent five years overlooking them with dreary regularity.  I am certainly capable of generating plenty
of missed citations on my own, but the PSR’s format didn’t help.  I caught most of the bad citations in sermons
before Sunday morning rolled around, but I also got to spend plenty of time
telling my Bible classes that they really should be looking in Matthew 5:6, not
Matthew 5:4.  Of course, the silver
lining to the paragraphing of the PSR was that it was an easy read when I read
an entire book through before beginning a workbook.

**CONCLUSION.** Despite my sentimental
attachment to the PSR, and even though it lasted longer as my primary Bible
than any other Bible I’ve ever owned, it isn’t really suited for use by a
full-time preacher, at least not one with my tastes.  The small size and references are nice for
teaching, but the paragraphed format causes me trouble in preaching and study.  For preaching, teaching, and study, I give it
an overall 3/5.
