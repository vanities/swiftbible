A year ago, I embarked on a grand (for me) experiment.  As most Bible geeks know, the Cambridge
Wide-Margin Reference and Cambridge Pitt Minion for a given translation share a
setting.  In other words, not only do
they contain the same text, but they also have the same layout.  The first word on Page 878 in the Wide-Margin
Reference is the same as the first word on Page 878 in the Pitt Minion.

This suggests that Cambridge means for the two editions to
be used as complements.  Because I had a
matched-set of a calf-split NASB Wide-Margin Reference and NASB Pitt Minion
(the former a review copy and the latter a gift), I thought I’d try using the
combination together.  I liked it so well
that I continued to use them long after my review period for both Bibles was
over.  Here’s what I think works, and
what I think doesn’t.



**Positives**

**The Wide-Margin
Reference as a pulpit Bible.** So far
as I’m aware, there are four wide-margin NASB’s in print at the moment.  The first is the Zondervan NASB Note-Taker’s,
which takes one of my favorite NASB settings (the Zondervan NASB Thinline) and
converts it to a wide-margin Bible.

I have yet to review (or even see in person) a Note-Taker’s,
but I can only imagine that this setting would disappoint me in use.  A double-column wide-margin Bible must have a
gutter margin large enough to write in; otherwise, you can’t put notes on the
text of the inner column next to the actual text.  The Note-Takers doesn’t appear to have a
gutter margin much larger than its thinline ancestor does.  This wide-margin Bible was a great idea, but
it wasn’t executed well.

The next two wide-margin NASB’s, The Foundation Wide-Margin
In Touch and wide-margin Side-Column Reference, are much better Bibles.  I’ve reviewed both, and I like both.  In their favor, both have considerably larger
print than the Cambridge Wide-Margin Reference, but with the larger print comes
greater weight.  The In Touch and SCR
both weigh more than three pounds; the Cambridge Wide-Margin weighs less.  In years to come, I may be forced to tote
around one of the hefty Foundation products to accommodate poorer vision, but
in the meantime, I’d rather wield the lighter Cambridge edition.

This means, then, that if I’m going to preach an expository
sermon from an NASB, the Cambridge Wide-Margin is my weapon of choice.  I prefer to preach such sermons only from
notes and underlinings in my Bible, and the WM offers me all the white space I
need in a fairly light Bible. 
Admittedly, the WM does have a significant wingspan, but when I’m actually
holding it while preaching, I simply turn the text block in my hand to focus on
whatever column.  Until my eyesight
deteriorates or somebody produces an NASB like the Crossway (ESV) Single-Column
Journaling Bible, the Cambridge WM is the gold standard for wide-margin NASB’s.

**The Pitt Minion as a
pulpit Bible.** Of course, some of the
sermons I preach are topical rather than expository.  In that case, I write my sermon outlines in a
small notebook rather than in the margins of my Bible.  The Joliet church building has a smaller
pulpit without much room on top, so the best companion for my sermon notebook
is the Pitt Minion.  Its print is large
enough for me to read easily in the pulpit (when I have my glasses on), and it
fits next to my notebook on the pulpit without crowding it.  Unless I’m reviewing another Bible, I’m going
to preach topical sermons out of the Pitt.

**The Pitt Minion as a
lectern Bible.** When I’m teaching an
adult Bible class, I want three things in my Bible:  text large enough to read with my glasses on,
cross-references (for when one of the students throws me a curveball), and
small size (so I can roam freely with the Bible instead of leaving it on the
lectern).  The Pitt Minion offers me all three
of these things.  So long as my vision
remains capable enough, it’s hard to imagine a Bible better for my teaching
style than the Pitt is.

**The Pitt Minion as an
office Bible.** This isn’t a place
where I really need large print either, so the 6.75-point Lexicon No. 1 that
the Pitt offers is perfectly acceptable. 
I will use the cross-references sometimes as I’m trying to run down a
passage, and the small size of the Pitt is a bonus.  It means that I can set the Bible in my lap
as I’m sitting at my computer without having to hunch over it as I type.  Hooray for Bibles that promote good
posture!  Again, outside of review
periods, if I’m using a Bible to prepare a sermon, a blog post, or class
materials, I’m using the Pitt.

**The Pitt Minion as a
carry Bible.** It wins here over my
goatskin-rebind Zondervan NASB Thinline, but only by a nose.  Once again, small size,
readable-with-glasses, and cross-references are all important in Bible I grab
to take to a study.

However, generally I don’t need cross-references in a
kitchen-table study.  In real life, if
I’m leaving for the study from home, I’m going to take the Thinline, which I
keep at home.  If I’m leaving from the
church building, I’ll take the Pitt, which I keep there.

**Negatives**

**The Wide-Margin
Reference as a reading Bible.** I
haven’t spent much time doing daily readings from my Wide-Margin
Reference.  I dislike reading from Bibles
I’ve written in, and by now, the WM is pretty heavily marked up.  An unmarked WM would probably be a good daily
reader, though it doesn’t seem optimal to me to read from a Bible made larger
and heavier by a bunch of white space you’re not going to write in.

**The Pitt Minion as a
reading Bible.** Unlike preaching,
teaching, and study, I carry out my daily reading with my glasses off.  I’m extremely nearsighted, and I believe that
extended reading with my glasses on makes my nearsightedness worse.  The Pitt is fine for vision that’s corrected
to 20/20, but it’s not quite good enough for uncorrected vision that’s
20/miserable.  Especially when I’m doing
readings first thing in the morning, I have to strain a little bit to read out
of the Pitt.  I don’t see a reason to do
that when I have better alternatives.

At the moment, my favorite NASB reader is my Zondervan
Thinline.  It’s a beautifully laid out
double-column text Bible, and its 7.8-point ITC Weidemann is noticeably easier
to read.  If somebody sets a
single-column NASB with the magical proportions of the Tyndale Select/Schuyler
Caxton, I would sell my children (assuming they were misbehaving at the time)
to get a copy, and it would become my favored NASB reader.  Until that day, though, it’s the Zondervan
Thinline for me.

**The Pitt Minion as a
travel Bible.** If I’m preaching for a
different congregation, unless I know I’m only preaching a topical
sermon/sermons, I’m taking my Wide-Margin Reference with me (once again, review
periods excepted).  If I don’t anticipate
preaching, the Zondervan NASB Thinline is my preferred travel Bible.  The Thinline is a slightly better all-rounder
than the Pitt because it’s a better reader, and its slightly larger size is
inconsequential.

**Conclusion**

Together, the Cambridge NASB Wide-Margin Reference and Pitt
Minion are the mainstays of the work I do as a preacher.  Of the NASB’s currently available, there
simply aren’t any other Bibles that are better at what I want my work Bibles to
do.  I might see things differently if I
were primarily an ESV user (Crossway has produced a multiplicity of worthy
Bible settings), but as an NASB guy, Cambridge makes the best tools for my
trade.

However, there’s a hole in the lineup.  I think the WM/Pitt setting could make for a
good daily reader, but that setting isn’t currently published in the right size
(nor, for that matter, are the proportions of the Cambridge Clarion what I
prefer in a reader).  If the WM and the
Pitt had a mid-sized sibling, I could see that Bible overtaking the Zondervan
NASB Thinline in my preference.  Until
that time comes, though, the Cambridge Duo needs a third, non-Cambridge,
companion.
