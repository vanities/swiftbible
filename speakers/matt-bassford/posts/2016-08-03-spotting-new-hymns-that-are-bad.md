Back when I was a child, my parents and I worshiped with a
small congregation in New Jersey.  The
hymnal we used was *Great Songs of the
Church*.  It is easily the most
influential and widely used hymnal in the history of the churches of Christ,
our *Hymns Ancient and Modern*.  It’s a little more gospelly than *Hymns A&M*, but it still has what
people in our tradition would consider a high-church slant.

By the time I arrived on the scene, *Great Songs* was about 50 years old. 
Some large church in Texas took pity on our small, struggling group on
the verges of civilization and sent us a couple of boxes of what was then the
hip and happening hymnal, *Sacred
Selections*.  Even though *Sacred Selections* contains a moderate
number of the classics, it is largely based around the hymnody of 20th-century
Southern gospel.  These are the radio-era
quartet-driven songs that people have in mind when they talk about “Church of
Christ carnival music”.



My parents were by no means hymn experts, but they took one
look at *Sacred Selections*, were
instantly reminded of the *Grand Ole Opry*
radio specials they used to listen to as children, and said, “We’re not going
to be using these.”  *Great Songs* remained in the pew racks; the boxes of *Sacred Selections* were relegated to the
changing room beside the baptistery, from which they appeared for occasional
singings.  Very occasional singings.

I’m sure this caused more than a little bit of grumbling at
the time, but 30 years later, my parents’ judgment has been proven
correct.  *Sacred Selections* isn’t hip and happening anymore.  Southern gospel is no longer particularly
timely.  Its best representatives
continue to be sung widely.  “Victory in
Jesus”, “This World Is Not My Home”, and the like are worthy additions to the
body of our hymnody.

However, the vast majority of radio hymns are still being
sung only by congregations with an average age of 75.  Soon, they won’t be sung anywhere.  As popular as they were, they were never
good.

This points to something that I think is generally
true.  Whenever some new genre of hymn
appears on the scene and people complain about it, the complainers may be a
bunch of curmudgeons, but they probably have a point.  They’re wrong when they start making sweeping
generalizations about all new hymns (“These are worthless, and we should never
sing anything written after the year of Mozart’s death!”), but they likely have
correctly identified the excesses of the genre.

This has been happening for centuries.  Take, for instance, the story of ["Hold the Fort"](http://www.hymnary.org/text/ho_my_comrades_see_the_signal_waving_in).  It was written by P.P. Bliss in 1870, which
is usually a promising beginning for a hymn. 
However, rather than being based on a particular passage of Scripture or
universal experience, it was based on [this Civil-War battle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Allatoona).  The music can only be described as
tub-thumping, and the whole hymn represents an excess of the othewise
impressive tradition of 19th-century gospel.

Indeed it was recognized as excess at the time.  John Greenleaf Whittier found “Hold the Fort”
so repellent that it provoked him to write a poem called ["The Brewing of Soma"](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Brewing_of_Soma), which compares the noise and
wild emotion generated by such hymns to the bacchanals of pagan worship (sound
familiar?).

Ironically, the last few stanzas of “The Brewing of Soma”
are still sung as “Dear Lord and Father of Mankind”.  The critique has outlived the hymn it was
critiquing (though not, alas, the tendency to substitute exhilaration for
devotion to Christ).  Whittier was right
too.

It is nearly certain that if the world and the church
endure, the time will come when the excesses of the contemporary era will be
recognized as such and not sung.  Some of
today’s praise songs will ascend to join the pantheon of the greatest hymns of
all time; most won’t.  Taste will have
moved on, and there will be some new, as-yet-unimagined, genre of hymn for
curmudgeons to complain about.

This should call us, then, to a critical examination of the
contemporary repertoire.  We see the
problems with the thousands of awful hymns that Charles Wesley wrote (as
opposed to the dozen or so that are still sung).  We see the problems with “Hold the Fort”
(except for Civil-War buffs, the whole hymn is just kind of. . . odd).  We increasingly see the problems with the
Golgotha Fun Park-like treatment of the crucifixion in ["He Bore It All"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LOVIxMvTXuQ)  (even non-curmudgeons can recognize that the humiliation, suffering, and
death of the Lord Jesus should not be syncopated).

Although contemporary songs seem timelier to us than Wesley,
Bliss, or Stamps-Baxter, we should not allow their timeliness to blind us to
their problems.  There are excesses in
today’s new hymns and praise songs too, and such songs aren’t suitable for
congregational use.  Boxing up the
hymnals (or segregating the offending PowerPoint files) may be an extreme
solution, but we cannot be undiscriminating in our embrace of novelty.  Otherwise, the songs we sing now will be the
songs our grandchildren shake their heads over later.
