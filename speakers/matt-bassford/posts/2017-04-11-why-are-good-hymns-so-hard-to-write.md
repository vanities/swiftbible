The other day, my friend and brother Zach Olson posed a
question on a hymn-centric Facebook group. 
He wanted to know what hymnists should do to write a hymn that people
want to sing.  In response, I advocated
prayer.  These days, I don’t think it’s
possible for a human being to develop a skill set that will allow them to
consistently write hymn texts that will become repertoire hymns.



This is a grim conclusion, but it’s borne out by history and
personal experience.  Of all of the
extremely talented hymnists in the past 300 years of English-language hymnody,
none have more than a tiny percentage of their work still being sung.  A few years back, I went through *Psalms, Hymns, and Spiritual Songs* and
compared the number of hymns by a particular writer it contained to the total
number of hymns written by that writer (based on hymn counts on hymnary.org, I
believe).

The winner was Victorian hymnist Frances Havergal, who had 4
percent of her hymns still in use.  Even
a weather forecaster couldn’t get away with a 96 percent failure rate!  Giants of the genre such as Fanny J. Crosby
and Charles Wesley have a success rate below 1 percent (Note, by the way, that
I’m not considering composers in this. 
It’s apparently easier to write repertoire-level hymn tunes than hymn
texts.).

Them’s ain’t good odds, and I have no hope that my work will
fare any better.  At this point, I’m
pretty sure that I’ve written a few hymns that will be sung after I’m
dead.  However, even though I wrote the
first of those hymns in the late 1990s, and my skill as a writer is much
greater now than it was then, my ability to produce repertoire-level hymns has
not improved.

Clearly, as I replied to Zach, it is not in the man who
wills or the man who runs, but in the Lord who has mercy.  But why? 
What is it that keeps human beings from consistently writing hymns that
will be regularly used in worship?  The
greatest writers of all time miss much more than they hit.

I think the problem is that writing hymns requires not one
but two gifts.  First, the author must
have a gift for the technical side of hymnwriting.  It’s very difficult to take a spiritual
thought and express that thought in the form of a hymn.  I’ve often compared learning to write in
rhyme and meter to learning to speak another language.  Congregational hymns have their own rules and
grammar, and writers who try to dodge them generally don’t fare well.

In the preface to the early 19th-century *Christian Psalmist*, James Montgomery
(the author of “In the Hour of Trial”, among other hymns) complained of
contemporary-to-him writers who wrote with unpredictable rhythm and poor
structure.  The verdict of centuries is
that Montgomery was right.  His hymns are
sung, and the hymns by sloppy writers aren’t. 
200 years from now, the sloppy praise songs of today won’t be sung
either.  Poor writing can be faddish, but
it will not long outlive its author (if it makes it that far).

However, mere technical quality is not enough to ensure that
a given hymn is sung.  Isaac Watts was a
talented writer and a fine technician (far more proficient than Wesley or
Crosby).  However, the vast majority of
his work hasn’t made the cut either, even though his writing is generally good.

This points us to the second and far more elusive gift that
the ideal hymnist must have.  They must
be able to discern what the church will want to sing.  Frankly, I have no idea how to do this.  I know how to write truthfully and
self-expressively, but I don’t know how to write something that most/all
Christians will want to claim as their self-expression, and those are two
dramatically different things.

Unlike nearly any other form of writing, a hymn must be
perfectly transparent.  I can read and
appreciate, say, Shelley’s “Ode to a Skylark” and yet still retain my distance
from the poem.  Shelley doesn’t speak for
me, and I don’t need him to in order for the poem to fulfill its aim.

However, the hymnist must speak for the congregation.  The worshipers can’t be conscious of any
difference between what the writer feels and what they themselves feel.  As soon as they sense a difference, they will
withdraw emotionally, and the hymn will fail.

Here, perhaps, is why most Christians aren’t as conscious of
the writers of their favorite hymns as they are of the writers of their
favorite novels.  Even a beloved novel
remains a thing apart, but a great hymn incorporates itself into the fabric of
our being, so that when we sing it, our own souls are speaking, and we are
conscious of no one but ourselves, our fellow worshipers, and God.

This mystery is great, at least to me.  However, I know other hymnists who have this
gift.  They write hymns that resonate,
and they can tell whether one of my hymns is going to work or not.

However, hymnists like this are not, in my experience,
skilled technicians.  Indeed, I’ve never
met anyone, nor even considered the work of anyone, who appeared to possess
both gifts.  Watts, for all his ability
with language, missed the congregation a lot. 
Wesley is known for writing emotionally expressive hymns (think “And Can
It Be?”), but 99 percent of his work was sabotaged by bad technique and is not
used.  In short, the twin gifts of
technical ability and emotional insight seem not ever to reside within the same
human head--at least, not at the level that would allow said human to
consistently write great hymns.

Upon reflection, I don’t think this is happenstance.  Coming before the Almighty isn’t supposed to
be easy, nor is helping others to do the same. 
It can never become a matter of routine, of serene confidence in our own
abilities and their inevitable outcome. 
Instead, hymnists must feel their way toward Him and perhaps find Him,
constantly aware that His grace is sufficient, and that His power is perfected
in weakness.
