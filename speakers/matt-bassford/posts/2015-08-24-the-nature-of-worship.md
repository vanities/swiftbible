Every once in a while, in all of the in-person and online
conversations I have about spiritual topics, one topic in particular keeps
coming up over and over again.  For me
these days, that topic seems to be, “Is worship about us?”  A friend of mine wrote a blog post about the
question, and I’ve had a couple people tell me it’s been discussed in the adult
Bible class recently too.

As is often the case when I’m confronted with a yes-no
question, my answer is “That depends”. 
On the one hand, our worship is directed to God, not ourselves, so it’s
obviously about Him rather than us in that sense.  On the other hand, it’s not something that
God asks us to do because He’s mean and selfish.  Worship is good for us, and we benefit from
it.  Rather than trying to provide an
either-or answer to a both-and question, let’s look this morning at the
worship-related issues that the Bible actually does discuss.  Let’s consider the nature of worship.



**Worship Is Not. . .**

As we examine this topic, let’s begin by considering the
things that worship, at least Biblical worship, is not.  First, worship is not **OPTIONAL.** Consider the
import of the throne scene in Revelation 5:11-14.  This, brethren, is the kind of worship to
which our God and our Savior are entitled. 
Everything else in the physical creation praises the Lord, and even
though we have a choice in the matter, our duty is clear.  Because God is so much greater than we are
and has given us so much, we have no reasonable grounds for refusing to worship
Him.

Here, we come to one of the issues underlying the
is-worship-about-us question.  All of us
have talked to people who don’t assemble to worship God because they say they
don’t get anything out of it.  I think
it’s equally worthwhile to ask what we get out of not worshiping God.  As with any other insult that we offer to the
Almighty, any other form of rebellion against His will, if we refuse to honor
Him as He deserves, He will surely reward us with condemnation and eternal
punishment.  Every knee will bow.  Every tongue will confess. We might as well
do it here while we still have some choice in the matter, rather than waiting
until choice and life alike are taken from us.

Second, worship is not **PASSIVE.** Ponder with me the implications of Ephesians
5:18-20.  Just as worldly people become
drunk with wine, so Christians are to be filled with the Holy Spirit.  Drunk people say and do all sorts of things
that they would never do while sober, and in the same way, the Spirit within us
influences us to do something that the lost would never do.  We sing to one another and to God, teaching,
glorifying, and giving thanks.  By
contrast, if there is someone who claims to be a disciple of God and is
physically able to sing, yet chooses not to, that’s evidence of a serious
spiritual problem.  Their silence shows
that the Spirit of God does not dwell within them, and as Romans 8 says, if we
don’t have the Spirit of Christ, we don’t belong to Him.

This experience of active, congregational worship is alien
to much of the denominational world.  I
have denominational friends who report on Sundays that the worship at church
this morning was really great, when what they actually mean is that the praise
band up front was on fire, and they found the music really moving.  They may never have sung so much as one
syllable.

Friends, we’re not here to be audience members.  We’re here to be participants.  That’s what God expects.  He doesn’t expect us to produce professional-quality
sound.  I certainly can’t do that.  However, He expects us to give our best,
because He deserves our best.

Next, we see from the Scripture that worship is not to be **MINDLESS.**Look at what Paul writes in 1 Corinthians 14:15.  I’m sure many of you have seen, as I have, that
video on YouTube of the worship service in the charismatic church where
somebody who’s supposedly “filled with the Spirit” races around the auditorium
and dives into the baptistery in the middle of a song.  I laugh every time I see the thing, but really,
it’s no laughing matter.  That’s somebody
who is taking the solemn worship of our Creator and making a mindless mockery
out of it.

We have to be equally careful, though, to make sure that our
minds are involved in our worship.  One
of my most common struggles in worship, particularly when I’m singing an old,
familiar hymn, is the struggle not to put the hymn on autopilot and sing it
without thinking about it.  Like David on
the threshing floor of Ornan, we can’t offer to God something that costs us
nothing.  Mindless worship is a problem
precisely because it is effortless, zero-cost worship.  It doesn’t demand anything from us, and it
doesn’t change us in any way.  Instead,
we have to make sure that our worship is effortful.  We have to think about what we’re singing and
mean it.

Finally, worship is not **A
GUARANTEED HIGH.** Look at the example
of Job in Job 1:20-21.  Job has just lost
everything, not merely his fortune, but every one of his 10 children.  In a chapter or two, he’s about to express
the wish that he had been a miscarriage, because that would have been better
than the life that he is living.  And
yet, sandwiched between those two things, we see Job bless the name of the
Lord, and we see him worship.  I daresay
that this was not a mountaintop experience for Job, and yet he still worshiped,
because God was still God.

By contrast, there are lots of folks out there who demand
that worship be a mountaintop experience for them, every single time they come
to church.  Every Sunday morning, that
song leader or that praise band had better hit a spiritual home run, or else
they’re not coming back, because they aren’t “getting anything out of it”.

Talk about unrealistic expectations!  Is there anything else in our lives that is
one mountaintop experience right after the other?  For all of us, our marriages, our
relationships with our children, our jobs, and even our favorite TV shows all
have their high and their low points.

Worship is the same way. 
Some days, it is a mountaintop experience, when the whole congregation
shows up amped about glorifying God and the song leader picks half a dozen of
our favorite hymns in a row.  Some days,
though, we don’t sleep well the night before, and getting out the door on time
is a struggle, and half the congregation shows up with a cold, and worship is
just plain work!  Whether it comes easy
or comes hard, though, we are still responsible for worshiping.  What was true in Job’s time is still true
today.  God is still God, and we must
give Him the glory.

**Worship Is. . .**

Now that we’ve explored the negative, let’s examine the
positive.  Let’s consider what worship
actually is, or at least what it’s supposed to be.  First of all, it’s supposed to be **A TEACHING OPPORTUNITY.** Here, let’s consider Colossians
3:16.  In many ways, this verse is the
companion passage to the text from Ephesians 5 that we read earlier, but it has
some differences in shade of meaning. 
Rather than urging us to be filled with the Spirit, it instructs us to allow
the word of Christ to dwell richly within us. 
This is another way of saying the same thing.  After all, the Holy Spirit influences and
controls us through the word.

Once we have the word of God in our hearts, we naturally
want to tell others about it, and singing gives us that opportunity.  Of course, our ability to instruct others in
the word of God can be limited by the quality of the hymns we sing.  We can’t make the hymn say more than it says,
so only hymns that richly express the Scripture will allow us to richly express
the Scripture ourselves.

Sometimes, though, it is our presence and not our voice that
does the teaching.  Here, I’m
particularly thinking of mothers of small children.  Every mother has to go through worship
services that, as far as she is concerned, are a complete intellectual
blank.  She knows that hymns were sung,
but she couldn’t tell you what any of them were because she spent the whole
time wrestling with a two-year-old. 
However, simply by being there with that child, she is teaching by her
example that worshiping God matters. 
Take heart, mothers!  The Sunday
mornings you spend doing battle with the small and incorrigible might be the
most important teaching moments of your lives.

Of course, there’s a counterbalance to this point.  Even though worship is not always going to be
emotionally uplifting, it’s supposed to be **EMOTIONALLY
EXPRESSIVE.** Look at the attitude
expressed in Psalm 95:1-3.  Ever wonder
why God commanded us to sing?  It’s not
like singing was the only alternative. 
God could just as easily have commanded us to recite Scripture in
unison, so why didn’t He?  Unlike most,
I’ve actually spent some time reciting hymns in a group rather than singing
them.  It’s an exercise that we do during
the Hymninar just about every year, and let me tell you, it gets pretty
grim.  By the time you’re done, you’re
just about ready to draw a pentagram on the floor and sacrifice a goat!  There’s no beauty to the practice.

Singing, by contrast, touches something inside us.  A well-written hymn tune acts like an
emotional amplifier.  It fills our hearts
with whatever emotion the hymn is expressing. 
We are then able to share not only what we think, but what we feel.  As I said above, this is not always how
things work.  However, if we aren’t able
to find that emotional groove most of the time, we may need to spend a few
hours on self-examination, figuring out why what should be happening, isn’t.

Third, our singing together is supposed to be **AN AFFIRMATION OF FELLOWSHIP.** Look, for instance, at how Paul depicts
praise in Romans 15:5-6.  When we look at
what the Scripture says about our singing together, there’s a lot of emphasis
on the “together” part.  This is no
accident.  If you’ve got some spare time,
do a Google search on the psychological and physiological effects of singing,
especially singing in a group.  It causes
the release of the hormone oxytocin, famously known as the cuddle hormone,
which increases bonding and feelings of togetherness.  It relieves stress.  It even improves cognition in dementia
patients.  In short, group singing is
good for us individually, and it’s good for us as a group.  The more we sing together, the more united we
will become in everything.  It’s almost
like God knew that when He commanded us to sing!  None of this has anything to do with skill,
only with willingness to join in.

Last of all, but certainly not least, our singing is **PLEASING TO GOD.** Consider the way that praise is presented
in Hebrews 13:15.  What’s interesting to
me about this text is that it’s not the first time in Scripture that we see
singing compared to sacrifice.  In Psalm
69, David promises that he is going to glorify the Lord with thanksgiving, and
he says that it will please God more than the offering of a bull or an ox.  That’s a big deal!  Even today, a whole cow isn’t cheap, and back
then, a bull or an ox was the most valuable sacrifice the Israelites could
offer.  However, David says that
heartfelt song would be even more pleasing to God than that.  Amazing though it may seem to us, when we
sing to our Creator, He likes it.  It
makes Him happy, no matter how off-key we are.

Even here, though, we can’t climb off of the
for-God-or-for-us fence.  We also benefit
from the praises we sing Him.  They
remind us of His magnificence, of His many virtues, of all the things that He
has done for us, and the promises that He has made us about the future.  Our song worship is an opportunity to refocus
our lives on the things that matter.  If
we keep those lives focused on the things we sing about, we aren’t going to go
far wrong.

**Worship in Practice**

We’re going to spend the final portion of our study this
morning considering how worship works in practice.  Not so coincidentally, our subject matter
here will help us to prepare our minds for the Lord’s Supper.  Biblically speaking, worship always begins in
the same place—with a spiritually profound **THOUGHT.** For an example of such a thought, let’s
consider the great text of Romans 5:5-9. 
These are some of the most important sentences ever written.  They identify us in our natural state as
sinners, people who freely chose to violate the law of God.  Because of our sin, God had the right to pour
out His wrath on us.  We earned
destruction by what we had done.

However, rather than pouring out His wrath on us, God poured
out His love on us.  Instead of sending
destruction upon us, He sent Christ, who came to earth and died.  He didn’t die for the righteous, those
hypothetical people who have never sinned. 
He didn’t die for the good, those equally hypothetical people who had committed
a sin or two but lived for God 99 percent of the time.  Instead, He died for the ungodly, those who
had chosen an entire manner of life that was opposed to the will of God.  He died for us.  By His lifeblood, we are justified.  That blood erases our sins and aligns our
lives with the perfect standard of God’s law. 
Because we now measure up, we are saved from the wrath of God, for where
there is no crime, there can be no punishment.

This thought necessarily generates an **EMOTIONAL REACTION** in those who have been saved.  We see Paul’s emotional reaction to this in
Romans 11:33-35.  Paul here is
summarizing his reaction not only to Romans 5, but to everything that he has
discussed in the first 11 chapters of the book, and when he considers the
wisdom and mercy of God, he is overwhelmed with awe.

There are many emotions that we should experience when we
contemplate the sacrifice of Christ and God’s plan for our salvation, but
gratitude and awe should definitely be on the list.

We must never allow the familiarity of our salvation to
lessen the awe we feel.  We were standing
there flatfooted in front of the runaway 18-wheeler, and Jesus knocked us out
of the way, only to be struck and killed Himself.  That’s not the kind of thing that you can
remember and honor for the next two weeks and then forget about.  That’s the kind of thing that you remember
for the rest of your life, because now you’re living a life that somebody else
gave you.

Once a worship-worthy thought has produced an emotional
response in us, it then leads to the **EXPRESSION**of worship.  This is where Paul heads
in Romans 11:36.  Because of Paul’s awe
at the greatness of God, he expresses the wish that God be glorified
forever.  Quite simply, when we consider
what God and Christ have done for us, isn’t that what we should be doing
too?

Even for the most reserved man or woman out there, there
comes a time when our emotions drive us to speak.  There are some things that you can’t stay
quiet about.  I never come home from the
office without going up to my children, upending them, and pretending to eat
them.  I don’t know why I do this, but
apparently it means “I love you” in daddy language, and I would never think of
not doing it except in dire emergency. 
Likewise, our expression of worship and song should well up naturally
from within us.  We worship because we
can’t stay quiet about God and His salvation. 
If we don’t want to do this, maybe we need to go back and revisit the
suffering and love of Jesus until we do want to.

Of course, the reverence and awe that we feel for God should
not find its final expression in mere words. 
Ultimately, it should lead to **SERVICE.** Look at where Paul ends up in Romans
12:1-2.  Those of you who are using a
Bible app here may note that there’s a translation split here.  Translations such as the KJV and NKJV say
“service” at the end of 12:1, but translations like the NASB and ESV mention
worship.  Here’s what’s going on.  The Greek word that the translators are
wrestling with literally does mean “service”, but it refers particularly to
religious service.  Think, for instance,
of the priests and Levites who served in the temple.  Even if they weren’t the ones who were
offering the sacrifices, in carrying the wood and water and dead animals
around, they were still serving God. 
This is Paul’s “Therefore”. 
Because of all that God has done for us, even when we aren’t involved in
actively worshiping, we’re still supposed to offer our lives in service to Him.

Before the Lord’s Supper, then, let’s reflect on all this and
how it comes together in our lives.  If
we believe the Bible at all, the sacrifice of Christ is emotionally
overwhelming.  Who could believe that
sinless God would die for sinful man, except that it already has happened?  That emotion, though, isn’t supposed to slosh
around inside us.  Instead, it’s supposed
to be a tidal wave that transforms our lives, that moves us to sing when we
would otherwise be silent, that moves us to live for God when we would
otherwise serve ourselves.  Is that how
we actually are worshiping and living? 
Let’s reflect on all this now as we partake.
