I am not what they call an “active reader”.  I am a habitual reader and a voracious
reader, but I never sit down, writing implement in hand, to underline or
highlight my way through a text.  This is
just as true when I read the Bible today as it was when I did the required
readings (yes, I actually read all those Great Works of Western Literature) in
college 15 years ago.  I do fine
on reading-comprehension tests, but active reading has never been for me.

However, even though I would never want to employ a
wide-margin for the purpose of active reading, another possible use of such a
Bible does appeal to me:  as a
respository for sermon notes.  Here, I
don’t mean notes on the sermons of others (I rarely hear them, and when I do, I
will almost certainly have a small travel Bible with me), but rather notes from
which to preach my own sermons.  I’d
actually been batting the idea around for several months before my Cambridge
Wide-Margin came into my possession.



The first obstacle to overcome in turning my Wide-Margin
into a sermon notebook was my aversion to writing in it in the first
place.  Yes, I know those wide margins
are *for* writing in, but the Bible is
currently the most expensive edition I own. 
What if I copied down half a sermon and realized that it wouldn’t fit or
I didn’t like it?  I’d hate to give a
marked-up Bible like that to somebody else!

In response, I decided to listen to Jesus (always a good
idea!) and count the cost before I committed. 
I took a blank sheet of paper, marked off a section an inch wide and
equal in height to the correct section of text in the Wide-Margin, and wrote
about the first third of a sermon’s notes in that section with a Pigma Micron
005 pen.

This assured me that I could, indeed, write notes small
enough to fit into that space, yet large enough for me to read them.  However, the notes I produced with that
exercise weren’t any different from the notes I might have written out on a
sheet of notebook paper.  They didn’t
interact with the text.  I tried a couple doodles on the same sheet of
paper in which I pretended to be interacting with a text, but I soon realized
that the only way to truly experience that interaction was to write in the
Bible.

The next Friday, I plunged in.  I sat down with the Bible, took a deep
breath, and started writing the high points of my sermon (an exposition of the
last part of John 20 and all of John 21) in the margin.  I used the same Pigma Micron pen as before,
and I underlined key words using a metal ruler. 
I invariably type out a full-sentence script of my sermons as part of my
preparation process, so my marginal summaries were essentially a final draft.

As to be expected, there were hiccups.  I realized too late that when writing in the
outside margin of left-hand pages, I ought to be propping my (left) hand on a
book of equal height.  The difference in
height led to some shaky penmanship (not that my handwriting isn’t awful
anyway).  I made two mistakes and
scratched them out.  Someday, I may white
them out if they offend me sufficiently.

I also had trouble
writing in the inner margins.  It
honestly escapes me why Cambridge doesn’t make those wider!  However, propping a book under the right side
of the Bible made both inner margins more accessible.  Finally, when I was writing in the
free-and-easy (for me) outside margin on the right-hand page, I wasn’t paying
attention and crumpled up the lower left page corner with my elbow.

Even with all the mishaps, though, I loved the
experience.  First of all, I simply
enjoyed the sensation of copying my sermon notes by hand into something
(relatively) permanent.  It felt like I
was creating an object of worth, which I suppose I actually am.  It was like being a scribe working on the
Book of Kells (although in real life, as terrible as my handwriting is, the
abbot would have consigned me to peeling turnips instead).  Even if nobody else ever looks at the thing,
I’ll be able to revisit my prior thoughts on the text myself.

Second, though, the process of annotation taught me quite a
bit about how I read the Bible.  Though
my prep process is very structured, it’s also very intuitive.  When I’m writing an expository sermon (which
is my norm), I first scratch out an outline of the major divisions and
subdivisions of the text in about 15 minutes. 
Then, with my Bible in my lap, I spend about three hours of work time
(plus the inevitable potty breaks, stretch breaks, Internet breaks, etc.)
composing the full-sentence 3700-word script in Microsoft Word.

During the process of composition, I write almost entirely
by feel.  I don’t laboriously reason my
way from proposition to proposition; instead, I say, “That feels right,” or
“That doesn’t feel right; I need to go back and rewrite that.”  I’m a natural enough writer that what feels
right to me also makes sense.

However, when I returned to the text to physically connect
my arguments with it, I saw for the first time the analysis under my
intuition.  In dealing with each
subsection of Scripture (in this sermon, 2-4 verses), I identified a few key
words or phrases and used those key words as a skeleton for the rest of my
argument.  With very sparing underlining,
I was able to identify essentially every part of the text I discussed.

This, I think, will make me a more analytically rigorous
preacher than I have been before—because my understanding of my own analysis is
now better.  I’ll be able to say to the
congregation, “In this chunk of Scripture, focus on this phrase and this
phrase.”  Those who are so inclined will
be able to mark those key points in their own Bibles.  They may not agree with my analysis, but at
worst, I will have spotted the issues for them.



In sum, it appears that my fears about writing in the
Wide-Margin were groundless.  I can see
myself using this Bible for a long time, week by week summarizing my thinking
about the Scripture, creating my own Book of Kells.
