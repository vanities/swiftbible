I’d never heard "I Am the Bread of Life", or even heard of it, before somebody
posted a link to a YouTube of it on the “I’m fed up with bad church music”
Facebook group.  [Here's the link.](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJQd-qG4qGQ)  Many of the comments concerned the
performance, but I wasn’t terribly interested in that.  I can only imagine what the Internet would
say about a recording of me singing!

What did interest me, though, was what the performance
revealed about the strengths and the weaknesses of the hymn.  I don’t believe that the true test of a
congregational hymn is what a really good singer/choir/band can do with
it.  Instead, the quality of a hymn
appears when you hand it to a bunch of ordinary people in an auditorium
someplace and ask them to worship with it.



First, the good.  I
think the content is strong here.  When I heard the hymn, I knew at once that the lyrics were taken from John 6.  It’s an extraordinarily rich text, and a
hymn that is able to capture it will be a good hymn.

Of course, the trick lies in the capturing.  I’ve been defeated by John 6 a time or two
myself.  Generally speaking, both the
gospel of John and the Johannine epistles are extremely difficult to
paraphrase, and hymnists who venture there had better know what they’re doing.

It is true that “I Am the Bread of Life” is a “voice of God”
hymn, a hymn written from the perspective of deity.  That approach bugs a lot of people, and I
sympathize with them to some extent.  It
does seem a little presumptuous, after all.

However, it’s worth noting that there are several psalms
(Psalm 50 comes particularly to mind here) that are largely written in the
divine first person.  Apparently Asaph
didn’t have any issues with voice-of-God psalms.   It’s
not a technique I use, really, but if you’re going to use it, John 6 is a good
place for that.

There are lots of hymns, though, that have sound Biblical
content that nobody wants to sing.  “I Am
the Bread of Life” has something that they don’t.  It has a phenomenal core thought.  The chorus reads:

*And I will raise him
up,*

*And I will raise him
up,*

*And I will raise him
up*

*On the last day.*

Now that is something that believers want to sing about!  It’s really, *really* important that Jesus promises a resurrection of life to His
disciples.  That’s what I’m going to be
thinking about when I’m dying (assuming I get the chance to think first), and
it is well worth singing and meditating on in the meantime.

The music works well here too.  The range is only an octave, and the melody
builds nicely with each repetition.  I’ve
found myself singing this in the shower, and anything that sticks in my head
like that is good.  I’m nearly certain
the chorus was written first.

Sadly, the rest of the hymn is an illustration of how you
can begin with strong content and a powerful chorus, yet produce a hymn that
isn’t suitable for the congregation. 
There are two fatal flaws here, and both of them show up in the
performance.

The first is disastrously broken lyrical meter throughout
the verses.  Generally speaking, a hymn
that people can sing together easily will have the same number of syllables in
the corresponding line of each verse. 
The first verse of “Amazing Grace” has eight syllables, as does the
second, third, fourth, fifth, and so on. 
When a hymn with consistent meter is set in the staff, you will see neat
little columns of syllables and words from top to bottom.

As the above photograph reveals, though, “I Am the Bread of
Life” doesn’t look like that.  Instead,
its appearance reminds me of something I saw growing up in semi-rural
Missouri.  My folks lived out in the
country enough that we had plenty of redneck neighbors, and every so often, one
of the locals would take it into his head to practice his marksmanship on the
speed-limit sign across the street from our house.

Basically, the sheet music for “I Am the Bread of Life” looks
like it’s been shot up by a good ole boy with a .22.  There are only a few neat columns of
words.  Instead, you’ve got a semi-random
collection of words and holes where words ought to be.

That doesn’t look pretty on the page, and it sounds even
worse than it looks.  Broken lyrical
meter forces all sorts of counter-intuitive rhythms on the music.  A soloist can navigate a hymn with badly
broken meter, much like the soloist does here. 
She pretty much makes up her own rhythm as she goes along, and if she’s
the only one singing, hey, whatever.

However, the congregation can’t follow her because they
can’t anticipate what the musical rhythm is going to be.  They want to. 
You can tell.  At the very
beginning of the hymn, they’re pretty enthusiastic about “I am the bread of
life”.

After a line or two, though, most of the congregational
singers have dropped out.  They’re
worried that they won’t be able to follow the soloist through the rat-a-tat
rhythm of “and who believes in me shall not thirst,” so they choose not to sing instead.  They come back in on the
chorus and on other parts where the rhythm is predictable, but if they ain’t
certain, they ain’t singing!

The second problem is the range.  The range on the chorus is fine.  The range for the hymn overall is nearly two
octaves, from A3 to F#5.  The soloist
obviously bottoms out during the low parts, and the congregation fares even
worse.  By contrast, most hymn tunes that
have endured don’t have a range of more than an octave because that’s about
what a group of untrained singers will be comfortable with.

The sheet music above (from *The Presbyterian Hymnal*, I think) gives the game away when it notes
that the verses should be sung by “Leader or All”.  In real life, this means that the verses
should be sung by “Leader” because the congregation can’t manage them.  The broken meter and extended range are too
much for untrained singers to overcome.

People who think that a worship service should be a
performance are OK with that.  I’m
not.  The power of congregational worship
lies in ordinary Christians singing together, and a good hymn will invite the
people in the pews to participate.  “I Am
the Bread of Life” has buckets of potential, but it spends half its time
broadcasting the message “You’re not wanted.” 
The result can only be worship that is half what it should have been.
