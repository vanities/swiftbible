The second verse of the contemporary song “Light the Fire”,
plus the chorus, reads:

*I feel Your arms around me*

*As the power of Your
healing begins*

*Your Spirit moves
through me*

*Like a mighty rushing
wind*

*Light the fire*

*In my soul*

*Fan the flame*

*Make me whole*

*Lord, You know*

*Just where I've been*

*So light the fire*

*In my heart again*

Why not sing it just as it is written?

“Light the Fire” undeniably has a lot going for it.  Even a curmudgeon like me will acknowledge
that the tune is fitting and beautiful. 
I’d love to have written to it, though the composer and I would have had
a talk about following the rules of common-practice harmony.  The lyrics are obviously sincere, and they’re
emotionally powerful.  For that matter,
they even contain a strong Scriptural link. 
There’s not a doubt in my mind what Biblical text this verse is about.

Why not sing it?



For most (members of charismatic faith traditions obviously
excepted), the content is the problem.  The
language of the verse is clearly taken from Acts 2:1-4.

*When the day of Pentecost arrived, they
were all together in one place. And suddenly there came from heaven a sound
like a mighty rushing wind, and it filled the entire house where they were
sitting. And divided tongues as of fire appeared to them and rested on each one
of them. And they were all filled with the Holy Spirit and began to speak in
other tongues as the Spirit gave them utterance. (ESV)*

“Light the Fire” is, quite literally, a plea for a
Pentecostal experience.  That’s what you
get when you add Spirit plus mighty rushing wind plus fire.  Words mean things, and that’s what this song
means.

Your mileage may vary, but 99 percent of the people in my
religious tradition do not accept the existence of modern-day miraculous
spiritual gifts.  If the original second
verse of “Light the Fire” were to show up very much (more commonly, we use a
rewritten second verse of dubious legality), even people who don’t care about
hymn lyrics would start objecting.  “That’s
false doctrine!” they would cry.

From our perspective (and no, I’m not going to argue about
latter-day spiritual gifts on the Internet!), that’s exactly correct.  Others with different perspectives can
doubtless think of other hymns that would strike them in the same way.  For instance, Catholics probably shouldn’t be
singing the Martin Luther hymn about seeking God’s protection from the Pope and
the Turks.  Everybody’s got something
they would choke on in worship, even the Unitarians.

If asked, once they got past the “because it’s false doctrine!”
question-begging stage, our ecumenical assemblage of objectors (objecting to
different sacred songs, to be sure) would probably come up with a list of
reasons not to sing false doctrine (/heresy/whatever) that looks something like
this:

* It displeases God.
* I don’t believe that, so I can’t sing it without lying.
* It contradicts what we teach.
* It would confuse new believers, visitors, and children.

Those are all valid concerns, and a great many more could be
added to them.  Through all these possible explanations, though, a common thread runs. 
It is the intuition that hymns are supposed to teach, and indeed are
very good at teaching.  As a result, when
they teach the wrong thing, it’s a serious problem.

Hymn content matters. 
If it doesn’t matter, then it also doesn’t matter whether we sing hymns
containing false doctrine.  Conversely,
if we’re strongly opposed to false doctrine in hymns, it follows that hymn
content generally must be very important to us.

As a result, we should cock a quizzical eyebrow at those who
defend a doctrinally shallow hymn by exclaiming, “But at least it doesn’t teach
false doctrine!”  People who say such
things are implying that they care about content that is false but not content
that is true.  This doesn't make sense.  If hymn content
is important, then little to no content must be as problematic as bad content.

Let me illustrate. 
Imagine that I instruct my son, as I often do, to pick up his toys.  He responds in three ways (as he often does,
sequentially):

1. Rather than putting toys in the toy bins, he begins to pull
   more toys out.
2. He plays with the toys already out while making no effort to
   pick them up.
3. He takes a single toy car on the far side of the room from
   the toy bins and begins to “drive” it across the floor toward the bins, slowly,
   making engine noises as he goes.  When
   rebuked, he replies indignantly, “I *am* picking
   up!”  Indeed, the car probably is going
   to arrive at the toy bins in, oh, half an hour or so.

Maybe I’m just a mean dad, but none of this strikes me as particularly
expressive of a desire to obey.

Our heavenly Father has made His will clear to us.  He wants us to teach and admonish one another
in psalms and hymns and spiritual songs. 
That’s a goal that requires content, and the more the better.

Nor can we legitimately argue, "This hymn is for worship, not teaching, so it doesn't have to say much."  The book of Psalms, the only inspired hymnal we've got, contains 150 expressions of worship.  How many of them would we describe as low on content?

We recognize the issues with untrue content, but there’s no
reason why content-free or content-light hymns would be any more satisfying to
Him.  Pointing to the name of God here or
a toddler-level spiritual thought there and saying, “It’s fine to sing this,”
is akin to crying, “But I *am* picking
up!”  Let’s be honest.  When we sing a hymn that’s fun to sing even
though it doesn’t say very much, whom are we seeking to please?

Content is king.  If
you’ve got good content, you’ve got the makings of a good hymn.  Conversely, a hymn without good content
cannot be good.  False doctrine is
certainly a way for a hymn to have poor content.  However, we must acknowledge that it’s far
from the only one.
