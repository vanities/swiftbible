A few days back, my friend Steve Wolfgang linked to [this article](http://www.chronicle.com/article/Why-All-Humanists-Should-Go-to/237878).  It’s written by a humanities professor who
was used to teaching in the tech-saturated world of today’s colleges but spent
a year volunteer-teaching in a women’s prison, where there were no electronic
teaching aids at all.  During that time,
she learned that technology could be a crutch as much as an aid, that an hour
of instruction filled with PowerPoint and YouTube videos wasn’t necessarily
more effective than an hour with none of those “aids”, and might in fact be
less.

Of course, technology mania is hardly limited to academia.  In a world in which the latest iPhone release
is celebrated like it’s V-J Day, technology is as omnipresent as air and (we
assume) as beneficial.  Digital
everything is better, and we think that goes for religion too.



In fact, many Christians look to technology to solve some of
the most vexing problems of our faith. 
Have trouble getting your Bible reading in?  If you put the Bible on your smartphone, it will
travel with you wherever you go!  Is the
congregation given to mumbly, apathetic navel-gazing during worship?  Adopt a PowerPoint hymnal, and the singing
will improve immediately!  Trouble
reaching the lost?  Just do some. . .
thing on the Internet, and they’ll flock to our doors!  In reality, of course, few of these high-tech
solutions actually end up solving anything.

The problem here is not technology.  2000 years ago, our brethren were
technologically savvy.  Early Christians
were among the first to move from using scrolls to using codices.  A codex is made up of a number of sheets of some
writing surface (paper, parchment, etc.), usually bound with a cover, instead
of being a continual roll like a scroll. 
Codices are more durable, easier to transport, and easier to use, all of
which helped the gospel to spread. 
Anything that we can lawfully use to glorify God, we should use.

However, even though technology is good for any number of
things (such as maintaining a Bible-oriented blog that would have been
unthinkable 30 years ago), it is no match for problems in the heart.  I can keep the Bible on my phone.  I can find a website that will send me a
daily reminder to read my Bible. 
However, if I myself am not determined to spend time in the word, all of
that will do me no good whatsoever.  The
congregation that is uninterested in worshiping with hymnals will probably
still be uninterested in worshiping from the PowerPoint.  Similarly, even though a well-designed
website will generate visitors now and again, the secret to evangelism still lies
in ordinary Christians with a burning desire to share the gospel.  If the members of a congregation aren’t
willing to do that, no amount of high-tech wizardry will help.

In fact, if we look to technology to help us duck our
responsibilities rather than fulfilling them, it may actually take us
backwards.  Sure, you can put the Bible
on your phone, but for most of us, the Bible isn’t the only thing on there.  It’s tough to check Facebook on a paper
Bible, but it’s very easy to do that on a smartphone, even in the middle of
Sunday-morning services.  If our
attention isn’t focused on God’s word in the assembly, it’s not going to happen
anywhere else either.

Technology gives us power. 
It does not give us goodness.  It
does not make us better people, and every spiritual problem we have can only be
solved by becoming better people.  I can
use the Internet to confirm my recollection that early Christians were big into
codex use (thank you, Wikipedia!), but I can also use it to practice a dozen
different kinds of sin without ever rising from my office chair.  They make porn filters, but I don’t think
anybody has yet invented a gossip filter. 
How about an envy filter?  A
self-righteousness filter?  All these and
more are only a mouse click away.  Only
my unfiltered, unfettered heart will determine which way I go.

If we’re headed in the right direction, technology will help
us get there faster (Matthew 13:12 in operation again?), but it can’t get us
moving in the first place.  There is no
substitute for the spiritual disciplines that predate all technology:  unceasing prayer, unstinting self-reflection,
and an undying yearning, not for our own creations, but for the One who created
us.
