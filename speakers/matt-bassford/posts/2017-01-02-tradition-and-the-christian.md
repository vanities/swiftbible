A couple months ago in Bible
class, as we were studying our way through the book of Daniel, of all things,
the discussion turned to religious tradition. 
Honestly, that’s about as combustible a topic as can come up in a Bible
class.  In any congregation of the Lord’s
people, there will be a wide range of attitudes toward tradition.  Some Christians, particularly older ones,
have an attachment to our customary way of doing things and are suspicious of
change, while others, particularly younger ones, don’t understand the attachment
and don’t see any reason not to change.

Among ourselves, we can argue
back and forth about these things until the stars fall from the sky, yet never
get anywhere.  In this area, along with
anything else pertaining to life and godliness, if we want to uncover the truth
about the topic, we have to turn to the word. 
Let’s consider the Scripture this morning, then, so we can come to
understand the relationship between tradition and the Christian.



**Jesus on Tradition**

For the first part of this
study, let’s consider what Jesus had to say about tradition.  We learn about **THE TRADITION** in question in Mark 7:1-4.  There are two important elements in this
description:  what they’re doing, and why
they’re doing it.  Today, if we saw the
Pharisees in action, we would probably think they were germophobes.  About to eat? 
Wash first.  Come home from the
market?  Wash.  Got a dish? 
Better wash it.  However, they
didn’t go through all this washing because it was required by the Law of Moses.  Instead, as Mark says, they were following
the tradition of the elders.  The Law did
require some washings.  For instance, if
you touched a dead body or something else that was unclean, you were supposed
to bathe.  However, the Jews expanded
this so that you washed if you might have touched something unclean.

It’s extremely important to
recognize all of this because it tells us that here, Jesus is dealing with
human tradition.  These are traditions
that God does not require or bind, but that some religious group has come up with
anyway.  In fact, every religious group
does this.  Even in this church, we have
human traditions.  We traditionally meet
for worship at 10:00 Sunday morning, have our song worship conducted by a song
leader, sing in four-part harmony, and serve the Lord’s Supper by passing
around trays.  All of those things are
traditions, and there’s nothing inherently wrong about having a tradition.

However, as the story
continues, it reveals three problems that often accompany human tradition.  The first of these is that it can lead us to **CONFUSE IT WITH LAW.** This is exactly what we see the Pharisees
doing in Mark 7:5-8.  They have their
little hand-washing tradition.  That’s
fine; it doesn’t hurt anybody.  However,
Jesus hasn’t been teaching His disciples to wash before eating, so the
Pharisees demand an explanation for why they are eating with “impure”
hands.

Because Jesus is Jesus, He
counterattacks in devastating fashion. 
He points out that the real problem here isn’t the disciples.  Even though they’re eating with unwashed
hands, they aren’t breaking the Law in any way. 
Instead, the problem lies with the Pharisees, who have elevated their
tradition to the level of the commandment of God and criticize tradition-breakers
as harshly as they would commandment-breakers. 
The Pharisees have every right to follow their tradition, but they have
no right to bind it on others.

Despite the condemnation of
the Lord, the religious world around us is filled with those who insist on
binding their traditions on others.  Sadly,
churches of Christ are not immune to this, and we must watch ourselves to make
sure that we carefully distinguish between our own human tradition and the
commandment of God.  For instance, for
the past several years here, we’ve adopted a consolidated morning service
rather than meeting on Sunday night. 
It’s our right to do that, but we do not have the right to criticize
other congregations that do have an evening service on Sundays.  That would be binding our tradition on them.

Second, human tradition can
lead to **SETTING ASIDE THE LAW.** Jesus explains how this happened to the
Pharisees in Mark 7:9-13.  This time too,
it sounds like the Pharisees started with a commandment, the commandment to
thankfully give back to God from all He had blessed them with.  However, from that commandment, they
developed a tradition called Corban. 
Basically, Corban was like a tag or an earmark.  You said, “I’m going to pledge to give this
money to God at a later date, but I’m not going to give it now.”  Once money became Corban, though, you
couldn’t spend it on anything else because then you couldn’t fulfill your
vow.  Even if dear old Mom is starving to
death across the street, nope, that money now belongs to God even though you
still have it.  Sorry, Mom!  As a result, the Pharisees could use their
tradition of Corban to invalidate the black-letter commandment to honor their
parents.

This too happens all the time
today.  If we all randomly decided to
visit some denominational church this morning, we probably wouldn’t have to
wait five minutes before we saw somebody setting aside the law of God because
of their tradition.  The commandment
says, “Sing”, but they have a tradition of instrumental music in worship, so
that’s what they do.  The commandment
says that women aren’t to teach or exercise authority in the church, but their
tradition allows for female pastors, so they have them.  Here too, we must be extremely careful lest
one of our traditions assume more importance than the word of God.

Finally, tradition can lead
to **MISUNDERSTANDING RIGHTEOUSNESS.** The Lord elaborates in Mark 7:14-23.  He wanted His followers to understand that
even though ceremonially washing your hands all the time wasn’t sinful, it did
lead to a skewed view of spirituality. 
Being traditionally clean all the time didn’t make you a better
person.  Even if you ate your food with
unclean hands, the food still went into you and eventually passed out of you
without defiling your heart. However, because the Pharisees were so caught up
in obsessing over things that didn’t matter, it led them to downplay the things
that did matter, that did defile the heart. 
Having clean hands didn’t matter if you had an unclean heart.  All the evil thoughts, the hatred and the lust
and the greed, would eventually make their way out and entrap you in sin.

Once again, we could wag our
fingers at plenty of others who get this wrong, but we will be much better off
if we soberly consider ourselves to make sure that we aren’t getting this
wrong.  I can certainly understand the
appeal of tradition.  I was raised in the
church, and I can’t remember a time when I did not attend services that were
very, very similar to ours here.  I like
knowing that when I attend some other church of Christ when I’m on vacation,
things will go very similarly there too. 
However, joining in comfortable, traditional worship does not make me
righteous if I live wickedly all week. 
The tradition counts for nothing. 
What counts is obedience from the heart.

Before the Lord’s Supper,
let’s think about these things.  Let’s
remember our Lord and His uncompromising insistence on truth over
tradition.  Let’s remember His death at
the hands of His enemies, a death that He died for us.  Let’s also examine ourselves and ask whether
we have embraced the genuine faith that pleases Him, or a traditional sham that
pleases only ourselves.

**Apostolic Tradition**

However, human tradition,
tradition that is at best spiritually neutral and at worst soul-destroyingly
disastrous, isn’t the only kind of tradition in the New Testament.  Instead, New-Testament writers also spend
some time dwelling on apostolic tradition, which is a very different kind of
tradition entirely.  In this context,
we’re instructed to **HOLD TO
TRADITION.** Look at 2 Thessalonians
2:13-17.  On the surface, this passage
follows a familiar story arc.  We are
chosen and called by God through His word. 
We are saved through the sanctification of the spirit and our faith in
the truth.  In all of this, God’s goal
for us is that we should attain the glory of Christ and spend eternity in
heaven with Him.

There are many passages that
say similar things, but the so-what here is unusual.  Because we’ve gotten this far, Paul says,
because God has such great things planned for us, there’s something that we
must do.  We must hold firmly to the
traditions that Paul handed down.  In the
case of the Thessalonians, whose church had been established by Paul, this
tradition was twofold.  First, they had
the traditions he imparted by letter. 
Second, they had the traditions he had passed on by word of mouth.  Today, of course, 2000 years later, neither
we nor anybody else have the foggiest idea what Paul said unless somebody then
wrote it down.

However, we still have the
epistles that he wrote, to the Thessalonians as well as to other churches and
people, and in them, along with the other books of the New Testament, we have
the apostolic tradition preserved.  Human
tradition is irrelevant; the tradition of the apostles is essential.

If we have any doubt about
this, we should consider what Paul says about the connection between **JESUS AND TRADITION.** This is indirect, but I think it’ still
important.  Read with me from 2
Thessalonians 3:1-5.  Paul begins here,
as he often does, by requesting the prayers of the church to which he is
writing.  If Paul thought he needed
prayers, how much more do we!

Second, though, we see Paul
expressing his confidence.  He’s
confident first of all that Jesus is faithful to strengthen and protect His
people.  Next, he’s confident that the
Thessalonians are determined to follow the traditions that they have received
from Paul.  Put those things together,
and we see the reason for the indirect prayer he offers next.  He wants Jesus to help the Thessalonians stay
faithful to his teaching.

This offers important balance
to our understanding of the Lord’s perspective on tradition.  We might come away from Mark 7 thinking that
Jesus was anti-tradition, but that’s hardly the case!  Even when it came to human tradition, the
Pharisees’ problem wasn’t their tradition of hand-washing. It was all the other
things that tradition led them to.  As a
result, Jesus never tells them to stop. 
When it comes to apostolic tradition, though, it’s obvious that Jesus is
emphatically in favor.  Following the
apostles is following Jesus.

Finally, let’s contemplate
what Paul says about **LEARNING
TRADITION.** Consider 2 Thessalonians
3:6-10.  This reading is actually part of
a larger context about Christians who refuse to work, but this morning, we’re
not interested in the point of Paul’s argument, merely how he gets there.  Paul wants us to understand first of all that
this is a matter of tradition.  Christians
who are providing for themselves are living by the tradition, but those who
live an unruly life aren’t.

Next, though, Paul tells us
about how he communicated this tradition to the Thessalonians.  We’re going to take his methods here in
reverse order.  The second method is one
we’ve already seen.  While in
Thessalonica, he taught the church that those who wouldn’t work shouldn’t eat.

The first, though, is
unfamiliar territory for us.  Paul says
that he communicated tradition not only directly, by word of mouth, but
indirectly, by example.  While in
Thessalonica, even though he had the right to support from the church, he chose
to support himself by his own hard work so that he could teach the importance
of hard work by actions as well as words. 
Today, then, when we look for the teaching of the apostles, we must
consider what they did as well as what they said.  Never in Scripture do we see Paul commanding
Christians to assemble on the first day of the week to partake of the Lord’s
Supper, but when he assembles for that purpose in Acts 20, his example has as
much force for us as his command would.

**Judging Tradition**

With these things in mind,
then, let’s turn our attention to three questions that we can ask to judge
tradition today.  The first of these is **“IS IT APOSTOLIC?”** Paul himself points out why the question
is important in 1 Corinthians 11:1-2.  As
his language reveals, following apostolic tradition is equivalent to imitating
Christ.  The first-century church was
built on the foundation of the apostles and the prophets.  If we no longer have any interest in
apostolic teaching, we have lost our connection to that church.

This is all the more
important to remember when so many cultural influences and even churches are
encouraging us to abandon the teaching of the apostles.  Sure, it might seem like a trendy, even a
compassionate, thing to do to tone down the apostolic revelation on sexual
immorality and the reality of eternal punishment, to name a couple of unpopular
subjects.  A lot of outsiders might be
more interested in worshiping with us if we didn’t step on their toes quite so much.  However, making sure that we’re in step with
contemporary culture should be the least of our concerns.  It’s much more important to keep our feet on
the path that was blazed by the earliest followers of Christ.  To follow them is to follow Him.

However, if a tradition is
not genuinely apostolic in its origin, we must ask, **“IS IT GODLY?”** Consider
Paul’s warning in Colossians 2:8.  In
this text, we must first recognize that Paul assumes we’re going to be taken
captive by somebody.  We’re going to belong
to somebody.  The only question is
whether we are going to be taken captive according to Christ or according to worldly
thinking and the tradition of men.

This is not to say, of
course, that a church that belongs to Christ cannot have any human traditions.  I think it has to.  As a practical matter, the New Testament
doesn’t go into enough detail about every aspect of church function that we can
rely entirely on it.  For instance,
despite all the jokes about Scriptural meeting times, we all know that there
are no such things, and yet, unless we develop some tradition about that, the
brethren won’t know when to assemble.  We
understand that while Jesus and the apostles have handed down the essentials to
us, they also give us liberty to suit our practice to our circumstances.

That’s not the question.  The question is who has taken us
captive.  Is it our traditions, or is it
Jesus?  When it came to the Pharisees, there
was no question.  They clearly loved
their own made-up rules more than they loved God.  This isn’t just a Pharisee problem.  It can easily be an us problem too, and when
we reach that point, we too have crossed over into teaching as doctrine the
commandments of men.

Finally, if a tradition isn’t
taken from the Scriptures, but at the same time doesn’t look like an attempt to
supersede the Scriptures, before we adopt and continue it, we must ask, **“IS IT WISE?”** This is, of course, a general principle
of godly living, as we see Paul indicate in Ephesians 5:15-16.  As Christians, we have an obligation to live
wisely and make the most of the opportunities we have, and this is no less true
for our church than it is for us as individuals.  We have liberty in Christ, but we ought to be
using that liberty as effectively as possible for His glory.

This is a simple enough
statement, but in practice, it gets complicated.  What to one Christian looks like a pointless
ritual unthinkingly carried forward since Noah got off the ark looks to another
Christian like a cherished part of our spiritual heritage.  Generally, the truth lies somewhere in the
middle.  Somebody once had a reason for
beginning every tradition we practice, but it can be easy to continue the
tradition even when the reason no longer applies.

The answer here, as it is
with so much of our walk together, is a mixture of wisdom, love, and bearing
with.  On the one hand, some of us need
to recognize that making a minor improvement is not a sufficient reason to
cause a major blowup.  On the other hand,
others of us need to see that one of the strengths of the first-century pattern
is its flexibility and adaptability, and if we don’t take advantage of those
things, the church will be less than it could be.  Only when we embrace both of those truths can
we work together effectively and advance the Lord’s work.
