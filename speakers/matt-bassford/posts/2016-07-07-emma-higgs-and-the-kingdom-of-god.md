We live in a world that grows increasingly impatient with
nuance.  Either you are on Their Side and
are all wrong, or you are on My Side and are all right.  That doesn’t leave much room for people who
are on Their Side but have managed to be right about something.

I think the author of [this post](https://emmahiggs.com/2015/10/07/rethinking-christianity-the-message-of-jesus/) is one of those people.  I don’t know why
it wandered across my Facebook feed nine months after it was written, but it
did.  All I know about the author is what
she puts in her “About Me”.

When first I started reading it, I could feel my eyes
beginning to drift irresistibly backward in my head.  I thought to myself, “I’ve seen this movie
before.”  Yeah, yeah; traditional,
conservative religion is too focused on heaven and hell and not enough on
Jesus.  Never mind that the New-Testament
figure with the most to say about hell is. . . Jesus.



However, I kept reading, and much to my surprise, the author
started saying some interesting things about the kingdom of God.  Even though the kingdom stands at the heart
of Jesus’ teaching, it remains a slippery subject.  The author seems to think a lot of
traditional Christians equate the kingdom of God with heaven.  That’s part of it.  I worship with plenty of folks who think the
kingdom of God is the church, founded on the day of Pentecost in 33 AD.  That’s part of it too.

The kingdom, though, is more than that, maybe more than we
want it to be.  If I may attempt to add
to Matthew 13, the kingdom of heaven is like folding up a Pack N Play.  You keep on trying to collapse it down into a
nice neat little bundle, but there’s always a joint you’ve forgotten to unlock
or a button you’ve failed to push, so that rather than ending up with a nice
neat bundle, you end up with something that looks like a newborn giraffe having
an epileptic fit.

That, I think, is what the kingdom of heaven is like.  However, the untidiness isn’t in our failures
to live up to God’s standard (though I certainly do fail with dreary
regularity).  The untidiness is in what
is God is doing, intruding Himself into our lives in ways that we do not expect
and are not prepared to handle.

Human beings are great systematizers.  Look at what we build:  houses, buildings, and streets with lots of
sharp lines and right angles.  If we
could make square wheels and they would work, we probably would.

That’s not how God builds. 
He is a God of great order, but He doesn’t move in straight lines a
whole lot.  If He did, weather
forecasters wouldn’t be wrong more often than they are right!  On top of the order, there’s always a whole
bunch of chaos and mystery that conceals the end from everyone but the One who
willed it.

I’ve spent my life committed to the proposition that an
ordinary man can read and understand the Bible for himself.  That’s true, and yet at the same time, the
word is ultimately unfathomable.  I
know preachers in their eighties who affirm that they’re constantly learning
new things from studying passages they were studying 50 years ago.  We’re not supposed to be able to conquer the
Bible.  It’s supposed to conquer us.

We need to be careful, then, of the conviction that we’ve
got everything about faith figured out. 
We have to work from what we know, but we should never think that we
know everything, nor that God is satisfied with our current working.  Saying, “I have arrived!” is a very human
thing to do, not a very God-like thing to do. 
It’s another way of saying, “I don’t want the kingdom of God to grow in
me more than it already has.”

That spirit has nothing to do with the spirit of Christ and
His earliest followers.  Jesus bewildered
everyone but Himself.  He never
encouraged anybody in the conviction that they had arrived.  The early disciples bewildered everyone including
themselves.  They had no idea where this
“the Way” thing was headed.  They went
where they went, in weakness, fear, and trembling, because they knew Jesus was
Lord, and they were going to follow that conviction wherever it led.

Here is where I start to part company, again, from Emma
Higgs.  I like a lot of what she
says.  I agree with her that the coming
of the kingdom of God involves Christians going about doing good.

However, that too is only a part of its coming.  I sense in her an unwillingness to deviate
from what she personally believes is right. 
She’s the type to declare the practice of homosexuality OK because she
thinks it’s OK and besides, aren’t a whole lot of gay people really nice?  She is, I think, attempting to impose her own
order on the kingdom of God.

I don’t think we get to do that.  None of us will ever get the divine
Pack N Play fully stowed for travel, and if we do, it’s because we’ve taken a
hammer to the parts that stuck out inconveniently.  Instead, we have to say “Jesus is Lord” and
follow where that leads.

If we’re doing it right, “Jesus is Lord” will lead to places
that frighten us, that pierce our hearts, that transform us.  Safe, predictable discipleship isn’t really
discipleship at all.  It’s a ceasefire
with the flesh, where we promise not to launch any more attacks on evil without
and within so long as it pretty much leaves us alone (note:  the devil always lies about ceasefires).  The times in our lives when things change
under our feet, when we head off in a new direction with no sense of our
destination but a certainty that Jesus wants us to go there, those are the
times of the inbreaking of the kingdom of God. 
Only when our spirit cries out “Jesus is Lord!” and every part of our
bodies and our lives replies “Amen!” has the kingdom truly come.
