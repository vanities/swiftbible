When first I began to post Bible reviews, the very first
series I did was called “My Perfect Bible”, and in it, I set out the
characteristics that I thought made for the best possible Bible for me.  I based this, of course, on the Bibles I had
opportunity to experience.  Since that
time, though, in a year of Bible reviewing, I’ve had quite a few more Bibles
pass through my hands.  In the course of
examining them, I learned some things I didn’t know before, and, just as
importantly, unlearned some things I thought I knew.

I decided, then, that rather than continuing to judge Bibles
against a standard I knew was flawed, I was going to revise the standard.  This begins, of course, with re-assessing
what I like and don’t like in the physical form of Bibles.



**SIZE.** This is one of the largest changes to my
evaluation system.  I spent all of 2014
writing Traveling posts about how various editions served as carry Bibles.  Really, though, those posts were much more
about form than they were about utility, which made them less like Preaching,
Teaching, and Study  and Reading on the
one hand, and more like Physical Form on the other.  Also, I feel like my reviews have unfairly
penalized big Bibles for being big.  Size
is important, but it isn’t nearly as important as a Bible’s effectiveness in
the pulpit!  In consequence, I decided to
collapse the Traveling category into Physical Form and allot each part of my
new three-part system 10 points, for a new Overall Score out of 30 instead of
20.

**COVER.** When I wrote the “My Perfect Bible”
series last year, my Bible collection was fairly proletarian.  There was some genuine leather, but it was
devoid of goatskin, calfskin, or even cowhide. 
Today, my collection is still pretty much proletarian, but I have
accumulated some high-end Bibles too.

Now, I tend to evaluate Bible covers according to two main
criteria:  durability and
lovability.  The first is pretty
obvious:  How long does the cover look
like it’s going to hold up?  This begins
with the material of which the cover is made. 
I’ve never used a bonded-leather Bible to the point where the cover is
destroyed (traditionally, the binding fails first), but I have trashed up a
Bible with a polyurethane cover (the Crossway Personal-Size Reference I
reviewed about a year ago) in five years. 
Today, I spend more time in the word, and would destroy a poly-Bible
cover quicker. 

Of course, not all poly Bibles are created equal.  Some have thicker covers than others; still
others have seams that feel like they would become failure points.  Though I’m really unlikely to use a poly
Bible to the point of destruction again, I try to assess the cover quality and
determine how well it will last.

For Bibles with covers made from the skins of various dead
animals, cover quality really shouldn’t be an issue.  I’ve learned this from observing my dad’s
genuine-leather 2002 Foundation Side-Column Reference.  Genuine leather is the lowliest grade of
leather cover, the covers on the genuine-leather ’02 SCR’s weren’t great
(later  SCR’s seem like they have thicker
covers to me), and the ’02 SCR text block is famously good, but in my dad’s
Bible, the text block is failing before the cover has failed.  The edges of the pages have frayed and
crumbled off, so that it looks like the Bible has been nibbled on by
silverfish, but the cover, though thoroughly abused, is still intact.

To my way of thinking, if a Bible cover has enough quality
to survive the text block, it has done its job, and any cover made out of
actual leather of any kind should outlast the paper of the text block.

Second, I look at endpapers to determine their
durability.  My tastes and my budget
don’t run to edge-lined Bibles, so my experience is limited to Bibles with
paste-down bindings.  These are Bibles in
which the cover is joined to the text block with some sort of endpaper,
generally either stiff paper or leatherette. 
Leatherette is a kind of paper with a vinyl coating on top that has been
impressed with a leather grain.  It takes
abuse better than paper endpapers do (I’ve had a hardback Bible fail at the
paper endpapers before), but sometimes, it can be prone to splitting where it’s
folded in half at the spine.  For
instance, the leatherette endpapers of the Crossway Legacy I reviewed last
September have begun to fail at the fold.

Finally, and much more enticingly, I evaluate covers
according to lovability:  to wit, does
the cover make me want to use the Bible? 
Is the cover pleasing to the eye? 
How does it feel when I hold it? 
Is the cover likely to soften up with use?  How does it smell?  I own an old Nelson ASV rebound by Leonard’s
in calfskin, and it smells like a leather boutique in the middle of a library.  I think they don’t make leather-and-library
women’s perfume because of 1 Corinthians 10:13. 
God knows it would be more temptation than I could handle, so He doesn’t
allow it to exist!

**TEXT BLOCK.** The more things change, the more things
stay the same.  I started off demanding
that my Bibles be Smyth-sewn, and a year later, I feel exactly the same
way.  There are a couple of ways to tell
whether a text block is sewn.  You can
gently push back the headband at the top of the spine inside the cover and look
at the binding.  If it looks like a bunch
of flat pages backed up against a glue strip, the binding is glued and utterly
detestable.  If it looks like a bunch of
little booklets packed tightly together, the binding is sewn, and it makes
angels sing with delight when they behold it.

Those little booklets are called signatures.  If you’re not sure whether you’re seeing
signatures at the top or not, you can check inside the Bible.  At the middle of a signature, if you press it
open as far as you can, you will see bookbinding thread running down the middle
of the gutter, where the two pages meet. 
Also, many sewn text blocks have cockling.  These are little bumps down the gutter that
crackle when you open the Bible or when you run your finger firmly down the
gutter.  They are annoying, and I won’t
use Bibles with bad cockling, but they are a sign that a Bible is sewn.

Sewn bindings are important for two main reasons.  First, sewn Bibles lie much flatter than
glued Bibles, so when you have the thing open, you aren’t constantly fighting
to keep it open.  Some glued Bibles feel
like they’re spring-loaded like a mousetrap!

Secondly, and most importantly, sewn Bibles are much more
durable.  Back before I knew anything
about Bibles, I owned a number of glued Bibles, and inevitably, within six
months to a year, the strip of glue down the back would split.  As a result, the Bible would begin to shed
pages or even clumps of pages.  By
contrast, I’ve never had a sewn Bible fail on me.

**VALUE.** Finally, I evaluate Bibles according to
the value they give for money. 
Otherwise, the physical-form rating would simply be a tiresome
restatement of cost, with expensive Bibles at the top and cheap Bibles at the
bottom.  Yes, an Allan Longprimer is a
better-constructed Bible than some TruTone ESV; so what?



Instead, I ask whether a Bible stands out from others at its
price point.  A hand-size poly reference
Bible that retails for $40 is nothing to write home about.  A hand-size calfskin reference Bible that
retails for $40, on the other hand, offers impressive value for money.  Because most of us aren’t Bible reviewers who
get Bibles for free (even I buy a fair number of Bibles), value for money
matters.
