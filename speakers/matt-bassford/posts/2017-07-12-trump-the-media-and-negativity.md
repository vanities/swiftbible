Donald Trump is the leader of our country.  It’s my Biblical responsibility to honor and
pray for him.  However, I am not a Trump
fan.  Unless he does some pretty
impressive repenting, I’m not ever going to be a Trump fan.

Having said that, and speaking as someone who has literally
not taken a partisan position this century, I can’t help but roll my eyes at
the unrelentingly negative media coverage of the president since his
inauguration.  Many in the media appear
to have abandoned any pretense of objectivity in their eagerness to bring down
a man they view as evil.



**The Result of
Negativity**

At least from my perspective, though, the media frenzy has
had the opposite effect from the one intended. 
Let me pick just one example.  When
Trump retweeted the GIF of him beating up on CNN, WWE-style, I thought it was
in bad taste.  I wouldn’t have done
it.

An awful lot of media types, though, reacted with pious
horror.  “Trump is inciting violence
against reporters!!!”  No; he’s not.  He’s trying to be funny and talk smack to his
opponents.  I see GIFs just like that one
on college-football fanboards all the time. 
Nobody thinks they’re trying to incite LSU fans to shoot Mississippi
State fans.  Lighten up, Francis.  (Also, unless some new information comes to
light, my reading of the relevant statutes is that Donald Trump, Jr.’s meeting
with a Russian lawyer was another bad idea but about a mile away from illegal.)

As a result of the unrelenting negativity, I find myself
much less willing to listen to Trump-haters (even though I share many of their
concerns).  If you’re so unhinged that
you’re going to clutch your pearls and scream over every little issue, simply
because it’s Trump who’s doing it, at least as far as I’m concerned, you’ve
lost your credibility on all things Trump. 
That kind of thing may whip hardcore Democrats into an even frothier
frenzy, but it alienates those who are not already ideologically committed.

**Religious Negativity**

Of course, I’m a religious writer, not a political
analyst.  I’m not so concerned with the
tactical mistake that the Democrats are making as I am with the possibility
that the Lord’s people might make the same mistake with respect to those who
differ from us.  I think it’s tempting
for preachers, especially preachers in the non-institutional churches of
Christ, to make a rhetorical living off of bashing the other guy.  All the denominations are bad.  All the mainstream churches of Christ are
bad.  Aren’t we glad that we’re not like
them in their badness!

The problem is that if we define ourselves by what we’re
against, we have failed to define ourselves by what we’re for.  All the other religious people out there
doing whatever should be irrelevant to our spiritual identity.  However, if all we do is complain about them
and what they’re doing wrong, we make them central to it.

**Obscuring the Gospel**

This has several ill effects.  First, it obscures the positive message of
the gospel.  I believe that we are called
to follow the pattern of the New Testament church.  I believe that Jesus’ call to go and make
disciples imposes significant limitations on the work of our local churches.

The logical implications of these convictions keep me from supporting
what a lot of other churches are doing. 
Those activities are inconsistent with my beliefs.  However, that’s a side effect, not the point.  The point is to devote ourselves in all
things to doing the will of Christ and to let the chips fall where they may.

**Limiting Discipleship**

Second, it lowers the bar for discipleship.  If all we care about is what others are doing
rather than what we should be doing, once we have succeeded in being not like
them, we have arrived.  Praise the
Lord!  I’m not a Baptist!

However, it is all too possible (and, I fear, all too
common) for Christians to worship all their lives in a building that says
“church of Christ” (note the lower-case “c”) on the sign out front, yet never
become transformed by the renewing of their minds.  They get so focused on not-Baptisting that
they never fix their eyes on Jesus, and they lose their souls as a result.  “Not X” is never the standard.  Christ is the standard, and we can never
forget it.

**Crippling the Church**

Third, the not-them attitude hinders the work of the
church.  After all, if not being
denominational is the highest goal, then anything some denomination does must
be bad simply because they’re the ones doing it.  We must not do it ourselves because if we do,
we will become like them.

However, this line of thinking is fatally flawed.  It turns the denominations into our negative
standard rather than looking to the word of God as our positive standard.  If some practice is both lawful and
expedient, we should adopt it, regardless of who came up with the idea
first.

It’s no secret to anyone that the religious climate of the
modern-day United States is increasingly hostile to faith.  In this environment, if we want to survive
and thrive, we’re going to have to pick up and use every Scriptural tool we can
find.  If we go around rejecting tools
not because they are unlawful but because we don’t like the source, that will
diminish and possibly destroy the Lord’s church in this country.

**Obstructing
Evangelism**

Finally, we will nullify our efforts to lead the lost to the
Lord if all we’re actually doing is leading them to a negative.  As the media harpies demonstrate, negativity
is appealing to those who are already in the fold but repulsive to those who
are not.  It promotes disengagement
rather than conversion.  After all, if we
convince somebody that they need to be a not-Catholic, the path of least
resistance for them is not to join up with us. 
It’s to become a religious none and keep their Sunday mornings free.

This does a tremendous disservice to the stirring call of
the gospel.  It is marvelous to think
that in Christ, we can have our sins forgiven and learn to be like Him!  There is definitely a right way to do that,
but the right way is infinitely more important than all the wrong ways.

Furthermore, for the good and honest heart, the positive
approach is infinitely more appealing. 
Who wants to join up with some group that exists to tear other people
down all the time?  That’s a display of
arrogance and bitterness, not love.  On
the other hand, what happens when outsiders see a group of people who love
Jesus and are humbly striving to imitate Him? 
That is when the Lord can draw people to Him through us.

**Conclusion**

Back during the brotherhood controversies of the 1960s,
advocates of the institutional position labeled non-institutional Christians as
“antis”.  Here, as always, we do well to
listen to the arguments (and even epithets) of those who disagree with us.  Our identity cannot be determined by the
things we oppose.  Instead, it must be
defined by the things we support.  We
need to be for the authority of Scripture, for the Lordship of Jesus Christ,
and for the upward call of His gospel.

There is richness and power and meaning in defining
ourselves by those things.  However,
there is nothing to be gained by defining ourselves against those with whom we
disagree.  If that is the path we take,
we will never be more than a spiteful shadow of those we hold in contempt.
