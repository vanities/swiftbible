In response to my recent post http://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2017/07/how-to-rhyme-in-hymns.html
about rhyming in hymns, a friend of mine noted that he found my analysis of
Frances Havergal’s “Is It For Me?” helpful. 
I talked about the process of writing the hymn, how Havergal overcame
technical obstacles to write a text that worshipers wanted to sing.  That, after all, is what hymnists aim to do,
and it ain’t easy, so the study of someone else’s method is helpful.



**Introducing “Teach Me
Thy Way”**

I can see that, and I’m a hymn geek anyways, so I figured
I’d do the same thing with the superb “Teach Me Thy Way”, presented above.  The author of the hymn is Benjamin Mansell
Ramsey.  According to [hymnary.org](http://hymnary.org/person/Ramsey_BM) and [The Cyber Hymnal](http://hymntime.com/tch/bio/r/a/m/ramsey_bm.htm) , Ramsey
was never a writer of a thousand hymns (like most people who write great hymns are), but he apparently honed his skills writing secular
songs.

The Internet knows nothing of the circumstances in which he
wrote “Teach Me Thy Way”, but it obviously isn’t a happy hymn.  Hymnists who write about sadness, doubt, and
fear are generally in the midst of experiencing those things themselves.  Ramsey was 70 when he wrote both text and
tune, and he would die four years later. 
It may well be that the suffering that often attends old age prompted
him to write.

**The Core Thought**

Regardless, it’s clear that in his unhappiness, he found
himself in Psalm 27:11, which reads in the KJV, “Teach me thy way, O LORD, and
lead me in a plain path, because of mine enemies.”  He realized that a whole hymn was implied in
the verse, particularly in the first four words.

Many phrases can’t bear the weight of being repeated in a
hymn, but “Teach me Thy way” certainly can. 
Its first and most important attribute is its spiritual power.  Every single Christian on the planet should
be able to say with conviction, “Teach me Thy way, O Lord!”  It is a plea that is literally inseparable
from being a disciple of Christ.

Second, “Teach me Thy way” is applicable to every area of
life.  Some spiritual ideas are powerful
but narrow.  “You must be born again,” is
one example of this.  Spiritual rebirth
is extremely important, but it is an event rather than a process.  If you write a hymn that repeats that phrase,
it’s going to be very narrow.  By
contrast, a hymn built around “Teach me Thy way” can be about whatever the
hymnist wants it to be about.  There is
no such thing as an earthly situation where the concept isn’t relevant.

Third, “Teach me Thy way” is a complete sentence.  This is spiritually insignificant but
technically crucial.  Because it is a
complete sentence, if you use it as a refrain, lines around the refrain don’t
have to be complete sentences themselves. 
They can be clauses that modify “Teach me Thy way.”

**A Brutal Meter**

This, along with the broad scope of the phrase, gave Ramsey
the room he needed to write in an extremely restrictive meter.  “Teach Me Thy Way” is in
6.4.6.4.6.6.6.4.  This is not an
unheard-of meter.  “Nearer, My God, to
Thee” and “Something for Jesus” both use it; “Jesus Is Mine” uses it and adopts
“Jesus is mine!” as a refrain.  I suspect
Ramsey borrowed both meter and rhyme scheme from “Jesus Is Mine” because he
realized that both fit what he wanted to do with “Teach me Thy way.”

However, 6.4.6.4.6.6.6.4, rhyming every line, is technically
daunting.  You have to hit a rhyme every
six syllables of non-refrain.  Worse yet,
in the back half of the hymn, you have to hit the same rhyme three times in a
row.

Catherine J. Bonar, the author of “Jesus Is Mine”, couldn’t
pull it off, at least not completely. 
She uses a number of half-rhymes, like “joy” with “tie” and “wilderness”
with “place”.  As far as I’m concerned,
she gets a pass because of the great difficulty of what she attempted, but the
result is still not as strong as it could be.

**Getting the Key
Elements Right**

Ramsey was certainly aware of these metrical issues as he
was writing, and his preoccupation shows in what he did with his source
text.  “Teach me Thy way” became his
refrain, of course.  He also made the
obvious but correct call and adopted “Teach me Thy way, O Lord!” as his first
line.  It gives you the direct address
you need to cast the whole rest of the hymn in second person, and it’s a
compelling direct quote from Scripture. 
What’s not to love?  In fact, the
opportunity to use it as his first line was probably one of the things that led
Ramsey to select the meter he did in the first place.

It’s worth noting, however, that the second half of Psalm
27:11 doesn’t appear near the first half. 
Instead, it’s wayyy down toward the end of the third verse, at the end
of one of those rhyme-rhyme-rhyme stretches that Ramsey surely knew would be
difficult to manage.  Clearly, the
problem had his attention.  The back
quatrain of the third verse is almost certainly one of the first parts of the
hymn that Ramsay wrote, chronologically speaking, and he built it around the
word “plain” from Psalm 27:11.

Interestingly, “plain” in the hymn carries a different
meaning than “plain” in the psalm.  By
“plain”, the KJV meant “level”, as in the place where the rain in Spain mainly
stays.  In “Teach Me Thy Way”, though,
“plain” means “clear”.  “Make Thou my
pathway plain” isn’t a plea for easy walking, as in the psalm.  It’s a plea for enlightenment that even today
is moving in its heartfelt longing. 
Despite clouds both physical and mental, Ramsey earnestly desired for
God to show him the way forward.

**Filling in the Rest**

From that starting point, he fleshed out the hymn.  All the other verses follow logically from
the core thought:  v. 1 defining what “Teach
me Thy way” means, v. 2 about learning God’s way in generic sadness, v. 3 about
God’s way in perplexity, and v. 4 about the duration of instruction.

Just like his choice of refrain gave Ramsey more freedom
than is immediately apparent, so too his content left him with lots of space to
work.  There are only a couple of places
in the hymn where he paints himself into a corner.  The rest of the time, he can say pretty much
whatever he wants to as long as it leads back to his generic content for each
verse and to “Teach me Thy way,” so that’s what he does.

To see the difference this makes, just consider the two
places in the hymn where Ramsey has content that narrows his rhyming options.  For instance, I’m pretty sure that he uses
“storms o’erspread the skies” in 3.3 to justify the use of “rain” in 3.5 to
rhyme with “plain” in 3.7.  That’s a
solid cover of a weak spot.  In fact, it
makes me wonder if Ramsey put “plain” in 3.7 so he would have space to cover
what he knew would be a difficult rhyme.

Less impressive is his inversion into a rhyme with “Lord” in
1.3, which is the weakest line of the hymn. 
Admittedly, though, hymns can have worse problems than a weak 1.3 forced
by an extremely strong 1.1!   That’s a far stronger combination than an
average 1.3 and an average 1.1, which will probably keep a hymn from being sung.  If you get away with murder in 1.1 and end up
burying the body in 1.3, oh, well.

At this point, the mental heavy lifting is basically
over.  Ramsey doesn’t need super-strong
six-syllable lines because he’s going to tie them back to his refrain, which is
already super-strong.  He spends half the
hymn writing rhyme-driven parallel clauses, which is easy to do and leads to
pretty-sounding results.  Overall, he
takes advantage of the freedom that he’s built into the structure of the hymn to
nail every single rhyme.  He aces
something that should have been nearly impossible for him to do because his
opening setup is so good.

**Conclusion**

That, kids, is how you write a great hymn.  You start with a quotation from the Bible
that tugs at the heartstrings of every Christian.  You decide you’re going to build a hymn
around it (because all great hymns have great core thoughts), and you think
through the structural implications of the decision you’ve made.  You focus your initial attention on the most
important parts of the hymn:  first line,
last lines, hard-to-rhyme quatrain. 
Finally, you take advantage of the openness your content and structural
choices have created to fill in the rest of the blanks with technically sound
writing.

Do all that, and people will be singing your hymn a hundred
years after you’re dead.
