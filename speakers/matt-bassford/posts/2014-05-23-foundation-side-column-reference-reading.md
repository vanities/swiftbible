Life is filled with surprises.  Sometimes, they are even good.  I don’t expect that, but I’m grateful when it
happens.  So it is with the SCR and
reading.  For reading Bibles, I prefer
smaller Bibles that are double-column, paragraphed, and text-only.  The SCR is a larger Bible that is
single-column, verse-by-verse, and cross-referenced.  In every respect, it looks like a Bible I
would hate as a reader.  Indeed, I set up
this test expecting to hate it.  However,
the more I began to actually use it, the better I liked it.  My impressions are as follows:

**SIZE.**  At 6” x 9” and nearly two inches thick, the
SCR is as large a Bible as I would ever consider buying for myself.  Small and one-handable it is not.  However, that turned out not to bother
me.  I just kicked back in my desk chair,
propped it on the top of my stomach, and read away happily.



**LAYOUT.**  Here is where the weirdness started.  Every other single-column Bible I’ve used as
a reader gave me problems at the end of each line of prose.  After extensive testing, I concluded that the
problems started at about 3¼” from the left margin.  The column of the SCR is about 3¾”.  Therefore, I expected to have to do that
annoying eye-flick over to read the last half-inch of each line.



[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEg1PUIxiq2mAi3wcJp4bDL230OazZ-Z86iwTONCTMeKu1PNiDZGwci-_VjJkEalmswdzFkot14jSAg79Udv8iJDJHC1QWhChLd0Ab9F75wrnXAPa0UoXj-dsr3z_Q9OK0TrMFqmnNjYKg/s1600/SCR_R2.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEg1PUIxiq2mAi3wcJp4bDL230OazZ-Z86iwTONCTMeKu1PNiDZGwci-_VjJkEalmswdzFkot14jSAg79Udv8iJDJHC1QWhChLd0Ab9F75wrnXAPa0UoXj-dsr3z_Q9OK0TrMFqmnNjYKg/s1600/SCR_R2.jpg)

That’s not what happened. 
When I settled the SCR where I naturally wanted to read it with my
glasses off, there wasn’t a blind spot at the end of each line.  Instead, the entirety of the line was within
my visual field.  Rather than being
distracted by the eye-flicks all the time, I was able to lose myself in the
text.

According to my prior testing, this should not have
happened, but I have two hypotheses for why it did.  First, the large, readable 11-point font of
the SCR may have allowed me to hold the Bible farther away from my face than I
normally do, which increased the width of my visual field.  Second, it may be a characters-per-line
issue.  The Crossway Legacy, for
instance, which has about 73 characters per line, gives me trouble as a
reader.  By contrast, the SCR has about
57 characters per line of prose.  That’s
within in the range of 40 to 66 characters per line recommended by Robert
Bringhurst in his typesetting classic The Elements of Typographic Style.  It’s entirely possible that my problems
derive from a problem with field of comprehension, not field of vision, and if
so, the lines of the SCR are of a length I can comprehend.

Because the SCR is a single-column Bible, I would expect it
to be a good poetry reader, and this has proven to be true.  The fairly short line length for a
single-column Bible means that some lines of poetry get chopped, but that
doesn’t happen more than two or three times per page.  The layout is often more utilitarian than it
is beautiful—Lamentations, for instance, is laid out in a book-long blurt, only
broken by chapter headings, instead of in poetic verses—but it reads just fine.

The other big theoretical obstacle that the SCR offers as a
reader is its verse-by-verse layout. 
Surprisingly, this wasn’t a problem in practice either.  In poetry, the verse-by-verse format had no
effect.  Because almost all poetry verses
end on a poetic line break, a verse-by-verse Bible doesn’t break lines any
differently in a poetry section than a paragraphed Bible does.

Even in prose, though, a verse-by-verse setting doesn’t
really mangle the text.  Because the line
length of the SCR is fairly long, it doesn’t generate many “widows”, lines of
prose which contain only one word.  Casual
inspection reveals only a widow or two per page.  That isn’t enough to break the flow when I’m
reading, and it certainly doesn’t generate anything like the continual
eye-flick I am forced to adopt in most single-column, paragraphed
settings.  Even though the verse numbers
in the SCR are huge, because they appear only along the left margin, I was able
to tune them out.

Finally, the cross references along the outer margin weren’t
an issue either.  The text was all in my
visual field; the references weren’t.  In
consequence, I was able to ignore  them
while reading.  This was a different
experience, from, for instance, a double-column Bible with center-column
references.

**READABILITY.**  Here too, the SCR’s brute-force approach to
readability triumphs.  The paper isn’t
great, the setting isn’t line-matched, but the type is large enough to be
legible from outer space.  If the font is
that big, you can skimp on all the other stuff and produce a fine, readable
Bible.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEj_gXmd0rOFWkJ3sVlgGEMlfKeAaWYz7-Q1wEmNmkr-DeKG2Ri_MspepIVRCIUYDQQYKBuom2SW5NXOlj4zRbBpEUQilMxYjaJfGiaiiJoXKgqCphpvl28ir5s-iLAUMyi1XwFIbhpeKw/s1600/SCR_R4.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEj_gXmd0rOFWkJ3sVlgGEMlfKeAaWYz7-Q1wEmNmkr-DeKG2Ri_MspepIVRCIUYDQQYKBuom2SW5NXOlj4zRbBpEUQilMxYjaJfGiaiiJoXKgqCphpvl28ir5s-iLAUMyi1XwFIbhpeKw/s1600/SCR_R4.jpg)

**CONCLUSION.**  The cramped layout of the narrow-margin SCR
doesn’t look like it would be good for reading at all.  Appearances can be deceiving.  So far, the SCR is the smoothest-reading
Bible I’ve ever tested for that purpose. 
It deserves a 5/5.
