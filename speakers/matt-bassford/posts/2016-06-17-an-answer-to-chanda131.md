Last week, I posted [this response](http://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2016/06/replying-to-ugly-in-christianity.html) to a HuffPo blog post entitled “The Ugly in Christianity”.  At the bottom of my post, a woman who
identified herself as Chanda131 left a lengthy comment.  There was so much in the comment that I
wanted to talk about that I told her I would prefer to answer her in a dedicated
blog post.

Like the original HuffPo blogger’s story, Chanda’s story is
a familiar one.  She was raised in the
church and connected with all sorts of church institutions (“entrenched”, she
puts it).  However, she ultimately ended
up falling away because of bad experiences with predominantly older members,
like one man who said publicly that he hadn’t sinned in five years.  However, on reading my post, she found
herself wondering if in her absence, the church had become more enlightened and
loving so that she should give it a second chance.



That’s a complicated question to answer, at least in part
because it’s coming at an extremely important topic from a different direction
than I usually do.  My short answer is “Yes,
but that’s not why.”  Let me explain.

Almost all of my religious experience has been among the
non-institutional churches of Christ. 
They tend to be even more austere than mainstream churches of
Christ.  No church-supported colleges for
us.  No church-supported high
schools.  No cooperation among
churches.  Non-institutional brethren
tend to spend a lot of time insisting loudly that they aren’t legalists, or if
they are, that legalism isn’t a bad thing.

And yet, even though I certainly had my share of
frustrations with my local congregations when I was growing up, as I got older,
I started to realize something. 
Underneath the arguments about necessary inferences and expedients,
there was a core insight that was basically right.  If we want to be disciples of Jesus, we ought
to do what the Bible says.

To me, discipleship is what Christianity has to be
about.  Say what you will about austere,
stripped-down religion, but it leaves you with two choices.  Either you ignore the Bible, or you have to
take up your cross, yourself, and follow Jesus. 
Think there should be music in church? 
You’d better sing.  Think the poor
should be helped?  Get yourself off to a
soup kitchen.  Want hospitality to be
shown to the stranger?  You’d better
invite him over for a meal.  Want the
lost to be taught?  Teach!  Because there are no institutions to pick up
the slack, you either do it or deal with the guilt of knowing that it’s not
getting done.

What’s more, once you admit that you have to follow the
Bible, you have to follow the *whole*
Bible, even the scary parts, and the scariest part of all is the command to
love.  Love God.  Love your brother.  Love your neighbor.  Love your enemy.  Love everybody.  The rest of the New Testament just tells you
what love looks like.

There are a lot of things that I get right spiritually.  I’m faithful to my wife.  I don’t tell dirty jokes or use bad
language.  I work hard.  I give of my means on the first day of the
week.

However, I know that when it comes to love, I fall
spectacularly short, and every time I am confronted with the cross, I am also
confronted with my failure.  Next to
Jesus, my best efforts vanish into insignificance.  Gospel preacher that I am, I rely for my
righteousness on an ocean of grace. 
Though I intend to spend the rest of my life struggling after Jesus, I
know that I will never outgrow that reliance.

Any church, then, can never be more than a collection of
failures.  Some failures are more
conspicuous than others, but they are all failures.    It’s common to speak of the rise of the Stone-Campbell
movement as the Restoration, but there are two ironies in that.  The first is that it is an attempt to restore
a pattern that no first-century church ever fully implemented.  The second is that no modern church has fully
implemented the pattern either.

The Bible speaks with no less force on church unity than it
does on worship.  Until every one of us
has attained the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ, the
Restoration is a work in progress.  It
can never be anything more.  We will always
fall short corporately as I will always fall short individually.

My answer to you, then, Chanda, is this.  I think you should go back.  However, don’t look for a loving church.  Don’t look for an enlightened church.  It will always be possible to indict any
congregation (and correctly so) for being insufficiently loving or insufficiently
enlightened.

Instead, look for a church that has its eyes fixed on Jesus
instead of in the mirror.  Look for a
church where the people know they don’t have it together.  Look for a church that will set your feet on the
hard road of discipleship.
