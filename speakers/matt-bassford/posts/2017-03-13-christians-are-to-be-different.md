There are many things that make following Christ difficult,
but one of the most significant is that He walked a narrow path rather than a
broad one.  He tells us up front that
being His disciple isn’t for everybody or even for most.  Instead, choosing to imitate Him means that
we are also choosing to be different from the vast majority of the people
around us.

This is hard for us to contemplate with a light heart.  We know that even in a nation that idolizes
individualism and claims that everybody should be free to follow their own
paths through life, reality doesn’t live up to the ideal.  If you doubt me, try going to the comments
section of a major newspaper and announcing that you believe that it’s a sin
for people to engage in homosexual activity. 
You’ll get called some names you never knew existed!

As a result, there is tremendous pressure on Christians to
conform, to blend in with the world around us. 
However, that’s exactly what we must not do.  As James incisively observes in James 4:4,
friendship with the world is enmity with God. 
Christians must be different from the people of the word.  Let’s consider now how to put that into
practice.



**Friendship With the
World**

Logically speaking, we first have to begin by grasping what
friendship with the world is.  I think it
starts with **THE DESIRE TO BE SPOKEN WELL
OF.** Jesus comments on the result of
this pursuit in Luke 6:26.  Of the
shocking statements of Jesus, this one has to rank toward the top!  After all, we all learned back in junior high
that the most important thing was to be popular, to get as many people speaking
well of you as possible.  Even today, we
don’t want to be That Guy at work, the one nobody wants to talk to because of
some undesirable personal trait.

However, the sad truth is that we can’t serve two masters,
and getting all the worldly people we know to speak well of us is incompatible
with receiving honor from God.  The world
doesn’t love the people who are different from the world.  The world loves its own.  The world loves people who are worldly.  If we want the world to love us, we have to
speak and behave in a worldly way. 
However, the less willing we are to conform to that worldly pattern, the
less willing the world is going to be to love us too.  Following Jesus will ensure that we get the
same reaction He did.

In other words, if we are truly disciples of Jesus, we don’t
ever have to worry about this passage.  Imitating
our Master will absolutely guarantee that we won’t be universally spoken well
of.  However, if the praise of others is
our goal, we will inevitably forfeit the praise of Christ.

The pressure to conform to the world most commonly manifests
itself in **SILENCE ON THE TRUTH.** Look at the bad example of the rulers of
the Jews in John 12:42-43.  These rulers
knew the truth about Jesus, no doubt. 
They believed that He was the Son of God.  However, they weren’t willing to proclaim
that truth because they weren’t willing to pay the price.  Rather than gaining favor with God, they kept
silent to maintain the favor of men.

Now, the point here is not that Christians should strive to
be obnoxious jerks.  We should be honest,
loving, kind, and compassionate in our dealings with everyone, and mostly,
people will appreciate it when we apply the Golden Rule to them.  However, speaking the truth in love is
speaking the truth in love.  When we
allow fear of rejection to keep us from sharing the gospel with others, we
aren’t doing that anymore.  We’re
revealing that we love ourselves and the life of ease that comes from following
the world, rather than the life of conflict that comes from following
Christ.

This is not an easy thing. 
I think most Christians struggle with putting this into practice.  However, whenever we find ourselves with an
opportunity to speak up, or not, we have to get into the habit of asking
ourselves, “Whom do I want to please?”  Once
we figure out whether we want to please God or please men, our choice and the
consequences of that choice will also become clear.

Finally, friendship with the world is marked by **APPLAUDING SIN.** Consider Paul’s illuminating description
of the fallen Gentiles in Romans 1:32. 
The people whom God has given over to evil are distinguished not only by
their practice of sin, but by their approval of sin.  This is clearly apparent today.  There are tens of millions of people in our
country who don’t have the slightest desire to become transgender, and yet
they’ll stand up and cheer themselves hoarse for the likes of Caitlyn Jenner.  Soon, we’ll reach the point where no sin is
too bizarre to celebrate.

There’s a fine line that we have to walk here.  On the one hand, as Christians, we are
supposed to love our enemies and pray for those who persecute us.  How much more should we love and pray for
those who have never harmed us but are lost in sin!  That love should reveal itself in every
aspect of our conduct toward them.

However, we must be careful to reject the world’s lie that
love and approval are the same thing.  I
know people who are practicing homosexuals. 
I know people who are transgender. 
I love them, but I cannot approve of their actions, because approving of
actions that will result in eternal damnation cannot be loving.  The same is true of the less dramatic sins.  The fornicator, the drunkard, or the greedy
are no more pleasing in God’s sight.  If
we forget this, it won’t be long before we start practicing what we accept.

**What Made Jesus
Different**

Next, let’s consider what made Jesus different, not only
different from the brazen sinners of his day, but from those who were outwardly
religious, like the scribes and Pharisees. 
His separation from people like that began with His **REJECTION OF TRADITION.** Look
at the contrast that appears in Matthew 15:1-3. 
The Pharisees have the reputation of righteousness, but as Jesus reveals,
they love their tradition more than God’s word.

Today, there are prominent religious groups that make no
bones about doing exactly the same thing as the Pharisees.  They will tell you that their religious
practice is based on the traditions of their church as much as it is on the
word of God.  In practice, this leads to
the same result that the tradition of the Pharisees did.  Once you start setting up your tradition  as the equal of the Scripture, it won’t be
long before that tradition starts contradicting the Scripture, and when that
happens, everybody picks the tradition over the text.  It is precisely this process that explains
the massive sprawl of denominations all around us.  Even those who claim to be following only the
word of God are actually following their traditions instead.

In the churches of Christ, we make something of a hobby out
of criticizing the excesses of the denominations, but simply because we’ve got
a sign out front that says “church of Christ” doesn’t make us immune.  We too have our human traditions.  It’s inevitable that any church will develop
those.  However, we must recognize them
for what they are and not allow them to assume equal prominence with the
Bible.  If we fail in this, we’ll end up
just like the denominations.

Second, Jesus’ difference from the religious world of the
Jews appears in His **LOVE FOR THE
UNLOVED.** Interestingly, this is
revealed, among other places, in the words of Jesus’ enemies.  Consider Matthew 11:18-19.  Today, we consider “Friend of sinners” to be
among the titles of Jesus.  However, in context,
“Friend of sinners” is not meant as a compliment!  Instead, this is the Pharisees sneering at
and criticizing Jesus.  They’re saying,
“Eww!  Jesus associates with people we’re
too righteous to associate with!”  In
reality, this didn’t have anything to do with religious purity.  It had everything to do with class
consciousness and snobbery.  The
Pharisees shunned sinners because they thought they were better.

Today, this same attitude appears in religious people
generally and all too often in Christians. 
It’s all too easy for us, like the Pharisees, to turn religion into a
vehicle for class prejudice.  In our
everyday lives, we’re happy to associate with people who share our social and
economic status and the values that come with that status.  We look at their nice houses and manicured
lawns and we say to ourselves, “They’re not far from the kingdom of God.”  But let somebody actually come to our
assemblies dressed in not-so-nice clothes and with other markers of coming from
a lower class, and we say, “I think I’ll let the preacher talk to them.”

Brethren, we didn’t learn that from Jesus.  He wasn’t impressed with the social and
religious elite of His day.  Instead, He
skewered their hypocrisy and self-righteousness, and believe me, there’s plenty
of both going around in the American middle and upper-middle class!  Instead, He loved the poor, the people nobody
else loved, because they were more likely to have hearts that would receive His
word.  Let’s be like Him.  Let’s see the overlooked.  Let’s love the unloved.

Third, Jesus was different because He had **A SERVANT’S HEART.** Let’s ponder His words in Luke
22:24-27.  This surely must be among the
most amazing things ever said.  Here, we
have Jesus, somebody who is literally God. 
He is literally doing every human being a favor by lowering Himself to
share the planet with them.  He’s better
than we are, plain and simple.  And yet,
even though He bore the title “Master”, the Son of God did the serving.

It’s easy for us to shake our heads at the disciples, to
marvel at how thick they could be to argue about who was the greatest in the
presence of the Christ.  However, this is
a passage that should call us not to contempt, but to self-awareness.  Every single one of us also experiences the
desire to be the greatest, to be the leader, to be the one who gets our own
way.  Either we recognize that temptation
and spend our lives struggling against it, or we fail to see it, so that we
become every bit as determined to be the one on top as the apostles were.

I think there’s a simple litmus test for whether we have a
servant’s heart or not.  None of us have
perfect lives.  None of us have a perfect
family.  None of us have a perfect
church.  When we encounter problems, do
we take it upon ourselves to solve the problem, or are we more interested in telling
others what they’re doing wrong and what they should do about it?  Do we act, or do we command and
criticize?  Jesus came among us as the
One who serves.  Let’s do the same.

Finally, Jesus was different because of His willing **SELF-SACRIFICE.** Let’s read His beautiful description of
the offering of His own life in John 12:23-28. 
Nowhere do we see the contrast between Jesus and the world more clearly
than in this text.  In John 11, we see
the leaders of the Jewish nation discussing what they think is a serious problem.  They think Jesus is getting too powerful and
is going to bring the Romans down on them, so that they will lose their high
position.  Their solution to the problem
is to kill Jesus.

Jesus here sees a problem too:  the problem of sin, sin that will doom every
human being unless He does something.  He
has already given up His high position, so that’s not on the table.  His solution also involves a death, but
rather than killing somebody else, He intends to die Himself.

The death of Jesus on the cross is not merely the central
act of Christianity.  It is the central
metaphor too.  Uniting ourselves with the
death of Jesus is what it means to be a Christian.  We begin the process when we are buried with
Him in baptism, but we continue it throughout our lives.  Every morning when we wake up, every one of
us has got a cross sitting next to our beds. 
Either we pick it up and carry it all day, or we don’t.  People in the world don’t have crosses to
bear.  They live for themselves and die
for themselves.  We’re disciples, and
discipleship means the cross.  Only if we
follow the death of Jesus by dying to ourselves do we bear much fruit and
glorify God.

**Friendship With God**

Finally, let’s consider what it takes to abandon friendship
with the world and finding friendship with God instead.  First, this requires **COMING OUT FROM UNBELIEVERS.** In
this, look at what Paul writes in 2 Corinthians 6:14-18.  We are supposed to love the people of the
world, but that’s a far cry from becoming partners with the world.  If we have a hard time distinguishing between
the two, consider Jesus and the Corinthians. 
Jesus loved the people of the world. 
He expressed His love by eating with them and treating them with
respect.  However, with the Lord, that
was never the end of the line.  His goal
was always to call sinners to repentance, not to endorse their sin.  He didn’t want to join the sinners.  He wanted them to join Him.

At least some of the Corinthians, by contrast, had a very
different attitude.  They were willing
not only to eat with sinners but to join them in their idolatrous feasts.  Far from calling sinners to repentance, they
boasted about having a member who was openly engaged in incest.  When Paul tells them they need to come out
and be separate, that’s what he’s talking about.

Today, we must be honest about our interactions with the
world.  Is it always in the back of our
minds that our worldly friends are lost in sin, so that we’re constantly
seeking a way to lead them to Christ?  Or,
instead, are these friendships that the devil is using to try to convert us,
whether it be to the debauchery of the young and single or to the materialism
of American suburbia?  Somebody’s always
trying to convert somebody.  If we’re not
the ones doing the converting, we need to get out.

Second, friendship with God means **FINISHING WITH SIN.** Consider
Peter’s words in 1 Peter 4:1-3.  Whenever
I get to know a Christian who came to the Lord as an older adult, they almost
invariably express regret that they didn’t obey the gospel or repent
sooner.  They look back on those wasted
years and decades with tremendous sadness. 
This is what Peter is trying to get us to understand about sin:  it’s a waste! 
Sure, it looks enticing from the front side, but in the end, it only
leaves us with sorrow, guilt, and a feeling of dissatisfaction.  Peter wants us to see that no matter how much
time we’ve spent on sin, the time that we’ve spent is enough.

Once we come to that realization, once we truly get fed up
with sin, rather than living for earthly desires, we can live for the
Lord.  God, after all, is a jealous
God.  The devil is content with having
half of our lives.  He knows that if he
gets half of us now, he gets all of us forever. 
However, God isn’t, any more than my wife would be content if I were
faithful to her half the time.  Instead,
we must be so determined to devote ourselves to God that we are willing to
suffer if need be.

Last of all, friendship with God depends on **FORGETTING WHAT LIES BEHIND.** We see Paul encouraging us to do exactly
this in Philippians 3:13-14.  It’s not
enough for us to leave those relationships with people who are trying to drag
us down.  It’s not enough for us to put
our foot down and refuse to live in sin. 
Instead, we also must stop looking back longingly on those things
too.  Our eyes have to be fixed on Canaan,
not Egypt.  Otherwise, we’ll never get
there.

Recently, I was reading through the gospel of Luke when I
realized something about the prodigal son’s older brother.  When the brother’s mad at the end of the
parable, he’s mad because he envies the prodigal.  The prodigal got to go have all that fun with
prostitutes while poor older brother has been stuck at home, working the farm
with Dad.  In the same way, when his
father tells him, “All that I have is yours,” I don’t think he’s talking only
about the inheritance.  I think he’s
talking about the older brother’s experience of staying with his father when
the prodigal didn’t.

I’m afraid a lot of Christians have older-brother-itis.  Even if they aren’t engaged in sin, they
spend their lives longing for a life of sin and envying those who are living
it.  Brethren, there is nothing to envy
about the passing pleasures of sin!  If
we’ve chosen not to live that way, we have something incomparably more
precious.  We have the experience of staying
with our Father, and that’s the very best thing this life has to offer.  Someday, we will even receive the inheritance
He has promised.  Until then, we need to
rejoice in the present and future, not make an idol out of the past.
