Today, it’s probably true that the single biggest obstacle
to evangelism is fear.  Many Christians,
particularly those who don’t have much evangelistic experience, are terrified
at the thought of telling a friend or co-worker about Jesus.  As a result, rather than taking advantage of
the opportunities before them, they remain silent.  They might well feel guilty about this
silence after the fact, but regardless of remorse, the result remains the
same.  The gospel is not proclaimed.

When we consider our own fear, we must acknowledge that
Christians 2000 years ago had much more reason to fear than we do.  Our proclamation of Christ might lead to
social awkwardness, but theirs could lead to abuse, imprisonment, and even
death.  Nonetheless, when we consider the
records of the early church, rarely do we see our first-century brethren
shrinking back from the consequences of evangelism.  Even though they were aware of the risks,
they chose to preach Christ anyway.  This
morning, we’re going to study one such example of preaching and see what it can
teach us about our own efforts.  Let’s
learn from Peter about boldness in Christ.



**Peter’s Second Sermon**

This particular text begins in Acts 3, and its first and
largest part centers around what we call Peter’s second sermon.  Peter is given the opportunity to preach this
sermon because of the encounter that he and John have with **A LAME MAN.** The story begins
in Acts 3:1-5.  Because Peter and John
are devout Jews in addition to being followers of Christ, at this point, they
are still following the Jewish religious customs, including going to the temple
to pray at 3 o’clock in the afternoon. 
At the gates to the temple, they meet a lame man, a man who has never
walked in his life.  This is somebody
whom all the religious Jews would have known, and they would also have known
that absent the power of God, he was never going to walk.  Peter could have tuned him out or possibly
thrown him a few coins, but instead, he engages the man because he senses
an opportunity.

In the same way, we should learn to see opportunities for
the gospel in others’ needs and others’ problems.  Too often, I’m afraid we tune those problems
out.  Our co-worker just had a baby.  What does that have to do with us?  Rather than hardening our hearts against
those around us, whenever we see someone who needs help, we should treat it as
a call to action.  If we’re too busy to
help, then we’re too busy.

Peter solves the particular problem before him by **HEALING THE LAME MAN.** Look at Acts 3:6-10.  He takes this man who has never walked,
raises him up, and he walks!  This is an
astonishing miracle.  Not only has Peter
repaired whatever the problem was, he has condensed months of physical therapy
into a few seconds.  What’s more, it has
occurred as publicly as it possibly could. 
There is no doubt in any of the onlookers that this was the power of
God.

Today, none of us can help as powerfully as Peter did, but
we can still help.  Provided, of course,
that we don’t have all of our time and money and energy tied up in tending to
ourselves, we can generously give all of those things to others who need
them.  This can’t be a *quid pro quo*.  It has to be a no-strings-attached act of
mercy.  This is our sign that we are what
we claim to be.  After all, Jesus has
instructed His disciples to be merciful. 
If we aren’t merciful, we aren’t His disciples.  Even if our act of mercy never leads
anywhere, it was still the right thing to do. 
Sometimes, though, when people see our hearts by our actions, those
actions open the door for more.

The crowd that immediately gathers gives Peter the
opportunity to **EXPLAIN THE MIRACLE.** The explanation appears in Acts
3:11-16.  The strategy that Peter follows
here is both logical and relevant.  He
starts with what the people have seen—his healing of the lame man.  However, he makes clear that the healing
isn’t about him.  Instead, he shifts the
focus to God, the God he and his entire audience agree on, the God of Abraham,
Isaac, and Jacob.  That takes him to his
third point, a point that his hearers don’t yet agree with him on.  The reason why the God of Israel gave him
power to heal was to glorify Jesus of Nazareth, who was condemned to die by
these very Jews but is actually the Messiah. 
It’s hard to deny the Messiah in the presence of the miracle!

Likewise, friends, when we help somebody else, we need to
start by shifting the focus away from us. 
Rather than simply saying “You’re welcome” when someone thanks us, why
not give the glory to Jesus?  After all,
isn’t everything good that we do something that we learned from Him?  Without His example, wouldn’t we be as
self-centered and unhelpful as most worldly people?  Then, once we see how those we help take
that, we can figure out where the conversation should go next.

Peter next turns the subject of his sermon to **REPENTANCE.**Consider what he says in Acts 3:17-21.  He acknowledges that most of his audience
didn’t know what they were doing when they cried out for Jesus to be
crucified.  Remember, all of this is
probably happening only a couple months after the crucifixion did.  He’s talking recent history.  However, even though they were ignorant, they
still need to repent and seek the forgiveness that only Jesus can provide.

This is a shocking thing for Peter to say:  that these good, respectable, temple-going
Jews need to acknowledge that condemning a rabble-rousing prophet to death was
a sin, and that they need to ask this very prophet for forgiveness.  However, a call for repentance is no less
shocking in our time.  Many people don’t
want to repent because that would force them to acknowledge that they are
sinners in need of help, and they’re too proud to admit it.  However, without repentance, there can be no
salvation.  We can’t let people shy away
from the humility that God demands.

Finally, Peter describes Jesus as **A PROPHET LIKE MOSES.** This
appears in Acts 3:22-26.  Just as is true
a chapter earlier, Peter uses a three-legged stool to support this
assertion.  He argues that the Jews should
believe that Jesus is such a prophet for three reasons.  The first (implied in this part of the
argument) is the miracle that Peter was able to work in Jesus’ name.  The second is all the prophets from Moses on
down who predicted that such a prophet would arise.  The third is the fact that Jesus was raised
from the dead.  Because all of these
things are true, Peter argues, Jesus is established as Moses’ successor, and
that means that everybody had better listen to Him.

This point is no less forceful today.  We should work to give people reasons to want
to serve Jesus, but even failing that, everyone has to serve Jesus.  His resurrection from the dead proves that He
is Lord, which means that people only defy Him at their peril.  Obeying Jesus isn’t a nice extra for people
who feel like it.  It’s a necessity for
anyone who wants to avoid hell.

Before the Lord’s Supper, let’s reflect on these
things.  Peter’s arguments that Jesus is
the Christ, and the conclusions that follow from those arguments, have lost
none of their ancient power.  Jesus is at
once Teacher, Savior, and Lord.  We honor
Him as Teacher when we learn from His example. 
We honor Him as Savior when we seek forgiveness through Him.  We honor Him as Lord when we obey Him.  Are our actions in line with our
beliefs?  Let’s think about this as we
partake.

**Confronting the
Sanhedrin**

The next part of the story describes a confrontation between
the apostles and the Sanhedrin.  We see
the battle lines being drawn in **TWO
REACTIONS** to Peter’s second sermon. 
Take a look at Acts 4:1-4.  This
is clearly a good news/bad news situation for Peter and John.  The bad news crops up first.  Because all of this is happening in Solomon’s
portico on the grounds of the temple itself, Peter is preaching his sermon right
under the noses of the high priests and their cronies.  The guards arrest him before he’s done
speaking.

This arrest happens for two reasons.  First, Peter is preaching the resurrection
from the dead.  The Jewish religious
leaders are Sadducees, so they don’t accept the resurrection.  Second, Peter is preaching the resurrection
in Christ, and Jesus died in the first place because these same guys plotted
His death.  Peter’s sermon, innocuous as
it seems to us, is a doctrinal and
political threat.

However, that’s not all that happens.  The Jewish religious leadership isn’t honest,
but many Jews are.  Enough obey the
gospel so that now there are 5000 disciples among the men alone.

In only a little while, Peter and John are haled **BEFORE THE COUNCIL.** Luke sets the scene for us in Acts
4:5-7.  Let’s pause for a moment to
consider how intimidating this would have been for two Galilean fishermen.  This is a gathering of the Sanhedrin, the 70
most powerful men of the Jewish nation. 
Among them, we see Annas, the former high priest and continuing power
behind the throne.  We see John and
Alexander, former high priests, and Caiaphas, the current high priest.  Everybody who has any earthly authority among
Jews is there, and they ask Peter and John, “By what authority have you dared
to proclaim Jesus as Lord?”  This is the
same group of men that met to condemn Jesus to death, and now two of his
closest followers are under their eye.

I don’t know about y’all, but when I think about Peter and
John standing before this roomful of evil, powerful old men, and I think about
the times I could have glorified Christ and didn’t, that makes me feel
ashamed.  We imagine all of the bad
things that might happen to us, but only a matter of weeks before, Peter and
John had seen the bad things that could happen to them when they saw Jesus
nailed to a cross.  They know that they
are in deadly danger, but they speak anyway. 
That’s what disciples of Jesus do. 
It’s up to us to prove that we’re disciples too.

Peter had every reason to shrink back, but he launches into
an impassioned **REBUTTAL** instead.  It appears in Acts 4:8-12.  This is a devastating counterattack!  Peter points out that what he has actually
done is perform an incontestable miracle, and the recipient is standing there
with them.  If they want to know who his
authority is, he’s perfectly happy to use the miracle to proclaim Jesus, who
was established as Lord when God raised Him from the dead.  Nor is Peter shy about pointing out that
these leaders, before whom he is standing trial, are the same ones who
crucified God’s anointed in the first place. 
This is nothing less than a gauntlet flung in the Sanhedrin’s face.

From Peter’s example, we should learn that we don’t get to
soft-pedal the truth when we fear that somebody won’t like it.  Jesus didn’t win any popularity contests when
He was on earth, and His disciples aren’t going to either.  If what’s most important for us is for
everybody to like us, we might as well go home now, because we aren’t going to
heaven.  However, if what’s most
important to us is glorifying God, then we must be willing to speak truth even
to people who don’t like it.

**The Results**

The third section of the story reveals the results of this
confrontation.  First, we see **THE COUNCIL DELIBERATE.**  This is recorded in Acts 4:13-17.  It’s clear from this account that the
Sanhedrin realizes that Peter has called its bluff.  If a lame man can be healed by the power of
Jesus, then Jesus is clearly an acceptable religious authority to cite!  However, rather than being persuaded by
Peter’s argument, the Jewish leaders continue to look for ways around it.  They remind me all too clearly of modern
politicians who would rather die than concede that the other guy has a point!  They decide to fall back on threats and
bluster.  They command the apostles to
speak no more in the name of Jesus, even though Peter has shown that Jesus’
authority exceeds theirs.

The lesson here is plain. 
No matter how clearly the truth of the gospel is presented, some aren’t
going to accept it because they don’t have honest hearts.  There’s no way around this.  If we try to teach others, we’ll run into the
problem too.  We simply have to accept
it.

Next, Luke recounts **PETER’S
REPLY** to the threats of the Sanhedrin. 
Here, consider Acts 4:18-22.  Once
again, Peter demonstrates that since the resurrection, he is
intimidation-proof.  Let the Jewish high
council assert its authority.  Peter is
going to obey the higher authority of God. 
He’s going to continue to repeat the truth about Jesus, and if the
Sanhedrin doesn’t like it, too bad.

As I often am when I consider our first-century brethren, I
am struck by the simplicity of their thought process.  It’s evident from the stories we have of
Peter, Paul, and the rest that they had one overriding concern in life.  They wanted to bring glory to Jesus, and they
were going to do that, regardless of who or what stood in their way.  The only way to shut them up was to kill
them.  By contrast, Christians today are
often pulled in a thousand different directions.  As a result, deciding whether to glorify
Jesus is a paralyzingly complex decision for us.  We need to ignore the noise and focus on the
signal instead.  If Jesus is Lord, we
need to honor Him.  The end.

Once Peter and John are released by the Sanhedrin and return
to the others, the group next considers **A
PROPHECY FROM DAVID.** The story
unfolds in Acts 4:23-28.  Once again,
this is a story segment that has two sources of authority in it.  The apostles begin by appealing in prayer to
God, who has authority as the Creator of heaven and earth.  However, they point out to Him that His
authority and the authority of His Son are being challenged by earthly
authorities, just as David had predicted in Psalm 2.  It happened during the trial of Jesus, and
it’s happening again.

It is always true that God’s authority is going to come into
conflict with earthly authority, even in a country with freedom of religion
like ours.  When we talk about the bosses
who frown on religious discussion at the workplace, even when it’s on our own
time, aren’t we talking about earthly authority?  For that matter, when we talk about the
social customs that proclaim that tolerance is in and sharing your faith with
someone else is impolite, isn’t that a kind of authority too?  Certainly, we’re supposed to honor earthly
authorities, but not when they contradict the will of God.  Let’s make sure we see the distinction.

The apostles conclude their prayer, and indeed our story,
with **A PLEA FOR BOLDNESS.** It shows up in Acts 4:29-31.  This is striking, particularly in
context.  After all, this prayer takes
place maybe a couple of hours after Peter and John have faced down the
Sanhedrin.  They have literally defied
death for the sake of the gospel.  Now,
here they are, back with their friends, but rather than thumping their chests
about how wonderful they are, they seek God’s help so that they can continue to
be bold.

This should remind us all of the importance of prayer in our
evangelistic efforts, particularly prayer for boldness.  If heroes of the faith like Peter and John
relied on God for the strength to continue proclaiming Him, then how much more
should we!  The prayer for boldness in
Christ is a prayer that God will answer. 
If we aren’t bold, it is because we aren’t praying.  Just like our gospel isn’t about us, so too
our sharing of the gospel isn’t about us. 
It’s about God.  Let’s continually
seek His help as we carry out His work.
