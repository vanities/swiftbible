This morning’s sermon came about as the result of yet
another sermon request spawned by the ladies’ Bible study.  They’ve been going through the book of
Ephesians, and they came to Ephesians 2:10, which tells us that we have been
created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared so that we could
walk in them.  Obviously, good works are
very important.  They’re the distinguishing
mark of the Christian.  But what kinds of
things count as good works?

I decided that answering the question would make for a fine
sermon, so I went to blueletterbible.com and ran a concordance search on “good
works” and its cousin “good deeds”, which is simply a different translation of
the same two Greek words.  After I did so,
I noticed something interesting.  The
book of Titus, which isn’t a very long book at all, uses the phrase more than
any other book in the Bible.  After I
flipped over to Titus and began to look at the contexts where the phrase
appears, I saw that the practice of good works is nothing less than the key
concept of the book.  Let’s look this
morning, then, at what Titus teaches us about good works.



**Godly Leaders and
Good Works**

Paul begins the book by focusing on the connection between
godly leaders and good works.  Before we
get to that part of the discussion, though, we need to consider the book’s **INTRODUCTION.** It appears in Titus 1:1-4.  This introduction is much more flowery than
many of the introductions to Paul’s epistles, but if we assume it’s not important
and skim it, we’ll miss out on some important concepts.  Let’s start by taking particular note of
Paul’s threefold definition of his apostleship. 
First, he says that he is an apostle for the faith of those who are
chosen in God.  God has a special people,
and He has sent them Paul to strengthen their faith.

Second, Paul says that he is an apostle for the knowledge of
the truth which is according to godliness. 
To put things another way, Paul accomplished his faith-strengthening
work through the truth that he proclaimed, which in turn led his hearers to
live godly lives.

Third, Paul is an apostle in the hope of eternal life.  He proclaims the truth to others and
encourages them to maintain their trust in God because of the reward that is
waiting for him and them both.  There is
no other road to eternal life than faith in and obedience to the gospel.  This is not some historical accident, Paul
says.  Instead, God has always planned to
bring life to His people, but He only carried out his promise in the first
century through the word of Christ.

All this is addressed to Titus, whom Paul describes as his
true child in a common faith.  Titus is a
Gentile, but the universal gospel of Christ has made him part of Paul’s
spiritual family, even though Paul is a Jew who before his conversion would
have had nothing to do with Gentiles.

Now that Paul has framed his message and purpose in this
way, the first thing he tells Titus to do is to appoint **ELDERS.**  We find this famous
text in Titus 1:5-9.  According to Paul,
one does not become an elder in the Lord’s church by living a long time or
being part of an influential family. 
Instead, it has everything to do with good works.  In fact, Paul says that the works of the man
qualified to serve are so good that they make him above reproach.

Paul then proceeds to describe what this irreproachable
character and life look like.  This is a
man who first of all has demonstrated his spiritual leadership skills in his
relationship with his wife and with his children.  If his own children aren’t faithful, how can
he keep other Christians faithful?

Similarly, the elder’s conduct has to be free from
vice.  As we all know, being a Christian
does not insulate one from sin.  There
are Christian men who are determined to get their own way, hot-tempered, prone
to drink, quarrelsome, or greedy.  On the
one hand, we should consider such failings with grace; after all, we all are
works in progress!  On the other hand,
though, such men should never be elevated to positions of leadership in the
church.

Instead, the would-be elder must demonstrate his good
character by opening his home to strangers, by his delight in the things of
God, by his sound judgment, and by his self-control.  Finally, he must have demonstrated his
command of the Scripture.  He has to know
it well enough that he can effectively teach it to others, whether to feed the
flock or to drive off the wolves.

In contrast to these good and godly leaders, Paul warns
Titus to look out for **FALSE
TEACHERS.** Consider Titus 1:10-16.  These aren’t men outside the church; these
are men who have already set themselves up as leaders in it.  They’re motivated both by greed and by the
desire to teach false doctrine.  Ideally,
the elders of the churches in Crete would refute these men, but in the absence
of Scriptural shepherds, Titus has to be the first line of defense.

As part of this, Titus must understand the problems with the
doctrine that they’re teaching.  These
are apparently men from a Jewish background, and they’ve brought Jewish baggage
with them, insisting that things that are holy and right are actually corrupt
and defiled.  Paul here is probably
talking in particular about foods that were unlawful for Jews but are lawful
for Christians.  These men want to return
to the Law of Moses with all its fleshly prohibitions.

Ironically, this man-driven vision of purity, rather than
making its followers better, actually makes them worse.  These men who claim to be so devoted to God
aren’t actually obeying Him.  Their focus
on the works of the Law makes their hearers unsuitable for works that are
actually good.

Today, of course, we also must be on the alert for
error.  We’re not likely to be faced with
Judaizing false teachers, but there are plenty of others teaching strange
doctrines that will make us worthless for any good deed.  Only those who cling to the truth in its
purest form will be able to glorify God by their actions.

**The Christ-Filled
Life**

Next, Paul explains what it means for Christians to live a
Christ-filled life.  In this section, he
first identifies the duties of **OLDER
CHRISTIANS.** Let’s read here from
Titus 2:1-5.  The first thing we see in
this reading is Paul’s instruction for older men.  The overall impression that this description
gives is one of stability and calmness. 
The older man of God can’t be given to outbursts of anger or wild
enthusiasms.  Instead, he has to be
reliably godly in his knowledge and behavior.

Similar things are true of older women.  They’re first of all supposed to be reverent,
to live lives that reflect respect and even fear for God and His will.  They can’t be gossips, always anxious to talk
about the latest dirt on everybody, nor can they be addicts.  The text specifically mentions wine, but the
principle applies to gambling or any other out-of-control behavior.

However, at this point in the text, something unique
happens.  It’s Titus’ job to teach older
men, older women, younger men, and bondslaves, but he is not to teach the
younger women.  Instead, that’s a job
that belongs to the older women of the congregation.  Even though Paul doesn’t explain this, you
don’t have to be a genius to figure out why. 
The more a preacher involves himself with younger women, the greater the
chances of temptation and sexual sin become.

As a result, personal spiritual instruction for younger
women is the responsibility of the older women. 
It’s their job to teach those younger women what it means to be a woman
of God, how to navigate the responsibilities of family and home in a
God-glorifying way.  Many older women
find this responsibility intimidating, but for this church to thrive, we need
older women who will teach.

After this, Paul addresses the responsibilities of **YOUNGER MEN AND BONDSLAVES.** They appear in Titus 2:6-10.  Because Titus himself is a fairly young man,
Paul combines his general instructions here with his instructions for Titus
personally.  The picture he paints is a
familiar one.  As is true for older men, younger
men must be dignified and sensible.

However, there’s an added nuance to Paul’s exhortation
here.  He’s particularly concerned about
the impression that younger Christian men will leave on outsiders.  He’s worried that through their words and
actions, they’ll bring discredit to the church. 
The only solution is for younger men to be careful in what they say and
do, to exemplify good works and sound words. 
That way, even those who are looking for a pretext to condemn Christians
won’t find any in them.

Bondservants, and by extension employees today, have special
responsibilities to their employers.  We
know what a bad employee looks like: 
they’ll steal from their employer whenever they have opportunity,
they’ll backtalk and badmouth, and generally will offer as little usefulness as
they think they can get away with.  By
contrast, the Christian employee is honest, hard-working, submissive, and
agreeable.  Employees like that please
their employers and glorify God.  Paul
says that in their good behavior, they are an adornment—an ornament—to the
doctrine of Christ.

Christians are to behave in these ways because of the
transformative effect of **THE GRACE OF
GOD** in our lives.  Paul makes this
argument in Titus 2:11-15.  All of the
things we’ve looked at are the way that Christians are to be, but they aren’t
the way that people naturally are.  Few
indeed are those who live the way we’ve discussed without having been exposed
to the gospel.  Without Christ in our
lives, we wouldn’t live this way either. 
We would be lawless people too.

However, because Christ has redeemed us from our lawless
deeds, we have responsibilities that we owe to Him.  He didn’t save us so that we could go on
being wicked and simply escape the consequences.  Instead, He rescued us so that from this
point on, we would belong to Him, so that instead of being zealous for
wickedness, we would be zealous for good works.

In other words, we aren’t people who live these sensible,
godly, righteous lives grumbling and whining the whole time because we’d rather
be wicked.  Instead, we love the idea of
being the kind of people we’ve just finished reading about.  That’s the way that we want to live.

We have this attitude first of all because of our
salvation.  We know that we have been
purified only because Jesus gave Himself for us, and even if we spend the rest
of our lives paying on that debt, we will never pay it down.  Second, we have this attitude because of our
hope.  As Jesus appeared once to give
life to our souls, so He will appear again to give life to our bodies.

Before the Lord’s Supper, let’s give some thought to all of
this.  Without the Lord, we would be
hopeless and doomed.  We would have no
way out of the disaster we had brought on ourselves.  With Him, though, our lives have been
transformed.  Let’s first of all remember
all that He has done for us.  Second,
though, let’s consider ourselves.  Let’s
ask whether we are indeed living this redeemed life, joyfully practicing the
good works of God’s word.  Let’s reflect
as we partake.

**Interacting with
Others**

Finally, let’s consider Paul’s advice about how the redeemed
should interact with others.  This begins
with his description of the impact of **THE
KINDNESS OF GOD.** Here, consider
Titus 3:1-7.  In this text, Paul first
informs us how we should behave.  This exhortation
is perfectly in line with everything we’ve already seen from the book.  We’re supposed to be a submissive, obedient
people, anxious only to do more good, peaceful, kind, and considerate.  As we’ve already observed, this is a tall
order, especially in a world filled with people who aren’t this way!

However, Paul says, we should treat even the ungodly with
grace because we used to be ungodly like them. 
We were trapped in sin.  We were
vicious, nasty people, utterly undeserving of any sympathy or help.  However, even though we were unlovable, God
loved us and rescued us, not because we had somehow merited our salvation, but
solely because of His mercy.  We have
experienced the washing of regeneration—note here that the word translated as
“washing” is the Greek *loutron*, which
is a bathtub, so that you could say that what I have behind me is not a
baptistery, but a bathtub of regeneration! 
We have been renewed through the teachings of the Holy Spirit.  As a result of these things, we eagerly
anticipate our inheritance of eternal life.

If we keep this in mind, it will help us bear up under all
the unpleasantness of the world.  If
others treat us badly, well, we used to treat others badly ourselves.  We are debtors to Christ for His grace, and
one of the ways we show our gratitude is by extending to others the same mercy
He has shown us.  When we have been
forgiven so much, how can we refuse to forgive so little?

Next, Paul points out the importance of **AVOIDING DISPUTES.** Read on
in Titus 3:8-11.  As he does throughout
the book, Paul advances this argument by setting up a contrast.  On the one hand, Paul urges constant teaching
about the grace of God and all that He has done for us.  We might think that’s awfully abstract and
doesn’t tell us anything about how to live, but Paul advises that if we
understand it, it will make us careful to engage in good deeds.  We should be overwhelmed by the magnitude of
the grace that God has expended on us and treat others accordingly.

On the other hand, though, if our lives have been informed
and illuminated by God’s grace, there are some things that we will not do.  Among them, we won’t spend our lives getting
caught up in petty arguments with brethren. 
Those quarrels are always the result of importing a fleshly mindset into
a setting that is supposed to be spiritual. 
All of us have heard the stories of churches that have split over the
dumbest things, and sometimes it seems like the more pitiful the controversy,
the hotter the passions rage!  That’s the
way that Christians who think they’re spiritually rich behave.  It’s not the way that Christians who know
they’re spiritual debtors act.  Rather
than being so impressed with our understanding and being so sure that we’re
right, let’s work instead on listening to and loving others.

Sadly, some brethren simply don’t understand this.  They might claim the name of the Prince of
Peace, but they don’t act like Him at all. 
Wherever they go, they leave quarrels and confusion in their wake.  When we see that behavior in somebody, we
need to warn them.  If they don’t listen
and keep not listening, it’s our responsibility to exclude them from our
number.

Finally, let’s turn our attention to Paul’s **CLOSING COMMENTS.** We find them in Titus 3:12-15.  The first thing that this text does is give
us insight into the later part of Paul’s life, after the conclusion of the book
of Acts.  It tells us that Titus must
have been written after the successful conclusion of his first trial before
Caesar.  It doesn’t make sense to put it
before Paul’s first missionary journey because he hadn’t met Apollos yet, and
never in Acts do we see Paul going anywhere near Nicopolis, which is on the
west coast of the Greek peninsula. 
However, it’s clear that even after Paul’s release from prison, his preaching
of the gospel continues unabated.

In connection with preaching, Paul highlights the importance
of generosity.  Apparently, Zenas and
Apollos are going to be passing through Crete, and while they’re there, Titus
is to make sure that the Christians on the island give the two men everything
they need to continue their work. 
According to Paul, this will make the Cretan Christians fruitful.  Here, once again, we find the Biblical
principle that those who support preachers share in the benefits of their labors.  This is the final exhortation to good works
in the letter, and after it, Paul entrusts Titus, and indeed all of us, to the
grace of God, the grace that frees us to do good works in the first place.
