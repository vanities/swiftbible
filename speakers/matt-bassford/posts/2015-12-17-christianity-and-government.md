In the current discussion of terrorism and Islam, I’ve seen
one theme in particular emerging.  It is
a blurring of the distinction between what individual Christians should do and
what the government should do.  I wrote
last week that Christians should love and do good for Muslims, even if those
Muslims have proven to be their enemies. 
The post generated more than a few Facebook comments about the Syrian
refugees:  whether we should admit them
at all, and how we should vet them if we do.

I find this blurring of boundaries more than a little
worrisome.  There are three major world
religions that are generally lumped together as “religions of the book”:  Judaism, Christianity, and Islam.  The New Testament, the most central text of
Christianity, differs from the Hebrew Scriptures and the Quran not only in
proclaiming Jesus of Nazareth as the Son of God, but in what it says about
government.



As just about everybody is aware these days, the Quran is a
political as well as a personal document. 
It is one of the most important sources of sharia law, which, if
implemented completely, creates a Muslim theocracy.  Similarly, the Hebrew Scriptures,
particularly in the Pentateuch, set out the ground rules for a Hebrew
theocracy.  Note, by the way, that there
are plenty of self-identified Jews and Muslims alike (most of them?) who don’t
believe that what their sacred texts say about government should be implemented
today.

The New Testament, by contrast, takes an entirely different
view of government.  Rather than aiming
to establish the kingdom of Christ on earth, it quotes Jesus as saying, “My
kingdom is not of this world.”  It has
nothing to say about what a Christian government should look like, but a great
deal to say about how Christians should live under worldly governments.  The largest Christian community in the New
Testament is not the state, but the church.

Of course, for American Christians today, our experience
with government is very different from the experience of our brethren 2000
years ago.  The only way they could even
attempt to replace an evil, corrupt, or ineffective leader was through revolt,
which Christian leaders from Jesus to Peter to Paul told them not to do.  By contrast, the Constitution gives American citizens
the right to have a say in choosing their leaders, and, indirectly, to
influence public policy.

Especially for those who regard the United States as “a
Christian nation”, it’s tempting to use the New Testament as a basis for
government.  However, because the New
Testament is not and was not intended to be a political document, this process
is fraught with difficulty.  I think the
New Testament is a marvelous guide for the conduct of the individual and the
church.  However, that doesn’t
necessarily make it a marvelous guide for the conduct of a nation, any more
than a marvelous boat will necessarily make a marvelous airplane.

Perhaps the biggest difficulty lies in determining which
parts of the New Testament should be reflected in public policy.  The most obvious answer—“All of it!”—is one
that few, if any, actually want to see implemented.  For instance, Hebrews 10:25 rather famously
instructs Christians not to forsake the assembling of themselves together.  Does that mean that the United States should
make church attendance compulsory?  Other
nations have certainly done so (possibly even on the basis of Hebrews 10:25),
but I, for one, think it would be a terrible idea!  If my congregation were filled with people
who didn’t want to be there, they would inevitably corrupt the faith of those
who did.

If we’re going to pick and choose what parts of the New
Testament get enshrined in law, then, how do we decide which parts get picked
and which parts get left out?  There are
people in America today who believe that the values of 1 Corinthians 6:9-10
should be reflected in our laws. 
Similarly, there are people in America today who believe that the values
of James 1:27 should be reflected in our laws.

Strangely, though, there’s not a lot of overlap in the Venn
diagram here.  People who believe the
former will usually not believe the latter, and people who emphasize the latter
usually reject the former.  In fact, both
groups will make basically the same argument for why they reject what they
reject.  They’ll say that it’s not the
government’s place to make mandatory a private conception of morality, even if
it is founded on the Bible.

I say all this not to criticize the efforts of all the
sincere people who try to work out their faith in the political realm.  I say it to make the point that such a
working out is neither easy nor simple. 
Following Christ as individuals is not easy, but at least it’s clear
what we’re supposed to do!  My own
spiritual problems are usually the result of failure of will, not of
confusion.  I know the right thing to do,
but I don’t do it.  By contrast, setting
a whole nation on the path of Christ can only be both difficult and uncertain.

In consequence, whenever we encounter a moral issue, our
first thought should not be of the political process.  Our first thought should be of our individual
responsibility to God and to others. 
What am I personally, in my daily life, supposed to do about this?

If I were the supreme ruler of the United States and
presented with the question of whether I should admit Syrian refugees, I don’t
know what I would do.  I haven’t thought
it through, at least in part because I have no reason to think it through.  It’s not my call.

By contrast, I know exactly what I’m supposed to do if a
Muslim moves in next door to me.  I know
exactly how I’m supposed to interact with the Muslims I encounter at work.  I know exactly how I’m supposed to talk about
Muslims on Facebook (and hopefully most of us realize by now that sweeping,
negative generalizations about a religion are usually perceived as personal
attacks by those belonging to that religion). 
I know exactly what I’m supposed to teach my children about how they
should treat Muslim children at school (well, at homeschool co-op, but you get
the point).  There are Scriptures that
are precisely on point in all of those cases, Matthew 22:39 not least among
them.

Maybe the government shouldn’t be trying to follow the
spirit of Matthew 22:39 in its dealings with Muslims.  To be honest, I can’t even get my head around
what that would look like.  However, we
must never allow our views about politics to obscure the clarity of our role as
disciples.
