**OVERVIEW.**  The size of the In Touch is larger than is
ideal for a reader, but the paper is excellent, the print is beautiful, and the
layout does a lot of things right.



Once again, no surprises here.  I used the In Touch as my daily reader and
employed it for whatever other large readings I felt the need to do.  Here’s what I thought of it:

**SIZE.** The In Touch is still a big Bible.  This has not changed since the first two
segments of the review.  It’s big enough
that I noticed it.  The size wasn’t
enough of a debit to take away from my pleasure in using the In Touch as a
reader, but it was a debit.

**LAYOUT.** Typically, the Bibles that I enjoy most
as readers are double-column text Bibles with a little bit over 40 characters
per line.  The In Touch isn’t quite up to
the ideal here.  Yes, it is a
double-column text Bible, but it’s also a wide-margin Bible, and that forces
further compromises on the design of the page. 
The In Touch has a very similar setting to Foundation’s Large-Print Pew
Bible (which is a Bible that I like enough that I’ve harassed Foundation about
printing it with a sewn binding and edge gilding), but the columns are a little
bit narrower in the In Touch.  They had
to be in order to maintain the generous margins to either side.  As a result, the In Touch probably has
something like 38-40 cpl instead of the 42-44 cpl common in other double-column
text Bibles.

| The In Touch (bottom) has slightly narrower columns than the Foundation Large-Print Pew Bible (top), but it's not a significant difference. |



However, this isn’t a terribly big deal.  As all Bible geeks know, double-column Bibles
are most problematic in the sections of the Old Testament that are set in verse
rather than prose.  The poetic lines are
frequently longer than the maximum width of the column, which forces the rest
of the content of the poetic line to the next line of text.  This results in undesirable one-word “widows”
and a decrease in reading speed and comprehension.

While I was reading the In Touch, though, I didn’t notice
any of these problems.  While I was
reviewing it, none of my daily readings were in poetry, but I did read
Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and Song of Solomon, all of which are at least
partially poetic.  The In Touch has its
share of widows, but there aren’t enough of them to give me substantial
problems.  In my experience, the minimum
desirable number of characters per line is somewhere in the neighborhood of
35-37 cpl, and the In Touch meets the standard. 
Also, the In Touch does not indent lines unless they are overflow lines,
which helps keep the cpl count up.

Good gutters also make for a good reading Bible, and boy
howdy, does the In Touch have good gutters! 
Even if you aren’t writing in that one-inch inner margin, it still
serves a useful function by pushing the text all the way out of the gutter.  Indeed, I felt like all the margins of the In
Touch contributed to my enjoyment in reading. 
Foundation settings are generally not known for their great beauty, but
the In Touch truly is a pretty setting, Times New Roman and all.  The white space really makes the text stand
out.

I also appreciated that the In Touch is a black-letter
Bible.  I know; I know.  All kinds of people love their red-letter
Bibles, and they’re decent, good people, but the fact remains that red
lettering is awfully difficult to execute well. 
Even a premium red-letter Bible isn’t going to be as easy to read as a
black-letter Bible.

The In Touch isn’t a line-matched Bible, but, as I observed
earlier, this isn’t really a problem.  The
paper is so good that it laughs at ghosting.

Finally, the print in the In Touch is nice and big.  10.5-point Times New Roman might not be the
most aesthetically pretty choice, but it’s fairly easy to read.  I wouldn’t recommend this Bible for someone
who needs strong reading glasses, but for the rest of us, it’s a pleasant break
from squinting at Pitt Minions.

**PRODUCTION
QUALITY.** In a word, superb.  I think the text in the In Touch is as crisp
and sharp as anything Jongbloed has ever produced.  I own a 2008 China-print Foundation SCR that
my father gave me.  It’s set in 11-point
Times New Roman, but there’s simply no comparison between the print quality in
it and the print quality in the In Touch. 
I can read either Bible just fine, certainly, but the beauty of the In
Touch is much greater, and I will probably find it more readable once my vision
starts its inevitable decline.

Likewise with the paper. 
This is the kind of paper I would expect from a premium-quality
wide-margin Bible, yet I find it in a mass-market hardback.  Probably the only mass-market competitor
would be the Crossway Legacy, but the Legacy has other problems for me that
I’ve already explored elsewhere.  You
simply can’t ask for better paper in a Bible than this.

**CONCLUSION.** Other than size and heaviness—again—I
just don’t have anything bad to say about the In Touch.  At this point in my Bible-reviewing career, I
much prefer a heavier Bible with better paper to a lighter Bible with more
translucent paper.  That being the case,
there’s not a whole lot wrong with the In Touch.  I think it warrants a 9/10 as a reading
Bible.  Overall, it scores an extremely
respectable 25.5/30.
