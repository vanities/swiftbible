One of the distinctive features of most New-Testament
epistles is that the closer to the end you get, the more practical they
become.  Paul and others will generally
start off by addressing one or more doctrinal topics, establishing the
superiority of Christ or something like that, but before they wrap up, they
nearly always include a section of advice on godly living.

The book of Hebrews follows this same pattern.  The main purpose of the letter is to convince
Jewish Christians to remain faithful to the Lord instead of returning to the
Law of Moses.  As a result, there are all
sorts of admonitions not to fall away throughout the book.  However, in the last couple of chapters, the
Hebrews writer’s admonitions branch out a little bit.  Instead of being so concerned with
perseverance, he starts addressing other spiritual behaviors, both good and
bad, as well.  For our own sakes, then,
let’s consider what Hebrews looks like in practice.



**Contrasting Two Covenants**

However, the writer begins this practical section with one
last doctrinal argument, this one based on the difference between the old and
new covenants.  He compares these two
covenants by describing **TWO
MOUNTAINS.** These two descriptions
appear in Hebrews 12:18-24.  There’s a
sense in which this comparison isn’t covering any new ground.  After all, Paul compares Judaism to Mount
Sinai and Christianity to Jerusalem in Galatians 4, which is likely earlier
than Hebrews.

What is new about this description, though, is the vividness
of the language that the Hebrews writer is using.  Let’s look first of all at the way that he
describes Mt. Sinai.  If we were to go
back to Exodus, we would see that the scene of God giving the Law to Moses is a
dramatic one, and this depiction summons up all that drama.  We have fire, darkness, gloom, and
whirlwind.  We have a trumpet and an
unbearable voice.  We have a mountain
that can be touched, but that means death if you touch it.  Even Moses was terrified at the sight.  We come away from this description thinking
that Sinai must be the next best thing to Mount Doom in Mordor!

I think that’s the point. 
The Hebrews writer wants us to understand that the Law of Moses wasn’t
really about access.  Instead, its
purpose was to emphasize the holiness of God and the impossibility of sinful
mankind approaching that holiness.  We’re
not under that covenant, but it’s still a lesson we need to take to heart.  Many Americans treat God with familiar
contempt.  They call on Him for help when
they’re in trouble, but the rest of the time, they scoff at His commandments
and use His holy name as a cuss word. 
Brethren, that’s extraordinarily dangerous behavior.  If we take God lightly, we will surely have
eternity to regret our error.

By contrast, the Hebrews writer paints an inviting picture
of Mount Zion.  Mount Sinai was filled
with terrifying things, but the heavenly Jerusalem is filled with inviting
beings.  The angels, the righteous who
have gone before us, the church in its assembly, and God Himself are all there
waiting for us.

Most of all, of course, there is Jesus and His sprinkled
blood, which the Hebrews writer says speaks better than the blood of Abel.  Here’s the point:  when Cain killed Abel, God told Cain that his
brother’s blood cried out against him from the ground.  The shed blood of Christ cries out too, but
rather than crying out for our punishment, it cries out for our
forgiveness.  It is only because of that
blood that Mount Zion is an inviting place for us at all.

Next, the writer explains the importance of having **AN UNSHAKEN KINGDOM.** Consider Hebrews 12:25-29.  The point here, I think, is that access has a
flip side.  God offered the Israelites a
comparatively minor blessing, yet He destroyed them in the wilderness when they
rejected it.  Today, now that God has
provided us with a way to heaven at the cost of the blood of His only Son, we
must take advantage of it!  Otherwise,
we’re guilty of the worst kind of ingratitude.

In fact, being a part of God’s kingdom is the only way of
escape open to us.  When God’s voice at
Mount Sinai was so loud that it shook the earth, it was impressive.  However, we ain’t seen nothing yet!  When God speaks from heaven not only to shake
the earth but to destroy it, not only to shake the heaven but to destroy it,
that will be an awesome sight!  It will
also spell the doom of everyone who has set their hopes on the created things
that can and will be destroyed.

Instead, the only things that will remain are those that
cannot be shaken:  God and His
kingdom.  That’s what being a Christian
means.  It means being a part of
something that will outlast the universe. 
Everything else will be gone, but God’s people will continue.

What the writer says next is completely logical.  If God gives us a choice between seeking His
kingdom and His righteousness or not, but that kingdom is the only safe place,
there’s only one reasonable course. 
Filled with the fear of God and His destructive power, we should serve Him
reverently, gratefully, and above all faithfully.  I always tell my kids, “Don’t mess with
stuff,” and God is at the top of the list of things not to be messed with.  Only an idiot tries to see how much he can
get away with when it comes to the Almighty! 
If we know we aren’t offering Him everything we can, we need to repent
and put Him at the top of every list in our lives.

**Godly Behavior**

Now that the writer has emphasized the importance of
godliness, he goes on to explain what godly behavior looks like.  It starts with various activities of **DAILY LIVING.** Read with me from Hebrews 13:1-6.  Too often, I think we think of discipleship
as something that only happens inside the church building.  Here, though, the writer is concerned with
activities outside the assembly.

The first of them are closely connected:  hospitality and caring for prisoners.  To put things another way, this is about two
different ways of expressing love for our brethren.  The first is by inviting them to where we
are.  The second is by going to where
they are.

This begins with hospitality 
If we want to be disciples of Jesus, we need to practice hospitality
regularly.  That means not only inviting
our friends into our homes, but inviting strangers.  In Greek, the word “hospitality” literally
means “love of strangers”.  When we show
those strangers that we love them by sharing a meal with them, it glorifies God
and opens doors for the gospel.

Similarly, we need to care for prisoners.  2000 years ago, this referred to those who
were imprisoned for the Lord’s sake. 
Today, we don’t have to face that, but there are still several members
of this congregation who are imprisoned in their own homes by their failing
health.  Visiting shut-ins is another
part of the regular practice of godliness, and I have always found that as much
as shut-ins gain from the times when I visit them, I gain even more.

Third, we must honor the marriage bed.  Sex within the bonds of lawful marriage is
good and holy in the sight of God.  Sex
in any other context invites His condemnation. 
Whether we are married or single, we must remember that our bodies
belong to the Lord.  If we don’t use
those bodies according to His purposes, we will surely be judged for it.

Finally, the writer warns us against the love of money.  We need to listen to this one, brethren.  Certainly, we have a duty to provide for
ourselves and our families, but few of us lead subsistence-level lives.  If it is our wants rather than our needs that
are driving us to work and crowding out the Lord, we’re in the middle of a
spiritual problem and revealing our lack of faith.

Next, the writer explores how we should handle **SPIRITUAL CHANGE.** Here, let’s look at Hebrews 13:7-9.  This is a passage about looking back, looking
elsewhere, and why the first is better than the second.  Let’s start with the looking-back part.  The writer instructs us to consider those who
first led and taught us, how they lived, and what they got out of it.  In context, he may well have had the apostles
in mind, but today, I think we can apply the text to those who taught us the
truth, especially if it happened a while ago. 
The older I get, the more those people pass from the scene.  I can remember preachers, elders, Bible-class
teachers, and my own mother, all of whom aren’t here anymore.  However, I know how they lived, I know how
they died, and I believe what the Scripture tells me about where they are
now.  Brethren, if all of us can live the
life that they did, then we have succeeded in the most important thing, and we
can’t improve on that!

Of course, that doesn’t stop people from trying.  Rather than seeking to imitate the godly men
and women of years past, they’re always interested in some shiny new religious
teaching that offers more excitement than the predictable same old, same
old.  In the Hebrews writer’s day, that
involved restrictive teaching about foods that appeared to offer some spiritual
benefit.  In our day, the new teachings
are about departing from the ancient pattern and/or embracing sexual
permissiveness, among other things.  However,
departure in any era only means disaster.

In between encouragement and warning, the Hebrews writer
gives us the reason for both.  We should
seek continuity rather than innovation because Jesus Christ is the same and
will always be the same.  He is the reason
why we must not stray from the ancient paths. 
This is not to say, of course, that we must do everything exactly the
same way as brethren did in the 1950s, but we must make sure that any change
occurs in an area where we have been given liberty.  We can proclaim Jesus as Lord on the Internet
too, but our actions must always reveal that He is Lord indeed.

The final discussion in this section concerns **AN ALTAR.**It appears in Hebrews 13:10-14. 
For the final time in the book, the Hebrews writer here ventures into
Law-of-Moses land.  He points out that according
to Leviticus 16, when the high priest went into the most holy place once a year
on the Day of Atonement to sprinkle blood on the Ark of the Covenant, once he
had extracted blood from the goat, the body of the goat was burned outside the
camp.  Nobody got to eat it.

However, we have something better.  Our sin offering was Jesus.  We are sprinkled by His blood, but His body
was not burned.  In fact, every Lord’s
Day, we symbolically share in His flesh as we partake of the Lord’s
Supper.  However, if we want to share in
Christ, we have to go where He suffered. 
We have to go to Him outside the camp.

To the original audience of Jewish Christians, this would
have been a powerful statement.  They
would have understood that the writer wasn’t talking about the physical camp of
the Israelites during the wilderness wanderings.  Instead, they would have recognized that he
was talking about spiritual association with the Jews.  If they wanted to follow Christ, they were
going to have to leave their earthly nation, and even their earthly families,
behind.

The rule is no different for us, friends.  We too have a camp.  So far as I know, all of us were raised in
the United States.  We are very familiar
with our society and culture, and I think all of us are aware that it is
hostile to godliness.  There is no sitcom
on TV that is going to help you or encourage you to follow Jesus.  Even beyond that, some of us may well have
families who are trying to make us make a choice between them and Jesus.

Brethren, we can’t have it both ways.  We can’t have the camp and Jesus.  We have to pick the camp or Jesus.  If we go to Jesus, we will have to bear
reproach like Jesus did.  He Himself
promised that the world will not love us for it because the world only loves
its own.  However, only if we go to Jesus
will we receive the eternal life that He offers because He offered Himself.

Before the Lord’s Supper, then, let’s think about this.  Let’s reflect on Jesus’ sufferings outside
the gate.  He was despised, rejected, and
ultimately killed because He loved God and loved us more than anything
else.  Second, let’s think long and hard
about what it means to be His disciples. 
Our goal is to be like Jesus. 
That means that we too have to love God and love others more than
anything else, no matter what it costs us. 
Are we going to Him, or are we shuffling around vaguely while staying in
the camp because it’s comfortable here? 
Let’s ponder as we partake.

**Closing Comments**

Finally, let’s consider the closing comments of the
book.  They begin with a discussion of **BEHAVIOR IN THE CHURCH.** Consider Hebrews 13:15-19.  Within this reading, the writer first
addresses sacrifices.  He identifies
three in particular:  praise, doing good,
and sharing.  When we see the word
“sacrifice”, we read all kinds of implications into it.  We think that it has to involve pain,
suffering, hurting, and giving up something important to us.  However, that’s not really the sense of
sacrifices in the Old Testament or the Bible generally.  Sure, there’s a cost associated with Biblical
sacrifice, but it’s a price we gladly pay rather than one that hurts us to
pay.  Instead, the idea is one of
excellence, of offering a sacrifice that is unblemished, that is the very best
we have to offer.

With this in mind, then, the sacrifice of praise doesn’t
mean that we have to sing with an expression on our faces that looks like we’re
passing a kidney stone.  Instead, it
means that our worship is the best we can give, with our whole bodies and our
whole hearts involved.  The sacrifice of
doing good doesn’t mean that we grumble about how there are things at home we
could be doing.  Instead, it means that
we cheerfully offer our time and energy as to the Lord.  So too with the sacrifice of sharing.  It’s cheerful rather than grudging, because
our pleasure pleases God.

Next, we see a couple of pieces of advice about how to treat
church leaders.  Hebrews 13:17 on its
face only speaks of generic leaders, but here, as with a couple of other
passages in Scripture, it’s obvious that the leaders the writer has in mind are
actually elders.  When it comes to
elders, ordinary Christians have two responsibilities:  obey and submit.  They want to help you on to heaven.  Why not make their jobs easier and your life
better by doing what they tell you to do?

Second, the writer discusses prayer, prayer for himself and
his fellow workers particularly.  This is
worthwhile advice when it comes to any leader, for lack of a better term, in
the church.  Elders appreciate prayers,
deacons appreciate prayers, and preachers certainly appreciate prayers!  There are few things that encourage me more
than somebody telling me, “I’m praying for you!”

The writer then wraps up the book with **A FINAL BLESSING.** It appears
in Hebrews 13:20-25.  I think there’s a
tendency to read through the prayers in Scripture without paying attention to
what they say, but in reality, they’re often quite doctrinally rich.  So it is here.  The writer begins by identifying the past
work of God.  His wisdom and power are
shown in His resurrection of Jesus.  The
implied point is that if God can make Jesus alive, He can make us useful.  It’s within His power, if only we ask Him to
do it, to equip us to glorify Him and His Son.

I was talking with Lauren the other day about a discussion
the ladies had been having in the Saturday ladies’ class, about how our prayers
can reveal our spiritual maturity or lack of it.  When we’re praying for others, what kinds of
things do we pray for?  Do we pray for
health, for a new job, or for comfort for them in some earthly trial?  Or do we pray for spiritual blessings, so
that God will equip them to glorify Him? 
Brethren, as important as they seem to us right now, health and jobs and
earthly comfort don’t count for a whole lot. 
Glorifying God, though, is the whole shebang.  That’s why we’re here.  If we miss that, we’ve missed everything.

After this, we see a typically first-century set of
greetings.  This tells us basically
everything we know about the Hebrews writer. 
He knew Timothy, though apparently a little later than the era of the
book of Acts, in which we never see Timothy imprisoned, and he was writing from
Italy.  The Hebrews writer may have been
Paul, though I tend to think not, or it may have been somebody else.  However, it’s clear here, as it is throughout
the book, that its true author is the Holy Spirit, and its timeless wisdom
continues to benefit us today no less than our brethren millennia ago.
