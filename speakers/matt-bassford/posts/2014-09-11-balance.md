When I was talking to my dad the other night, somehow we got
on the subject of child-rearing.  In the
course of this conversation, for the first time that I can remember, I
articulated the virtues I most wanted to instill in my children, and which I
believe would be valuable for any child. 
The first of these is balance.

“Balance” is not a word that appears in Scripture in the
sense in which I am using it, but I think it’s a Scriptural concept.  I refer, of course, not to a literal,
physical sense of balance.  Any children
of mine who take after me are doomed not to have it!  Instead, by “balance” I mean an adroitness of
soul that enables one to navigate the obstacles of life without stumbling.

 This balance is inseparable from trust in God.  As Habakkuk observes in Habakkuk 2:4,
“Behold, as for the proud one, his soul is not right within him, but the
righteous will live by his faith.” (NASB) 
New-Testament authors commonly focus on the second half of this verse,
but I’ve always been fascinated by the description of pride in the first
half.

I sense pride, whether in myself or others, as a disquiet,
an absence of peace, as a flaw in the soul. 
Because pride is not calm, is not centered, it always has to push, to be
loud, to insist on itself.  The balanced
soul does none of these things.  It is
able to be still because it rests on God.

The balanced soul is also able to deal gracefully with the
ups and downs of life.  Our hymn “In His
Time” quotes Ecclesiastes 3:11 from the KJV, which says, “He hath made every
thing beautiful in his time.”  The NASB’s
rendering, though less striking, is perhaps more profound, saying, “He has made
everything appropriate in its time.”

Balance requires an unerring sense of what is appropriate
through each season of life.  It knows
when to mourn, when to rejoice, when to work, and when to rest.  However, balance is able to keep these things
in perspective.  It understands that
things are never so bad nor so good as they appear and is able to moderate its
reaction, no matter the circumstances.

Similarly, balance knows how to address differing viewpoints
with grace.  Modern-day America strikes
me as a fragmented, polarized society, in which people associate mainly with
those who already agree with them.  This
creates an intellectual echo chamber, in which repetition of shared opinion
makes those opinions more extreme and engenders a false certainty of their
rightness.  When we encounter those who
disagree with us, we too often respond with shrill self-righteousness
(see:  the Internet) or, worse still,
retreat to the shelter of the like-minded and refuse to engage others at all.

Balance is able to remain secure in its beliefs while still
being open to discussion and re-examination of those beliefs.  It is rare for others, even others with whom
we disagree strongly, to be 100 percent wrong. 
Generally, our opponents have a point (though that point may not be as
powerful as they think it is), and it is intellectually dishonest for us to
dismiss what they say without first considering it.  Balance will listen for truth in the words of
even the most heated contradiction, embrace that truth when it is found, and
use it to build bridges and persuade.

I have never really thought about how to teach my children
to do this.  I tend to subscribe to a
Zen-like view of parenting.  I believe
that in training my children, I cannot be other than who I truly am.  No matter how hard I try, they will recognize
and learn from my actual values rather than the values I merely profess.  As I think in my heart, so will I be, and
that is what I will teach my children to be. 
I need to worry less about working on them and more about working on
myself.

Having said that, here are some values to teach that will
lead to balance:

·        
**Rely on
God.** As Proverbs 3:5-6 so aptly
says, “Trust in the Lord with all your heart, and do not lean on your own
understanding.  In all your ways
acknowledge Him, and He will make your paths straight.” (NASB)  When our lives are out of balance, it is
often because we are trying to make things turn out the way we want them to
turn out, rather than leaning on the Lord. 
We’re trying too hard because we’re not trusting.  Instead, we need to teach and show our
children that if they give their lives to God, we can trust Him to work things
out for us rather than having to work them out on our own.  Faith does not know all the steps of the
journey it will take, but it is certain of the destination.

·        
**Keep
Perspective.** It’s fine for us or our
children to experience negative emotions. 
Sadness, frustration, and fear are all part of the human
experience.  However, we must keep a
proper sense of proportion.  Molehills
are not mountains, and most of the problems of our lives are molehills.  We cannot allow negative emotion to fester in
our minds, and we certainly cannot permit it to become an excuse for ungodly
behavior.  However, some emotions, like
grief over the loss of a loved one, can take months or years to process, and in
those situations, perspective urges us not to force an unnatural resolution.

·        
**Be
Thoughtful.**  Most of us spend too
much time reacting rather than reasoning and then acting.  I certainly do!  However, only rarely is an immediate answer
the best answer.  All of us, children and
adult alike, will benefit from learning to take a deep breath, count to ten,
pray if necessary, and then respond.



·        
**Listen.**  We should not use the time when someone else
is talking to compose our reply!  Every
human being is created in the image of God, so every human being is worthy of
our attention.  Others can tell whether
we are listening or not.  When we listen,
we show respect and give ourselves the opportunity to learn.  I’ve never yet learned a thing from my own
words, but the words of others have taught me quite a bit.
