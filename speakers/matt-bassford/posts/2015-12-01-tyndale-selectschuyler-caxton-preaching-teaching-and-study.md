**OVERVIEW:** I can’t write in this text block, which
is what I want to do with preaching Bibles, and its layout is very different
from what I’m used to in the pulpit. 
Those things notwithstanding, these are solid Bibles for use in
preaching.

This review is going to be a little bit unusual for me.  As I’ve mentioned before, the Select and the
Caxton have exactly the same text block. 
As a result, I chose not to put both Bibles through a two-week trial
period (although, as I’ll be talking about later, I foresee a lot more use for
the Caxton).  When both Bibles would
function the same way, why bother?  As a
result, I only field-tested the Select, but all of my observations about it will
apply with equal force to the Caxton.

That field test, though, was pretty extensive.  From the Select, I preached three sermons,
taught four Bible classes, led one Bible study, and used it in my office for
two weeks.  Here are my impressions:



**PREACHING.** In theory, the Select and Caxton are
everything I can’t stand in a pulpit Bible. 
I like to write my sermon outlines in the margins; they generally don’t
offer me space for that.  I have trouble
locating verses in single-column paragraphed Bibles.  The Select text block is single-column
paragraphed.  This is a Bible that should
have bombed, but it didn’t.

Of course, there are some things that the Select text block
can never be, and one of those is a wide-margin Bible.  I think the designers made a great choice in
making the inner margin larger than the outer margin, but there was a price to
pay for that.  Nobody can put much in a
quarter-inch outer margin, not unless you’re one of those guys who does
paintings on grains of rice.  I saw no
reason even to attempt it.  In places,
the reference column has few enough references in it that there’s room for
notes, but that space is least in the portions of the Bible where I would want
to say the most.

In addition, I’m not sure that the paper is heavy enough for
much writing.  It’s 28-gsm India
Thinopaque, which is much thinner than the 38-gsm Tervakowski Thinopaque found
in, for instance, the Cambridge Wide-Margin. 
I think you can get away with underlining in the Select text block, not
least because of the massive leading.  I
don’t think, however, that you could write notes on both sides of the page
without bleedthrough problems.

However, even though the Select didn’t offer me any scope
for my dubious penmanship, it also didn’t give me any trouble while I was preaching.  In the past, I’ve found that I miss citations
when I’m using single-column, paragraphed Bibles.  In many cases, I think this is due to the
design constraints of single-column Bibles generally.  Single-column Bibles have more pages than
double-column Bibles, so designers of such Bibles try to keep page count down
by using very long lines and cramming lines as closely together as
possible.  That makes verse numbers hard
to distinguish.

By contrast, the designers of the Select appear to have had
a more page-centric design philosophy. 
They were going to make each page of the Bible look good, and the Bible
was going to be however long it was going to be.  That’s the reason for the thin paper—when a
Bible is 2100 pages long, you don’t have a choice.  However, it means that each page of the
Select has elegant proportions and generous leading.

As a result, I didn’t miss any verse citations in any of my
three sermons.  In addition to the
leading, which kept my eyes from skipping lines, I also benefited from the
verse numbers, which had a lighter weight than the body text.  When verse numbers have the same weight, I
get them mixed up with quotation marks.

My evaluation of Bibles in the pulpit always includes a “size”
component, but nothing really stood out about the Select here.  Because I wasn’t reading notes out of it, I
didn’t feel the need to have it in my hand the whole time, but when I was
holding it, I wasn’t bothered by it.  It
is true, though, that in the pulpit, I couldn’t put it side by side with my
sermon notebook.  Of course, no Bible
much larger than a Pitt Minion is going to pass that test.

There is one final trait of the Select that’s worth
mentioning, though it has more to do with translation than with Bible.  I’m very time-conscious in the pulpit these
days.  I’ve even come up with an equation
that tells me how long my sermon manuscripts should be for a given number of
verses in order to reach a predetermined length.  As a result, my sermons all wrap up within
five minutes of each other.

However, all the sermons I preached from the Select ran
short.  Way short.  Like, 10 minutes short.  I think the culprit was the NLT.  Because it’s a dynamic-equivalent rather than
formal-equivalent Bible, the text was already fairly easy to understand, so I
didn’t spend much time rephrasing and explaining it.  The moral of the story may be that if you
think your preacher is too long-winded, you should buy him an NLT!

Sermon-length speculations aside, the Select/Caxton text
block didn’t fare any worse as a preaching Bible than any double-column Bible
without room to write notes.  Not ideal,
but not bad either.

**TEACHING.** Not much to report on here.  I’m teaching at a table these days, so
there’s no need for me to be holding my Bible. 
Neither do I remember tracking down any passages via cross-reference.  The Select text block is indeed a full NLT
reference Bible, but the NLT reference suite isn’t as large as those of some
other translations.  For instance, an
NASB reference Bible has 96,000 cross-references, and an ESV reference Bible
has 80,000.

If you want All The References in Bible class, the NLT isn’t
for you, but this may be a case of quality over quantity.  Through the years, many of the references in
the NASB and ESV alike have struck me as being of dubious utility, so it’s
possible that the architects of the NLT simply got rid of a lot of the dead
wood.

My most noteworthy experiences with the NLT as a teaching
Bible had to do with translation.  I’m
currently teaching a junior-high class that is a textual study of Revelation,
and at times, I felt that the NLT read enough differently from the ESV’s and
NASB’s that the students were using that it might have been confusing to
them.  However, if there was any
confusion, I didn’t feel that it remained after we discussed the text.  As always, translation differences aren’t a
huge deal, but they’re something to consider.

**STUDY.** Here, we come to something that the
Select and Caxton do better than my normal preaching Bible, which is a
Cambridge Wide-Margin.  They fit more
comfortably in my lap when I’m sitting at my computer than it does.  It’s not a big difference—the Wide-Margin is
about three-quarters of an inch taller—but I did notice it.  I didn’t have to hunch over quite so far to
reach the keyboard.

The NLT references were there, had I wanted to use
them.  I didn’t.  I did, however, want to correctly cite
verses, and the user-friendly single-column layout of the Select allowed me to
do that.

**CONCLUSION.** I don’t think the Select was designed
primarily for pulpit use, but the design is so strong overall that the Bible
serves capably in that role.  I think I’m
willing to give it an 8/10 for preaching, teaching, and study.
