**OVERVIEW.** As is invariably the case with
“large-print compact Bibles” these days, this Bible would be better described
as a “large-print-for-a-compact Bible”. 
However, as long as you come to it with the right expectations, it
delivers decent mass-market quality for a reasonable price.

This Bible is a review copy that Foundation graciously sent
to me.  As always, they didn’t place any
conditions on the sending other than that I review it.  I’ve already given it away anyway, so it’s
not like I had any incentive to shade the truth!



**VITAL STATISTICS**

**Translation:**  New American Standard Bible (1995)

**Cover:**  Polyurethane (“Leathertex”)

**Binding:**  Sewn

**Trim Size:**  4 ¾” x 7”

**Text Block Width:**  1”

**Weight:**  1 pound, 3 ounces

**Column:**  Single

**Layout:**  Paragraphed

**Font:**  9-point ITC Weidemann

**Words of Christ:**  Red

**Cross-References:**  No

**Ribbon Marker:**  1

**SIZE.** The LPC has dimensions that are just
about ideal for a carry Bible.  It’s a
little thicker than your average thinline, but it’s a good inch shorter.  Its closest analogue in my collection is the
Pitt Minion.  It has almost exactly the
same length and width, but about 150 percent of the Pitt’s thickness.  This is a Bible that will fit easily in all
backpacks and briefcases, many purses, and all glove boxes that don’t contain
insurance cards from 1997.  This is one
of the best grab-and-go NASB’s available.



**COVER.** This is a poly Bible, but it’s a rugged
poly Bible.  Unlike many poly Bibles out
there, the cover is all in one piece, which, in addition to being cheaper to
manufacture (I’m assuming), also should add to the durability of the
Bible.  I’ve reviewed Bibles that felt
like they might start to split at the seams; a Bible that doesn’t have any
seams isn’t going to do that. 
Interestingly, the LPC doesn’t have perimeter stitching like most poly
Bibles do, but even without that reinforcement, I don’t get the sense that the
polyurethane is going to start coming unstuck from the bookboard.  Both the poly itself and the bookboard seem
thicker than is normal for a poly Bible.

In addition, Foundation made another production decision
that bodes well for the durability of the LPC. 
Rather than having endpapers that are merely thick paper, the LPC has
leatherette endpapers.  I think
leatherette is paper with a vinyl coating; I know leatherette endpapers hold up
better than the paper variety.  In
consequence, leatherette is always a welcome sight.  I expect it in high-end paste-down Bibles
(think calf-split Cambridge Bibles here), but it doesn’t normally appear in
small mass-market Bibles.

Basically, the LPC is as solidly constructed as a poly Bible
can be.  It will have the usual
vulnerability of poly Bibles to sunlight, but other than that, the cover will
stand up to all sorts of abuse.  This is
the kind of Bible that is a great choice for a child.  Even if your son doesn’t take great care of
his Bible (and most boys won’t), this cover will endure a lot of punishment
before it expires.

I really am not sure how it would fail.  An endpaper split, maybe?  Of course, the best way to avoid that problem
is by *not* putting church bulletins,
Bible-class lesson sheets, etc., inside your Bible cover.  Bible covers should only contain Bibles!

I would much rather have a Bible cover that is durable than
a cover that is lovable, but when it comes to cover lovability, I would rate
the LPC about average.  The cover doesn’t
have an impressed grain, so it has something of a rubbery feel.  It might fool somebody who doesn’t know
Bibles into thinking that it’s made out of leather, but that’s about it.  The blind-stamped cover design—an intricate
cross in the center, surrounded by a perimeter line—is simple but reasonably
attractive.

Alone of all the Bibles I can remember reviewing, the spine
does *not* say “Holy Bible” on it, nor
does it have a profusion of translation and publisher logos.  Instead, it says nothing but “NEW AMERICAN
STANDARD BIBLE” straight down the spine in some sans-serif typeface.  It gives the Bible a very utilitarian,
instruction-manual feel, which might be what Foundation is going for!

I’m neutral on the choice. 
I like the traditional Bible-spine look, but I like different too.  I don’t believe this would work for a
high-end Bible, but I think it works here.

**TEXT BLOCK.** Like every Foundation Bible I’ve ever
reviewed, this one has a sewn binding.  I
think only Foundation pew Bibles and true compact Bibles have a glued
binding.  This reflects a significant
change for the better all across the Bible-publishing industry over the past 10
years.  Glued Bibles are showpieces.  They fall apart if you actually try to use
them for any prolonged period of time. 
You eventually end up with a Bible cover that is basically a folder for
storing unattached pages and clumps of pages.

Sewn bindings, on the other hand, can stand up to 15 years
or more of daily use.  My dad’s sewn 2002
Foundation Side-Column Reference has seen an astounding amount of wear, but it
has never shed a single page.  The paper
itself is failing (yes, that legendary French-milled paper will wear out if you
study your Bible enough), but the binding never will.

**VALUE.** The LPC has a retail list price of
$27.  In real life, of course, you can
find it for about two-thirds of that online (which is why the Christian
bookstore near me has just about given up on stocking Bibles).  That’s a dollar less than its closest
competitor, Crossway’s ESV Large-Print Compact. 
The Holman NKJV Large-Print Compact is considerably cheaper, but at
least I when I reviewed it in late 2013, it had a glued binding, which
disqualifies it from serious consideration.

The Crossway has more attractively styled covers, but it
doesn’t have leatherette endpapers. 
Personally, I care much less about what a Bible looks like and much more
about how long it’s going to survive.  On
that basis, I think it’s fair to say that the Foundation LPC offers
above-average value for money.



**CONCLUSION.** Small, cheap, and nearly
indestructible—that’s a pretty good formula for success in my Physical Form
ratings.  How can I do other than give
the LPC a perfect 10/10?
