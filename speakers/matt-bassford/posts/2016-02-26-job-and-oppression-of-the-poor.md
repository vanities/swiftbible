Recently, my 90-day Bible-reading plan took me on a
three-day tour of the book of Job.  Over
the past couple of years, I’ve found that Job is one of the unexpected gifts
that God gives to daily Bible-readers. 
For some reason, our study of Job usually focuses on the frame tale of
Job 1-2 and 42:7-17.

However, that bald narrative certainly is not the point of
the book.  Instead, the dialogue between
Job and his friends is the point, as they grapple with the uncomfortable truth
that in a world where God is sovereign, trial still comes upon the
righteous.  Our society prefers to
pretend that bad things never happen to anybody, so many Christians find
themselves woefully unprepared for the trial that comes upon them.  If we spent more time in the middle 40 chapters
of Job, we would be better equipped to face it.

Job is always a delight, but Job is particularly delightful
this time through.  For one thing, I’m
reading out of the NLT, and Job is a place where the freer, down-to-earth
renderings of the NLT shine.  In the NLT,
Job actually sounds like a bunch of friends talking to each other instead of a
group of actors reciting Shakespearean drama. 
I think that’s valuable.  Second,
the NLT that I’m using is the single-column Schuyler Caxton, and here, as
elsewhere in the poetic sections of the Bible, a well designed single-column
Bible presents the text with a clarity that no double-column Bible can
match.  Job simply looks beautiful on the
page.

As a result of this happy combination of translation and
setting, I have a much better overall handle on Job than I’ve ever had
before.  I’m able to see not only the
arguments of Job and his friends, but even the things they assume and take for
granted.

Most notably, throughout this reading, I’ve been struck by
the way that all of the speakers define evil. 
To them, the archetypal evil man, the paradigmatic evildoer, is not
someone who lies or is sexually immoral. 
Instead, what distinguishes the wicked more than anything else is that
they oppress the poor and vulnerable in order to get rich.

Consider, for instance, Job’s presentation of evil in Job
24:1-12:

*Why doesn't the
Almighty bring the wicked to judgment?*

*Why must the godly
wait for him in vain?*

*Evil people steal land
by moving the boundary markers.*

*They steal livestock
and put them in their own pastures.*

*They take the orphan's
donkey and demand the widow's ox as security for a loan.*

*The poor are pushed
off the path; the needy must hide together for safety.*

*Like wild donkeys in
the wilderness,*

*the poor must spend
all their time looking for food,*

*searching even in the
desert for food for their children.*

*They harvest a field
they do not own,*

*and they glean in the
vineyards of the wicked.*

*All night they lie
naked in the cold,*

*without clothing or
covering.*

*They are soaked by
mountain showers,*

*and they huddle
against the rocks for want of a home.*

*The wicked snatch a
widow's child from her breast,*

*taking the baby as
security for a loan.*

*The poor must go about naked, without any
clothing.*

*They harvest food for
others while they themselves are starving.*

*They press out olive
oil without being allowed to taste it,*

*and they tread in the
winepress as they suffer from thirst.*

*The groans of the
dying rise from the city,*

*and the wounded cry
for help,*

*yet God ignores their
moaning.* (NLT)

There are many similar passages in the Old Testament, and
even several in the New Testament (see, for instance, James 5:1-6).  The Biblical witness is clear.  Oppressing the powerless and helpless is
evil, and it is an offense to God, even when it is done under color of
law.  It is commonplace for Christians to
bemoan the moral decline of America. 
What is less often bemoaned, however, is the decline of concern for the
poor and the increase in laws that allow the wealthy to exploit them.

To pick one example of many, it is currently
difficult-to-impossible to discharge student-loan debt in bankruptcy.  That is a flatly immoral law.  It exists for one reason:  student-loan companies are loaded and can
hire lobbyists; college students aren’t and can’t.  I’ve watched friends of mine suffer greatly
because of this law, and I know there are hundreds of thousands of others who
have endured similar suffering.

Yes, I know; all of those college students willingly signed
the loan agreements.  The orphans and
widows in Job 24 “willingly” pledged their oxen and donkeys as security
too.  Under the Mosaic system, every
seven years saw a general cancellation of debts, and every 50 years, land
returned to its ancestral owners.  U.S.
bankruptcy law fulfills a similar function—to balance the interests of rich and
poor.  A law that does not allow such
balancing is exploitative, even if it is legal.

There is an obvious personal application for all Christians
here (“Be merciful to the poor!”), but there is a political one too.  If we are going to base our advocacy and
votes on the godliness of a particular candidate, we had better use the whole
counsel of God to evaluate that godliness.

In Biblical terms, abortion is clearly, indisputably, a
great evil.  Disciples of Jesus should
defend the sanctity of all life, both of the very young and the very old (the
recent movement toward legalized euthanasia is an extremely troubling one).  However, while we do that, we must also
acknowledge that the writers of the Bible spent far more time warning against
abuse of the poor than they did proclaiming life in the womb or even condemning
child sacrifice.  If our laws should
reflect the latter, they should certainly reflect the former too.
