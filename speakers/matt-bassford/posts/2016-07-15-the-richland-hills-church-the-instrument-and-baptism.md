One of the amusing things about the Internet is that it
tends to preserve things long after the writers of those things have forgotten
them.  The other day, I found myself
wondering, “Whatever became of the Richland Hills Church of Christ”?  I don’t know and have never known too much
about them; they belong (belonged?) to a very different section of my religious
tradition than I do.  However, I did read
a book some years back that really took the church to task for adopting
instrumental music, so I decided to investigate what has happened to them
since.

My investigation began with [this 2006 essay](http://preachermike.com/2006/12/03/leroy-richland-hills-and-instrumental-music) by Leroy Garrett.  I was hoping to find
therein a justification of the decision to embrace the instrument, but, alas,
was disappointed.

However, it does contain some interesting predictions of the
future.  Bro. Garrett implied that other
large churches of Christ that adopted the instrument went off the rails shortly
thereafter.  Among such churches, he
probably would have counted the then-Oak Hills Church of Christ in San Antonio,
TX, Max Lucado’s church, which soon removed “of Christ” from the sign and began
to deny that baptism is necessary for salvation.



However, he appeared to be convinced of better things than
that concerning Richland Hills.  He
describes their pattern of change as “cautious” and “moderate”.  The church is “an exemplary congregation, one
that has kept its balance amidst decades of change, and it has remained loyal
to its heritage in Churches of Christ.” 
He anticipates that the Richland Hills church will be useful in leading
other churches to a more expansive conception of fellowship.

That’s not how things went down.  As of today, about ten years later, the
Richland Hills Church of Christ has renamed itself “The Hills Church”.  Concerning baptism, [their website](http://www.thehills.org/about/beliefs/) has this to say, “We receive salvation by trusting and believing in Jesus. When
we decide to follow Jesus, we demonstrate our trust in Him by being baptized in
water.”

I know how to parse that. 
Baptism is an outward sign of an inward grace.  Get baptized whenever you feel like it.  We don’t hold to that stuffy for-the-forgiveness-of-sins
stuff.  Ironically, exactly the same
thing happened to Richland Hills that happened to Oak Hills ten years earlier.

I don’t mean to revisit the debates about the use of the
instrument in worship (I think it’s unscriptural—insert next 20 pages of
utterly predictable argument here), but I think this pattern says something
worth noting about the decision to go instrumental.  It seems to impart a momentum to churches of
Christ that leads them beyond the Restoration movement.

It doesn’t appear to have this effect in the Christian
Churches.  They’ve been instrumental
since the big split at the turn of the 20th century, but so far as I
know, they still generally hold to baptism for forgiveness of sins.  That’s important.  “One baptism” is one of the seven ones of
Ephesians 4:3-6, Paul’s litmus test for the unity of the Spirit.  I might (and do) disagree with them about the
instrument and lots of other things, but they’re still brethren.  Anybody who holds to the seven ones is.

In 2006, Richland Hills might have appeared to be in the
same place as the Christian Churches, but its organizational velocity was very
different.  The Christian Churches were
planets maintaining their orbit, but Richland Hills was a comet headed out of
the solar system at a brisk clip.  Now,
their teaching on salvation has them resembling Baptist churches and community
churches much more than they resemble churches of Christ.

From my perspective, that’s dreadful.  I leave it to our merciful God to judge
brethren who worship with the instrument (though I will do my best to persuade
them out of it, given opportunity), but people who aren’t baptized for the
forgiveness of sins aren’t brethren in any Biblical sense.  I don’t have to wonder what happens to them
on the Day of Judgment.  I mourn it.

This suggests that the impulse among some churches of Christ
to use instrumental music in worship isn’t an attempt to enlarge the tent of
New-Testament Christianity.  Instead,
it’s an attempt to abandon New-Testament Christianity for something more culturally
palatable.  Once the stumbling blocks of
a-cappella worship and regenerative baptism have been removed, nothing
separates us from the rest of American evangelical churchdom.  We’ve become like the nations around us, and
that was probably the (desired if not admitted) objective all along.

To my brethren in churches of Christ who are flirting with
the instrument, then, I will say this: 
Be careful.  You do not know what
spirit you are of.
