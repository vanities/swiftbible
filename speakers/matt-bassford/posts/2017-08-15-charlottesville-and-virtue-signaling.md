Over the past several days, I’ve noticed a strange
ambivalence in myself about the online reaction to the Charlottesville
protests.  Obviously, the events that
unfolded over the weekend are awful enough. 
I think racism in all its forms is sinful, let alone virulent Neo-Nazi
racism, let alone using your car to murder people you disagree with.  I don’t even think there’s room to argue the
opposite case, at least from a Biblical perspective.

And yet, there was something about all of the folks on my
Facebook friends list lining up to affirm their outrage that didn’t sit quite
right with me.  That’s awfully easy moral
high ground to claim, for one thing. 
Even among my very conservative group of friends, I didn’t see anybody
taking the side of the skinheads.



I’m suspicious of stands for righteousness that are easy to
take.  I don’t see a lot of those in the
Bible, (unless you count Luke 6:26), and they lead me to suspect
that something, somewhere, isn’t lining up quite right.  Generally, the more determinedly we follow
Jesus, the more our popularity will start to look like His.

This is also a stand that is easy to take because it does
not require change.  I haven’t even read
about any KKK members who were sickened by the violence and determined to
abandon their racist ways once and for all. 
I certainly don’t know anybody like that.

All those nasty-bad racists need to change, we agree.  But we don’t need to change.  We’re fine. 
Offering helpful suggestions about how somebody else needs to fix their
spiritual life, especially when we’re powerful and they’re not, is also a
cost-free activity.

Put the two together (popular stand + finger-wagging at the
unpopular), and you’ve got a textbook example of virtue signaling.  Usually, that has not much to do with
righteousness, and a whole lot to do with self-righteousness.  I have very little problem imagining a
modern-day Pharisee praying, “God, I thank you that I am not like other
men:  fascists, skinheads, neo-Nazis, or
even like the racist two cubicles down.” 
Then, we proceed to list all the ways in which our behavior, and the
behavior of our friends, establishes our moral superiority.  Aren’t we all wonderful together?

The problem, of course, is that we are not actually
wonderful.  We are not actually people
who love God with all of our being and our neighbors as ourselves.  There has only ever been one man who ever
did, and none of us are He.

We fall short in our love. 
In fact, we fall so far short that nothing less than the blood of the
Son of God could redeem us.  We are
wretched failures, all of us.  That’s the
kind of virtue we have the right to signal.

The Charlottesville racists fell short.  Duh. 
However, rather than directing our outrage at their shortcomings, we
would be much better served to direct it at our own.  Where am I, in my actions, in my words, and
most of all in my heart, when it comes to:

Black Lives Matter?

Muslims?

Immigrants?

Opioid addicts?

LBGTQ+ people?

Progressives of every stripe?

Trump supporters?

Blue Lives Matter?

Racists?

Nativists?

Bigots?

That guy at church?

Wrong people the world over?

We don’t have to agree with these groups.  Nobody agrees with all of them.  We do have to love the people who are in them.  We do have to overcome the temptation to
consider them in our mind’s eye and say, “I’m better than they are.”  We have to abandon the pleasures of judging
our neighbor and holding them in contempt and begin the hard work of loving and
serving them.  If we would not give a cup
of water to a man in a white sheet and otherwise do whatever we could to help him, we are doing nothing more than seeking to overcome evil with evil.

Virtue-signaling doesn’t change hearts.  Digital denunciations don’t change
hearts.  I think all the Hitler-lovers
are laughing it up right now over all the online manifestoes.  There is, after all, no such thing as bad
publicity.

Let me tell you what does change hearts.  I have a friend who is a disciple and gospel
preacher, and also happens to be African American.  A few years back, he had the opportunity to
study with a dyed-in-the-wool good ole boy. 
He baptized him just a few days before he died.

My friend was invited to preach his funeral.  It was literally a Confederate-flag-themed
funeral.  There were TV monitors set up
in the funeral parlor that displayed Confederate flags rippling in the
breeze.  There was a Confederate flag
draped over the casket.

My friend preached the funeral.  After the funeral was over, at the family’s
request, he folded up the Confederate flag on the coffin and placed it on his
dead friend’s chest.

I asked him why he did it.

He replied, “Because I loved him.  Because I loved them.”

This is the power of the gospel, that it can take even A
CONFEDERATE FLAG and, in the hands of a black man, transform it into a symbol
of forgiveness, reconciliation, and love. 
I don’t think anybody who attended the funeral missed the symbolism, and
afterward, I don’t think that flag was the only thing that was buried.

We don’t need more protests. 
We don’t need more self-righteous diatribes, online vitriol, or hearts
crammed full of bitterness and resentment.

We need more acts of grace. 
We need more people who will step up and, out of love, do more than
anybody short of God has a right to ask. 
We need more people who willingly surrender everything because they have
been filled to overflowing with the fullness of Christ, so that trying to empty
them out is like trying to empty out the ocean.

Racism is the least of the evils that such a spirit will
erase.
