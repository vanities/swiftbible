A couple days back, I posted [Mike Rogers' comments](http://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2017/05/mike-rogers-on-difference-between-hymns.html) on the difference between hymns and contemporary praise songs.  It’s a thoughtful look at the issue from
someone whose perspective is different than mine, and I think it merits further
exploration and discussion.

Mike’s first point is that when I describe anything that is
sung in church as a hymn, I automatically alienate many who write and sing
contemporary praise songs.  They don’t
consider the songs in their preferred genre to be hymns and don’t appreciate
them being lumped in with songs they consider stuffy and antiquated.



**Definitions of
“Hymn”**

I admit that this isn’t a distinction that I use, nor do I
usually hear it from those who advocate the use of a more traditional
repertoire.  There are a couple of
reasons for this.  First of all, the use
of “hymn” (from the Greek *hymnos*) in
the Bible is more generic.  It doesn’t
appear to refer to anything more specific than a song of praise to a god.  Far be it from me to argue that a church that
worships using only songs written in the past 50 years is *not* “addressing one another in psalms and hymns and spiritual
songs”!

The English-dictionary definition of “hymn” is similarly
broad.  According to my friend and yours,
dictionary.com, a hymn is “a song or ode in praise or honor of God, a deity, a
nation, etc.”  Any direct-address
contemporary song qualifies as a hymn under this definition, and I think the
argument could be made that any spiritually themed song is sung in honor of
God.

**Mike’s Distinction**

However, none of this settles the question of whether there
is a significant difference between the body of sacred song written from 1700
to 1970 and those sacred songs written between 1970 and the present.  I’m not terribly attached to 1970 as a
dividing line, and obviously songs that are closest to one another in time are
going to be most like each other, whether or not they’ve got a dividing line
between them.  However, I still think
it’s a useful generalization.

Mike argues that it’s best to distinguish the songs in Group
1 by calling them “hymns” and the songs in Group 2 by calling them
“contemporary Christian songs”.  He
defines each group as follows:

*I would say that (musically) hymns are
Christian songs written in homophonic texture and characterized by common
practice tonality with regular phrasing, non-syncopated melodic lines, and a
preference for contrapuntal independence of the voices.*

*Regarding the text
elements, my opinion (from a non-linguist's point of view) is that hymns are
typified by standard poetic forms and rhyme schemes, often dense in their
theology.*

*Contemporary Christian
songs, on the other hand, are a mix of homophony and polyphony, characterized
harmonically by a modern variation of common practice tonality. Syncopated
melodic lines are the norm, and they often feature irregular phrasing as well.
Contrapuntal independence in this style is less emphasized.*

*Lyrically, modern
Christian songs often implement standard poetic forms and rhyme schemes, but
these forms are often only loosely followed. Many times, these songs follow no
preconceived poetic form at all, and are typically theologically less dense.*

For the benefit of those who aren’t hymn or music geeks
(assuming any such people are reading this!), let’s define some of the language
he uses:

homophonic texture: 
In a church-music context, this means that the soprano has the melody,
and the other three parts are harmonizing with the soprano rather than singing
their own melodies.

common-practice tonality: 
Hymn-tune composers are following the rules for melody and harmony laid
down by the composers of the 17th-19th centuries.

regular phrasing:  If
we think of a hymn tune as a book, phrases are the chapters of the book—the
musical sub-thoughts that make up the complete thought of the tune.  When phrasing is regular, the chapters are
the same length.

contrapuntal independence: 
In practice, this means that the parts move either in contrary motion or
at intervals of thirds/sixths with each other (like a duet between soprano and
alto).  When parts move at intervals of
fifths and octaves instead, this destroys independence.

The resultant sound is less full and more stark.  Think, for instance, of the way that the
third line in CORONATION (the usual tune for “All Hail the Power of Jesus’
Name”) sounds.  It sounds that way
because it’s got lots of parallel octaves between upper and lower parts.

**Revolution or
Evolution?**

Now, when you compare the archetypal hymn to the archetypal
contemporary Christian song, Mike’s definition is spot-on.  “O for a Thousand Tongues to Sing” will check
every one of the “hymn” boxes; “You Are My All in All” will check every one of
the “contemporary Christian song” boxes (though “You Are My All in All” is not
nearly as theologically diffuse as many).

However, I think the question is not so much what’s at the
poles as what’s between the poles. 
Ultimately, Mike’s argument is that one shouldn’t compare hymns to
contemporary songs because there’s such a sharp division between the two
classes.

To be honest, that’s not what I see.  Instead, I think the difference between contemporary
songs and the hymns closest in time to them is no greater than the difference
between the hymns of any two eras that are adjacent to one another.  This becomes evident when we evaluate
Southern gospel hymns, the Stamps-Baxter hymns written between 1920 and 1960
(roughly), according to Mike’s criteria:

*Homophonic or
polyphonic?* Stamps-Baxter hymns are
usually homophonic in the verses and polyphonic in the chorus.  Alto and bass leads are common. This mirrors
the mixed homophony and polyphony of contemporary songs.

*Regular phrasing?* Usually, yes, though not as regular as
the phrasing in Victorian hymns.  This is
more traditional.

*Syncopated
melody?* Frequently.  Who can forget sniffing their way through “He
Bore It All”?  This is a more
contemporary trait.

*Contrapuntal
independence?* Typically.  Stamps-Baxter composers weren’t given to
parallel fifths and parallel octaves.  In
this, they follow the style of traditional hymn tunes.

*Standard poetic
form/rhyme?* Half and half.  Southern-gospel writers generally didn’t use
the defined metrical forms of earlier eras. 
You don’t really see Stamps-Baxter hymns in common meter or
7.6.7.6D.

However, the writing does reflect a great attention to
rhyme, often at the expense of content. 
The lengthy list of Southern-gospel clichés (“He saved my soul/He made
me whole”, “the home o’er the foam”, etc.) is often rhyme-driven.  This is a traditional trait, though
traditional writers generally used rhymes that were more skillful and less
trite.

*Theologically dense
content?* Um, no.  It’s a rare Stamps-Baxter hymn that has a lot
to say.  Much of what such hymns do have
to say is based on tradition and fanciful imagining rather than the Bible,
particularly when it comes to their descriptions of heaven.  No, the new Jerusalem is not beachfront
property!  This indifference to doctrinal
depth is a distinctively contemporary trait.

**Conclusion.**

When we add it all up, the Southern-gospel genre looks more
like traditional hymnody in two of Mike’s categories, more like contemporary
praise songs in three, and a mix in one. 
This is what we should expect.  After
all, “He Bore It All” is closer in time to “You Are My All in All” than it is
to “O for a Thousand Tongues to Sing”.

What it does not do, though, is support the case that
contemporary Christian songs represent some sort of quantum leap in
worship.  They are no more different from
the hymns that came immediately before them than the hymns of the 19th-century
American gospel era are different from the hymns that came immediately before
them.  In fact, the worship wars of today
are nothing more than a rehash of the arguments of 150 years ago about what was
appropriate in worship.  For instance, “Dear
Lord and Father of Mankind” was actually written in angry reaction to the
gospel song “Hold the Fort, for I Am Coming”.

What has been is what will be, and what has been done is
what will be done, and there is nothing new under the sun.  Contemporary Christian songs are not an
attempt to step in the same river twice, but they are definitely part of the
same stream.  It’s nearly impossible to
carve them off from the category of hymns without splintering it entirely. A
study of their historical context reveals not that they don’t belong in it, but
that they do.
