I consider Casey Head a brother and friend, and indeed have done so for several years now.  However, I live a thousand miles away from him and don’t know much more about his doings than I see from him on Facebook.  As a result, I was both surprised and grieved to learn that not only has he stopped preaching the gospel, he has ceased to associate himself with a church of Christ.

Casey is very open about this decision.  In fact, he has quite literally written a book about it, which you can find [here.](https://m3rcytriumphs.wordpress.com/) I love and respect Casey, but I do not agree with his conclusions.  In particular, I object to his conclusions about baptism, which he outlines [here.](https://m3rcytriumphs.wordpress.com/baptism-for-salvation/)

As arguments go, this is a strange one.  Casey continues to affirm that passages like Acts 2:38, 1 Peter 3:21, and so on teach that baptism is necessary for forgiveness of sins.  In fact, he says, “Let me state emphatically that I still believe and teach that baptism is for the remission of sins, and in a sense, for salvation. My position has changed somewhat, but I do still affirm this language for the simple reason that it’s scriptural language. If I’m leading someone to Christ, I’m still going to instruct them to be baptized for the reasons stated in Scripture.”

However, he also advances the old baptism-equals-circumcision argument to “prove” that just as Abraham was saved before he was circumcised, so Christians are saved before they are baptized.  I think that this argument makes Colossians 2:11-12 bear much more weight than it should.  There are some ways in which baptism and circumcision are similar (both are symbols of entering a covenant with God, for instance), but there are many ways in which they are dissimilar.  For instance, neither Casey nor I would accept the Reformed-tradition argument that because infants were circumcised, Colossians 2:11-12 means that infants should be baptized too.

As a result, when we consider whether baptism and circumcision are alike in their saving effect, we can’t simply say, “Well, Colossians 2:11-12, duh.”  Instead, we need to look at the abundance of Scriptural evidence that baptism saves and the absence of Scriptural evidence that circumcision ever did.  It indicates that in this way, baptism and circumcision are not parallel.  Abraham’s justification by faith before circumcision does not establish that we are justified by faith before baptism.

There’s a larger problem here, though, that exists even outside the baptism-equals-circumcision argument.  It is that in addition to the other six ones of Ephesians 4:4-6, Paul reports that there is one baptism.  What is this one baptism?  I say that it is baptism for the forgiveness of sins.  Baptists typically say that it is an outward sign of an inward grace.  However, I acknowledge that I don’t agree with Baptists, and they acknowledge that they don’t agree with me.  We teach two different baptisms.

Which is the one baptism, then?  Like *The Highlander*, there can only be one.  Are the Baptists right, or am I right?  Both can’t be true.  Casey, then, can’t say that he believes Acts 2:38, etc., while arguing that baptism as an outward sign of an inward grace can have salvific effect.  If baptism for the forgiveness of sins is Bible baptism, Baptist baptism isn’t and has no more spiritual effect than a dip in a swimming pool.  It’s either one or the other.  There’s no logical way to straddle that fence.

Casey tries to straddle it by pointing to a couple of examples from the Old Testament:  the Israelites circumcising their children in Joshua 5:1-7 after not having done so for 40 years in the wilderness, and the unclean people eating the Passover in Hezekiah’s reign in 2 Chronicles 30:18-20.  In both cases, he notes, God is lenient with people who were trying to do the right thing but didn’t get it exactly right.  From this, he argues that God is going to be lenient with people who were trying to the right thing in seeking His salvation but didn’t get it exactly right by being baptized for forgiveness of sins.

In a lot of ways, this argument is similar to the Baptist hypothetical about the man who is on his way to be baptized when he gets in a car accident and is killed.  Is this man, who was doing all he could to obey the gospel, saved even though he never actually did?

In both cases, my answer is the same.  My concern is not with the dead, either hypothetically or actually.  They’re in the hands of God, and in His infinite justice and mercy, He will do with them what is right.

Instead, my concern is with the living.  My concern, particularly, is with the people I’m studying with who have never been baptized for the forgiveness of sins (and I care about reaching that point with actual people, not getting the Baptist church told!).  What they did before they heard the Scriptural truth on baptism is irrelevant.  Now they have heard it.

If they are truly people who are desire to obey God’s will in everything, they will obey the commandment to be baptized (not “re-baptized”) for the forgiveness of sins.  If they reply with hypotheticals about fatal car wrecks and creedal verbiage about the outward sign of an inward grace, they demonstrate that they lack the desire to obey God’s will in everything.  In either case, the situation has nothing to do with Joshua 5:1-7 or 2 Chronicles 30:18-20.

I certainly do not believe that baptism must occur in a building that says “Church of Christ” out front or be at the hands of a self-identified “Church of Christ preacher” to save.  In fact, I think it’s entirely possible for someone who comes to the baptistery in a Baptist church building to be saved.  It all depends on what they are seeking.  If they believe that they are being baptized for forgiveness of sins, then they have been baptized into Christ, regardless of what the guy doing the baptizing teaches.  That’s the one baptism of the Bible.

However, there’s no Scriptural reason to believe that someone can find that one baptism while seeking something else.
