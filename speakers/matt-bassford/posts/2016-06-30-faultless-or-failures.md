A couple weeks ago, I wrote [this post](http://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2016/06/an-answer-to-chanda131.html) in answer to a woman who had left the faith and was wondering if she should
return.  In my answer, I set out what I
thought a local church ought to be:  a
collection of flawed disciples who were working on their flaws together and
were fighting to become more like Jesus.

Generally, the replies to this post were very positive, but
one reader raised a question about what he perceived as an overly negative view
of Christians.  In his view, it’s unfair
to insist that Christians are moral failures, people who are generally rude,
unloving, selfish, and bigoted.

As always, I’m interested in hearing from people who
disagree with me, and certainly, this gentleman’s critique reveals that I at
least need to clarify my thinking on the subject.  In fact, it points to a much larger issue,
the issue of how we view ourselves.  I
think there’s a tendency among brethren, just as there is in the larger
religious world, to gravitate toward one of two extremes.



The first extreme is to insist that really, we’re pretty
good.  Sure, we need the grace of God,
but we don’t need it very often, because we’ve got this Christianity thing
under control.  The war against sin isn’t
a life-and-death struggle; it’s more akin to trying to kill the dandelions in
an otherwise verdant lawn.

I think this attitude is a distortion of spiritual reality,
and as with all such distortions, it creates problems.  First, it leads us to look away from Jesus,
because once we start considering Him, we also see our shortcomings much more
clearly.  Second, it promotes pride in
our own spiritual attainments.  Third, it
promotes complacency and unwillingness to change.

The solution, however, isn’t to flip to the other extreme,
because the other extreme is equally dangerous. 
People who find themselves on this side of the spectrum tend to believe
and argue that Christians are utter failures as disciples.  We’re so far from where we should be that
none of the things we do right count for anything.  We are miserable, wretched people, and the
church is a collection of miserable, wretched people who are awful to one
another and everyone else.

This attitude too
brings spiritual problems with it. 
First, it tends to inspire despair among those who hold to it.  If you get up in the morning, look yourself
in the mirror, and say to yourself, “You’re a miserable wretch,” your day
probably isn’t going to go well!  Second,
people who have this attitude also often have a negative view of the
church.  They will talk about their own
sins in vague terms without specifying them but zero in on the sins of their
brethren with laser-like focus.  Bad
church!  Bad!  Third, this view is often accompanied by an
indifference toward righteousness.  If
it’s all about the grace of God anyway, why should I bother trying to reform my
life or do good?

These people are also often disinclined to the study of
Scripture.  When you expect to be floated
to heaven on a rising tide of grace, you’re not going to be terribly concerned
about the details.

As is often the case with two extremes, there’s a measure of
truth to both positions.  Yes, we are
saved by grace through faith.  However,
it is also true that we are created in Christ for good works, which God
prepared beforehand so that we would walk in them.  Christianity is not about picking one of
these truths over the other.  It’s about
embracing both.

It is certainly true that every church is going to be filled
with more or less imperfect people who are always going to struggle.  However, it is similarly true that we who
have died to sin cannot go on living in it. 
It is precisely because we are under grace that sin can no longer be our
master.

As a result, if Christians act like people in the world do,
there is something terribly wrong.  It
may be that a new convert still has many worldly habits, but the longer we
follow Jesus, the more those habits should die out.  Christians should be better at working out
disagreements than worldly people.  We
should be more loving.  We should, in
short, shine like lights in the world.

It’s right to celebrate our good works.  Hebrews 13:16 tells us that good works please
God, and when God’s happy, I’m happy! 
However, we must acknowledge that it is only God’s grace that allows Him
to consider those good works at all.

We must acknowledge too just how far we have to climb.  Our righteousness should stand out in
comparison to the world, but our spiritual deficiencies will stand out when we
compare ourselves to Jesus.  Christianity
has to be about Christ.  This ought to be
stating the obvious, but in practice, it is often overlooked.  When we fix our eyes on Jesus, when we
consider the flawless magnificence of His character and meditate on His
perfection, we can only come away humbled.

The problem isn’t so much the thou-shalt-not’s (though those
can be problematic!).  It’s generally
easier to not do something.  The problem
is the thou-shalt’s.  Particularly, the
problem is imitating the love of Jesus. 
I might have a lot of the thou-shalt-not’s nailed down, but when it
comes to love, I miss the mark pretty dramatically, and I always will.

This realization, however, shouldn’t dispirit us.  It should inspire us.  Our love for Jesus should provoke us to love
and good works, and we should do the same for one another.  However, the goal is not to justify
ourselves.  It is to glorify Him.  In the end, Christians are neither faultless
nor failures.  We are, and always will
be, disciples with a lot to learn.
