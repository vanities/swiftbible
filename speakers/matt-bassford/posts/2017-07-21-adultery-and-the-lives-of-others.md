About a month ago, I stumbled across Guy Gavriel Kay’s *A Song for Arbonne* in a used bookstore, on sale for a buck.  I’d read it back when I was a teenager, but I didn’t own it, so I bought it (even though my house currently needs more books in it like a stray dog needs more fleas).  About a week thereafter, I reread it.

Honestly, I didn’t care for it very much.  I was particularly unhappy with the fact that so far as I could tell, the only major character in the book who didn’t commit adultery was the villain.  I’m much less copacetic with that kind of thing at 38 and married than I was at 16 and unmarried.  Having since watched several friends’ lives get destroyed by adultery, I have considerable trouble buying into an all-fun no-strings depiction of courtly love.

However, about two-thirds of the way through, I did find one of the best descriptions of the problem with adultery that I’ve ever encountered (though ironically, in context, it’s not actually about adultery), as follows:

*This was not a man whose life she had a right to share.  He was a friend; she knew that now, knew that he would make some place for her in the pattern of his days, however long or short they were to be.  But for more than that, more than that small place, she had no right and he no proper space in what his life had now become.*

In some ways, this is a restatement of John the Baptist’s warning in Matthew 14:4, “It is not lawful for you to have her.”  However, I think it goes a step beyond that.  Yes, adultery is undeniably evil in the sight of God.  Why?  Why does God care so much?  What makes adultery not only not lawful, but not right?

The problem is that all of us have lives that are made up not only of our consciousness and the decisions we make right now, but of the consequences of past decisions and current relationships.  None of us have an “I” that is only an “I”.  It is also inevitably a “we”.  My life is the life that I live with my God and my wife and my family and my friends.  This is what I have become and made.

That life has a narrative power and identity of its own.  It is its own coherent thing, and now that my life has one woman in it, that doesn’t leave any place for another.  My wife is more powerfully part of my “I” than any other human being because I am one with her.

Adultery, then, has to be a radical redefinition of “I”.  It means taking a life that doesn’t have a place for another, at least not in that way, and forcing the other in anyway.  It is an act of spiritual violence, to the self not least.

Adulterers often justify this redefinition by engaging in another.  They pretend that “I” is just “I”, that we are essentially self-contained, and that another intimate relationship can be added to our lives without demolishing those lives (and the lives of those close to us) and attempting to reform the wreckage into something else.

This is an inviting fiction, but it’s still a fiction.  Others are included in my “I”, but I am included in theirs too.  If I commit adultery, I am rewriting all of those lives without the consent of their owners.

I am first of all doing to my friends what my maritally unfaithful friends have done to me.  I am making their trust in others more grudging, their cynicism harsher, and their hope fainter.  Preacher that I am, it’s quite possible that my sin would lead others to leave the Lord entirely.  If the light that is in you is darkness, then how great is the darkness?

Of course, this is nothing compared to the damage I would wreak on my immediate family.  Anecdotally, I’ve observed that daughters of adulterous fathers often end up as the victims of adultery themselves.  Women marry their fathers, it’s said, and an unfaithful father teaches exactly the wrong lesson about what to look for in a husband.

I know men who had fathers like this too.  What do you do when the man who was supposed to be your example in everything is instead an example of sin?  A man who is determined to be good will fight against that example all his life.  A man who isn’t determined won’t bother fighting.

Most of all, spouses bear the brunt here.  They discover that one was actually two, or maybe one with somebody else.  Hell hath no fury like a woman scorned, or maybe no confusion or despair like a woman who discovers that her “I” has been so shattered that she doesn’t know who she is anymore.

In cases of double adultery, all of the harms above are reflected on the other side too.  Adulterers don’t merely disrupt the lives of those closest to them; they visit destruction on innocent strangers as collateral damage.

We are not our own.  None of us are.  We are all so intertwined with those we love that we cannot be separated from them, nor they from us.  We make a life together, particularly with those to whom we are married.  Like a thriving flowerbed that leaves no room for weeds, our lives in their fullness leave no room for sexual sin.

There is no proper space in them for others, not in that way.  We can make space.  We can cram an adulterous relationship in where it does not fit.  We can rip apart the flowerbeds of our lives, inevitably blighting others in the process.  However, we cannot eat, and wipe our mouths, and say, “I have done no wrong.”

God knows better.
