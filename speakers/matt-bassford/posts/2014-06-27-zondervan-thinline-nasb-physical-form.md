This Bible came into my life as one of a set of twins.  One day, I happened to be nosing around in
the Bibles section of the local Christian bookstore.  I flipped it open and said to myself, “Hey,
that’s a smooth-reading Bible!  Too bad
it’s not sewn-bound. . . wait a second, it is sewn-bound!”

From that point on, the Bible went on my watch list.  A few weeks later, christianbook.com put one
of the styles of the Zondervan Thinline, the one with a bewitching
pink-and-chocolate poly cover, on sale. 
I bought two, one for myself and one for my daughter.  It can be her first Bible once she learns to
read.



Even though my father-in-law and brother-in-law both offered
me money to use my new pink Bible in the pulpit (it’s like they’re related or
something), keeping it in its original two-tone glory was never part of the
plan.  Instead, I promptly sent it off to
the good people of Leonard’s Books to be rebound in a less eye-catching soft-tanned
goatskin.  This review, then, will
address both the original poly cover and the goatskin rebind.

In this portion of the review, I’m going to be dealing
exclusively with the straight-from-the-bookseller polyurethane-cover version of
this Bible.  If you want to know how the
rebind from Leonard’s did, you’ll have to read the final section of this
review, which will appear in about a month.

As I said, both of my Thinlines began life with a poly
cover.  Zondervan calls their poly covers
Duo-Tone, so if you see a Bible marketed as “Italian Duo-Tone”, that’s what it
is.  I don’t automatically consider this
a bad thing; after all, I had a very good experience with my poly-covered
Crossway PSR.  However, my brief
acquaintance with the Zondervan poly cover has led me to suspect that as not
all genuine-leather covers are created equal, so all poly covers aren’t
necessarily created equal either.

Maybe it’s that the Thinline has a larger trim size than my
PSR, so there’s more room to flex, but the cover doesn’t feel as solidly
constructed.  Many of the poly covers on
new Crossway Bibles have a stiff, thick feel to them, so that they resist
opening a little bit at first.  The
Zondervan Thinlines, though, have never given me that sense.  There’s a flimsiness to them, as though
sooner than one would hope, the cover will fail at the flex point by the spine
or the endpapers (which are mid-weight paper only and not leatherette) will
pull away from the cover.

Allow me to insert here a lament about Zondervan’s marketing
strategy for this Bible.  The typesetting
and layout for this Bible are gorgeous: 
clean and elegant without being overly modern.  In my opinion, they’re much better than what
one finds between the covers of many premium Bibles.  Zondervan had the opportunity to make this a
killer Bible.  If they’d kept the
typesetting, kept the sewn binding, but printed it on better paper and put it
between a genuine-leather or premium-leather cover, they really could have
challenged for mid-range or high-end market share.

Sadly, Zondervan didn’t. 
The most expensive version of this Bible that you can buy has a
bonded-leather cover, and Crossway remains as the only non-niche publisher
trying to sell Bibles to people who actually like them.  Don’t get me wrong; I delight in the
profusion of thoughtfully designed ESV’s, and the NASB is actually better
served than many other translations (as of the time of this writing, I have yet
to locate a non-Cambridge NKJV on which I would be willing to spend
money).  But it could still be so much
better!

However, rather than continuing to mourn what is not, I
shall return to praising what is.  In
this case, it involves a sewn binding. 
No matter how alluring the text block, I would not have bought two of
these without it.

Now that I’ve fussed about some of Zondervan’s other
decisions, I must acknowledge that this is a great deal.  Even with the pink-and-chocolate hideousness
on the outside, I rejoiced to pick up two of these on christianbook.com for $13
per.  True, that was when they were on
clearance, but these Bibles are generally available online for less than
$20.  It’s tough to find a sewn text
block in a non-compact Bible for that price. 
Even if the cover is a little sketchy, we can be sure that the sewn
binding is going to go the distance.

Nonetheless, though the Bible is a decent product at its price point,
it’s still not a particularly impressive Bible overall.  I like the sewn binding, but the
flimsy-feeling poly cover gives me pause. 
I rate it at 3.5/5.  Note, though,
that with the rebind, I would give this Bible a 4.5/5 in physical form.
