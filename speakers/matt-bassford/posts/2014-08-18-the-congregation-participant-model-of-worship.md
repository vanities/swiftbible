Conversely, hymns used in the congregation-participant model
will be like this:

1.      
**SIMPLE**.  Performers may be skilled musicians, but the
ordinary members of the congregation emphatically are not.  Indeed, every congregation contains those who
do not enjoy singing, do not sing well, and only sing in the assembly because
they believe God has commanded them to do so. 
Most congregants are not able to read music.  They do not gather weekly for rehearsal;
instead, they show up on the first day of the week and sing without
preparation.  In a setting like this, only simple songs survive.  A band can navigate a praise song with
inconsistent meter and tricky rhythm; a congregation will train-wreck every
time.  When the congregation has to
actually *sing* interesting music, its
love of interesting music diminishes greatly.



2.      
**FAMILIAR**.   The audience-performer model creates a
tremendous pressure to innovate.  By
contrast, the congregation-participant model brings with it a tremendous
resistance to novelty.  It is relatively
easy for a choir or praise team to learn a new song.  Trying to introduce a new song in
congregation-based worship is generally as awkward as an eighth-grader’s first
date.  Non-musicians sing by memory;
their experience of singing an unfamiliar hymn ranges from uncomfortable to
terrifying.  All other things being
equal, they would much rather sing a hymn they’ve been singing all their lives,
with which worship is comparatively effortless. 
Too many new hymns will provoke murmuring about just what does that song
leader think he’s doing, anyway?

3.      
**ENDURING**.  The rule is that a congregation wants to sing
only familiar songs in worship.  For a
new song to be adopted, it must be quite literally exceptional.  It has to provide the church with an answer
to the question, “Why should we sing this one, instead of the 300 we already
know?”  A congregation will only go
through the trouble of learning a new hymn if the prospect of using the
now-familiar hymn to worship is compelling. 
This works both ways.  Once a
sacred song enters the congregational repertoire, it will generally stay there
at least until the people who originally learned it have died off.  Sometimes, it will remain for centuries.  As a result, the repertoire in this setting
is never changed over completely. 
Instead, it is cumulative.

These are the types of hymns that churches on either end of
the spectrum will use.  Churches toward
the middle of the spectrum will probably have a more mixed repertoire.  Problems arise, however, when sacred songs
from one end of the repertoire are introduced into the worship context from the
other end.  As Warren observes, what
possible connection can a mass of unchurched Californians in the 1990s make
with “The Old Rugged Cross”?  The hymn is
an artifact from another era, and it primarily resonates with faith communities
that have been singing it for the past 100 years.  You don’t hear renditions of “The Old Rugged
Cross” on a Top 40 radio station, and there’s a reason for that.

On the other hand, unless an a-cappella congregation is a
crack “singing church”, it isn’t going to be able to easily use praise songs
from the performer-audience end of things, either.  They may sound great on YouTube, but they
aren’t going to work nearly so well on Sunday morning, as blue-haired Sister
Nettie is trying to figure out where the altos come in and Brother Fred is
wondering what’s wrong with singing “Trust and Obey”.  A praise song that requires more from a
congregation than the hymns they are used to singing will not adapt well to use
by that congregation.

However, if the issue is pressed, if the music minister/head
song leader is determined to adopt an up-to-the-minute repertoire, something
else will start happening.  The worship
of the church will begin to shift from congregation-based to performer-based.  It has to, because only performers can digest
a corpus of unfamiliar, difficult praise songs. 
There may be doctrinal justifications attached to the change (“Now we
understand that. . . !”), but the change actually will be rooted in
practicality.

Those who are interested in maintaining the vitality of a
congregation-participant worship tradition, then, should be wary of introducing
too many performer-audience praise songs too quickly.  There are, of course, exceptions to the
rule.  So many praise songs have been
written over the past 30-40 years that the law of large numbers dictates that
at least some of them will work congregationally.  There’s nothing wrong with introducing a
sacred song that a congregation can sing and wants to sing, regardless of its
provenance.  However, before we adopt the
content of another church’s repertoire as our own, we should ask whether we
want to adopt the form of its worship too.
