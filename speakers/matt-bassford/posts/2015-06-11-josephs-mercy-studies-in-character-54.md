*Joseph said to them, "Do not be afraid, for am I in the place of
God?”*

*--Genesis 50:19*

When Jacob finally dies,
at the exceedingly ripe old age of 147, that puts Joseph’s brothers in a newly
precarious position.  After all, they are
still the men who sold the second most powerful man in Egypt into slavery, they
are still totally dependent on him for their sustenance, and it’s totally
possible that Joseph has only withheld vengeance because of his respect for his
father.  Now that Jacob’s dead, though,
the brothers fear that Joseph is about to blast him, so they come to him,
begging his forgiveness and reminding him that Jacob really wanted them kept
alive.



Joseph responds to this
in characteristic fashion.  He’s so upset
by their obvious fear of him that he himself weeps, and he assures them that
he’s going to care for them and their children. 
This is all based, though, on the rhetorical question that he asks in v.
19.  Even though he was certainly wronged,
he does not regard himself as having the right to avenge, simply because he
isn’t God.  All this time, he hasn’t been
constrained by the desire to please Jacob. 
He’s been constrained by his own sense of what is right.

We need to apply Joseph’s
reasoning not only when we are tempted to take revenge, but in our dealings
with sin generally.  Sometimes, we resist
temptation because we believe sin is unwise, not because we believe it is
wrong.  For example, we might resolve
never to commit adultery because we have considered the consequences.  We see that adulterers frequently slip up and
get caught, and we know that if we get caught, it will ruin our marriage, our
family life, our standing with brethren at church, and possibly even our
employment future.  The reward of sin is
clearly not worth the risk, so we reject the sin.

That reasoning is fine,
as far as it goes, but it is not complete. 
If our resolve to do right is based on our situation, what will happen
if the situation changes?  What will we
do if the devil convinces us that we’ve got this new sneaky way to commit
adultery so that we are certain never to be discovered?  Of course, God always knows, and most sneaky
adulterers aren’t as sneaky as they think they are, but something doesn’t have
to be true for the devil to get us to believe it.  If we aren’t convinced that adultery is wrong
regardless of consequences, that may well be a temptation to which we succumb.

The only sure protection
against sin is the conviction that it is wrong, not because of its bad results
for us, but because of its very nature. 
Those bad results are always there. 
That’s why God decreed certain thoughts and actions to be sinful in the
first place.  However, even if we can’t
see the bad results, we must be like Joseph and reject it.
