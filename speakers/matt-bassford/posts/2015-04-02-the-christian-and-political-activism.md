It’s been hard to read or watch the news recently without
encountering the uproar over religious-freedom legislation in Indiana.  My Facebook feed has been filled with posts
from those on both side of the issue.  It
has been a display of the American political system at its most dramatic and
colorful, but also its least thoughtful and empathetic.  It has, indeed, been an episode of envy,
strife, abusive language, evil suspicions, and constant friction.  It seemed good to me, then, to revisit what
the Bible does and does not say about political activity.



In the New Testament, the most important relationship is
that which exists between the Christian and God.  Every other relationship is viewed through
its lens.  We are to love one another as
Christ has loved us.  Husbands are to
love their wives as Christ loved the church. 
Servants are to be obedient to their masters as to Christ.  We are to submit ourselves to every human
institution for the Lord’s sake.  However,
in all of these relationships, our duty to God supersedes our duty to anyone
else.  In the final analysis, we are to
obey God rather than men.

This certainly applies to our obedience to government.  If a human law does not compel us to violate
the law of God, we must obey it.  If it
does compel us to violate the law of God, we must disobey it and embrace the
consequences.  Our brethren in the first
century accepted joyfully the seizure of their property, and they were
instructed to resist to the point of shedding blood in their striving against sin.  Would Christ, who obeyed God to the point of
death at the hands of a corrupt government, expect any less from us today?

Today, however, we live not in the first-century Roman
Empire, but in the twenty-first-century United States.  Our citizenship in this earthly country
entitles us to a wide range of political freedoms.  It is lawful for Christians to use those
freedoms in a godly way, but we must be careful not to mistake political
activity for service to God.  So far as I
am aware, the only political commandment given to Christians is the commandment
to pray for kings and all who are in authority, so that we may lead a tranquil
and quiet life in all godliness and dignity. 
Anything beyond that is a use of the liberty that we have in Christ, but
it is not the same thing as conforming ourselves to His likeness.

It is not sinful for a Christian to read political news,
form political views, express political opinions, or seek to persuade others to
his viewpoint (though politically motivated contentiousness is another story).  However, every minute we spend on political
activism is a minute we DON’T spend:

* Praying
* Examining the Scriptures
* Visiting orphans and widows in their distress
* Remembering the poor
* Speaking forth the mystery of Christ
* Turning a sinner from the error of his way
* Encouraging one another
* Or engaging in any of the other myriad expressions of the
  love of Christ.

It’s easy to confuse the issue.  It’s easy to get so caught up in watching and
reading and arguing online and maybe even rallying and voting that we associate
the self-righteousness that comes with these things (and there’s been plenty of
self-righteousness exhibited by both sides) with the righteousness of God.  The Lord doesn’t want to see us engaging in
endless, embittering political strife. 
He wants to see us get out there and start obeying the second great
commandment.  I have it on good authority
that there is no law against that one.
