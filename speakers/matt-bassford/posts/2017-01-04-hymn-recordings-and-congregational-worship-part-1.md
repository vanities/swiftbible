On Monday, I shared [a link](http://www.christianchronicle.org/article/voices-only-sumphonia) from *The Christian Chronicle* that contained a couple of YouTubes of the most recent Sumphonia recording
session.  This occasioned a handful of
likes and a thoughtful comment from my brother Jeremy Hodges.  He said, “Helping people learn new hymns is
good, but I worry about the celebration of fleshliness in videos like these.
The distance between this and choirs seems to grow ever shorter.”

He went on to expand, “To clarify, I'm not saying that hymn
recordings = choirs. My wife and I benefited greatly by the Kleinwood Church of
Christ recordings when we were learning hymns that I had forgotten and she
didn't know. However, I've also seen a great deal of celebration about how
‘big’ singings were special for very fleshly reasons. They sound good, or they
were a demonstration of importance, etc.

“The best ‘singing’ I can read about in the Bible was two
guys chained to the floor in a jail cell.”

I asked Jeremy if I could respond in a blog post all its
own, and he agreed.



The first thing that I see here is that Jeremy, I, and
indeed the Sumphonia board (full disclosure: 
I’ve worked with Sumphonia on any number of projects, but I’m certainly
not a decision-maker for them, and nobody asked me to write this) share two
very important concerns.  The first of
these is the preservation of the tradition of a-cappella congregational
singing.  Even though the tradition
remains fairly healthy among non-institutional churches of Christ, it is very
much under threat among our institutional brethren (which is probably why *The Christian Chronicle* is promoting
a-cappella YouTubes—those folks are worried!). 
The threat comes both from the old nemesis of instrumental music and
from the formation of praise teams, which to me, at least, look suspiciously
like choirs.  My concern for the future
of a-cappella worship is one of the main reasons why I write hymns.

The second of these concerns is the promotion of worship,
rather than singing.  When it comes to
“singings”, I’ve seen many of the same things that Jeremy has.  Back when I still lived in Texas, I was able
to attend the Kleinwood singing in person on any number of occasions.  On one of those occasions, we had just
finished singing, “Our God, He Is Alive”, the defiant anthem of the churches of
Christ, when I heard one of the people sitting behind me exclaim to another,
“Wow!  That was *loud*!”  Urk.  Talk about missing the point!  In truth, it is all too easy for our worship
to become mere singing, focused on the fleshly sound of things with little
concern for the spirit.

These things are definitely, unquestionably problems.  However, I believe that Sumphonia aims to solve
these problems rather than unthinkingly add to them.

Let’s start with the choir issue.  I’m convinced that, after Scriptural teaching
on the subject, the single most important thing we can do to continue in the
ancient pattern is to make sure that our tradition of congregational singing
remains healthy and vibrant.  Like
anything that is alive, it will die if it is not fed and tended.

There are many ways we can do this, but one of the most
significant is the promotion and adoption of new hymns.  The instruction of Psalm 98:1 exists for a
reason.  Singing a new song to the Lord
snaps the congregation out of singing on autopilot and gives it new spiritual
nourishment.  It also heightens interest
in worship generally and promotes a better understanding of its importance.  Without a regular influx of new hymns,
worship in any congregation will diminish in quality and significance, which will
lead to the inevitable and fatal, “Our singing is rotten!  Why don’t we. . . ?”

Congregations need new hymns, and ever since sound-recording
technology appeared, such recordings have been used to teach them.  We owe much of our Southern-gospel repertoire
to quartets singing those hymns over the radio from the 1920s through the
1950s.  I guarantee you that brethren
didn’t learn the Southern-gospel standards by sightreading them!  Some of the rhythms they employ are quite
complicated and much easier to hear than see.

We’re foolish if we don’t take advantage of the same
strategies to promote new hymns today.  Jeremy
would certainly acknowledge the value of this. 
He himself pointed to the utility that he’s gotten from the recordings
of the various Kleinwood singings.  I
agree that recordings of singings can be useful, but I think their usefulness
is limited for two reasons.

First, the purpose of such singings isn’t to comprehensively
teach a collection of new hymns.  They
are designed first and foremost for the spiritual benefit of the worshipers who
are there.  If they happen to teach some
new hymns along the way, that’s fine (though I know that when the Kleinwood
singings include too many unfamiliar hymns, the organizers get complaints about it).  However, those singings don’t have to involve
any new hymns at all.

What, then, are the Sumphonia people supposed to do?  They’ve published a hymnal, *Psalms, Hymns, and Spiritual Songs*, that
contains hundreds of new hymns, many of which do not appear in any other hymnal
in use among churches of Christ.  The
editors didn’t stick these hymns in because they had to fill up a signature!  Instead, they believe that churches will
benefit from learning and using them, and brethren are most likely to learn
them if they’re able to hear recordings. 
Sumphonia can’t rely on Kleinwood singings for that; Kleinwood doesn’t
even use *Psalms, Hymns, and Spiritual
Songs*.  There’s no way out of the box
but for Sumphonia to produce its own recordings.

Second, it’s entirely possible that a song leader at a
singing will lead a hymn incorrectly. 
This can be as subtle as a hymn being sung at an incorrect tempo.  Charli Couchman and I have joked for years
that “Exalted” sung too fast should be called “Excited”, and when it’s sung too
slow, it should be called “Exhausted”.  A
hymn, even a good hymn, that isn’t led right is going to sound less than its
best and may not be adopted even when it deserves to be.

The Sumphonia CD’s, which predate the hymnal by a number of
years, came about for this reason. 
Authors and composers in the church wanted to make sure that brethren
had access to recordings of their hymns being sung correctly.  Certainly, Sumphonia isn’t going to refuse to
sell CD’s to people who just want to listen to pretty music, but that’s never
been the point.  Instead, the point has
always been to help congregations learn good, new hymns as easily as possible
so that our tradition of congregational worship could thrive.

I still want to address Jeremy’s second concern, but I’m
going to have to do it in another post. 
I’ve been even more prolix than normal in this one!  However, I will conclude it by noting and
agreeing with Jeremy’s concern about the drift toward the choir.  If we go by appearances, those YouTubes of
the recording sessions look like part of the drift.  There’s Scott Wyatt, doing choir-director-y
things.  There are the singers,
segregated by parts.

However, I believe that while the appearance may be one
thing, the effect is quite another. 
Nobody has the Sumphonia singers installed behind the pulpit on Sunday
mornings, and they would refuse such an invitation even if one were
extended!  Instead, the goal is
simple:  to promote the adoption of new
hymns all across the brotherhood.  The
more we learn and embrace new congregational hymns, the farther off the threat
of worship apostasy will be, and Sumphonia aims to accomplish precisely that.
