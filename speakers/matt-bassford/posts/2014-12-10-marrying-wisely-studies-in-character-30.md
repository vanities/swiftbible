*When Esau was forty
years old, he took as wives Judith the daughter of Beeri the Hittite, and
Basemath the daughter of Elon the Hittite. And they were a grief of mind to
Isaac and Rebekah.*

--Genesis 26:34-35

As we’ve seen already, the life of Esau was a life marked by
bad decision-making without regard for the consequences.  This held true not only in his dealings with
his brother Jacob, but also in his choice of wives.  Although the family of Abraham lived in
Canaan, they were not themselves Canaanites. 
They served God, but the Canaanites were already on the wicked path that
would lead to their extinction at the hands of Joshua five hundred years later.  They were an idolatrous people, with a very
different conception of the purpose of their existence.



Esau being Esau, it’s from among the people of the land that
he takes his first two wives, and from that point on, the wives of Esau make
life miserable for Isaac and Rebekah.  We
don’t know precisely what this conflict involved, but when we consider just how
different Judith and Basemath were from their in-laws, some sort of discord was
inevitable.  Although the Scripture
doesn’t say, Esau’s marriage may well also have displeased God.  When we consider God’s later prohibition of
intermarriage with the people of the land, it’s entirely likely that foreknowledge
of Esau’s choice led God to favor Jacob.

There are two lessons that we can draw from Esau’s mistakes
today.  First, unmarried Christians
should learn to make wiser decisions in their marriages than Esau did.  The Bible never says that it is sinful for a
Christian to marry an outsider, but such a decision frequently leads to years of
trouble and misery.  It’s hard enough for
us to serve God with the support of a godly spouse.  When a husband or wife is a source of
spiritual weakness rather than strength, they make the climb to heaven that
much steeper.  They also hinder our
efforts to raise godly children, if only by the negative force of their
example.  It may seem hard to turn away
from a person we love who does not share our faith, but spending our lives with
them is often harder.

Likewise, we need to remember that when we marry someone, we
marry their family, and they marry ours. 
Personality clashes between girlfriend and mother or boyfriend and
brother don’t usually vanish once “I do” is said.  Instead, they have a nasty tendency to grow
and blossom into decades of enmity.  That
too is a cost of marriage that we must count. 
If the level of tension is low, and the family member in question lives
hundreds of miles away and only appears at Christmas and Thanksgiving, the
conflict may be manageable.  On the other
hand, if the future mother-in-law is hateful, lives in town, and expects to be
invited over for dinner three times a week, she may well pose a threat to the
marriage itself.  Romance is all well and
good, but when we marry, we need to consider the practical effects too.
