**OVERVIEW.** This is
an extremely impressive design, in which Cambridge did things nobody ever
thought of before to fit a full-sized Bible in a nearly-compact form
factor.  The edge-lined cover is a joy.  The thought of paying so much money for a
single book is not so joyful, but a Bible this good might well be worth it.

This one was another review copy, courtesy of the good
people of the Cambridge marketing department. 
As soon as I got it, I gave it to my photographer, who began calling it
“my precious”.  I borrowed it from him
for two weeks so I could review it.  I
spent the whole time carrying it around in its box for fear that I would
destroy it before giving it back to him.

**VITAL STATISTICS**

**Translation:**  New American Standard Bible (1995)

**Cover:**  Edge-lined goatskin

**Binding:**  Sewn

**Trim Size:**  5 ⅛” x 7 ⅛”

**Text Block Width:**  1 ⅜”

**Weight:**  1 pound, 12 ounces

**Column:**  Single

**Layout:**  Paragraphed

**Font:**  8.75/10.5 Lexicon No. 1

**Words of Christ:** Black

**Cross-References:**  No

**Ribbon Marker:**  0

**SIZE.** This is the truly remarkable feature of
the Clarion.  There are other
single-column Bibles out there.  There
are other Bibles out there with standard-shading-to-large print.  There are other Bibles out there with the
full NASB reference suite.  None of those
Bibles, though, have about the same trim size as a Pitt Minion and are slightly
thinner than two Pitts stacked atop each other.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEjnCug9iEgcGkajgSea9ZXet08qj_yklG017grbxZCZCc47ULUytkHPnoXM4IhJdEm4TiiL8IOVgfj4RS9MvJhkmqXj_ylQNuZnAScjsRmACIDEUFUkP25bjjan7kK3UloVb2msF91r4w/s640/PF+Cambridge.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEjnCug9iEgcGkajgSea9ZXet08qj_yklG017grbxZCZCc47ULUytkHPnoXM4IhJdEm4TiiL8IOVgfj4RS9MvJhkmqXj_ylQNuZnAScjsRmACIDEUFUkP25bjjan7kK3UloVb2msF91r4w/s1600/PF+Cambridge.jpg)



The Clarion is very nearly a compact Bible, despite having
the features of a full-size Bible.  It
passes the glove-box test with room to spare. 
I approached said test with some trepidation, mainly because I didn’t
want to give the Clarion back to my photographer bearing glove-box fang
marks.  However, the Bible fit smoothly
and easily.

Cambridge accomplished this feat of design legerdemain with
some truly outside-the-box thinking.  The
Pitt Minion is a clever design; the Clarion might be brilliant.  I haven’t asked the Cambridge folks what
their design process was, but I think they started with a vision of the perfect
Bible page.

I think their perfect page was single-column, had full
marginal references, contained about 500 words in a prose page (the actual NASB
Clarion has 409 words per page, but the average is skewed by the poetry of the
Old Testament), and had lines of about 65 characters in length.  All of those things are in accordance with
the canons of typography.  That’s the way
you would set a single-column Bible if you didn’t have to worry about how long
the Bible was.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEj8f0KCokgKBujNEFEiZC4EP7uCOmNVIaU7mzsY6umGdCKqb0Q2Jvgape1xRCGuH6fbfJJZHzYgt2V44lDRmA53ec8hXOe4q9MDky3xVVHd92qzOzQbaTHu1WlRtkv_cLGTVlvxS09lrg/s640/PF+Genesis+1.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEj8f0KCokgKBujNEFEiZC4EP7uCOmNVIaU7mzsY6umGdCKqb0Q2Jvgape1xRCGuH6fbfJJZHzYgt2V44lDRmA53ec8hXOe4q9MDky3xVVHd92qzOzQbaTHu1WlRtkv_cLGTVlvxS09lrg/s1600/PF+Genesis+1.jpg)



Of course, all of those decisions have consequences.  Single-column Bibles are larger than
double-column Bibles because of all the “wasted” white space in the poetic
sections of the Old Testament.  Also, 500
words per prose page is not a very large page, especially when dealing with a
volume that is 782,000 words long.  With
all the white space, the text of the Bible itself in the Clarion (exclusive of
concordance, preface, etc.) runs to 1915 pages.

That is a lot of pages! 
In my Bible collection, the Bible with the next highest number of text
pages is the Foundation SCR, with 1749 pp. 
It’s a single-column design too, you notice.  Lots of Bible geeks love the SCR, but it’s a
brute.  The older versions with the good
paper are more than an inch and a half thick. 
A Clarion printed on similar paper would have been an inch and three
quarters thick.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEhNF8_zROtBFew7gDWEL0wmsc3_dGsZEGdJoPsudkeBmV_J4_HavZQWPwt5gEaKZC_Dlqwxjt16a5pKgRifWZYw_QuVRXcBFW_ubrV7gL-EL-kNeSeKqjVJEXkTZgtvmOwPat3MgcZxag/s640/PF+size.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEhNF8_zROtBFew7gDWEL0wmsc3_dGsZEGdJoPsudkeBmV_J4_HavZQWPwt5gEaKZC_Dlqwxjt16a5pKgRifWZYw_QuVRXcBFW_ubrV7gL-EL-kNeSeKqjVJEXkTZgtvmOwPat3MgcZxag/s1600/PF+size.jpg)



For a Bible with such a small trim size, so much thickness
would have been unacceptable.  However,
printing the Clarion on thinner, less opaque paper introduces a tremendous
amount of ghosting to the design.  That’s
acceptable in a discount-store thinline, but not in a Bible that Cambridge
foresaw would retail for more than $200.

Their solution?  Line
matching.  Even though just about every
new Bible design these days is line-matched, the Clarion was the first.  Ghosting doesn’t matter nearly as much when
the ghosted text is neatly aligned under the text you’re reading.  With the Clarion’s generous leading, there’s
a thick creamy-white stripe between each line of text.  The line matching makes an otherwise unworkable
design work, and I think it’s the result of Cambridge’s desire to fit their
perfect page into a text block that was too small for it.

**COVER.** For factory-produced covers, this Clarion
is as good as it gets.  The outer cover
is goatskin, the classic wrapper for a high-end Bible.  Instead of being paste-down like a lower-market
Bible would be, it is edge-lined.  There
isn’t any stiffening bookboard in the cover, and it isn’t attached to the text
block by the endpapers.  Instead, the
lining of the cover is made out of polyurethane, and a trapezoidal flap of the
polyurethane is glued to the text block, with a sheet of vinyl glued on top of
it.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgVy0k-7sQxHzjliCKjEYmVdIRCaL0VxkW5WFNZ4YWkBrGeArONaV-rAmPYDiElmGzWWixq2BvapGsf6SY2KMzN5fN2zD2zTZy02ZHXTQSmKWyhJiHFa6SYgV2mASjRyubrZCwn49gVZw/s640/PF+edge-lined.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgVy0k-7sQxHzjliCKjEYmVdIRCaL0VxkW5WFNZ4YWkBrGeArONaV-rAmPYDiElmGzWWixq2BvapGsf6SY2KMzN5fN2zD2zTZy02ZHXTQSmKWyhJiHFa6SYgV2mASjRyubrZCwn49gVZw/s1600/PF+edge-lined.jpg)



I’m a fine-Bible philistine. 
I like my Bibles like I like my blue jeans—cheap and durable.  I tend to figure that even the stiffest
paste-down binding will stiffen up with use. 
However, even I have to admit that the feel of an edge-lined Bible is
awfully lovable.  As small as the Clarion
is, the binding is limp enough that it will snuggle down against your
hand.  Edge-lined Bibles don’t come
cheap, but their charm is undeniable.

I’ve never used an edge-lined Bible for a prolonged period
of time, but I suspect that the Clarion would hold up well.  Paste-down Bibles, with their relatively
stiff covers, flex mainly at the hinge. 
If they are opened and closed enough, the endpapers will start splitting
there.

By contrast, the Clarion flexes across the entire width of
the cover, so that even when I have the Bible open in my hand, the hinge isn’t
moving very much.  That, I think, is the
secret to the fabled durability of edge-lined Bibles.  They’re flexible enough that the cover
doesn’t have a single failure point.  I
suppose that eventually the polyurethane hinge would start to tear, but it
seems to me like that day would take a long time to come.

**TEXT BLOCK.** I can’t imagine that any Bible publisher
would have the gall to edge-line a text block that was glued.  Cambridge didn’t.  This is a sewn Bible that flops open easily
at Genesis 1.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEift4WGa_z8nciY7ZIYXNK-QJJ8JEQSOvXWjY3S8wA4wPNoUxZkrT2nYJLbSS7O3KpGzzyPgTA6cpUTut2fOULxbPe-EWdcvt3En8h118e7IY3x04IeMrUs5Tg7wvhyvNC21Skv8hyphenhyphen0RA/s640/PF+signatures.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEift4WGa_z8nciY7ZIYXNK-QJJ8JEQSOvXWjY3S8wA4wPNoUxZkrT2nYJLbSS7O3KpGzzyPgTA6cpUTut2fOULxbPe-EWdcvt3En8h118e7IY3x04IeMrUs5Tg7wvhyvNC21Skv8hyphenhyphen0RA/s1600/PF+signatures.jpg)



Also, this is a text block that is printed with the grain
rather than cross-grain.  The first sign
of with-the-grain-printing is the complete absence of cockling.  The second is that in my humid garden-level
office, I see very slight waviness in the gilding on the top and bottom of the
text block.  However, even in this
extremely unkind environment (I made some Bible-class workbooks this morning,
and the copier paper had so much moisture in it that it literally felt damp to
the touch), I see no page-curling in the Clarion.  I find this reassuring, especially in light
of the reports of rampant curling in first-printing ESV Clarions.

**VALUE.** I’ve said it before, and I’ll say it again.  When you get right down to it, the part of
the Bible that I care most about is the text block.  I can’t think of a text block that shows more
thoughtfulness in its design than the Clarion. 
If you like single-column Bibles and aren’t particularly inclined to
write in them, this is a Bible well worth having.

It won’t come cheaply. 
At the low end of the scale, the calf-split Clarion, which has a
paste-down binding, retails for $160.  If
you want the edge-lined goodness of the black goatskin, that will set you back
$250 at retail.  As always, discounts are
available online, but even a discounted Clarion will cost you a good chunk of
change.

Then again, though. 
In a world where people queue up to spend hundreds of dollars on the
latest Apple iWhatever (and repeat the process in a year or two), is it really
outrageous to spend low three figures on a superlatively designed Bible?  The Clarion doesn’t really fit with my usage
patterns these days, but it’s probably the best all-round Bible on the
market.  I think it deserves a 9/10 in
Physical Form.
