I kept the GPR at the church building and used it as my
primary Bible there for two weeks. 
During that time, I preached two sermons, taught a young-adult class,
and kept my usual course of office study. 
Here are my impressions of its performance:

**PREACHING.** The size of the GPR is a little bit
larger than I prefer in a preaching Bible. 
It certainly does an adequate job of concealing, nay, swallowing a
sermon outline.  However, at 6 ½” x 9 ¼”,
the Bible’s just a little bit too big to be handy for me in a pulpit
setting.  During one sermon in
particular, I really put the Bible through a workout.  I read from thirteen different passages that
I didn’t have marked in advance, so I had to turn to each one.   The turning process was awkward.  The pages of the GPR are large enough that I
felt like I was drawing back a bedsheet. 
It wasn’t a huge deal; it’s not like the congregation watched me wrestle
with Bible pages for five minutes at a time or anything.  However, it was a small distraction for me in
an arena in which I do not like to be distracted.



The layout of the pages of the GPR, however, was fine for
preaching.  The GPR is arranged verse by
verse rather than in paragraphs, so I didn’t have to spend any time worrying
about whether verse numbers were quotation marks or vice versa.  Indeed, the verse numbers are the same
14-point font as the text of the Bible generally.  Anyone who can’t make them out easily is
either legally blind or well on his way there. 
It is true that the GPR is double-column rather than single-column, but
the font is so large that this never became an issue.  When a full page of a Bible only contains
about twenty verses, locating one verse in particular doesn’t take much work.

**TEACHING.** When I substitute-taught the young-adult
class, I spent the entire session seated at a table with the Bible open in
front of me.  I never had to pick it up
or pace around with it.  However, my
impression of the GPR is that it wouldn’t be as hard to hold in a setting like
that as its size would indicate.  For one
thing, at just under 3 pounds, it doesn’t weigh substantially more than the
Foundation SCR that is my weapon of choice in Bible classes these days.  If I can manage the SCR, I can manage the GPR
too.  Also, the flexibility of the GPR’s
binding is an asset here.  Despite the
Bible’s size, I can still one-hand it when it’s open because the flexible cover
and text block curl down around the sides of my hand.  It’s much more comfortable to hold than, for
instance, a 6” x 9” bonded-leather NKJV that I own.  The better cover material makes a significant
practical difference.

The GPR is cross-referenced, though they appear at the end
of each verse rather than collected in a single column.  I would imagine that the designers of the
Bible chose this option in order to save space. 
I didn’t happen to use them when I taught, but they were there.

**STUDY.** The GPR really shone during the time that
I used it in my office.  Even though the
Bible is large, it nonetheless rested quite comfortably in my lap.  The giant-print font couldn’t have been
easier to read.  Between the type size
and the verse-by-verse layout, the verse numbers stood out like a police
officer at a moonshiners’ convention.  In
the materials I prepared during that two weeks, I didn’t get a single citation
wrong.  I can’t say I enjoyed using the
Bible to preach or teach, but I did enjoy studying out of it, and if I ever
were to use a different Bible for study than the Bible I use in the pulpit, I
might well choose something like the GPR.

**CONCLUSION.**  The GPR is a full-size Bible, designed with
the primary purpose of being easy to read. 
It fulfills that purpose admirably. 
Its size annoyed me sometimes, but I never had any trouble reading from
it.  A preacher whose eyesight is failing
might well find a Bible like this invaluable. 
For preaching, teaching, and study, I rate it at 4.5/5.
