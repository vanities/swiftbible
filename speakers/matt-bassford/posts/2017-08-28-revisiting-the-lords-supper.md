Sometimes, the things that we pay the least attention to are
also the most familiar.  When I come home
from work, I don’t spend a lot of time gaping out the window at the other
houses on my street.  I’ve seen them all
a million times before, so I’m not particularly interested in looking at the
house with the weird statues in the front yard yet again.  If something changes about one of them, I
might notice, but otherwise, I’m going to tune them out.

The same thing can be true of our worship.  For instance, we partake of the Lord’s Supper
on each first day of the week, every first day of the week.  To most of us, it’s as familiar as our
neighbor’s houses, so there’s a temptation to go through the motions without
really considering what we’re doing. 
When it comes to remembering the suffering and death of the Lord Jesus,
this is obviously a problem!  Let’s spend
some time this morning, then, revisiting the Lord’s Supper.



**Its First-Century
Observance**

Let’s begin our study by re-examining the form of the Lord’s
Supper, particularly what we can learn from the observance of the Last Supper
and the way that we see early Christians partaking.  When it comes to the Last Supper, though, we
must recognize that it is set in **THE
CONTEXT OF THE PASSOVER.** Here, let’s
look at Mark 14:12-16.  The Passover was
and is one of the great feasts of the Jewish religious year, and because Jesus
and His disciples were observant Jews, it’s no surprise to find them eating the
Passover on the appropriate day.

This is significant both in terms of what they did and what
they thought.  First of all, when Jesus
instituted the Lord’s Supper, He used foods that were already part of the
Passover meal.  According to the account
of Exodus, the bread of the Passover meal had to be unleavened, so we can be
certain that Jesus used unleavened bread too. 
We continue this tradition to this day.

Even though the Scripture doesn’t record this, it’s most
likely true that by the time of Christ, the ceremony of the Passover had
expanded to, among other things, the ritual consumption of four cups of fruit
of the vine.  According to Paul in 1
Corinthians 10, the third of these, the cup of blessing, was the cup that Jesus
shared around.

All of this, of course, was to remember the night when the
Israelites were delivered from Egyptian bondage by the angel of the Lord.  They smeared the blood of a lamb on their
lintels and doorposts so that the angel would pass over them and not destroy
their firstborn children too.  It’s not
surprising that the Lord’s Supper itself would be associated with the same
themes:  death, blood, and deliverance.

More subtly but equally importantly, the Lord’s Supper is
set in **THE CONTEXT OF BETRAYAL.** Mark tells the story in Mark
14:17-21.  Here, as always, we must pay
particular attention to the way that Mark arranges his material.  He doesn’t have to tell this story here, or
indeed tell it at all.  Luke puts the
betrayal sequence after the Lord’s Supper; John tells it but leaves the Lord’s
Supper out altogether.  Mark could easily
have done either.

It’s no accident, then, that he instead tells the stories
back-to-back.  In form, they’re quite
similar.  In both of them, Jesus takes
bread and shares it with somebody.  However,
even though breaking bread is a sign of fellowship in Scripture, in the case of
Judas, it’s a symbol of betrayal.  Jesus
does this knowing that, in the words of Psalm 41, the trusted friend who has
literally eaten His bread will lift up his heel against Him.

In this context, then, the second sharing of bread is a
question.  The disciples, and indeed all
of us, have eaten the bread of Jesus too. 
What are we going to do about it? 
In our lives, will the Lord’s Supper come to symbolize our
faithfulness?  Or will it predict our
betrayal too?

Finally in Mark’s account, we come to **THE BREAD AND CUP.** They
appear in Mark 14:22-25.  The first thing
worth noting here is the absence of argument. 
When Jesus handed the disciples bread and said, “This is My body,” none
of them argued back and said, “Nuh-uh! 
That’s just bread,” nor did they insist that the cup He said was His
blood was actually just fruit of the vine.

The gospels reveal that the disciples are fully capable of
beginning dumb arguments with Jesus when they don’t understand Him, so here, I
think we can conclude from the absence of argument that they do understand
Him.  They know perfectly well that when
He’s talking about His body and His blood, He’s speaking figuratively and
symbolically.

After all, Jesus commonly spoke in figures.  Just a few chapters earlier, they got
confused and started arguing about bread when Jesus told them to beware of the
leaven of the Pharisees.  The Pharisees
weren’t actually yeast, and the elements of the Lord’s Supper aren’t actually
blood and human flesh.  It’s clear that
the disciples pick up on this point, and so should we.

I don’t know that the disciples fully understood the figure
then, but we don’t have any trouble today. 
We recognize that He gave His body for us on the cross, and we recall
that incident as we share in the bread. 
Similarly, the cup that we drink is our reminder that His blood ratified
the covenant between us and God.  Today,
in the Lord’s kingdom, we celebrate this feast with Him.

Finally, we need to consider the timing of the Lord’s
Supper, on **THE FIRST DAY OF THE
WEEK.** This, of course, comes from
Acts 20:7.  This is the only time in the
narrative of Acts when we are able to tie participation in the Lord’s Supper to
a particular day of the week, and here it occurs on what we would call
Sunday.  This might seem like a weak
foundation for our insistence that we should partake every first day of the
week, but there are textual reasons to reach this conclusion.  First, there’s nothing in the text that
identifies this as a special occasion, nothing that would lead us to conclude
that the church in Troas gathered together to break bread on this first day of
the week and not on any others.

Second, we need to consider the subtext of Paul’s journey to
Jerusalem.  As the text makes clear in v.
16, Paul is hurrying to be in Jerusalem for the day of Pentecost.  In fact, he’s in such a hurry that after he
spends all night teaching the saints in Troas, he spends the whole next day
walking about 35 miles to catch his boat in Assos.  This is a man on a mission!  However, before that memorable night in
Troas, he and his companions have spent the past seven days waiting around.  There’s no other textual reason for this
delay but for Paul and his companions to make sure that they could partake of
the Lord’s Supper with the church in Troas. 
It was the practice of the first-century church to remember the Lord’s
death on the day of His resurrection.

**Our Observance**

As we imitate the pattern of our brethren long ago, let’s
next consider what we ought to be thinking about during our observance.  I think the first item on the list ought to
be **OUR SIN.** Paul summarizes our previous condition
quite well in Titus 3:3.  Of all the
things we might think about, this is surely among the most culturally
unpopular.  There are millions of people
in this country who think the only possible sin is not showing tolerance for
the immorality of others.  If they believe
in God at all, they think of Him as the heavenly equivalent of an indulgent
grandpa, who continues to smile benignly down on us even if we set the cat on
fire.  They’ve eliminated sin from
Christianity.

There are a couple massive problems with this.  First, it’s not true.  All of us have sinned.  We have willingly violated the law of God,
and even now, despite our hatred of sin, we still find ourselves sinning.  None of us can handle our sin problem on our
own.

Second, denying sin diminishes the value of what Christ did
for us.  If we weren’t in danger of
losing our souls because of our unrighteousness, then why did Christ die on the
cross at all?  That seems like an extreme
solution if the problem is either minimal or non-existent.  The soft-pedalers of sin in the wider
Christian world claim that Christ died to teach us about love, but suffering
for somebody who doesn’t need help isn’t loving.  It’s just kind of weird.  Unless our reflections on the cross start
with our sin, and so our share in what He endured, we’re going to miss the
point.

Next, let’s turn our attention to what the Hebrews writer
calls **THE END OF THE AGES.** Here, look at Hebrews 9:24-26.  I think “end” is kind of a confusing word to
use here.  It makes it sound like the
Hebrews writer was expecting the end of the world shortly.  The Greek word in question really means
something more like “entire completion” or “consummation”.  In other words, everything that had happened
in history to this point anticipated the death of Christ.

This is significant for a couple of different reasons.  First of all, it shows us the true scope of
the problem of our sin.  God foresaw the
problem before He ever created the world, and in His omniscience, He knew that
the only way to solve it was by spending thousands of years carefully preparing
to send His only Son to earth in human form so that He could die on the cross
as our ransom.  Remember—this is the same
God who created the universe in six days! 
In His eyes, our sin was so serious that only such a complicated and
extreme solution could address it.

Second, I think it tells us why God wants us to remember the
death of His Son every first day of the week. 
Even though it happened 2000 years ago, there is still no event that has
had and continues to have a greater impact on our lives than the crucifixion of
our Lord. Of course we need to keep it constantly in mind!  If we don’t, we won’t honor it as it deserves
to be honored.

Finally, let’s reflect on Jesus as **THE PERFECT SACRIFICE.** Consider
Hebrews 9:11-14.  Since the beginning,
blood has stood for life, and sin has demanded a death.  Under the Law of Moses, the Israelites
offered animal sacrifices for their sins, but as the writer points out, such a
sacrifice can never be anything more than wildly inadequate.  How can the death of some uncomprehending
beast possibly atone for the evil I chose to commit?  Offering a physically unblemished animal does
nothing to cleanse spiritual stains from the conscience.

Enter Jesus.  Unlike
the bulls and goats of the temple sacrifices, He knew exactly what He was doing
and willingly offered His own life for the sins of mankind.  Like the bulls and goats, He was
unblemished.  However, in His case, that
didn’t mean that He was physically perfect. 
It meant that He was spiritually perfect.  Though tempted in all things as we are, He
was without sin.  As a result, Jesus is
the most perfect sacrifice that has ever been offered or conceivably could be
offered.  There can be no more powerful
act than when He came before God with His own lifeblood.  As a result, where an animal sacrifice could
not do away with even one sin, His sacrifice erased the record of all of our
sins forever.  His death did for us what
nothing else could.

Momentarily, we’re going to partake of the Lord’s
Supper.  This is not some quaint little
ritual we go through every first day of the week to check it off our
checklists.  This is our memorial of a
mystery of awesome power.  The death of
Jesus on the cross is so astonishingly powerful that it altered the destiny of
the human race forever.  Let’s think
about this as we partake.

**The Lord’s Supper
Through the Week**

At this point, some of you have probably noticed that
something strange is going on.  I’m
preaching a whole 45-minute sermon on the Lord’s Supper, but two-thirds of the
way through, we have already partaken, apparently leaving me with nothing more
to talk about.

I suppose I could astound you all and sit down, but I think
there’s one final important point for us to consider.  It’s not enough for us to prepare our minds
for the Lord’s Supper.  Instead, the
Lord’s Supper should also prepare our minds. 
Let’s consider, then, what the impact of the Lord’s Supper on us ought
to be throughout the week.

First, I think it should make us **MINDFUL** people.  Look, for
instance, at the attitude Paul expresses in 2 Corinthians 4:8-10.  Obviously, Paul’s view of the meaning of
discipleship was about a million miles away from the attitude of Joel Osteen
and Creflo Dollar and all the rest. 
Rather than envisioning a life of prosperity and ease, he believed, and
indeed lived out his conviction, that following Jesus meant enduring
persecution and suffering.  In
particular, he describes his way of life as “always carrying in the body the
death of Jesus”.

This is a compelling thought!  We sometimes sing, “There is sunshine in my
soul today,” but I’m not aware of any hymn that says, “I’m carrying about the
death of Jesus in my soul today.”  We
enjoy daily contemplation of our salvation, but to constantly dwell upon the suffering
and death that purchased our salvation seems maybe a little strange to us, kind
of unhealthy.

And yet, I think that if we do constantly carry about the
death of Jesus within us, it will make us different and better people.  When I was at the Hymninar a couple months
back, during Wednesday night services, I was invited to lead a hymn and preface
it with a few comments.  The hymn I chose
was “Ah, Holy Jesus”, and I said, “The next time you are tempted, sing this
hymn, either out loud or silently to yourself, and see if you have the stomach
to go through with it and sin.”  I’ve
found that I can’t.  When I carry about
the death of Jesus, it leaves no room for the devil.

Second, remembering the Lord’s Supper through the week will
leave us **HUMBLED.** An expression of this humility appears in
2 Corinthians 5:14-16.  It is simply not
possible for us to spend very much time in contemplation of the cross and still
remain standing by the end of it.  The
more we dwell on it, the more the suffering of our Lord will come to dominate
our thinking and drive us to our knees. 
What Jesus did for us is simply incomprehensible.  No human language can define the love and the
suffering, the sorrow and the power that combined at the cross.

For us, then, the Lord’s Supper should be an encounter with
an event we can’t get over.  When we
confront His sacrifice, there shouldn’t be anything in our hearts or our lives
that we want to cling to and say, “Nope; this belongs to me.”  I suppose that Christ has the power to
literally control us.  He could make us
into robots or puppets if He wanted to. 
However, He doesn’t want. 
Instead, He wants us to consider His love for us and because of that
love, surrender to Him the everything that He has the power to take.

When we encounter this love and allow ourselves to be
controlled by it, that too doesn’t leave much room for sin.  Because Jesus died, I must die to myself with
equal finality and completeness. 
Instead, I must live for Christ. 
Daily, I must belong to Him.

Finally, the Lord’s Supper should remind us throughout the
week that we are to be **HOLY.** Peter makes this point in 1 Peter
1:14-19.  For every Christian, the blood
of Jesus isn’t just this historical curiosity that was shed on a hilltop in
Judea 2000 years ago.  Instead, it is
blood that is very personal to us because it has touched us as deeply as we can
be touched.  We weren’t ransomed out of
sin with gold or silver.  Instead, the
lifeblood of Jesus was our purchase price. 
We weren’t cleansed from sin by our determination to do better.  We were cleansed by His blood.

Because we have come into contact with the blood of Christ,
we are consecrated.  We are holy.  This doesn’t mean that we now walk around
surrounded by a mystical glow.  Instead,
it means that we have been dedicated to God’s purposes.  Under the old covenant, the priests didn’t
take the altar out of the tabernacle and use it to go roast an ox for their
next barbecue.  That altar was holy.  It belonged to God.  If you used it for anything else, you desecrated
it.

Today, we belong to God every bit as much as the furnishings
of the tabernacle.  Because we are holy,
serving God is what we are for.  Every
interaction of our lives should reveal and express that holiness.  Sin for us is not just sin.  It too is a desecration.  It destroys our usefulness in the kingdom and
insults God.  We can’t be partially
holy.  We have to be holy in all our behavior.
