A few months ago, I read an article (which, regrettably, I
cannot now locate) that argued that modern Westerners often fundamentally
misread the writings of classical authors. 
Today, we believe that the purpose of writing is to communicate ideas as
simply and clearly as possible.  However
(argued the author of the article), that wasn’t a goal that ancient authors
shared.  Rather than making the truth as
accessible as possible, they sought to obscure it.



This goal seems baffling to us, but it suited the purposes
of ancient writers for two main reasons. 
First, by making truth difficult to find, they ensured that only those
dedicated to truth would find it.  Our
democratic goal of spreading the truth as widely as possible was simply not one
that they shared.  Second, in a time when
free speech was not a right that governments recognized, veiled criticism
protected philosophers from the repression that plainer criticism would have
stirred up.

The author continued to cite a list of classic works (most
notably the myth of the metals in Plato’s *Republic*)
that should be read in this way, as an invitation to reflect rather than as a
plain statement of the classical writer’s meaning.  He also noted, cautiously (as a classicist
rather than a Biblical scholar) that at least some parts of the Bible appeared
to be written in this veiled style.

I don’t know very much about Plato, but I spend a fair
amount of time in the Scriptures, and I am willing to endorse the original
author’s statement much more strongly than he was.  There are many places in the New Testament
where the object of the writer is not to present truth as clearly as
possible.  Most Christians, I think,
would recognize that the book of Revelation falls into this category.  However, my study of the gospels over the
past few years has led me to believe that the same thing is true in them, both
of the gospel writers and of Jesus Himself. 
If we only read the gospels at a surface level, we are commonly going to
miss the point.

Let’s look here particularly at Jesus’ teaching.  His unwillingness to make the truth as
straightforward as possible is evident even in the smallest units of His
teaching.  Take, for instance, His
comments in Matthew 5:29-30 (and similarly in Mark 9:43-48) about how avoiding
hell is worth even tearing out an eye or cutting off a hand.  All of us understand that Jesus’ words here
are not to be taken literally.  He is not
teaching religion by excision.  Instead,
we have to think to figure out His real point, which is that entering the
kingdom of God is worth even the most severe spiritual sacrifices.

Interestingly, Jesus uses this to make a different
application in each context.  In Matthew,
He’s warning against sexual sin.  In
Mark, He’s warning against strife among believers.  Jesus could have said those things from the
beginning.  He could have said, “I know
you really enjoy lust, but you’re going to have to get it out of your life if
you want to go to heaven.”

However, that didn’t suit His goals.  He didn’t want everyone to understand His
point.  He only wanted those who cared
enough to think about it (or who cared enough to admit their ignorance to him)
to understand.

On some level, this concept bothers us.  Why isn’t Jesus making the gospel as accessible
to the lost as He possibly can?  However,
this is a clear feature of His teaching, both in the Synoptic Gospels and in
John.  Consider the following texts:

*And He was saying, "He who has ears to hear, let him
hear." As soon as He was alone, His followers, along with the twelve,
began asking Him about the parables.  And
He was saying to them, "To you has been given the mystery of the kingdom
of God, but those who are outside get everything in parables, so that WHILE
SEEING, THEY MAY SEE AND NOT PERCEIVE, AND WHILE HEARING, THEY MAY HEAR AND NOT
UNDERSTAND, OTHERWISE THEY MIGHT RETURN AND BE FORGIVEN." (Mark 4:9-12
NASB)*

*With many such parables He was speaking the word to them, so
far as they were able to hear it; and He did not speak to them without a
parable; but He was explaining everything privately to His own disciples. (Mark
4:33-34 NASB)*

*Therefore many of His disciples, when they heard this said,
"This is a difficult statement; who can listen to it?"  But Jesus, conscious that His disciples grumbled
at this, said to them, "Does this cause you to stumble?  What then if you see the Son of Man ascending
to where He was before?  It is the Spirit
who gives life; the flesh profits nothing; the words that I have spoken to you
are spirit and are life.  But there are
some of you who do not believe." For Jesus knew from the beginning who
they were who did not believe, and who it was that would betray Him.  And He was saying, "For this reason I
have said to you, that no one can come to Me unless it has been granted him
from the Father."  As a result of
this many of His disciples withdrew and were not walking with Him anymore.  So Jesus said to the twelve, "You do not
want to go away also, do you?"  Simon
Peter answered Him, "Lord, to whom shall we go? You have words of eternal
life." (John 6:60-68 NASB)*

Even if the crowds found Jesus’ teaching obscure, His
purpose here is pretty plain.  He is
using difficult, obscure concepts as a sorting mechanism.  The multitudes hear, do not understand, and
are not forgiven, but the disciples hear, seek clarification, and
understand.  The disciples grumble at a
difficult statement and leave, but the twelve remain to hear the words of
eternal life.  As with the story of the
Syrophoenician woman in Mark 7, Jesus only offers healing to those who first
prove their determination to find it.

The same thing is true of at least some of the gospels.  Mark’s famous note in Mark 13:14 advises the
reader that the writer isn’t going to spell everything out for him.  If you want to understand, you’re going to
have to think about it.  In Mark, this is
true not only of the cryptic stories about Jesus themselves, but of the order
in which those stories are arranged.  The
gospel is certainly not a comprehensive account of the ministry of Jesus, and
it is most likely not a strictly chronological account either.

I suppose one might argue that the arrangement of the
stories in Mark is random, like a child stringing together a set of play
beads.  However, this seems incompatible
with a high view of Scripture.  Do we
really want to contend that the Holy Spirit acted to inspire without purpose?

It seems much wiser to me to read Mark not verse-by-verse or
even story-by-story, but broadly, thematically, with an eye to the commentary
that adjacent stories offer on each other. 
I don’t claim to understand very much about this yet, but it seems clear
to me that the three stories in Mark 1:40-2:17 in the first case and Mark 10 in
the second case are meant to be read as a unit. 
The first set of stories proves Jesus’ power to heal spiritually by His
power to heal physically, and the second points out three common obstacles to
commitment to Him.  Of course, Mark never
says, “Hey, guys!  Read these three
stories together!”  However, I’m
convinced that he still expects the reader to understand.

This is not simple, bullet-point, easy-to-grasp stuff.  We commonly say that discipleship is
demanding and difficult.  Why would we
expect learning to be a disciple to be any different?

The ancient sorting mechanism is still in effect.  The words of Jesus still separate those who
are determined to learn from those who don’t care enough to invest the
effort.  As John 6 demonstrates,
disciples are not exempt from being sorted out.

When it comes to study of the hard sayings of Scripture, I
find myself greatly heartened by the promise of Luke 11:9.  I don’t think this means that I can expect to
figure everything out.  Instead, it looks
to me like a promise that the search for Jesus will last my whole life.  However, even if I’m not going to figure
everything out, I am going to figure out enough.

Seek, and you will find. 
Don’t, and you won’t.
