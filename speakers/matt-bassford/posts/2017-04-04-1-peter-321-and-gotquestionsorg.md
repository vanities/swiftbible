A couple of days ago, one of the sisters at my congregation
sent me [this link.](https://www.gotquestions.org/baptism-1Peter-3-21.htm)  She noted that a friend of hers had sent it
to her and invited a response.  Here is
mine.



**Is Baptism a Work?**

In the opening paragraph, the gotquestions.org writer,
whoever they may be (henceforth “the GQOW”), opines, “In the case of baptism
and salvation, the Bible is clear that salvation is by grace through faith in
Jesus Christ, not by works of any kind, including baptism (Ephesians 2:8-9).
So, any interpretation which comes to the conclusion that baptism, or any other
act, is necessary for salvation, is a faulty interpretation.”

If we were to reduce this argument to syllogistic form, it
would be as follows:

Major premise:  We are
saved by faith, not works.

Minor premise: 
Baptism is a work.

Conclusion:  We are
not saved by baptism.

I agree with the major premise.  It’s true that we are justified by
faith.  However, the minor premise is incorrect.  Baptism isn’t actually a work.

First, baptism isn’t a work in the Ephesians 2:8-9
sense.  There, Paul is using “works” as
shorthand for the concept he lays out in Romans 2:19-20 and surrounding texts,
“the works of the Law of Moses that earn salvation if performed perfectly”.  Throughout all of Paul's writing, every single
faith-versus-works passage is about this distinction:  earning salvation through perfect Law-keeping
versus being justified by faith in Christ.

People who are baptized for the forgiveness of sins aren’t
trying to justify themselves by perfect Law-keeping.  They’ve already failed, and they know
it.  In fact, their imperfection is the
very reason why they want to be baptized! 
It’s ludicrous to argue that the man who wrote Romans 6:1-11 didn’t
believe baptism was necessary for salvation.

This specific Biblical usage aside, baptism isn’t even a
work in the ordinary English sense of the word. 
The GQOW calls it an act, but it isn’t. 
It’s entirely passive.  During a
baptism, the baptizer is working.  The
baptizee isn’t.  They’re just standing
there and letting somebody else baptize them. 
If being baptized is a work, so is being hit by a bus.

In John 6:29, Jesus Himself calls belief a work.  This makes sense; when you decide to believe,
you’re doing something (even if all the action is happening inside your head), and nobody’s doing anything to you.  Note also that this makes nonsense out of
defining “work” as “anything but believing”. 
Substitute faith in as one of the works in Ephesians 2:8-9, and the text
reads, “For by grace you have been saved through faith. . . , not as a result
of [faith], so that no one may boast.” 
Clearly, Paul here does not mean to exclude the possibility that any
action could be necessary for salvation.

However, baptism is never described as a work in Scripture,
for the very good reason that it isn’t one. 
In consequence, any argument that depends on the premise that baptism is
a work is invalid.

**Are Miraculous
Spiritual Gifts a Sign of Salvation?**

The GQOW writes, “A good example of someone who was saved
before being baptized is Cornelius and his household in Acts 10. We know that
they were saved before being baptized because they had received the Holy
Spirit, which is the evidence of salvation (Romans 8:9; Ephesians 1:13; 1 John
3:24).”

It’s certainly true that the Holy Spirit falls upon
Cornelius and his household in Acts 10 prior to their baptism.  We know because they all started to speak in
tongues.  However, having and using a
miraculous spiritual gift is very different from the indwelling of the Holy
Spirit in Romans 8.  Anybody who has the
indwelling of the Holy Spirit (which, Biblically speaking, means that one is influenced
or controlled by the Spirit) is right with God, but this indwelling can’t be
established objectively.  By contrast,
the presence of miraculous spiritual gifts was objectively clear (you used them
to do miraculous things), but it didn’t necessarily reveal a right relationship
with God.

Indeed, there are people in Scripture who have and use a
miraculous spiritual gift yet clearly aren’t righteous.  Most notably, in John 11:49-52, the evil high
priest Caiaphas utters a true prophecy while he is in the middle of plotting
the death of Jesus.  Inspired by the Holy
Spirit (through his office as high priest)? 
Definitely.  Saved?  Definitely not.

As a result, we simply can’t conclude from the display of
tongues in Acts 10 that Cornelius and his household were saved at that
moment.  Instead, the tongues showed
Peter and the other Jewish believers present that the time had come for the
Gentiles to be saved through baptism too.

**What About the Thief
on the Cross?**

The GQOW also returns to that favorite chestnut of the
enemies of Bible baptism, the story of the thief on the cross.  They say, “Was Peter really saying that the
act of being baptized is what saves us? If he were, he would be contradicting
many other passages of Scripture that clearly show people being saved (as
evidenced by their receiving the Holy Spirit) prior to being baptized or
without being baptized at all (like the thief on the cross in Luke 23:39-43).”

In the passage the GQOW cites, Jesus says to the thief that
he will be with Him that night in paradise, with no mention of baptism.  So baptism isn’t required for salvation,
right?

Well, kind of.  We
must read the story of the thief on the cross in the light of what Jesus says
in Mark 2:10, where He proclaims, “The Son of Man has authority on earth to
forgive sins.”  Jesus could say to somebody,
“Your sins are forgiven,” and they would be. 
He told the thief on the cross that his sins were forgiven, and so they
were.

That’s great as far as it goes, but 2000 years after Jesus’
foot last trod the soil of this planet, it doesn’t go very far.  If Jesus, literally in the flesh, says to you
that your sins are forgiven, I’ll agree that they have been.  However, if He hasn’t told you directly and
you want to be forgiven, you’d better be baptized.

**An Appeal to God for
a Good Conscience**

The article continues, but the final quotation from the GQOW
that I want to discuss is this:  “Thankfully,
though, we don’t have to guess at what Peter means in this verse because he
clarifies that for us with the phrase ‘not the removal of dirt from the flesh,
but an appeal to God for a good conscience.’ While Peter is connecting baptism
with salvation, it is not the act of being baptized that he is referring to
(not the removal of dirt from the flesh). Being immersed in water does nothing
but wash away dirt. What Peter is referring to is what baptism represents,
which is what saves us (an appeal to God for a good conscience through the
resurrection of Jesus Christ). In other words, Peter is simply connecting
baptism with belief. It is not the getting-wet part that saves but is the ‘appeal
to God for a clean conscience’ which is signified by baptism, that saves us.”

Um, what?  Somehow,
the GQOW is able to take the phrase, “Baptism now saves you” and get it to
mean, “Baptism does not now save you.” 
They accomplish this transformation via sentences like, “While Peter is
connecting baptism with salvation, it is not the act of being baptized that he
is referring to.”

I’m confused.  Isn’t
baptism the act of being baptized?  Isn’t
that how the English language works?  Did
Peter say “baptism” when he actually meant “belief”?  My five-year-old wouldn’t make that mistake,
but apparently the GQOW thinks the Holy Spirit did.

The GQOW is also fascinated by the phrase “not the removal
of dirt from the flesh”.  They assert,
“Being immersed in water does nothing but wash away dirt.”  Well, no. 
According to Acts 22:16, it can also wash away sins, which is kind of
Peter’s point.  We don’t get baptized to
take a bath.  When I think back to my
baptism, I’m not conscious of having emerged from the baptistery any cleaner
than when I entered it.

Instead, grammatically speaking, baptism and Peter’s “appeal
for a good conscience” are exactly the same thing.  I want a good conscience, so I submit to
baptism, and in so doing, call upon the Lord to save me.  I am emphatically not saving myself.  I am placing my faith in the promises He has
made to all who are baptized.  Then, through
the resurrection of Jesus Christ, I too rise from the (spiritually) dead to
live as one of God’s redeemed people.

Baptism now saves you. 
It now saves me.  It now saves
everybody who needs to be saved.  That’s
the clear testimony of Scripture.  If
somebody wants to gamble their soul on rejecting this clear testimony, I guess
they’re free to do so (though I wish they wouldn’t!).  However, they won’t be able to argue on the
Day of Judgment that God never told them what they needed to know.
