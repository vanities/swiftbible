The other day, I ran across [this article](http://creedrehearsal.com/index.php/creed-busters/assembly/forsaking-the-assembly) on my Facebook feed, accompanied by some noncommittal comments from a
friend.  So far as I can tell, the author
is arguing for two things.  The first,
that Christians should spend more time one another outside of the assembly, is
something with which I heartily agree.  The
second, that the assembly itself isn’t that important, strikes me as worthy of,
um, closer examination.  Here are my
thoughts about it:



**“The Assembly” Versus
“The Assembling of Ourselves Together”**

In the introduction, the author makes much of a distinction
between “forsaking the assembly” and “forsaking the assembling of ourselves
together.”  He says that although they
“sound similar on the surface, they teach very different things.  An assembly is a gathering of people but
assembling is something people do.”

I’m not at all convinced that in the English language, these
are “very different things”.  “The
assembly” is a noun, and “the assembling of ourselves together” is a gerund
phrase, which functions as a noun, so we’ve got two nouns with the same root
word and an identical meaning.  That’s
not “very different”, exactly.

In fact, the Greek here, *episunagōgē*,
is another noun, one which means, according to Strong’s, “a complete
collection; especially a Christian meeting (for worship):—assembling
(gathering) together.”  In fact, I think
the only reason *episunagōgē* isn’t
commonly translated as “assembly” is to emphasize the completeness of the
resultant gathering.  This is where all
the Christians are, and the Hebrews writer is trying to encourage his audience to
be a part of it.

**“Church of Christ
Tradition”**

From here, the author shifts to the bulk of his argument,
with the overall heading “Church of Christ Tradition vs. the Bible”.  Whenever I see somebody talking about “Church
of Christ tradition”, I always wonder what they’re talking about, especially
when they quote “tradition” as saying things I’ve never heard of and don’t
believe.  Does *anyone* teach this?  If they
do, how come they count as belonging to the Lord’s church and I don’t?  I sense a version of [the no-true-Scotsman logical fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_true_Scotsman) lurking
underneath here someplace.

Concerning this supposed tradition, the author first claims,
“Tradition says that it is a sin to miss any service of the local church except
for sickness or circumstances beyond our control.”  Yup; this one is definitely on the list of
things that I’ve never taught nor heard anyone say.  I’ve heard discussions of how many assemblies
you can miss before you slip over the line into forsaking (which itself is a
terrible way to handle Hebrews 10:25), but nobody has ever insisted in my
presence that the number is zero.  I
wouldn’t be surprised if some brother somewhere has said this, but the Christians I know have better sense than to engage in such a deplorable reading of the text.  Speaking of logical
fallacies, I think this one is [a straw man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straw_man).

**Attendance as a
Spiritual Gauge**

The author next argues, “Tradition says that attendance is a
gauge of spirituality and faithfulness.” 
He seeks to discredit this by observing, correctly, that our assemblies
make up only a small and non-representative part of the Christian’s week.  He also notes, “Even the most superficial
among us should realize that ceasing to assemble with other Christians isn't
the first indication that someone is succumbing to spiritual problems, but the
last.”

I agree.  In fact, I
think any Christian with an ounce of discernment would agree with this.  I myself have known Christians--church
leaders, even!--who faithfully assembled while spending the rest of the week in
sin.  I don’t think that showing up for
services proves that a Christian is right with God, and neither does anybody
else—which calls into question the author’s claim that this belief constitutes
a church tradition.

Instead, the usual convictions of the Christians I know run
in the opposite direction.  Assembling
doesn’t prove anything about you, but forsaking the assembly sure does!  Hebrews 10:25 aside, any soul that is seeking
for God will also seek the gatherings where His name is praised and His word is
studied.  If a Christian doesn’t enjoy
those things and consider them important, why not?  Indifference to the assembly is hardly a sign
of spiritual health!  In fact, I can’t
think of a single Christian I’ve known who stopped attending services yet continued
to prosper spiritually.

**Worship in the
Assembly**

After this, the author states, “Tradition says that there
are acts of worship which must be carried out during the assembly.”  Again, there’s some strawmanning going on
here.  I’ve never heard it argued that
singing, corporate prayer, or even “the public reading of Scripture” can only
happen during official service times. 
However, it’s hard to imagine a situation in which the church would
gather together yet not want to sing, pray and study together.  These acts are not limited to our assemblies,
but they are certainly appropriate in that context.

As for the Lord’s Supper, the author’s Acts 20:7 issues
aside (it’s a lengthy discussion and not really germane to the point), it’s
still clear from Scripture that first-century Christians gathered to partake of
the Lord’s Supper.  In fact, Paul says in
1 Corinthians 11:33 that the brethren in Corinth were supposed to wait for one
another before partaking.

The Lord’s Supper was supposed to be not merely a memorial
of Jesus, but also a display of spiritual unity.  Paul writes in 1 Corinthians 10:17, “We who
are many are one body, for we all partake of the one bread.”  It’s impossible to make this statement
outside of an assembly.  Unless “the whole
church is gathered together”, the Lord’s Supper, whenever it is taken, becomes
a symbol of division rather than oneness. 
If you’re not partaking as a church, you’re doing it wrong.

**Assembling and
Willful Sin**

Finally under this subheading, the author contends, “Tradition
says that verse 26 is a commentary upon verse 25,” that the Hebrews writer is
citing failure to assemble as an example of willful sin.  I admit that I have heard brethren say
this.  I think it’s an imprecise reading,
but I have heard others make the argument.

However, the two verses are related (verses right next to each
other usually are), even if not quite in that way.  Hebrews 10:24 offers an instruction (“Let us
consider how to stir up one another. . .”), and 10:26 reveals what will happen
if this instruction is not followed (“we go on sinning deliberately”).  The Hebrews writer, though, does not leave it
to us to figure out how to stir up one another. 
Instead, he prescribes two applications in 10:25—not forsaking the
assembly, and encouraging one another. 
His point is not that I endanger my own soul by failing to assemble
(though that is true).  Instead, it is
that I endanger the souls of my brethren by not being present to provide them
the encouragement they need.  Among other
things, Christians who forsake the assembly are whiffing pretty spectacularly
on John 15:12.

**Conclusion**

In his conclusion, the author argues, “Missing isn't the
same as forsaking and we might do well to consider the good example of the very
first Christians from Acts 2:46-47:  ‘Every
day they continued to meet together in the temple courts. They broke bread in
their homes and ate together with glad and sincere hearts, praising God and
enjoying the favor of all the people.’”

To be honest, I think the author is talking out of both
sides of his mouth here.  On the one
hand, he’s arguing that Christians should spend as much time with one another
as possible.  I agree!  It’s a rare week when I don’t spend at least
some time with brethren outside of the assembly.  The more that Christians do this, the more I
think the church will prosper.

However, if it’s important for Christians to spend time with
one another, why is it unimportant to assemble? 
If we should seize every opportunity to encourage one another, why is it
OK to miss services as long as we aren’t forsaking?  Assembling and spending time with one another
throughout the week shouldn’t be an either-or. 
It should be a both-and.  The more
we seek God, whether with just a few or with an entire congregation, the more
we will benefit and the more He will be glorified.
