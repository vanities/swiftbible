Long ago and far away, during my odyssey through the
American higher-education system, I earned a degree in journalism from the
University of Missouri, which I will still loudly insist is the best journalism
school on the planet.  Even though I’ve
never actually worked as a journalist (which for me is kind of a trend when it
comes to “practical” degrees), I remain interested in the field and have
opinions about what it means to be a good or a bad journalist.



It was with some curiosity, then, that I read [this article](http://www.cjr.org/first_person/womens_march_washington_dc.php) in the *Columbia Journalism Review* (a
product of one of those schools that falsely claims to have the best journalism
school on the planet).  In it, the author
argues that she and other female journalists should have broken with the
longstanding journalistic prohibition against participating in public protests
and joined last weekend’s Women’s March.

She explains, “I will march not just because I understand
inequality to be real and would like to live to see its end, but also because
I’m deeply grateful for my right as an American to peaceful protest, and I
intend to use it to call for a basic tenet of journalism: fairness. Demanding
equality is a core tenet of journalism, a fundamental belief of many of its
practitioners, and should no longer be sidelined.”

This to me marks a fascinating shift.  I sense the author’s sincerity and passion in
her commitment to equality, but she seems to have forgotten something.  Back in the antediluvian days of the 1990s, I
was taught that the first responsibility of the journalist was to tell the
truth.  It didn’t matter which side benefited.  It didn’t matter how you felt about the
matter.  You told the truth.

The problem with insisting that other things are core values
of journalism is that sooner or later, those other values will conflict with
the truth.  You might, for instance,
believe that equality for women is predicated on an unrestricted right to
abortion.  No right to choose, no
equality.  What do you do, then, with a
lurid story about a Philadelphia abortionist who engages in all sorts of
horrible and indeed illegal practices?  If
you tell the truth, it might threaten your goal of equality for women.

Conversely, if you bury the story in the interests of
equality (which is what actually happened with the Kermit Gosnell trial a few
years back), you fail in your duty to tell the truth.  What’s more, your actions lead others to
conclude that you’re no longer trustworthy. 
I’m no conspiracy theorist, but these days, I assume that any mainstream
media story about abortion has as much advocacy in it as fact (if not
more).  Can any participant in a Women’s
March still be trusted to report evenhandedly on the issues the march
raises?  Indeed, the author of the *Columbia Journalism Review* notes that
American trust in the media has fallen to an all-time low, but she is blind to
the fact that it is precisely behavior like hers that has created the lack of
trust.

Of course, I am hardly a journalist these days, still less
an ombudsman or J-school professor.  The
behavior of journalists is no core concern of mine.  However, I am a Christian and a preacher, so
the behavior of Christians is of compelling interest, and I am concerned about
similar credibility problems among brethren.

No less than for journalists, truth must be a primary value
for Christians, and all other values must be understood in the light of truth.  A lie can never serve the interests of the
One who said, “I am the way, the truth, and the life.”  Following Him demands unflinching honesty
from us, no matter the cost.  We have to
be honest with the text, honest with ourselves, and honest with others.  If we are unwilling to repeat the hard
sayings of Jesus (like His teaching on marriage, divorce, and remarriage in
Matthew 19), our failure will surely undo us.

What’s more, our reputation for honesty is as important to
us as it is to the journalist.  In our
Scripturally illiterate age, few indeed are the non-Christians who know enough
about the Bible to accurately evaluate the truthfulness of what we
say about the gospel.  Instead, anybody
we study with will be taking what we say largely on trust.  If they don’t trust us, it doesn’t matter
whether what we say about the word is true or not.  They’re going to reject it.

If we want to be effective soul-winners, then, we must
diligently guard our reputations against the slightest whiff of
untrustworthiness.  We must be careful to
leave no doubt in anyone’s mind about our integrity.  We must not lie, deceive, or mislead, and we
must not yoke ourselves to those who do. 
All of those things will damage our credibility to the world.

This means that we must be extremely careful about the
secular causes with which we identify ourselves.  This is particularly true when it comes to
politics.  In our polarized age, 50
percent of Americans are convinced—with reason—that Democrats are liars, and 50
percent of Americans are convinced—also with reason—that Republicans are
liars.  If we loudly identify ourselves
with either party, and especially if we engage in partisan defense of dubious
conduct, that taint of deceptiveness will attach itself to us and deprive
us of access to the hearts of half the population.

If I teach somebody about Jesus and they reject the gospel,
I can live with that.  2000 years ago,
all kinds of people rejected Him too.  If
I teach somebody and they fall away, I can even make my peace with that.  The same thing happened to Paul.  However, if somebody won’t listen to me
because my words or conduct have led them to conclude (even incorrectly) that I
can’t be trusted, I will have gotten in the way of the gospel, and that would
be hard to bear.

Inevitably, a post-truth society also becomes a post-trust
society.  If all news is suspect,
everybody chooses the narrative they find most congenial, regardless of its
factual basis.  The gospel has never
depended on its congeniality; in fact, in many ways it is not congenial.  However, it is true, and because it is true,
it is desperately needed.  Let’s make
sure that our integrity, and even our reputation for integrity, are worthy of
the message we proclaim.
