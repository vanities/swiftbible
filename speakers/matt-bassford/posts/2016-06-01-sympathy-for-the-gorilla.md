Of all the things that could have occupied the attention of
the American public these past couple of days, [this story](http://www.msn.com/en-us/news/us/gorilla-killing-at-cincinnati-zoo-sparks-probe-into-possible-criminal-charges/ar-BBtH9Uc) seems to have risen
to the top.    For those who have been living under a rock,
a four-year-old child fell into the gorilla enclosure at the Cincinnati Zoo,
and in order to protect the child from harm, zookeepers shot a 450-pound bull
gorilla.

Online opinion has decried the shooting as sickening,
senseless, a tragedy.  Personally, I
think it’s another sign of the decadence of our society.  Here’s the deal.  I don’t approve of wanton cruelty to animals
any more than anybody else does, but animals are not people.  Gorillas are not people.



Biblically speaking, they are not created in the image and
likeness of God.  They do not have
souls.  Jesus did not suffer on the cross
to save gorillas from their sins.  When
they die, they are dead all over like Rover.

As a result, there can be no comparison between the value of
a gorilla and the value of a human child. 
Certainly, it would have been better had the boy not made his way into
danger, but once he did, the gorilla had to die to keep him from being injured
or killed.  Had to.  It was no more wrong to shoot the gorilla
than it is to swat a mosquito.  A gorilla
and a mosquito are much more alike than we are like either.

However, this is not the direction the story has taken.  Harambe the gorilla is being mourned as
fervently as if he had been human.  From
this, I can conclude only that the mourners no longer recognize the difference
between people and animals.

Actually, that’s not quite right.  The violent death of a gorilla isn’t being
given the same attention as the equivalent death of a human.  It’s being given more attention.  This same weekend in Chicago, six people,
human beings with souls, were shot dead. 
At least 56 more were wounded.

One of the dead was a 15-year-old girl.  The gorilla’s name is front-page news.  I can’t find that girl’s name anywhere.  Shooting a gorilla is a tragedy.  Shooting a teenager is business as
usual.

Perhaps the average suburbanite feels more sympathy for the
gorilla than the girl because he’s much more likely to visit the zoo than the
‘hood.  Black people, brown people,
yellow people, they are all “them”. 
Harambe the gorilla, though, he’s my friend!

Now that such an injustice has been done to Harambe (even
though he was actively hurting the boy when he was shot—would so much
consideration be given to an African-American man shot in the act of harming a
child?), the outcry for vengeance has begun. 
The buzzards are already circling around zoo officials.  An animal-activist group is filing a federal
complaint against the zoo, claiming negligence. 
They believe the maximum $100,000 penalty is appropriate.

The Cincinnati police are investigating too, not only the
zoo, but the mother of the child.  The
public clearly believes this is necessary. 
I see three petitions at change.org, one with more than 360,000
signatures, one with more than 124,000, and one with more than 50,000, all
advocating that the mother be investigated by CPS for neglect.

Ironically, the petition with 124,000 signatures, called
“Support Harambe’s Law”, was started by a Chicagoan.  I couldn’t find any petitions on change.org,
though, concerning justice for that murdered 15-year-old girl.  What was her name again?

At least we know the name of the mother of the strayed
child.  Along with Harambe, she has
entered the public consciousness.  Alas,
she’s entered it as a villain.  Everybody
is convinced that she is a bad mom because her boy got into the gorilla
pen.

Maybe.  I’m the father
of a four-year-old boy, though, and I’m convinced that if mine had a mind to,
he could break into Ft. Knox in about three seconds flat.  However, variables like the known perversity
of small children don’t seem to factor into the equation.  When such a grossly awful thing has been done
to an animal, we need to find some humans to suffer, even before it has been
ascertained whether they even did anything wrong.

Woe to those who put darkness for light and light for
darkness, who put bitter for sweet and sweet for bitter, who turn the death of
a brute into tragedy and a tragedy into business as usual.  We see here, I think, a system of values
based on nothing more profound than the feeling of the moment, not only in sympathy,
but also in hatred.  Today, the mob is
baying after zoo managers and inattentive mothers.  It remains to be seen who the victims will be
tomorrow.
