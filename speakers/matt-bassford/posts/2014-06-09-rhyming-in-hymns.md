One of the characteristics of
classic hymns as opposed to more modern praise songs is that the former almost
always rhyme, whereas the latter rarely do. 
The change is most likely caused by a modern impatience with rules and
structure.  This way of thinking often
regards the ancient pattern of rhyme and meter as a barrier to free expression.

It is certainly true that
learning to write in rhyme and meter is a skill that must be painstakingly
learned.  It is likewise true that no
writer, no matter how skilled, can express himself as fluently in verse as in
prose.  However, just as there are costs
associated with the traditional style of writing, so too there are costs
associated with its abandonment.  The
following is quoted from p. 63 of Daniel Kahneman’s *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011):



In addition to
making your message simple, try to make it memorable.  Put your ideas in verse if they can; they
will be much more likely to be taken as truth. 
Participants in a much cited experiment[[1]](file:///C:/Users/Downstairs/Documents/Hubband%20Misc/HEW/HEW%20Hymns/Rhyming%20in%20Hymns.docx#_ftn1)
read dozens of unfamiliar aphorisms, such as:

*Woes unite foes.*

*Little
strokes will tumble great oaks.*

*A
wrong confessed is half redressed.*

Other students
read some of the same proverbs transformed into nonrhyming versions:

*Woes
unite enemies.*

*Little
strokes will tumble great trees.*

*A
wrong admitted is half redressed.*

The aphorisms
were judged more insightful when they rhymed than when they did not.

I find this utterly
unsurprising.  I cannot speak for others,
but I know that when I am singing, I feel the first half of a rhyme pair as a
source of tension.  “Lord, we come before
Thee now,” even though it is a complete sentence, still does not feel
complete.  As I sing through the second
line, I pleasurably anticipate the resolution of that tension, and, as I sing
“bow” in “At Thy feet we humbly bow,” I am satisfied.  “At Thy feet we humbly kneel,” even though it
means the same thing, would not be as satisfying.

One of our cultural illusions is
the belief that we are wiser than those who came before us, that the traditions
they have handed down to us are the product not of imagination, but its
lack.  Therefore, we should be free to
discard those traditions without much thought.

Hymns that rhyme are certainly
traditional.  Rhyming in the English
language has been used since the Middle Ages, and this extraordinary
persistence can be explained by two words: 
it works.  Some element of the
human psyche is stirred by rhyme.  Hymns
that rhyme will therefore have a greater impact on the singer than those that
do not, and if we abandon rhyme, we are abandoning a tool that would make for a
more effective hymn.
