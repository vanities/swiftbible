A few weeks ago, one of my Facebook friends added me to a
group called “Fed up with bad church music”. 
It’s been an amusing read. 
Admittedly, I myself am not particularly fed up with bad church music at
the moment.  The song leaders where I
worship are a thoughtful bunch and do a pretty good job of insulating me from
it.  However, I still enjoy a good rant
now and again in an area where I have some knowledge.

Some of the most scathing posts I see on the group come from
Catholic commentators.  Apparently, some
portion of the Catholic body bitterly resents the changes that have come over
Catholic worship since the Second Vatican Council in 1962.  They long for a return to liturgy and
Gregorian chant, and they heap scorn upon modern replacements.  In fairness, if the musical past of my
religious tradition contained works by Mozart, Palestrina, and countless other
giants of Western music, and we were neglecting them, I might feel similarly.



One of the particular *b**êtes
noires* of these dissenters is a contemporary Catholic composer named Dan
Schutte.  I first encountered his name in
connection with what some of them have dubbed the *Missa di My Little Pony*, a 2012 setting of the *Glory to God* with a melody line that sounds awfully similar to the
theme song of a certain cartoon show.  [Here](http://www.ccwatershed.org/blog/2014/feb/13/Dan-Schutte-Mass-of-Christ-The-Savior/) is the link; judge for yourself.

I promptly decided that I wanted to find out more about this
Dan Schutte dude.  The imports to our
sacred music tend to come from the evangelical rather than the Catholic part of
the denominational world, so I’d never encountered his work in my own worship,
but I quickly realized that it has had a massive impact elsewhere.  I learned that his most widely used hymn is
entitled “Here I Am, Lord”. Like any
good 21st-century American, I YouTubed it and listened to it.  You can do the same [here](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EcxOkht8w7c.).

To be quite honest, the experience puzzled me.  I had a really strongly negative reaction to
it, and I couldn’t figure out why.  The
music certainly has a contemporary feel to it, but it isn’t badly constructed
like a lot of modern praise songs.  The
harmony made sense to me, and if there isn’t an SATB version already that would
be usable by an acappella congregation, it wouldn’t be hard to arrange one.

Nor is the problem lack of spiritual content, which is so
often the nemesis of praise-and-worship music. 
Schutte had something to say, he said it, and he used Biblical language
and concepts in the saying.  The hymn’s
genesis in Isaiah 6:8 is obvious; indeed, I’ve tried and failed to write a hymn
on that text myself.  I also enjoy some
of the imagery.  “The Lord of wind and
flame” is nicely evocative of Acts 2 and the day of Pentecost.

So. . . what gives?  It is true that the pictures chosen by the video uploader are a little, um,
heavy-handed, but I’m used to goofy videos accompanying spiritual music.  Even when I didn’t watch the video while
listening to the hymn, my dislike remained.

I think the problem is in the chorus.  After verses that end with “Whom shall I
send?”, the chorus replies:

*Here I am Lord; is it I Lord?*

*I
have heard You calling in the night.*

*I
will go Lord, if You lead me.*

*I
will hold Your people in my heart.*

It starts off with words from Isaiah, but it certainly
doesn’t resound with Isaiah’s spirit.  By
way of comparison, here’s Isaiah 6:8-10 (NASB):

*Then
I heard the voice of the Lord, saying, "Whom shall I send, and who will go
for Us?"*

*Then
I said, "Here am I. Send me!"*

*He
said, "Go, and tell this people:*

*‘Keep
on listening, but do not perceive;*

*‘Keep
on looking, but do not understand.’*

*Render
the hearts of this people insensitive,*

*Their
ears dull,*

*And
their eyes dim,*

*Otherwise
they might see with their eyes,*

*Hear
with their ears,*

*Understand
with their hearts,*

*And
return and be healed."*

Not exactly “I will hold Your people in my heart”, is
it?  And yet, no other Old-Testament
passage is quoted more in the New Testament than Isaiah 6:10, with its grim
warning that Isaiah’s ministry will be to convict rather than to save.  Jesus and Paul both cite this text repeatedly
with reference to their own preaching.

In the larger context of the Isaiah 6 theophany, the meaning
of this is unmistakable.  God has
revealed His glory and holiness to Isaiah, and now He is sending the prophet
forth to bear witness to that glory and holiness.  God would prefer to be glorified by the
willing obedience of His people, but even if they rebel against Him, He will
still be glorified--by the destruction of the rebels.  In either case, it is God whom Isaiah is
serving, not the people.

I think it is precisely this awareness that is missing from
“Here I Am, Lord”, and its absence robs our worship of and service to the
Creator of its power.  We are no longer
called to glorify God, even in the very teeth of a nation that has abandoned
him.  Instead, we are called to niceness,
to hold others in our hearts, to follow not *where*but *if* God leads.

This is not the message of the gospel of Christ, nor of the
defiant men who first proclaimed it. 
It is the message of our time, the gospel of tranquility, tolerance, and
love stripped of Scriptural content. 
Righteousness, self-control, and the judgment to come (to borrow from
Acts 24:25) are notable only for their absence. 
In the final analysis, for all of its virtues, “Here I Am,
Lord” makes me feel as though I were drowning in a vat of warm oatmeal.

Even as it soothes, it suffocates.
