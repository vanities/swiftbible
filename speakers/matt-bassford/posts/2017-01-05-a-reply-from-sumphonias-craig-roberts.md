*After I ran [yesterday's blog post](http://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2017/01/hymn-recordings-and-congregational.html) about the spiritual value and spiritual dangers of hymn recordings, I received the following reply from Craig Roberts, who is a member of the Sumphonia board as well as a close friend of mine.  I'm not an insider who makes decisions about recordings, but he is.  I thought his reactions made for a valuable read, and I'm posting them with his permission.*

Thanks Matt.  There is much to this.  But here is a bit of info that may be relevant.

Sumphonia was formed by authors who wanted their hymns to be made available and presented correctly to their fellow brethren.  Prior to Sumphonia CDs, authors depended on music directors, who for various reasons, gravitated toward recording weaker hymns while overlooking stronger hymns, and who often altered the tempos, arrangements, and even words.

The Sumphonia recordings were produced to provide each hymn in its original intent.  As much as possible, the authors, not the director, interpreted the hymn.  Sumphonia had a prayer answered when Scott Wyatt agreed to lead these recordings.  Scott had then and continues to have today the humility required for this approach.  He listens to authors and strives to replicate their interpretations.

When the Sumphonia recordings became available 15 years ago, they unintentionally filled a niche.  Nearly all other recordings were from school choirs, quartets, or other organizations.  These other recordings, many of which are excellent, included material of only one genre (e.g. contemporary) or of songs outside of the repertoire suitable for congregational worship.  The material in these other recordings included Latin hymns, camp songs, old fashioned radio music, or extremely difficult pieces.

The typical Sumphonia recording will include a large group of adults, who sing a wide range of genre, with hymns suitable for a congregation, presented correctly and as the authors intended.  It is true that these large groups can give a choir sound, as they are well trained to blend and breathe.  But the purpose of the large group is not to mimic a choir; it is to present each hymn correctly with the sound of an assembly (ekklesia)—a big sound instead of a small group.

The hymns on these recordings will be written fairly well, poetically and biblically, and the music will not be limited to a single taste bud.  Genres include contemporary, high church, folk, gospel, or a general sound.

The music does not include songs for children, scholarly choirs, or old time radio singin’.  The songs and hymns on these recordings are for actual worship in a congregation.  With the exception of two hymns in the past 15 years, all of the music can be learned by the typical congregation; the standard comment is that the music should not be more difficult than Sanderson’s “Be with Me, Lord.”

On a personal note, I am deeply grateful to my brothers and sisters who volunteer to work with Sumphonia.  Their singings and recordings broadcast, on behalf of the authors, the “do-it-like-this” message that reaches many congregations and song leaders.  Also, their singings and recordings, which are of high quality, comfort, teach and encourage many Christians.  Thanks be to God for these talented brothers and sisters.

This may be more than you wanted to read.  And there is still much more to say.  But I hope these comments are helpful.
