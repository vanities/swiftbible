**OVERVIEW.**  To me, a top-notch reading Bible will have
small-to-moderate size, two columns, a generous gutter, text at about 8 points,
crisp print, line-matching, and opaque paper.

I suspect that to a lot of non-Bible geeks, all of the
discussion of the physical characteristics of Bibles seems a bit
pointless.  Who cares whether Bible X has
a good characters-per-line count? 
However, these subtle distinctions can make a big difference.  Even though I’ve been a preacher for 10 years
and a Christian for 25, 2014 was the first year in which I read through the
entire Bible.  I credit this, of course,
to the Lord’s help, but also to my relatively new interest in Bibles.  Because I cared about and learned to enjoy
various physical editions, I had no trouble remembering to read from them.



In fact, it may be that it is more important to get our
reading Bible right than any other Bible. 
No matter what Bible a preacher has, he’s still going to preach out of
it on Sunday morning--unless he wants to find himself in another line of
work.  However, daily Bible reading,
which nearly every Christian will acknowledge is a good thing to do, is not
something that anyone will make us do. 
We have to want to do it, and the right reading Bible will help us want
to.

Having said that, here’s what I’ve found about Bibles that
make me want to read:

**SIZE.** Here, I’m not nearly as persnickety as I
thought I would be originally.  I do
enjoy reading out of small, paperback-sized Bibles that I can hold in one
hand.  However, I didn’t really find
myself troubled by reading from larger Bibles either.  I end up propping them against my chest/stomach
rather than one-handing them, but that’s an equally natural reading posture for
me.  Generally, I’ll be happy reading
from any Bible 6 x 9 or smaller, but further testing may establish that I
handle even larger Bibles just fine too.

**LAYOUT.** I see this completely differently than I
did a year ago.  Back in 2014, I correctly
noted that I prefer reading from double-column rather than single-column
Bibles.  However, I thought this was
about column width in inches.  It
isn’t.  Instead, it’s about characters
per line.  Whether a Bible be
single-column or double-column, I like to see it with between 40 and 55
cpl.  That’s in my reading sweet
spot.  There may be a little bit of fudge
room to either end, depending on the Bible.

Second, I’ve learned that I prefer to read from Bibles with
generous gutters.  When I have a Bible
open in my hand, there should be a respectable stripe of white space down the
middle, where the pages join, and there shouldn’t be a sizable amount of text
twisting into the abyss.  Most annoying
of all is when I have to press down on the gutter with my thumb to stretch open
the Bible to read the innermost words. 
At this point, that’s enough to keep me from using a Bible as a reader
entirely.

Third, black-letter Bibles make the best reading
Bibles.  In theory, red-letter Bibles
seem like a good idea.  What Bible
student doesn’t think the words of Jesus are important?  In practice, they are often poorly
executed.  The ink that is supposed to be
red often appears more orange or pink, which is low-contrast and difficult to
read.  Also, red-letter Bibles are
produced using two runs through a press, one for black ink and one for
red.  Even a tiny error in the process
can lead to the red text being misaligned with the black text and black verse
numbers, which is distracting.

Fourth, the best reading Bibles are line-matched
Bibles.  Back in the good ole days of
super-opaque Bible paper, this wasn’t such a huge deal.  Nowadays, however, unless you’re willing to
tote at least three pounds of Bible around (and even some big Bibles aren’t
immune), you’re going to be dealing with ghosting.  Ghosting, so dubbed by Mark Bertrand, is when
you’re able to read the words on the other side of the page through the
paper.  With particularly translucent
Bible paper, sometimes several layers of text are visible.

In a non-line-matched Bible, this effectively puts all the
text against a dingy gray low-contrast background, which makes the Bible
unnecessarily difficult to read.  Pink-letter
Bibles often have particular trouble with this. 
Pink text against a gray background does not make for pleasant perusal!

Line-matched Bibles solve this problem to some extent by
putting lines on the opposite sides of a page back-to-back.  This leads to alternating bands of color on a
page, with white space between lines and darker gray behind where lines
are.  I don’t know why it is, but even
this kind of contrast makes a Bible more readable.

Fifth, I still tend to like Bibles with at least 8-point
text.  Anything less than that, I have to
hold the Bible closer to my eyes than I prefer and squint at it a little
bit.  Neither one of those things is
natural, and they distract me from the reading.

**PRODUCTION
QUALITY.** Finally, I like Bibles that
are well-produced.  When I first started
reviewing Bibles, I thought that all printers were created equal.  Experience has taught me otherwise.  The presses used by premium-level printers,
such as Jongbloed in the Netherlands, are simply capable of producing sharper
text than the mass-market presses in China. 
A Jongbloed-produced Bible will be more readable than it ought to be
because the finely formed characters are clearer and sharper.  This, to me, is the best reason to buy a
premium Bible.  Goatskin covers are nice,
and if you’re more likely to read a Bible with a a goatskin cover, great!  However, premium-printed text blocks are
actually better at conveying information, and that’s truly important.

Finally, I prefer opaque paper in a reading Bible.  Line-matching will mitigate ghosting
problems, but it won’t solve them.  The
easiest Bibles to read are the Bibles with little to no ghosting.
