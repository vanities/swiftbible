I’ll probably return to writing about Macy more in the
future, but at the moment, I’ve got something else on my mind.  Yesterday, a good friend of mine and brother
in Christ acknowledged that for the past nine months to a year, he had been
plagiarizing in his sermons.  His remorse
could not have been more evident, and I admire his willingness to confess his
sin in a setting where he could easily have remained silent about it.  No one should read anything I say as being
directed at him.

However, this is something we need to talk about.  I’ve observed before that Christians can play
awfully fast and loose when it comes to intellectual property.  This is true when it comes to violating
copyrights on hymns and Bible-class materials, and I fear it’s equally true
when it comes to preachers plagiarizing sermons.



According to dictionary.com (which quotes from *The Random House Dictionary*), plagiarism
is “an act or instance of using or closely imitating the language and thoughts
of another author without authorization and the representation of that author's
work as one's own, as by not crediting the original author.”

In other words, plagiarism is when you take all or part of
somebody else’s writing (a sermon, for instance) and present it as your own,
without attributing it to the original author. 
Certainly, like every other preacher in existence, my sermons contain
ideas that others have given me.  Even
though I like to give credit whenever I remember where I got something, putting
another’s idea in my own words isn’t plagiarism.  Plagiarism is my taking both idea and
expression (and changing a word or two is still using the same expression)
without giving credit to the preacher I got it from.

I see several problems with this practice.  First, it’s dishonest.  When I get up in front of the congregation
and preach a sermon, I’m presenting the words of that sermon as my own.  Every member of the audience is going to
believe that they’re my own.  If they
aren’t, and I don’t reveal that fact, I’m deceiving my audience.

To many, this may seem harsh.  However, we would do well to remember that in
American colleges, plagiarism is known as “academic dishonesty”.  Students can be sanctioned or expelled for
plagiarism.  Professors can lose their
jobs for the same offense.  If such
godless institutions see the moral problem here, Christians certainly ought to
be able to!

When it comes to preachers who are partially or fully
supported, the charge of dishonesty takes on another dimension.  My father-in-law likes to joke that preachers
have the easiest job in the world.  They
only have to work one day a week, and they’ve got this big book that tells them
what to say.

In reality, of course, that is not what churches expect from
their preachers.  At least, it’s not what
the Joliet church expects from me.  They
want me not only to preach sermons, but also to prepare them, so that what I
present from the pulpit is the fruit of a week’s worth of study.  It’s not merely the product that matters to
them.  The process matters too.  Otherwise, the men of the congregation could
take turns downloading sermons every week and dispense with me and my salary
altogether!

If, by contrast, I am the one downloading sermons every
Saturday night, I’m not fulfilling their expectations.  I’m like a sports journalist who attends a
football game but copy-and-pastes his colleague’s story rather than writing his
own.  That doesn’t fly in the journalism
world either, by the way (speaking of generally godless people who still see
the problem).  I’m taking money for a job
that I’m not actually doing.  Worse
still, it’s the Lord’s money I’m taking!

I suppose that this fault might be less egregious in a
preacher who is so busy on other aspects of the Lord’s work that he doesn’t
have time to write sermons.  After four
dozen Bible studies this week, he just doesn’t have the brainpower to
spare!  I would like to believe that’s
the norm when it comes to plagiarized sermons, but I suspect the real culprits
are more likely things like Facebook and Candy Crush.

In addition to dishonesty, plagiarism also reveals a problem
with pride.  In my brother and friend’s
confession, he acknowledged that pride, wanting to be somebody he wasn’t, was
part of what drove him to plagiarize.

I get this.  Oh, do I
get this!  Some preachers have the gift
of Apollos.  They’re eloquent men,
blessed with the ability to preach truth in a way that inspires the hearts of
their listeners.  Most preachers don’t
have that gift.  I don’t have it.  If I can manage “truth, logically and clearly
presented”, I’ve just about peaked!

It might be that 200 years ago, that wasn’t such a big
deal.  Every preacher, big fish or little
fish, had a pond to himself.  Today, by
contrast, any one of us can be listening to the best preachers on the planet in
less than 30 seconds.  That’s true for preachers,
and it’s true for the folks in the pews too.

If we’re not careful, it can create unrealistic expectations
in both camps.  The brethren at Joliet
might start getting upset with me because I’m not a dynamic orator (though they
should know better by now).  I might
start getting upset with myself because, even though I’m giving my all, I don’t
have as much to offer as other men do.  That
kind of an environment can easily lead to plagiarism.  I don’t measure up, so I start stealing from
the guys who do measure up.

Even though this is understandable, it’s still not
acceptable.  My preaching is not about
me.  It’s not about my looking good to
the congregation.  It’s not about puffing
up my own ego.  Instead, it has to be
about God, first, last, and always.  If I
use plagiarism to take the spotlight off Him and put it on me, I have indeed
gone astray.

There’s more that I could say, but these things alone should
make it obvious that plagiarism is a moral and spiritual problem.  What should we do about it?  Here are a couple of thoughts:

**Attribute.** If you’re using somebody else’s words,
make sure that you supply his name along with it.  This isn’t a research paper.  “According to Bob Smith” works.  “Brother Jones has said” works too.  If you got a whole sermon outline from somebody,
say, “I heard this sermon from Peter Parker, and I thought it was so good that
I wanted to share it with you.”  If
you’re quoting Peter Parker word for word in certain places, be sure to cite
him there too.

This serves two useful purposes.  First, it makes sure that credit goes where
credit is due.  How painful it must be to
be praised for a paragraph you actually stole from somebody else!  Second, it has a way of limiting the use of
other men’s material, particularly by fully supported preachers.  The brethren will probably be OK with Peter
Parker’s sermon outlines once or twice a year, but if you’re preaching from Peter
Parker every week, you’re going to start seeing some raised eyebrows (and
rightfully so!).

**Come Clean.** I suspect that there are at least a few
preachers out there who have been reading this with a steadily mounting sense
of horror.  “I do that all the time, and
I never thought there was a thing wrong with it!”  If you’ve been doing this (again,
particularly if you’re a supported preacher), now’s the time to change your
ways and be open with the congregation about what you’ve been doing.  If you’ve never really thought about it,
explain that.  Promise them that you’ll
do a better job of citing sources in future. 
I suspect that most congregations will be pretty understanding, and it
will all blow over in a week or two.

If you don’t, however, you are inviting Numbers 32:23
problems.  It’s not hard to run a
copy-and-paste search.  College
professors routinely plagiarism-check their students’ submissions.  Sooner or later, somebody’s going to get
suspicious (especially if they’ve heard the preacher you’re plagiarizing from)
and find you out.  In that circumstance,
the congregation is not going to be nearly so understanding, and it will not
all blow over in a week or two.  Better
to face a little pain now than a lot of pain later.

This is not a pleasant subject, but it’s an important
one.  When preachers plagiarize, they
damage the reputations of all who preach the gospel.  I’ve had a brother at Joliet tell me how glad
he was that I wrote my own sermons because he had been a member at another
congregation where the preacher got his sermons off the Internet.  He knew the other guy plagiarized because he had
looked him up, and I suspect he knew I didn’t because he had looked me up.

Far better that all of us were straightforward, not merely
in our content, but in our sourcing! 
After all, we serve a God of whom David said, “Behold, You desire truth
in the innermost being.”  Let’s make sure
that truth is evident everywhere within us.
