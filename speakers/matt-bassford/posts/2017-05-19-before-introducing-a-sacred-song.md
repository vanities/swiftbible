| Perhaps not the new song I would introduce. |

I’m completely on board with Psalm 96:1’s injunction to
“Sing to the Lord a new song.”  I don’t
think the traditional repertoire of the church is good enough, nor is any
imaginable repertoire going to be good enough. 
As we travel through history with God, there will always be new
experiences that deserve to be sung about, new insights into His word that
ought to be expressed.  It’s hard to
imagine my spiritual landscape without “Nearer, My God to Thee” or “Abide With
Me”, but there were Christians many centuries before those hymns existed.  Our spiritual descendants will doubtless
prize songs that now have not even been written.

Every congregation of the Lord’s people should make a
practice of learning new songs.  However,
no one should do so hastily, thoughtlessly, or carelessly.  Instead, like a sculptor at a block of
marble, we should ask whether our proposed tap of the hammer is actually going
to result in an improvement.  Some hymns
should be introduced; others should not be. 
In order to distinguish the first category from the second, I propose
the following list of questions:

**How does the congregation feel about
learning new hymns?** Churches have
personalities just like people do.  As
with people, some congregations enthusiastically embrace new sacred songs and
will probably need to be restrained from replacing the existing repertoire
altogether.  At the other extreme are
churches that see no reason to sing anything written after 1965. 

Generally, the song-introducer should be careful to
understand his group’s appetite for novelty and not force-feed it more than it
wants.  It’s great that you’re 25,
learned all these cool new songs in devotions in college, and are eager to sing
them in the assembly too.  However, if
you try to ram 50 unfamiliar songs a year down the throat of a congregation
that would be more comfortable learning five a year, your efforts are going to
backfire and cause a result that is the opposite of what you want to
accomplish.  Sheep need to be led, not
driven.

**What’s the congregation’s
ability to learn new hymns?** Willingness
is one thing; capacity is another.  Some
congregations have a core of crack sightreaders who can nail almost anything
after the third repetition.  Others are
filled with people who look at a page of sheet music and wonder what all the shapes and lines mean.

Such people may be enthusiastic.  My own mother showed up for every
hymn-learning session she could attend despite not being able to carry a tune
in a bucket.  However, their practical
limitations still need to be taken into account.

Here, the song-introducer must be able to gauge the
difficulty of a new song and the relative ability of his church.  Does the hymn require four strong parts to be
effective?  Is it in a minor key?  Are there lots of weird, non-intuitive jumps
in the harmony?  Does the rhythm feel
like Morse code?  These characteristics
add to the degree of difficulty of a song, and it’s rarely a good idea to put
an 8/10 hymn in front of a 3/10 congregation. 
It won’t go well, and it may discourage the congregation from wanting to
learn new songs in future.

**How will the song
help the congregation?** Maybe you can
get away with introducing it, but “can” and “should” are two different
things.  Most congregations have a
repertoire of at least 150 hymns that get sung sometimes.  How is the church going to be better off if
it goes to the trouble of learning this one new hymn than it would be with the
150 hymns it already knows?  Does it fill
a topical gap in the repertoire?  Does it
confront us with God in a way that will evoke fresh worship?  Does it present a traditional idea in a new
and compelling way?  In short, if this
unfamiliar song becomes familiar, how will it benefit us in our walk with God?

Note that the answer to this question will always be
lyrically rather than musically based. 
Music can be a powerful vehicle for a song’s message, but it can never
replace that message.  Be wary when the
answers to the question, “Why do I want to introduce this song?” are things
like “It’s beautiful,” or “It’s fun to sing.” 
Jesus calls us to worship in spirit and in truth, not in spirit only,
and a song that’s heavy on spirit and light on truth can only contribute to
spiritual imbalance.

Of course, hymns that are light on spirit and heavy on truth
are equally problematic.  However, this
is a self-correcting problem.  Nobody
wants to sing hymns like this, so they don’t survive long enough to be
introduced!

**Am I prepared to
teach it to others?** Some men can
read an unfamiliar piece of sheet music as easily as they read text.  They would have no trouble teaching it to a
group 30 seconds after picking it up.

I try not to hate them.

My own abilities are more modest.  I can learn a melody from sheet music if I
work at it, but I learn better by ear.  I
generally only introduce hymns that I’ve sung elsewhere before or learned from
a recording.  Before introduction, I
practice it by myself until I’m confident in my grasp of it.  I may even enter it into Finale to make sure
I get it right.

All hymn-introducers, though, need to achieve mastery on
their own before they bring a song before others.  Make sure that you know the tune.  Don’t get excited and introduce it before you
do.

For that matter, make sure that the tune you know is the one
that’s on the page.  A little while ago,
I was visiting another congregation, and the song leader confidently sang a
familiar hymn to a tune (and it was an actual, coherent tune) that I’d never
heard before. This creates massive confusion for anybody who isn’t singing the
melody along with the song leader.

**Conclusion**

Introducing an unfamiliar song is one of the most difficult
things I do in the Lord’s service.  For
some, this comes easily, but for most, their experience is more like mine.  This is to say nothing of the difficulty that
the members of the congregation experience, most of whom probably don’t even
read music!  However, despite its
difficulty, learning a new song is still a noble and important goal.  If we do so wisely and carefully, our efforts
will bless the Lord’s people for generations to come.
