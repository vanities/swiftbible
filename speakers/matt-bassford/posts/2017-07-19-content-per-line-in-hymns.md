Some of the most technically masterful hymns in our
repertoire are translations.  Take, for
instance, the first verse of Catherine Winkworth’s translation of “Blessed
Jesus, at Your Word”.

*Blessed Jesus, at Your
word*

*We are gathered all to
hear You;*

*Let our hearts and
souls be stirred*

*Now to seek and love
and fear You,*

*By Your teachings
sweet and holy,*

*Drawn from earth to
love You solely.*

That’s technique that’s so strong, it’s flashy!  The meter is an über-Germanic 7.8.7.8.8.8, rhyming every line, with each of
the eight-syllable lines demanding a feminine rhyme.  Winkworth nails every one, and because of the
music, when you sing it, you can feel each of those feminine rhymes hit like a
truck.  This is a structurally exquisite
verse that, incidentally, is quite close in meaning to the German original.

I think the verse is so good for two reasons.  First, Winkworth was a skilled
technician.  The woman knew how to
write!  Second, it’s a translation, and
despite the difficulties in moving from language to language, it’s easier to
write a good translation of a hymn than it is to write a good hymn.



**Misery in
Paraphrasing**

This stands in contrast to the great difficulty associated
with writing a psalm paraphrase.  I’ve
written both translations and paraphrases, and of the two, paraphrases are much
more challenging.  I’ve never seen, much
less written, a psalm paraphrase as technically superb as the first verse of
“Blessed Jesus, at Your Word”.

Generally, at least one of three things is true of every
psalm paraphrase:  the rhyme scheme is
undemanding, the language is clunky and stilted, or the paraphrase does not
strictly reflect the content of the psalm. 
Note, for instance, that “All People That on Earth Do Dwell” is quite a
bit longer than Psalm 100, and the anonymous author deftly uses the extra space
to hit his rhymes.  Normally,
paraphrasers can’t do this because the average psalm is longer than a hymn can
be, so you’re stuck with an array of unpalatable choices.

**Thought Units**

The difference between translations and paraphrases
highlights a key but underappreciated attribute that the hymnist must
develop:  a sense of how much content a
line in a hymn can contain. 
Old-Testament psalms aren’t metered (at least in English), so each line
in a psalm can have nearly any amount of content.  As a result, writing a psalm paraphrase feels
like trying to zip an overstuffed suitcase. 
Stuff keeps on springing out and making you start all over again.

By contrast, when Winkworth turned her attention to
“Liebster Jesu, Wir Sind Hier”, she encountered a text that Tobias Clausnitzer
had already neatly divided into line-sized thought units.  When she translated the German thought units
into English, the English thought units naturally tended to come out about the
same size.  For a skilled writer, it
wasn’t nearly as much work to slide those thought units into rhyme and meter.

**Beginning Hymnists
and Content Fit**

Writing a hymn is about midway in difficulty between
translation and paraphrase.  The thought
units of the thought the hymnist wants to express probably aren’t the size of a
line in the meter they’ve chosen either, but they can be trimmed and made to
fit.

The trimming process, though, tends to give aspiring writers
fits.  There are two problems they can
encounter.  The first is when there isn’t
enough content to fill a line, so the author has to throw in filler syllables
that take up space and don’t add to the meaning.  In our repertoire, the most conspicuous
example of this is “Anywhere Is Home”. 
Phrases like “honored fame”, “earthly palace fair”, and “Let come and go
what may” reveal places where the author’s actual thought was much shorter
(“fame”, “palace fair”, and “Come what may”), but he had to stretch it out to
fit the music.  Generally, the preferred
solution here is not to add filler syllables, but to find content to fill the
meter.

Beginning hymnists, though, typically don’t struggle with
having too little to say.  They struggle
with having too much to say.  They have
all these wonderful, profound thoughts they want to express, but they find to
their dismay that they have to cram those thoughts into dinky little
eight-syllable lines (or lines that are even shorter).  The resulting writing process feels to them
about like trying to shove a 500-pound man into the back seat of a Geo
Metro.  No matter how hard you push, that
door just ain’t gonna close!

Even if you do get your passenger installed, he’s likely to
be disgruntled, and so it often is with the lines in the handiwork of beginning
writers.  They have their thought words
in there, but they don’t leave room for the thoughts to breathe, and they leave
out the connecting words that distinguish graceful English from Tarzan-like
inelegance.  Me worshiper.  You God.

In order to advance in skill, the lesson that they must
learn is to trim their content to fit the constraints of a hymn, a verse, and a
line.  Hymns just aren’t very long, and
what you say in a hymn, you must say sparely. 
You will never have room to say everything you could.  As a result, you will have to leave something
out.

**Essential Ideas**

In this leaving-out process, the writer must keep two things
in mind.  The first is the difference
between an interesting idea and an essential idea.  Assuming that you’re working with a
verse-sized concept in the first place, there are some things that you simply
must say in order to communicate that concept. 
Without them, the concept will not make sense to the worshiper.

Let’s suppose, for instance, that you’re Johnson Oatman,
Jr.  You’re writing the second verse of
“No, Not One!”, and the idea you want to communicate is that Jesus is both
really impressive and important, but is still willing to get down on our level
and help us (opposites that don’t normally go together, but do in Jesus).  You need to have both of those ideas
somewhere in your verse.  If you don’t,
the singer won’t get the point.  Any
other idea can go; those two can’t.

**Meter and Thought**

Second, the writer has to appreciate the constraints of his
particular meter.  An eight-syllable line
gives you some wiggle room.  A
ten-syllable line offers more space, honestly, than I’m comfortable with.

At the other end of the scale, six-syllable lines have to be
positively Spartan.  No adjectives need
apply!  Consider, for instance, the spare
elegance of “Blest Be the Tie” and “A Charge to Keep I Have”, two hymns in
which six-syllable lines predominate.  In
six syllables, you can get your point across and nothing more.  If you turn aside to the right hand or to the
left, you will run out of room.

It’s possible to write in meters with an average length of
less than six syllables, but to do so, you will usually end up taking one of
three paths:  using a repeated phrase
(“Teach Me Thy Way”), not rhyming every line (“Have Thine Own Way, Lord”), or
not using complete sentences (“Savior and Friend”).

**Making It Work**

When I am writing, I generally determine my meter very early
in the process, and I envision each line of a verse as a bucket waiting to be
filled with content.  As we’ve seen, some
buckets are smaller than others.  What
can I get away with pouring into this bucket before it starts spilling over the
side?

I’ve been doing this for 20 years now, but it’s still often the
case that I have to make difficult choices. 
There’s a non-essential idea that I love that is simply too big for the
bucket I’ve got.  I have to play with
these ideas and turn them this way and that before I am finally driven to admit
that they aren’t going to fit.  If I
leave them out, the verse will be smooth. 
If I include them, it won’t be.

Even though hymns are emphatically not normal English, a
good hymn will sound like it is.  The
only way to achieve this normal, conversational sound is to be brutally
realistic about how much content the form of your hymn can hold.  Too much content won’t fit easily, won’t be pretty,
and won’t be sung.  This is true at every
structural level, and it’s especially true at the level of the line.  Only if we know how much we can ask of our
lines will they do what we want them to.
