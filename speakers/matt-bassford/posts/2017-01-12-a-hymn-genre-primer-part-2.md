Yesterday, I ran [a post](http://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2017/01/a-hymn-genre-primer-part-1.html) that attempted to sort out the hymns
in the repertoire of the churches of Christ by genre.  However, I
only made it to about 1900 in Great Britain and never touched on American hymns
at all before I ran out of room.  Here’s
the rest!



**American Gospel
Hymns.** For some reason, the United
States started producing hymn-tune composers faster than it did hymn-text
writers.  The colonial era contained a
number of composers of varying capacity, but the first hymn written by an American
was “I Love Thy Kingdom, Lord”, which Timothy Dwight penned in 1800.

Even then, it took another 50 years or so before Americans
started writing words and music in numbers large enough to significantly
influence our repertoire.  However, once
they got going, they pretty well defined it. 
Writers like Fanny J. Crosby, composers like William Bradbury and John
Sweney, and do-everythings like Robert Lowry and P.P. Bliss all hail from this
era.  I semi-arbitrarily argue that it
ended in about 1890.  After that, gospel
music started to shift to something different.

Hymns from this time and place are much simpler than the
concurrently developed body of Victorian hymnody.  The words are less high-toned and probably
not as technically flashy.  The tunes
don’t rely on the subtle harmonies of a “Holy, Holy, Holy” or an “Abide With
Me”.  There’s lots of block-chording,
where basses only change notes when they cross a barline.  Nor are such hymns particularly rhythmically
sophisticated, especially when compared to later Southern-gospel hymns.  There are lots of choruses, but the choruses
don’t have lower-part leads.

However, all the simplicity means that hymns like this are
extremely well adapted for a-cappella congregational worship.  The content is usually excellent (as with
Wesley, thousands of mediocre-to-bad hymns by Fanny J. and her peers have been excised
by later editors).  The music is
singable, pretty, and not prone to creating distraction.  If I have to pick one genre of hymn to sing
exclusively, I’m going to pick this one.

Examples:  “Sweet Hour
of Prayer”, “I Am Thine, O Lord”, “Give Me the Bible”

**Transitional Gospel
Hymns.** Again, this one is somewhat
arbitrary, but I think of American gospel hymns written from 1890 to 1920 as
having a character that is distinct from the hymns that precede and follow
them.  They aren’t full-on radio hymns,
but neither do they resemble the hymns that P. P. Bliss wrote.  However, they clearly stand in between those
two styles.

It’s in these hymns that we start seeing a decline of
content.  In my experience, there is no
such thing as a bad hymn older than 150 years that is still in use.  Any hymn that survives for that long survives
because everything about it is good. 
However, hymns with poor content may survive for a hundred years on the
basis of appealing music.

I’ll probably
get in trouble for this, but I offer up “Beautiful Isle of Somewhere” as the
oldest hymn with bad content that we still sing (Heaven is an island?  Really?). 
Side note:  whenever I see a
balding man who still has that little tuft on top, I call it “the beautiful
isle of some hair”.  Anyway, some hymns
from this time have wonderful content; some don’t.

Musically, tunes from this era often have a swoopy feel to
them.  Compound meters are fairly common.  Chromaticism starts to show up.   Rhythm
continues to become more and more important in choruses, though you really
don’t have the lower-part leads yet.

Examples:  “Nearer,
Still Nearer”, “In the Garden”, “One Day”

**Southern Gospel
Hymns.** Even though there’s plenty of
blending on both ends, the core of the Southern-gospel era runs from 1920 to
1960.  These are the first genre of hymn
that really owed its popularization to mass media, as gospel quartets sang these
over the radio.  They’re also known as
Stamps-Baxter hymns, after the writer-composer duo that formed a publishing
company and bought the copyrights to many of these.

Lyrically. . . well. . . 
Let’s just say that hymn genres are like cheese.  If you don’t give them enough opportunity to
age and season, you’ll only end up with a runny mess.  There are certainly good Southern-gospel
hymns, hymns that Christians will be singing hundreds of years from now if
there are still Christians singing anything in the English language.

However, there are a lot of the other kind that we’re still
singing too.  Generally, their biggest
problem is weak, cliché-ridden content. 
It is hymns like these that have taught the church that heaven is the home
o’er the foam where we’ll nevermore roam.

Such triteness is the work of writers with inadequate technical
skill struggling to rhyme.  In the
Victorian era, the problem led to awkward syntax.  Among Southern-gospel writers, it led to
writers hitting rhymes via cliché. 
Interestingly, most of the hymnists in this genre who were associated with the churches of Christ wrote with strong content.

Musically, things are in better shape because the focus of
the genre is musical rather than lyrical. 
You have lots of looong tunes with syllables that writers had to fill up
somehow (most of the two-page hymns in our hymnals are Southern-gospel
hymns).  There usually aren’t
part-writing errors, but arrangements from this time period can tend to the trite
too.  Composers liked to use a lot of
chromaticism, and rhythmically elaborate choruses with alto, bass, or tenor
leads are common (Steve Wolfgang likes to refer to these choruses as “Every man
for himself”).

Such tunes are peppy and fun to sing.  Sometimes, as with “Victory in Jesus”, bouncy
and joyous is appropriate.  At other
times, as with “He Bore It All”, it is not. 
Maybe it’s just me, but I tend to believe that hymns about the suffering
and death of the Lord Jesus should not sound like they escaped from the
circus.  I am quite confident in the
ability of time to separate the wheat from the chaff here.

Examples:  “Thus
Remember Me”, “Victory in Jesus”, “Our God, He Is Alive”

**Contemporary
Hymns.** Basically, this includes the
mass of hymns written from 1960 to 2000. 
There may be another genre forming in this century, but it remains to be
seen how many of the hymns from Sumphonia authors, New English Renaissance
writers, and so on will be received by Christians.

Just as the dominant model for Southern-gospel hymns is the
quartet singing over the radio, the dominant model for contemporary hymns and
praise songs is a lead singer with a backup band.  This has important consequences for their
structure and style.  If you want a song
to be singable by a group, there are certain characteristics that it has to
have:  regular rhythm and meter,
intuitive melody, natural chord progressions, and the like.

However, because most contemporary praise songs aren’t
written to be sung by a group, they don’t necessarily have these characteristics.  For instance, contemporary lyrics are much
less formally structured than lyrics from any earlier era.  They often have irregular lyrical meter and rhythm,
which leads to musical rhythms that are complex and difficult for
congregations to follow.

Lyrically, their content is frequently Scripture-poor.  I think this has happened for two
reasons.  First, contemporary praise
songs are much more likely to have lyrics written by a music minister or
similar, instead of a preacher, pastor, or other religious teacher (note, by
the way, that a lot of female hymnists from the 19th century, though
not allowed to be ministers, were ministers’ wives and daughters).  This is unusual.  Even the switch-hitters of the 19th
century like Lowry and Bliss were preachers first and composers second.  In the latter part of the 20th
century, though, most lyrics were written by people more familiar with a guitar
than a Bible, and it shows.

Second, with the dawn of the postmodern era, our cultural
focus has shifted from objective truth to subjective experience.  This certainly colors our attitudes toward
worship, with the result that understanding what we sing has become secondary
to feeling.  In this environment, there’s
not much pressure to add Scriptural depth. 
Instead, it’s been replaced with extreme repetition of a catchy phrase,
a technique borrowed from Top-40 pop music.

Musically, many contemporary songs are a mess.  Composers to this point generally followed
the common-practice rules of harmony, and adherence to these rules makes hymn
tunes easier to sing.  However, an awful lot of contemporary tunesmiths don’t have any interest in common-practice
harmony—another result of the emphasis on “my truth” rather than “the
truth”.

The results are distinctive. 
It’s common for contemporary praise songs to have beautiful, gorgeous
melodies paired with dire arrangements. 
In the praise songs' original setting, this is OK.  A professional or semipro band can figure
things out as it goes along.

However, congregationally suitable hymn tunes work because
the harmony parts are intuitive and easy to sing.  Bad part writing introduces needless
complexity, which frustrates the congregation. 
It’s no coincidence that the rise of contemporary songs was followed in
some congregations by the rise of praise teams. 
The music was simply too difficult for congregations to thrash through
on their own.

Of course, there are a number of shining exceptions to all
of the above.  There certainly were and
are contemporary writers and composers who know what they’re doing.  They’ve written many valuable sacred songs.

Generally, any hymn with strong content and pretty music
that’s not too hard to sing will work congregationally, and plenty of contemporary
songs clear that bar.  These hymns
succeed because they embody the strengths of contemporary hymnody—beautiful
melody, interesting rhythm, and emotionally powerful content—while avoiding or mitigating its
weaknesses.  150 years from now,
contemporary songs like this will be the ones that survive.

Examples:  “We Will
Glorify”, “The Battle Belongs to the Lord”, “Come Share the Lord”
