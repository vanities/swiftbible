As I described last week, for a variety of reasons, I had
decided that I needed an REB for use as a change-of-perspective reader.  As far as I know, the only non-hardback REB
without the Apocrypha that’s currently in print is this one.  Sadly, I had long since run out of money to
spend on myself, so I was resigned to waiting until my birthday in September to
acquire one.

However, I mentioned my interest in the Bible to my father
on the phone one night, he was intrigued by my description, and he offered to
buy one for me if I ordered him one at the same time.  I promptly took him up on his offer, and so
the Standard Text REB passed into my hands much more quickly than I had
expected.

**VITAL STATISTICS**

Translation:  Revised
English Bible (1989)

Cover:  Polyurethane
(“Imitation Leather”)

Binding:  Sewn

Trim Size:  8 ¼”
x  5 ¼”

Text Block Width:  1”

Weight:  1 pound, 10
ounces

Column:  Double

Layout:  Paragraphed

Text Size:  8

Words of Christ: 
Black

Cross-References:  No

Ribbon Marker:  1

**SIZE**

In size, I consider the REB to be in the just-a-Bible
category.  This is a Bible that has about
thinline length and width, but thickness considerably greater than a
thinline.  As one would expect from a
just-a-Bible Bible, the REB went 1 for 2 on the glove-box test.  It fit fine in the Corolla glove box, but it
emerged from the Rondo glove box with the cover squished up.  Like me trying to fit into the next jeans
size down, it’s just a little too thick to manage.

Really, though, this is unsurprising.  The Standard Text wasn’t designed or marketed
as a compact or thinline Bible.  Its
dimensions don’t scream, “Take me on vacation!” 
Though it belongs in the hand-sized category, it’s much more like a
small full-sized Bible than it is like an upsized compact.  If the REB was designed to fit anywhere, it
was designed to fit in a pew rack, and pew Bibles are moderate in nature:  moderate dimensions, moderate text size,
moderate paper opacity, etc.  Nowhere,
except maybe in the tight gutter, is there a sense that its design is driven by
pressure to make the Bible smaller.

Having said that, the REB is certainly better suited to
travel than an Allan Longprimer would be. 
It weighs slightly more than a pound and a half, so you don’t need a
wheelbarrow to transport it.  In hand, it
isn’t particularly obtrusive.  Its sober
gray cover doesn’t stand out like the acres of gleaming black leather
surrounding a Foundation SCR.  It is
understated rather than intimidating, and I never felt ridiculous carrying it
any of the places I went.

**BINDING**

For whatever reason (lack of interest?), Cambridge ceased
publishing a French Morocco version of the REB some years ago.  At the moment, the setting is only available
in hardback and poly.  Many Bible buffs
look at poly Bibles about the same way that Pharisees used to regard lepers,
but this particular poly cover may merit more than send-directly-to-rebinder
consideration.

First, unlike most poly Bibles, it isn’t trendy and
hideous.  I don’t know why, but most
publishers can’t seem to resist the temptation to do outlandish things to their
poly-Bible covers.  Either they’re
two-tone blue and green with prominent stitching, they’ve got some design
splashed across half the cover, or both. 
Presumably, the marketers know what they’re doing there, but what they
aren’t doing is appealing to me.  I
prefer classic, conservative design, and I tend to suspect that today’s fad
will be tomorrow’s polyester leisure suit.

Thankfully, the good people of Cambridge Bibles also tend
toward the conservative.  With few exceptions
(imitation-leather Pitt Minion ESV’s, anyone?), their products adhere to
traditional standards of good taste, and the Standard Text REB does not
disappoint here.  The poly cover is a
sober dark gray, it has no stitching at all, and design work on the front cover
is limited to a line around the perimeter, a moderately sized gold title
reading, “The Revised English Bible”, and a blind-stamped medallion of the REB
logo, which appears to be quartered grapevine and olive branches.  It’s a classy look.

The spine is similarly restrained, with blind-stamped bands
and “Revised English Bible” and “cambridge”
in gold.  Unlike some publishers,
Cambridge apparently does not feel the need to fill the blank space on the
spine with multiple version abbreviations, logos, and so forth.  Less is more!

Also, the construction of the binding appears to be of high
quality.  Even though the cover isn’t as
thick, and therefore stiff, as Crossway’s poly covers (and I don’t mind the
latter; the thickness tends to help them survive), it still feels durable.  Because the cover is of a piece, there aren’t
any seams in it where it feels like the stitching might fail.  Unlike most poly Bibles, the endpapers are
leatherette rather than paper, so they promise added durability as well.  Finally, in a clever touch, the Bible came
packaged in a slipcase rather than a (quickly discarded) clamshell box, so that
I can use the packaging to store and protect it when it’s not in use.

I had originally bought the imitation-leather REB with the
intention of having it rebound.  It has
the requisite gilt edging and rounded corners necessary to make such a project
attractive.  However, now that I’ve seen
it, handled it, and used it, I’ve changed my mind.  I may have it rebound someday if the poly cover
wears out (unlikely if I’m only using it for a reader), but in the meantime,
I’m as happy with the poly as I would be with goatskin.  In particular, I enjoy the blind-stamped REB
medallion, and I shudder at the cost of paying someone else to re-create it,
which may not be legal anyway.

**TEXT BLOCK**

I’m equally pleased with the text block of the REB.  Like any Bible that I’m going to purchase,
the text block is sewn.  Peel back the
headband, and the signatures are obvious. 
However, it doesn’t have the problem that I’ve encountered in every
other sewn poly Bible that I’ve handled (and almost all of the genuine-leather
editions too).  It doesn’t have any
cockling.  None.

This is a surprisingly surreal experience.  When I open a Bible, I’m used to it sounding
like half a dozen people crackling their knuckles.  The Standard Text opens in complete silence,
without a whisper.  The absence of sound
is so striking that I notice and appreciate it every time I use the Bible.

I don’t think this is fortunate accident.  I think it’s the result of Cambridge’s
printer, Williams Clowes Beccles, taking good care of the text blocks during
the printing process.  Cockling is a
mysterious topic, but as far as I can tell, it’s the result of text blocks a)
being printed cross-grain, and b) being stored in a humid environment between
the time when they are bound and the time when they are trimmed and cased.  The humidity makes the paper in the blocks
expand lengthwise, but that expansion isn’t possible where the block is stitched
together.  The block cockles there
instead, with the unmistakable pattern of bumps and crackling that we’ve all
come to know and love.  The REB is
printed cross-grain, but cockling is absent nonetheless.  Williams Clowes Beccles must have gone to
great lengths to control humidity in its production facilities and its
warehouses to make this absence possible.

Amusingly enough, their care had an unintended side
effect.  My office in the church building
is set at garden level, sunk about four feet in the ground.  It has cinder-block walls and is generally a
fairly damp environment.  I kept the REB
in my office for a few weeks, which exposed the text block to the humidity it
had not previously encountered. 
Predictably, the paper expanded with the grain, which means that now
when I look at the page edges opposite the spine, I see some waviness in the
gilding.  However, the Bible opens as
silently as it ever did, so I’m not too upset about the waves.  Because the Bible is printed cross-grain, it
doesn’t have any issues with curling either.

I also enjoy this text block because of the thickness and
opacity of its paper.  Other publishers
would have produced the REB as a thinline. 
It only has 1054 pages, so if you print it on 19-gsm paper, it would be
much less than an inch thick.  Of course,
if you opened it to Genesis, you would probably be able to read Revelation from
the right-hand pages, but that doesn’t seem to bother most Bible publishers
very much.

By contrast, the REB is printed on thicker, heavier weight
paper, I’m guessing paper with a gsm in the high twenties.  This makes the text block thicker (and an
inch-thick text block at 1054 pp. is a thick text block), but it cuts the
ghosting way down.  The REB isn’t a
thinline Bible.  It’s just a Bible, and I
think that there’s a place for just-a-Bible text Bibles in the market.

I only have two objections to the form of the REB.  The first is the gutter.  I don’t know why, when they did so many other
things right, Cambridge printed the Bible with a super-tight gutter, but they
did.  Especially in prose sections toward
the middle of the Bible, I have to press down on the gutter to read the
rightmost words on the left-hand page and the leftmost words on the page opposite.  I suspect this has something to do with the
design constraints imposed on a pew Bible, but Cambridge would have been better
served, I think, to lay the Bible out with smaller type and a more generous
gutter.

Second, there appear to be some ink-absorbency issues.  When I was breaking the Bible in, after
drawing my fingertip down the gutter repeatedly, it came away ink-stained.  That’s not a huge problem, but it is an
annoyance nonetheless, and may lead to blackened fingertips after extended
reading.

**VALUE**

The REB is a poly-cover Bible that retails for $80.  That ain’t a good start.  However, in fairness to Cambridge, it’s a
really nice poly-cover Bible.  Those $15
gift Bibles on the endcaps of Christian bookstores don’t have nearly the fit
and finish that the Standard Text does. 
Also, so far as I know, this is the only in-print edition of the REB
with gilded page edges, which has some rarity value.  However, $80 is $80 (even if it’s more like
$55 on the Internet these days).  The
value here isn’t terrible, but it isn’t wonderful either.

**CONCLUSION**

I don’t love the physical form of the REB, but I like
it.  I’d like it more if it had a
genuine-cowhide cover for $80 retail, but I still like it.  It scores 7.5/10 on physical form.
