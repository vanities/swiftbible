I know that some of you may feel a little bit unsettled by
our coming quarter-long emphasis on note-taking during sermons.  After all, it’s a break from routine, and
those always up our stress level a little bit. 
For what it’s worth, I’m a little bit unsettled by it too.  It’s taking me out of one of my
longest-standing routines—that of preaching a year-in-review sermon on the
anniversary of my arrival in Joliet. 
It’s simply not a suitable subject for an expository sermon, so I can’t
put it in its usual place on April 12th.

Barring some unforeseen disaster, though, April
12th will mark the ninth anniversary of my first sermon here.  I don’t believe in jinxes, so I’m going to
review the year a little bit early. 
Let’s spend this morning, then, in the 2015 edition of looking forward
and looking back.



**Greatness in the Kingdom**

Such a sermon, though, is inevitably going to be a little
bit self-focused, and on several different levels, Christianity isn’t about us
at all.  Before we hold up a mirror for
ourselves, then, let’s consider what our Lord has to say about greatness in His
kingdom.  This story begins with **JESUS PREDICTING HIS DEATH.** Let’s read here from Mark 10:32-34.  In many ways, this is an eerie scene.  By this point in the story, Jesus has already
predicted His death to His apostles twice. 
He has identified his enemies as the chief priests and scribes, which
implies that the location of this dread event is going to be Jerusalem.  And yet, here Jesus is out in front,
practically hurrying on to Jerusalem. 
None of the apostles have really figured out what is going to happen
yet, but they’re still creeped out by the disconnect between Jesus’ words and
His actions.

Jesus detects their apprehension, so He pulls them aside and
makes it worse.  This time, He goes into
even greater detail about His arrest, mocking, flogging, and murder, and if any
doubt remains, He identifies Jerusalem as the place where it will happen.  Even worse, He repeats His prediction that
after His death, He is going to rise from the dead.  We’ve become accustomed to the thought of
Jesus’ resurrection, but the disciples at this point certainly have not.  What’s this going to be like?  Is Jesus going to come back as some kind of
undead monster?  They still don’t
understand, but they definitely don’t like it.

It’s in this somber context that James and John make **A REQUEST.**It appears in Mark 10:35-37. 
Honestly, friends, this is utterly bizarre.  We know from other passages that James and
John aren’t expecting Jesus to rise from the dead any more than anyone else
is.  Why in the world, then, are they
asking for goodies when He comes into His glory?  So far as I can see, the only explanation is
that they have concluded that Jesus doesn’t really mean what He’s saying.  After all, Jesus has spent a lot of time
teaching in parables and saying other things that have a different meaning than
the obvious one.  James and John were
there, for instance, when Jesus said that they had to eat His flesh and drink
His blood to have life.  Clearly, that
wasn’t going to literally happen, so James and John conclude that Jesus’ death
isn’t going to literally happen either.  This
fits neatly with their yearning for earthly glory anyway, so they go with what
they want.

Today, we can fall into the same trap.  We can listen to Jesus not for what He’s
actually saying to us, but for what we want to hear.  This is most obvious with all the
prosperity-gospel preachers out there, who somehow get away with claiming that
the point of the Bible is that God wants us to have earthly riches and earthly
happiness.  To the extent that they’re
studying and explaining the Scripture at all, they’re cherry-picking the 1
percent that lines up with their beliefs and ignoring the 99 percent that
doesn’t.

How about us, though? 
We may be more subtle about it than the health-and-wealth folks, but
it’s easy for us too to treat the Bible like a buffet.  Without even really thinking about it, we can
go through the word, pick out the pleasant parts, and ignore the hard
sayings.  We can twist the Scripture
until it does nothing more than confirm that we’re already doing what we’re
supposed to be doing.  In fact, brethren,
if we’re comfortable with the word, that’s exactly what we must be doing.  The standard for godliness is Christ, and
none of us measure up.  If we think we do
measure up, we must not be paying attention.

The next portion of this story details **THE LORD’S REPLY.** Let’s read
here from Mark 10:38-41.  If any doubt
remains in our minds about the brothers’ cluelessness, His answer should dispel
it.  He tells them plainly that they
don’t know what they’re talking about. 
Given that, do they really want to share His fate by drinking His cup
and being baptized with His baptism? 
This is not a reference to the Lord’s Supper nor to the plan of
salvation.  Instead, Jesus is describing
His suffering and death in these terms.

This shouldn’t have been a hard intuitive leap for James and
John to make.  After all, Jesus has just
finished telling them that He is going to Jerusalem to be killed.  However, they miss the point once again.  They eagerly affirm that yes, they want to
drink His cup and be baptized with His baptism, even though they still have no
idea what he’s talking about.  Now can
they sit at His right and left hands? 
Jesus sardonically tells them that they can have His cup and His baptism,
all right, but their preferment is not for Him to decide.

Now, the story takes a turn for the absurd.  James and John have just signed on for a
martyr’s death, which for James isn’t very far in the future, and all the other
apostles are upset because they think the brothers have stolen a march on them
somehow!  If James and John weren’t
listening, neither was anyone else.  By
their attitude, they clearly demonstrate that they have no idea what
discipleship is about.

This is a common problem for modern disciples as well.  I can’t tell you how many times I’ve seen or
heard someone justify leaving a church, or leaving the church, period, by
saying, “It just wasn’t meeting my needs.” 
Brethren, our connection to this church isn’t about whether the others here
can meet our needs.  It’s about whether
we can meet theirs.  If that’s not our
attitude, if our association with the Joliet church is all about that capital
“I”, we might be going through the motions of Christianity, but we’ve missed
the point.

Jesus sets everybody straight with a few well chosen words
about **SERVICE.** They appear in Mark 10:42-45.  He begins here by explaining the way the
world worked back then, and indeed the way that it still works.  According to worldly thinking, if you’re in a
position of power, you should use it to dominate others.  You prove that you’re important by having a
whole bunch of lackeys and underlings scurrying to do your bidding.  You’re not there for them; they’re there for
you.

By contrast, greatness and leadership in the kingdom of God
are shown by humility and service.  We
don’t make our mark in the church by getting our own way and making others do
what we want.  Instead, we reveal the quality
of our discipleship by our willingness to serve.  Whenever there’s a need, we step up, even if
service is going to be inglorious, difficult, or inconvenient.  The most impressive Christians are the ones
who place the fewest limits on what they will do for others.  Our example in this, of course, is Jesus
Himself.  Throughout His life, He did
nothing but serve others, from feeding the 5000 to washing the feet of His
disciples.  Ultimately, He came not only
to serve but to give His own life for the benefit of everyone else.

Before the Lord’s Supper, let’s consider all of these things.  First of all, let’s remember Jesus, the
suffering Servant, who came to earth knowing full well that He was going to be
crucified, but came anyway, for our sakes. 
Second, let’s consider ourselves. 
Who are we in the church?  Are we
here to get everybody else to take care of us? 
Are we here to boss around others and get our way?  Or are we here to serve others, and in so
doing, demonstrate our likeness to our Master? 
Let’s consider this as we partake.

**Looking Back**

As always, our appraisal of the state of the congregation is
going to begin by looking back on what we’ve done in the past year.  In many ways, it’s been **A QUIET YEAR,** much like the period of quiet we see the church
experiencing in Acts 9:31.  This was
something our brethren in the first century needed after enduring the
persecution of Saul, and I think it’s something we needed after the challenges
we’ve faced in the past several years.  A
couple of years back, Josh preached a sermon about the congregation’s need to
heal, and I think that healing process has largely been completed.

Now that it has, we’re basically in a good position.  It is true that we still don’t have elders,
and we’re not likely to get elders unless something dramatic happens, but in
most other metrics of church health, we’re performing well.  We’re a diverse congregation, both in terms
of racial makeup and in terms of age, and both of those things are a
strength.  Among other things, they help
make us attractive to families that move into the area.  We’re having some modest success with
evangelism.  I don’t get the sense that
there is any serious discontent bubbling away under the surface.  Our worship services are well conducted and
effective.

This peace, tranquility, and order sets the stage for **GROWTH TOGETHER.** According to Paul in Ephesians 4:15-16, the
natural state of a church is much like the natural state of a child.  Both of them are supposed to be growing, and
if that growth isn’t happening, something is wrong.  Notice what Paul says here about where church
growth is supposed to begin.  It starts
with one of my 500 favorite passages—speaking the truth in love.  Only when the truth is taught with love for
one another can the church flourish.  As
a congregation, we’ve been committed to study of the Bible and to the practice
of the Bible, and the more time we spend, both together and individually, with
the word of God, the more it will cause us to grow.

We’ve already talked about some of the causes for the
numerical growth we’ve been experiencing. 
It hasn’t been dramatic, but it’s been there.  Equally important, I’ve seen signs of
spiritual growth too.  Spiritual growth
is often the watchword of the dying church. 
Even as numbers dwindle, the remaining members insist that they’ve been
growing spiritually, even though they don’t have any evidence to back up their
assertion.

However, that’s not the way that it’s been here.  Our growth has been evident in the
willingness of members to do things they wouldn’t have done a few years
back.  It’s evident in Erick Detloff
stepping up and presenting a whole section of a sermon in my absence.  It’s evident in Derek agreeing to help with
teaching the auditorium Bible class. 
It’s evident in our increased contribution.  It’s evident in those tiny battles that I see
a number of people fighting, that continual struggle to be here more often to
hear more of God’s word.  Brethren, that
doesn’t merely give me satisfaction today. 
It gives me hope for our future.

In particular, I want to talk about the growth that some of
us have been undergoing as **LEADERS.** Paul gives leaders some timeless advice
in Romans 12:6-8.  Leaders need to be
diligent.  I can remember looking at this
passage back when I was in college and thinking that it was something of a non
sequitur.  Isn’t leadership more about
vision and charisma than diligence? 
Leadership is exciting, but diligence is boring.

Then, I actually started trying to do some spiritual
leading, and I quickly realized how important diligence was.  Indeed, it is probably the most important
trait of the successful leader. 
Why?  Because leadership is
hard.  Leadership is discouraging.  Leadership almost never works out the way
that we want it to.  If all we’ve got
going for us is vision and charisma, when we hit those tough times, we’re going
to give up.  Diligence, though—boring old
diligence might not be showy or flashy, but it’s going to keep plodding along
out in front, leading others to God.  Vision
and charisma are starters, but diligence is a finisher.

In our currently elder-less congregation, diligence is all
the more important.  None of us are
church officers.  I’m really the only one
with a role that the Scripture specifically defines.  If we give up and quietly quit pushing
forward where we’ve been pushing forward, nobody much is going to notice.  In circumstances like these, it’s easy for us
to quit, which is exactly why we can’t. 
Even a church without elders needs Christians who will spur others on to
godliness.  If it doesn’t have Christians
like that, it’s going to stagnate and die. 
So, for all of you who are leading, whether during our assemblies, our
classes downstairs, or less obvious efforts outside these walls, I have this to
say—keep leading!  Don’t get
discouraged.  Don’t give up.  Keep promoting what you know is right.  As Paul writes in Galatians 6, if we don’t
grow weary, we will reap.

Of course, if we want our leaders to keep leading, we as **FOLLOWERS** have to follow.  Paul tackles this topic in 1 Thessalonians
5:12-13.  It’s unclear from the context
whether Paul is speaking to actual elders or simply generic leaders, but his words
apply in either case.  Whenever we see
someone trying to lead us to do good, we need to respect that and support
it.  Kind words here are helpful but not
enough.  If someone is encouraging us to
give to the poor, we need to give to the poor. 
If others are going to all the time and effort to put together a VBS for
our kids, we need to make sure our kids are there.  If we’re in the teenager or young-adult age
bracket, we need to come to the Joliet Youth Day.

This is important for a couple of different reasons.  First, it’s good for our leaders.  As we’ve already seen, it’s difficult enough
for a Christian to climb out of the trenches and yell “Follow me!”  When nobody actually does, it’s incredibly
dispiriting, and our leaders may well give up entirely.  Second, and even more importantly, we need to
follow because they’re leading us on to God. 
It’s easy for all of us to drift slowly back into worldliness.  Discipleship requires effort, but for some
reason, following Jesus is easier when somebody gives us a concrete idea for a
way to go about doing that.  Let’s be
people, then, who are there for the singings and the special studies and the
work days.  That’s what we need to remain
a growing church.

**Looking Forward**

Now that we’ve considered some past and continuing matters,
let’s look forward to some changes.  The
first of these concerns our reminder to **GREET
VISITORS.** From now on, instead of
the verse in Ephesians, I’m going to be beginning and ending my slide
presentations with Colossians 4:5.  This
isn’t a big change, but I think it’s an important one to make.  During our prolonged difficulties with
various projectors and computers, I think we all got out of the habit of
looking for the reminder slide and remembering what we were supposed to do
about it.  Second, because it’s been the
same reminder slide for at least a year now, we’ve gotten used to it and no
longer remember that it has significance when it’s up.  It’s time for a change.

Let’s also pay attention, though, not merely to the verse’s
presence on the screen, but to its significance.  Colossians 4:5 uses much of the same language
as our old Ephesians 5 reminder slide. 
The two books are, after all, parallel in structure and message.  However, it’s even more explicit.  One of the most important ways that we need
to redeem the time is by walking in wisdom toward outsiders.

Let’s face it, friends: 
as used as we are to our congregation, coming here is an intimidating
experience to someone who isn’t used to our traditions.  If nothing else, where in normal American
society do you ever encounter a large group of people that sings without
musical accompaniment and doesn’t sound bad doing it?  We’re way out of the cultural mainstream on
that one.  Because the experience of our
worship isn’t a comfortable one, we need to do everything we can to make
visitors feel comfortable.  We need to
acknowledge them.  We need to be real,
genuine, welcoming, and loving.  If we
aren’t, they won’t come back.

Second, and indeed for the rest of the sermon, I want to
focus on various aspects of our Bible classes because I think there’s a lot of
room for improvement there.  In this
regard, obviously, the first thing we ought to do is to **ATTEND BIBLE CLASSES.** Peter
supplies us with the reason in 1 Peter 2:2. 
Here, Peter adopts a comparison we looked at earlier:  the ways in which spiritual growth is like
the growth of a child.  Every one of us
who has been the parent of a newborn knows how babies long for milk.  They literally will not shut up unless you
feed them.  They’re hungry, they need
that milk to grow, so they howl for it.

The word of God is every bit as essential for a Christian as
milk is for a baby.  Without it, we won’t
grow either.  Sadly, even though our need
for the word is as great, our urgency in seeking it often is not.  The alarm goes off at 7 Sunday morning, but
we decide we’d rather sleep in until 8:30. 
We come home from work on Wednesday afternoon already worn out, so we
spend Wednesday evening in the recliner instead of in the pew.  Believe me; I understand that.  My own family life is plenty hectic these
days.  However, that doesn’t make
skipping Bible class any less of a mistake. 
Sleeping in on Sunday mornings isn’t going to help us inherit eternal
life, and watching TV on Wednesday evenings definitely won’t!  God does not give mulligans, brethren.  We’ve got one chance to glorify Him in this
life.  Let’s make the most of it.

Second, we should make sure that we take advantage of our **CHILDREN’S BIBLE CLASSES.** Remember the words of Solomon in Proverbs
22:6.  I have to say, friends—sometimes
the increasing wickedness of the world around us makes me wonder how I’m ever
going to persuade my kids to follow Jesus. 
Here’s the problem:  I often think
that schools today are like the schools I grew up attending, that the children
in those schools have pretty much the same moral grounding as the children I
grew up with.

Those assumptions are not true, and I had a couple of
conversations in Texas that underscored the point.  A teacher friend of mine told me that she’s
seen fifth-graders pulling up pornography on their cell phones in the school
cafeteria.  A preacher I know mentioned
counseling a 12-year-old girl who was raised in the church about her
pornography addiction.  It is a nasty bad
place out there, and it’s getting worse.

If that’s the environment that our children are going to
face, we have to do everything we can to get them prepared to face it.  That includes all the teaching we can do by
instruction and example on our own, and it also includes getting all the help
we can here at the church building. 
Quite frankly, if we’re not making sure that our children are here for
every class just as soon as they’re old enough to attend, we’re taking a
terrible risk with their souls.  When the
danger is so extreme, they need all the help we can give them.

Finally, let’s give some thought to **TEACHING BIBLE CLASSES.** We
need to show the spirit that Isaiah exemplifies in Isaiah 6:8.  This is one of the problems that comes with
success.  Unlike those congregations that
have an average age of 75, we are blessed with scads of children.  However, those children come not only in
every color, shape, and size, but in a number of different developmental stages
as well.  We need a number of different
children’s classes, and we need a number of teachers for those classes.

I understand the temptation to say, “I signed up for my one
quarter a year, so somebody else can do the rest.”  Is that really fair, though?  Take, for instance, my own family’s situation.  Zoë and Marky are generally in different
classes, so between the two of them, they require eight quarters’ worth of
teachers.  If Lauren and I sign up for a
quarter apiece, we want eight, but we’re only willing to contribute two.  Does that seem right, especially when we have
members who don’t have young children at all but who teach anyway?  There are lots of ways to help.  Brette Dickison, for instance, recently took
her first shot at being a teachers’ helper. 
However, we do need to be doing everything we honestly can, not looking
to others to help us.
