A few days ago, I was reading [a particularly dreadful article](http://www.esquire.com/news-politics/a48031/the-falling-man-tom-junod/) about
9-11.  It concerned the Falling Man, the
unfortunate subject of the most iconic of the 9-11 jumper photos.  In the immediate aftermath of the tower
bombings, a reporter tried to uncover the identity of the Falling Man, but the
family of the victim he settled on angrily denied that he had the right
man.  In part, their denial was
religiously motivated.  They’d had all
kinds of people telling them that their brother/husband/father had condemned
himself to hell by committing suicide, so they dealt with the fallout by
denying that he was a jumper in the first place.

Stories about 9-11 are unavoidably awful, but I think this
one points to a larger issue.  There were
probably hundreds of jumpers before the towers fell, but even such a terrible
toll is only a small percentage of the annual total of suicides in the
U.S.  Something like 40,000 people in
this country kill themselves every year, mostly with firearms.  In fact, suicides outnumber homicides by about 4 to 1.



Suicide is always a terrible tragedy for those who have been
left behind, but the emotional toll is only magnified by the event’s spiritual
overtones.  Many, perhaps most,
self-described Christians believe that suicide is “the unforgivable sin”, that
those who kill themselves lose not only their lives but their souls as a
result.  In consequence, the families and
friends of suicides often must grapple with the conviction that their loved one
is now suffering in torment.

I’m not one to shy away from speaking where the Bible
speaks, no matter the consequences, but I’ve always struggled with this harsh
view of suicide, precisely because the Bible doesn’t speak clearly on the
subject.  Augustine and various medieval
Catholic theologians explicitly condemn suicide (which explains the influential
Catholic perspective), as does the Qur’an, but the Scriptures themselves don’t
contain such an explicit condemnation.

Many argue that suicide is a sin because of the Sixth
Commandment.  Thou shalt not kill,
suicide is killing yourself, so suicide is a sin.  Q.E.D. 
The problem is, though, that we recognize that the commandment is
actually about murder, not killing.  It’s
not sin *per se* for a soldier to kill
an enemy combatant, for instance.

Suicide can certainly be described as self-murder, but
whatever the rhetorical force of the description, it’s nothing more than the
foundation of a circular argument.  Nor
does the description appear to be particularly apt.  In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus identifies
the problem with murder as hatred, and suicides don’t think of themselves the
way that murderers think of their victims. 
Shame, guilt, and suffering all can provoke suicide, but those aren’t
the same thing as the fundamental refusal to love one’s neighbor that underlies
murder.

I do not mean to argue that suicide is always acceptable in
every instance.  The man who arrogantly
kills himself to make others suffer has clearly died without love in his heart,
and a moment’s thought would doubtless reveal other similar scenarios.  I’m also still not happy with euthanasia and
medically assisted suicide.  As with
legalized abortion, such laws exploit the most vulnerable members of
society.  When you’re sick of taking care
of Great-Aunt Ethel, you give her a few nudges until she agrees to go gentle
into that good night.

Likewise, I do not at all mean to promote or encourage
suicide.  Sin or not, it is undeniably
tragic.  Along with the apostle Paul, I
cry out, “Do not harm yourself, for we are all here!”

Seriously.  If you’re
reading this, and you’re thinking about taking your own life, don’t.  Even if you can see nothing good about
yourself, you are still valuable and precious in the sight of God.  Get help. 
Tell somebody you need help. 
Don’t suffer alone.

However, I think that blanket statements that suicide is
wrong are misguided.  There’s nothing in
the Bible that proves that it’s a sin, and it’s not my place to make bold
declarations on the subject when God hasn’t. 
In fact, I am content to leave all those who take their own lives in His
hands.  In His wisdom, He is able to
assess the mental state of the severely depressed.  He can properly appreciate the suffering of
those who jumped from the Twin Towers to escape the flames.  These things are beyond me, and I believe
they’re beyond any human.

Why not leave such judgments to the One who can make them?
