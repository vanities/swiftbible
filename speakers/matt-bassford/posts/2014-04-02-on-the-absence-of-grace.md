*For who regards you as superior?
What do you have that you did not receive? And if you did receive it, why do
you boast as if you had not received it?*

-- 1
Corinthians 4:7 (NASB)

In a 2011
commentary, David Bentley Hart describes his objection to the philosophy of Ayn
Rand as follows:

She had no concept of grace, even of the
ordinary kind: the grace of an existence we do not give ourselves, of natural
powers with which we could never have endowed ourselves, and of all those other
persons on whom even the strongest among us are dependent. She lacked any
ennobling sense that what lies most deeply within us also comes from impossibly
far beyond us, as an unmerited gift.



Rand regarded
this as a virtue.  Bentley Hart regards
it as a tragedy.  And yet, it is a
tragedy to which God’s people are peculiarly prone.  We struggle to acknowledge the presence of
blessing in our lives and its absence in the lives of others.

We look at
others whom we consider less righteous, and the question springs unbidden to
our minds, if not our lips, “Why don’t they just?”  Why don’t they just get and hold down steady
jobs and become productive members of society? 
Like us.  Why don’t they just get
that sex problem or that alcohol problem out of their lives?  Like us. 
Why don’t they just come to church every time the doors are opened?  Like us. 
Why don’t they just take a more active role in doing the Lord’s
work?  Like us.

These are
rhetorical questions.  We do not believe
that there is any kind of answer to them. 
We presume that because “they” are like us, they should behave like
us.  When they do not, it is evidence not
merely of failure, but of depravity.  They
had a free, unfettered choice between God and the devil, and they chose the
devil.  Because they are basically bad
people.

Of course,
we quickly climb down from our high horses once we start asking the much less
popular question, “Why don’t I just?” 
Why don’t I just do more to help the poor and needy?  Like Jesus. 
Why don’t I just talk to my neighbor about God?  Like Jesus. 
Why don’t I just show more patience to my children?  Like Jesus.

To us, at
least, these are not rhetorical questions. 
We want to do right—really we do!—but our circumstances and our emotions
and all kinds of other stuff get in the way. 
We’re not depraved people; we simply. . . fail sometimes.  We don’t deny the failure, but we do ask for
understanding.  In other words, we depend
on the same gracious regard we deny others.

Instead, we
would do better to acknowledge that if we fail because some things are
difficult for us, others fail because different things are difficult for
them.  It is true that I am gainfully
employed, not a sex addict or an alcoholic, a regular churchgoer, and involved
in the work of the Lord.  It is equally
true that I was raised by two phenomenal parents who gave me all the financial,
emotional, intellectual, and spiritual support I needed.  I made my own choices, as do we all, but the
way was certainly smoothed for me to make the right choices.

I don’t know
where I would be without those advantages, but it would almost certainly be
someplace different, perhaps even fighting on a daily basis the battles that
seem so trivial to me now.  My failures
in those battles would still be my own (as my failures today are), but they
would be understandable, and if I can understand why I might fail, I should
also understand why others do fail.

Ultimately,
all of us rely on the understanding of God for the grace that we need.  He gives no help to angels.  They did have an unfettered choice between
good and evil, and some chose evil, irredeemably, past all hope of salvation.



However, He
does give help to us.  Jesus, who put on
the flesh that we wear, faced our trials and overcame them all, but He knows
too why we fail where He succeeded.  He
gets it, and because doing right is hard for us, and failure is easy, He is
willing to show mercy.  When we so
continually drink from the spring of that knowledge and that mercy, we dare not
deny understanding and mercy to others.
