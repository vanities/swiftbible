Generally, I regard paragraphed status updates on Facebook
with a certain amount of unease.  They
mean that one of my FB friends feels strongly enough about something to spend
time crafting an expression of his views to the world.  For people who don’t write as easily as I do,
this indicates strong feelings indeed! 
Passion isn’t a bad thing, but it has a way of getting misinterpreted online
and causing arguments, especially strife among brothers.



Over the past week, I’ve seen lots of paragraphed Facebook
posts about allowing Syrian refugees into the US.  In fact, I can’t ever remember an issue that
left my online friends, most of whom are members of various churches of Christ,
so evenly divided.  Some good, sincere
Christians believe that admitting refugees is the right and godly thing to do;
other good, sincere Christians think it would be a disaster.  Both sides are obviously emotionally involved
in the discussion.

It won’t surprise my regular readers to learn that I fall
into the admit-the-refugees camp. 
Philosophically, I’m a big believer in, “When in doubt, be merciful,”
and this seems like an opportunity to be merciful in a doubtful situation.  I feel just as strongly about my view as
anybody else does about theirs, and where the passion of others produces a few
paragraphs, mine produces novels!

I had decided, then, that I was going to write a blog post
about how the US ought to help the Syrian refugees.  However, as I began turn the idea over in my
mind, I realized that I had a problem. 
From the beginning, I’ve dedicated my blog to the word of God:  the word of God expressed in hymns, the word
of God in the physical form of various Bibles, and the word of God expressed in
discipleship.  Despite the various
Scriptures that brethren have been tossing around, the Syrian refugee debate
doesn’t fit in any of those categories. 
My take on the matter, be it ever so sincere, didn’t belong on my blog.

The problem is that discipleship isn’t about forming an
opinion on some emotionally charged subject and debating it online.  Discipleship is about following Jesus,
learning His will and putting it into practice. 
I’ve talked before about the transformative nature of discipleship.  Jesus wants us to imitate Him so that our
character becomes more and more like His.

When it comes to the Syrian refugees, knowing and talking
about God’s will is certainly part of the process.  However, individual doing isn’t.  The whole debate is about what we think
somebody else should be doing, and whatever the somebody elses do, their
actions aren’t changing us to make us more like Christ.  If we lived in Lebanon, and there were Syrian
refugees wandering the streets of our villages, absolutely, we should be doing
what we could to help.  However, the
admission (or not) of a handful of refugees from half a world away to a country
of 300 million people is awfully abstract. 
No matter what happens, 99.9 percent of us will never lay eyes on a
Syrian refugee, much less be in a position to assist one.

Regardless of how self-righteous we might feel about our
particular position (and I have to admit that there have been times in the past
week when I felt pretty self-righteous!), we must acknowledge that such a
sensation is not the same thing as genuine spiritual growth.  It is not the same thing as becoming more
like Christ.  If anything, it fosters the
dangerous illusion that we have progressed when in fact we have stayed in the
same place.

Rather than trumpeting our compassion or our wisdom on Facebook,
let’s look for ways to actually put our compassion and our wisdom to work in
our communities.  Every one of us has
people who are hungry or homeless or inadequately clothed within 25 miles of
our doorsteps.    Let’s look for ways to
help them, indirectly if necessary, directly and in person if possible.  There’s nothing quite like looking into the
face of the person you’re helping.  That’s
a much bigger investment than even a paragraphed status update, but it’s the
only alternative that offers the hope of making a difference, not least for
us.

This can be scary, I know. 
It certainly scares me.  Following
Jesus is always scary because we have to surrender cherished parts of ourselves
in order to do it.  However, that’s
precisely what we’re called to do:  to
love not in word only, but in deed and in truth.
