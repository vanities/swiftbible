As some of you have probably noticed by now, I don’t have an
addictive personality, but I do have an obsessive one.  When I get some spiritual idea in my head, it
tends to come out in all sorts of formats: 
sermons, hymns, blog posts, and even conversations.  Recently, I’ve been meditating on an old hymn
that I discovered.  I think the hymn and
its content have such great spiritual merit that we’re going to be singing it
in a little bit here.  Before we do,
though, let’s consider the Scriptures behind “Give Me Christ, or Else I Die”.



The hymn was written by William Hammond in 1791.  Let’s start our analysis of it by considering
**THE FIRST VERSE.** As I read this verse, I can’t help but think
of the words of our Lord in John 8:23-24. 
The sentence “Give me X, or else I die” normally strikes us as a little
melodramatic.  It’s the kind of thing we
expect from the mouths of sixteen-year-old girls, who simply have to have that
car or that trip to Ireland or that whatever, or else they’re absolutely going
to *die*!

When we’re talking about Jesus, though, what is melodrama
applied to almost anything else becomes sober fact.  If we do not have Jesus, we will surely
die.  In fact, if we do not have Jesus,
we are already dead.  In the eyes of God,
the sins that we have committed are quite literally damning.  We can’t see them, but they’re eating away at
our souls like a cancer.  Jesus warns us
that He is the only cure for the disease. 
Unless we have Him, we are doomed.

The author explores some alternatives to Jesus in **THE SECOND VERSE.** He reaches the same conclusion about them
that the apostle John does in 1 John 2:17. 
There are an awful lot of things in this world that people run
after.  Some are interested in money and
material possessions.  They think that
the more stuff they can heap up, the better off they will be.  Others think it’s really important to have
others look up to them, admire them, and maybe even envy them.  They’re driven by pride. Still others are
concerned with the pleasures of the flesh. 
“Do the things that make you feel good,” they say, and they act as
though that’s all there is to life.

The first problem with the lust of the flesh, the lust of
the eye, and the pride of life is that none of them can ever satisfy us.  Thrills that we used to deeply enjoy quickly
become old hat.  The new car and the new
house become the old and boring car and house. 
If we seek the praise of others, we’ll never get enough of it that we
stop seeking it.  Trying to fill our
lives with those things is like trying to fill our stomachs with water.  Take all you want; you’re still going to be
hungry.

Second, none of these things can answer our biggest
problem.  None of these things can take
away our sins.  All they can do is add to
them.  If we want more, we must have
Jesus.

**THE THIRD VERSE** describes
the only terms on which we can have Jesus. 
This, I think, is the dark side of the parable of the pearl of great
price in Matthew 13:45-46.  Even when we
read that parable by itself, I think it makes us a little uneasy.  It certainly makes me uneasy.  Part of me thinks the merchant is an
idiot.  The guy sold his house so he
could buy a rock!

And yet, Jesus wants us to understand that we have to sell
out for Him.  He can only belong to us if
we belong to Him.  He has the right to
say to us about our most favorite sin in the whole wide world, “You can’t do
that anymore.”  If we refuse, we’ve lost
Him.  He has the right to say to us,
“Take up your cross, and follow Me.”  If
we’re not willing to suffer for His sake, we’ve lost Him that way too.  The bottom line is that Jesus is going to
demand from us everything that we have. 
Either we sell everything and buy the pearl, or we don’t, and we
don’t.

How can Jesus get away with asking us for so much?  **THE
FOURTH VERSE** explains.  As I read the
opening lines of this verse, I thought of Romans 3:19.  This comes at the end of Paul’s sweeping
condemnation of the human race in the first few chapters of Romans.  Jews, Gentiles, it doesn’t matter.  Everybody has sinned, including us, and that
means that none of us will on our own have anything to say to God on the Day of
Judgment.  Without Jesus, God would read
all of our sins into the record and ask us to explain ourselves, but we wouldn’t
be able to say a word in our defense.  We
would stand there in the guilt of our sins, and we would die for it.

The only hope that we can have is the blood of Jesus.  Because He died in our place, we can live,
and there is no other way to eternal life but through Him.  What He asks from us is nothing in comparison
to what He offers.  Before the Lord’s
Supper, then, let’s contemplate the greatness of His sacrifice.  Let’s ask ourselves too whether we’re living
like people who believe that the only two options are Jesus and death, because
unless we are living for Him, dead is what we will be.
