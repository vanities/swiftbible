*So Abram said to Lot,
"Please let there be no strife between you and me, and between my herdsmen
and your herdsmen; for we are brethren.”*

- Genesis 13:8

In context, Abram and Lot have reached a point in their
travels together where they suffer from an embarrassment of riches.  Their herds and flocks have grown so great
that there isn’t enough grazing room around any given encampment for the
animals of both men.  At this point, Abram
could have insisted on his rights as patriarch and commanded his nephew to go
back to Mesopotamia and let him have Canaan. 
Instead, the older defers to the younger.  Abram’s solution is to let Lot have his pick
of the land and take whatever is left over.



This is not the way that the world solves problems.  Whenever some conflict arises in the world of
business or politics, we never see one side graciously defer to the other and
offer to resolve the conflict in their favor. 
Instead, both sides grab for as much advantage as they can.  They insist on their rights and their way,
regardless of the consequences for the other side.  This is hardly surprising.  Indeed, in a me-first world, it is exactly
what we would expect to see.

Sadly, this me-first attitude is equally prevalent in the
church.  It is likely that every
Christian on the planet has been involved in a situation where another
Christian dealt with his brother in a self-centered, unloving way.  Sometimes, this sinful behavior doesn’t have
any major repercussions.  At other times,
it does lifelong damage to the relationship between saints who are commanded to
love one another.  It can even lead to
church splits and brotherhood division, all because a so-called disciple of
Jesus cared more about himself than he cared about somebody else.

As Christians, we should not hope to eliminate conflict from
our lives.  Whenever there’s more than
one person involved, conflict is inevitable. 
We all approach any given moment with different perspectives and different
experiences, and as a result, none of us are going to see eye-to-eye with
everyone all the time.  What’s truly
important, though, is that we handle that conflict in a way that is
other-focused and God-focused.

This is not an appeal for peace at any price.  There can be no compromise with the will of
God, and when someone opposes His revealed word, we have no choice but to
defend the line our Creator has drawn. 
Nor does it mean that the conflict must end with perfect agreement and
happiness.  After all, even Abraham
didn’t suggest that Lot remain with him and they work out the grazing situation
somehow.  Sometimes, we have to agree to
disagree.

However, what this does mean is that, particularly in a
family or a church situation, our first concern must be for the guy on the
other side.  We have to learn to see the
conflict from his perspective, ask what is best for him, and work toward
resolution with that goal in mind.  If we
practice this, we aren’t necessarily going to get “our way” all the time, for
whatever that’s worth. However, we will gain something far more precious:  peace with those closest to us.
