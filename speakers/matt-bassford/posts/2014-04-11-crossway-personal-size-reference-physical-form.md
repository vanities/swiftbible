I received
this Bible in September 2008, as a birthday present from my wife.  I promptly installed it in my office at the
church building, where it replaced a forgettable glue-bound clunker of a Bible
that had begun to shed pages.  For the
next five years, it remained inside the church building.  Its longest journeys were to the auditorium
upstairs on Sundays and Wednesdays. 
However, because I habitually do my studying at the church building, it
saw significant use every day of the week except Saturday.  I didn’t use it roughly, but I used it a lot.

After about
five years of this treatment, the Bible began to show signs of wear.  Unlike many, I don’t particularly care for
the look of a shabby Bible, particularly in the pulpit, so I retired it and
replaced it with a leatherbound side-column reference Foundation NASB that my
father had given me.  Today, it is my
beater Bible.  I take it to studies
outside the church building, and it travels with me on vacation.

**VITAL STATISTICS:**

Translation:  English Standard Version (2007)

Cover:  Polyurethane (“TruTone”)

Binding:  Sewn

Trim
Size:  7 ¾” x 5 ¼”

Text Block
Width:  1”

Weight:  1 pound, 4 ounces

Column:  Single

Layout:  Paragraphed

Type Size:  7.4

Words of Christ:  Black

Cross-References:  Yes

Ribbon
Marker:  1

Although
Crossway no longer makes the PSR, having replaced it with the similar Personal
Reference Bible, I suspect my observations about construction and wear will be
generalizable not just to the PRB, but to Crossway’s entire lineup of sewn-bound,
polyurethane-covered mid-range Bibles. 
If you buy one and don’t leave it on your dashboard to melt, this is
about how it will wear for you.

**BINDING.** As I mentioned, my PSR has a
poly cover, which Crossway markets as TruTone. 
Poly covers fare about as well in the sun as Count Dracula does, but if
you don’t expose them to undue amounts of either light or heat, they will
endure better than a bonded-leather cover. 
Of course, they don’t offer the durability of a genuine- or
premium-leather cover, but they don’t cost as much either.

When I first
received this Bible, I was Bible-ignorant, at least in terms of binding and
such.  I couldn’t have told a poly cover
from a porpoise cover.  I enjoyed the
soft feel of the cover and assumed it was made out of leather until a friend
clued me in.

Over the
course of five years of being tossed on desk, pew, and pulpit, the cover
acquired a variety of small nicks and gouges. 
Also, here and there, a few of the stitches around the perimeter of the
cover have popped.

More
significantly, where the cover flexes the most, at the edges of the spine, the
outer layer of TruTone has flaked off in large patches, exposing some sort of
fibrous underlayment.  Also, the outer layer
has vanished along the edge of the cover, leaving those edges faintly
fuzzy.  Inside, the endpapers have
wrinkled up pretty impressively, but they’re essentially intact.

**TEXT BLOCK.** Although neither my wife nor
I knew it at the time, her purchase of this particular Bible was a fortunate
one in two main ways.  First, the text
block was printed in Belgium rather than China, so it doesn’t partake of any of
the indifferent quality that has led the Chinese PSR text blocks to acquire an
evil reputation.

Second, and
more importantly, the binding is sewn rather than bound.  If my PSR had been glue-bound, my regular use
of it would have led to pages falling out within about a year.  As it is, I used it for five years without
any problems, and the text block looks likely to outlast the cover.

However, even
a sewn binding will start to show signs of wear if you use it enough.  Because I didn’t know enough to break in the
Bible in 2008, it’s cracking in the front at the title page instead of flexing
at the hinge of the front endpaper as it should.  There’s a similar crack at Page 1390 in the
back for the same reason.

More
seriously, the stitching between signatures has become strained at the
beginning of 1 John.  I don’t know
whether that will continue to worsen as I use the Bible further.  Of course, after five years of regular use,
the gilding on the Bible is trashed, but I don’t give two hoots about fancy
gilding anyway.

In terms of
flexibility, my PSR is a well-trained dog. 
It will happily open flat in both Genesis and Revelation, and its
flaking-cover limpness in the hand will put most $200 goatskin Bibles to
shame.  There may be a moral here for
Bible aficionados.  If you want a Bible
with a limp cover, ain’t no substitute for reading it!

Overall, this
Bible has provided excellent value for money. 
Even though my wife paid full retail for it in a Bible bookstore (NOT
the cheap way to buy Bibles!), the past five years have proven that it was
worth the $30 investment.  Crossway
offers a lifetime warranty on all its sewn Bibles, and even now that this one
is going on the road with me, I suspect it’ll be a few more years yet before
I’ll be able to collect. Especially at this price point, I have to give the PSR
a full 5/5 for physical form.
