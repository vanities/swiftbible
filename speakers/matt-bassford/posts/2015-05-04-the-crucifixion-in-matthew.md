I was talking to Brian the other day about how the
note-taking study in the junior-high class downstairs was going, and he
mentioned to me that Mallory asked an interesting question.  She said, “Why are we Christians?  The Greeks had their own gods and were wrong,
but a lot of people still believed it, so how do we know that we aren’t
mistaken too?”

I think that’s an extremely important question to ask.  If we are Christians simply because we’ve
always been, we are Christians for the wrong reason.  Unless we’ve thought about it on our own and
decided to follow Jesus because we believe He is the Christ, our faith isn’t
real.

There are a number of reasons to believe that Jesus is
indeed the Son of God, but one of the most important to me has always been His fulfillment
of prophecy.  Hundreds of years before He
was born, prophets predicted His life and death in great detail, and that’s
only possible through supernatural intervention.  We don’t have time this morning to look at
all these prophecies and their fulfillments, so let’s look only at those
relating to Matthew’s account of the crucifixion.



**The Sentencing of
Jesus**

Matthew 27 divides roughly into two parts, and the first of these
sections concerns the sentencing of Jesus. 
It begins by describing **THE CONSPIRACY**against Him.  Here, let’s read from
Matthew 27:1-2.  In many ways, this is a
remarkable event.  After all, Jesus is a
nobody.  He’s a Jewish peasant, and yet
here He is, appearing before the chief priests and elders of the Jewish people
and before Pontius Pilate, who is the personal representative of the Roman
emperor.

What’s more remarkable, of course, is that it was
predicted.  We see the prediction in
Psalm 2:1-3.  We know from a little later
on in Psalm 2 that the Anointed is the Son of God because God says to Him, “You
are my Son; today I have begotten You.” 
We would think that the Son of God would receive a welcome from the
mighty of the world, but David predicted that exactly the opposite would happen,
and, of course, in the case of Jesus, the opposite did happen.

Next, we come to the sad end of **JUDAS.** Matthew tells the
tale in Matthew 27:3-10.  Judas famously
accepts 30 pieces of silver to betray Jesus, tries in vain to give the money
back, eventually throws it at the chief priests, and goes off and hangs
himself.  They’re sanctimonious souls, so
rather than putting the blood money back in the treasury, they use it to buy
the Potter’s Field.

As Matthew observes, this too was foretold.  We do come to a slight interpretive
difficulty here, though.  The gospel
ascribes the prophecy to Jeremiah, when in fact it is found in the book of
Zechariah.  There are several other
places where the text we have does something similar—taking a prophecy from a
minor prophet and ascribing it to a major prophet.

There are several possible explanations for this.  Maybe Matthew liked to categorize minor
prophets by major prophets.  Maybe a
later scribe wrote incorrect ascriptions in the margins that were accidentally
incorporated in the text.  It doesn’t really
matter, though, because the prophecy itself is so obviously valid.  Read with me from Zechariah 11:13.  Too many details line up here.  God was valued at 30 pieces of silver, and
they were thrown to the potter in the house of the Lord.  No human could have known that it would
happen, and yet it was predicted.

Next in sequence, we come to **JESUS’ SILENCE** before Pilate. 
The story appears in Matthew 27:11-14. 
Pilate was certainly a world-weary and cynical man, yet Jesus’ behavior
here astonishes him.  As we know from
John’s account, Pilate is actually trying to get Jesus to say something that
will allow Pilate to release Him, but Jesus remains silent.  After all, Jesus can’t talk.  If He speaks in His own defense, He will be
released, He won’t die on the cross for the sins of mankind, and that will be a
disaster for God’s plan and for the entire human race.  Ironically, the only way that Jesus can get
what He wants is by remaining silent and being condemned.

This silence, which makes no sense in earthly terms, is
predicted in one of the most prominent prophecies about Jesus’ suffering.  Look at Isaiah 53:7.  In going silently to His death, Jesus must
have seemed as dumb, in both senses of the word, as a sheep headed to the
butcher’s.  Anybody should have had the
sense to speak up, but Jesus apparently didn’t! 
Because God knew Jesus’ purpose, He also knew and could predict Jesus’
silence.

During this sequence, the **HATRED** of Jesus’ enemies is clearly on display.  Consider the story of Matthew 27:15-23.  These people hate Jesus so much that when
Pilate gives them a choice between releasing Jesus and releasing a robber and a
murderer, they ask for Barabbas to be unleashed again, even though Jesus,
unlike Barabbas, hasn’t violated any laws. 
The hatred of the chief priests and elders is baffling.  As Pilate points out, Jesus hasn’t done
anything wrong.  However, their very real
hatred leads them to demand His death.

Once again, this strange behavior toward Jesus was
prophesied.  Here, look at Psalm
69:4.  Even though it doesn’t get as much
play as Isaiah 53 or Psalm 22, Psalm 69 is also one of the major Messianic
prophecies in the Bible.  We know that
this passage is Messianic because Jesus Himself says so, in John 15.  The original psalmist may have given his enemies
little reason to hate him, but Jesus had given His enemies no cause for hatred,
so that the prophecy is absolutely true.

Finally, during the trial section of the story, we come to **JESUS’ HUMILIATION.** Consider here Matthew 27:24-31.  Pilate, in an attempt to satisfy the chief
priests and the mob, has his soldiers hurt and humiliate Jesus.  They flog Him, which was a brutal process
that probably caused Jesus to die on the cross as quickly as He did.  They slap Him, they spit on Him, and they
dress Him up as a king and sneer at Him. 
This is an ugly, vicious scene. 
Hopefully none of us would want to see anybody treated in this way, but
this is the Son of God being abused!  He
had the power to strike back, but with incredible self-control, He meekly gave
Himself to His tormentors.

There are several passages that predict the scourging and
mocking of Jesus, but let’s look at only one: 
Isaiah 50:5-6.  We’re familiar
with the suffering Servant in Isaiah 53, but I think it often escapes our
notice that the Servant is a recurring character throughout the last half of
Isaiah.  This is another Servant passage,
and it describes pretty graphically what would happen to Jesus.  I suppose that if Jesus were a fraud, He
could have tried to live up to this passage, but why would He do that when He
was going to be dead in hours?  This only
makes sense if Jesus is the Son of God.

**The Death of Jesus**

From Jesus’ trial, Matthew’s account takes an even darker
turn into detailing His death.  This
begins with His journey to **GOLGOTHA.** Consider here Matthew 27:32-34.  I was actually talking about this with Matt
Sterba a few weeks ago.  Jesus is taken
to Golgotha, a hill outside the city walls of Jerusalem, probably near a major
road so that travelers could see the spectacle. 
While there, before they actually nail Him to the cross, the Roman
guards offer him some wine mixed with gall. 
Because the guards don’t have anything in particular against Jesus,
they’re trying to be kind to Him.  The
Roman legions habitually drank sour wine and vinegar.  Jesus, however, refuses to follow suit.

This event too appears in prophecy.  Let’s return to an old friend and look at
Psalm 69:21.  As we’ve observed already,
Psalm 69 is largely a Messianic psalm, and this is yet another one of the numerous
prophecies it contains.  There’s no way
that the psalmist, whoever he was, could have known how the Romans would offer
refreshment to condemned prisoners, yet this lines up exactly.

After this exchange, we come to the actual **CRUCIFIXION** of Jesus itself.  Let’s continue with Matthew 27:35-37.  It’s interesting that even though the
crucifixion of Jesus is one of the most important events of human history,
Matthew is more concerned with the actions of the Roman guard.  After they nail Him to the cross, they gamble
for Jesus’ clothes; after all, He clearly isn’t going to need them
anymore!  Then, they sit down and watch
Him, waiting for Him to die.

Once again, the behavior of the guards is predicted with
eerie accuracy.  Consider the text of
Psalm 22:11-18, which is all about this. 
The psalm predicts that Jesus’ hands and feet would be pierced.  He was crucified.  The psalm predicts that His enemies would
cast lots for His garments.  They
did.  The psalmist predicts that they
would gather around Him and watch Him. 
They did.  Psalm 22 is a detailed
description of a Roman crucifixion from somebody who never saw the Romans
crucify anybody.  Only divine
intervention can explain such an account.

Of course, the guards aren’t the only ones up on the hilltop.  Jesus’ enemies aren’t good winners, and they
add **MORE MOCKERY** to His
suffering.  Let’s read here from Matthew
27:38-44.  I mentioned earlier that Jesus
was likely crucified by the side of the road. 
Presumably, the passers-by who were pro-Jesus were pretty upset by the
whole thing, but the anti-Jesus foot traffic felt free to be as vicious as they
could.  The crucified thieves on either
side joined in, though I can imagine that whatever satisfaction they felt must
have been limited.  Finally, of course,
the whole Jewish leadership comes trooping out of Jerusalem to gloat over His
imminent demise.  They come up with what
they must have thought was a particularly witty insult.  If You’re really the Son of God, Jesus, why
don’t You have God get You down off the cross? 
Then we’ll believe in You!

The strange thing about this mockery is its
cluelessness.  The Jewish leaders really
should have spotted the resemblance between their words and Psalm 22:6-8.
Almost word for word, what they say is what David said they would say 1000
years ago.  Unwittingly, they have
identified themselves as the persecutors of God’s Anointed.

This points to an even more interesting trend in all the
prophecies we’ve looked at.  By the time
I’m done this morning, we’ll have considered 10 different prophetic references
in the text, and of those 10 prophecies, 7 were fulfilled by the enemies of
Jesus.  Maybe Jesus and His friends would
have angled to fulfill prophecies to make Him look like the Messiah, but His
enemies wouldn’t!

After three hours of agony on the cross, **JESUS DIES.**Matthew records the event in Matthew 27:45-56.  There are a lot of strange and spectacular
events that accompany His death, but we’re going to focus on what is apparently
one of the least remarkable things in this context.  When Jesus cries out, “My God, My God, why
have You forsaken Me?”, He isn’t simply expressing His state of mind.  He’s pointing His hearers to a prophecy.  For the third time in a row, let’s go back to
Psalm 22, this time to Psalm 22:1-2.

It may be that God actually did forsake Jesus on the cross;
there’s a lot of debate about that, and it’s not worth getting into here.  What is certain, though, is that Jesus wants
everybody to consider His death in the light of Psalm 22.  As we’ve seen already, the first two-thirds
of the psalm is extremely specific in its prediction of the last few hours of
Jesus’ life.  He certainly doesn’t want
that overlooked!  Just as much, though,
Jesus wants them to read the last third, where the psalmist stops crying out to
God for deliverance and starts praising Him for the deliverance he has
received.  Jesus cites the first verse as
if to say, “You think you’ve got Me, but God will save Me!”

The final event in Matthew 27 that we’re going to look at is
**HIS BURIAL.** Look here at Matthew 27:57-61.  Here, we see one of Jesus’ disciples, a
hitherto-secret follower named Joseph of Arimathea, ask Pilate for Jesus’
body.  Joseph is rich, so he has a new
tomb all carved out to put it in.  It’s
now close to the start of the Sabbath, so they hurriedly bundle the body in and
mark the location so that when the Sabbath is over, they can come back on
Sunday to prepare it properly.

Here too, though, we encounter a prophecy.  Look at Isaiah 53:9.  Neither of the two thieves ended up in
Joseph’s tomb.  Joseph didn’t have any
regard for them.  However, as one of the
dissenting members of the Sanhedrin, along with Nicodemus, he thought that a
great injustice had been done.  He showed
his conviction of Jesus’ innocence by burying Him in his own tomb, which is
just what Isaiah had said would happen to the body of the Servant 700 years
ago.

Before the Lord’s Supper, let’s reflect on all these
things.  Even if Jesus had been nothing more
than a man, the account of His suffering and death wouldn’t make for pleasant
reading.  To us, though, this is much
more than another story of human cruelty. 
It’s the story of the death of the Son of God for our sins, and we can
be sure of its significance because of all of the prophecies that proclaimed
Him as the Christ even as He suffered. 
Let’s think about this as we partake.

**The Meaning of Jesus’
Death**

As we left the story before the Lord’s Supper, this is a
truly grim tale.  However, the Bible
doesn’t leave Jesus in the tomb, and we shouldn’t either.  We’ve quoted twice from Isaiah 53 already;
let’s round out our study this morning by finishing with a quotation of the
last three verses, Isaiah 53:10-12.  Yes,
Jesus suffered, but.  Yes, Jesus died,
but.  Jesus Himself was blessed for His
obedience to the point of death, and through Him, we are all blessed too.  His death satisfied the wrath of God.  He justified us.  He bore our sins.  He interceded for us.  Because He died, we can live forever with
Him.  The cross, then, is not ultimately
about injustice.  It is not ultimately
about tragedy.  It’s about mercy.  It’s about victory, victory for Him and
victory for us too.
