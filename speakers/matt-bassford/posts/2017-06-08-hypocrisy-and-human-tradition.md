All my life, I’ve been associated with the churches of Christ, and even though I was “raised in the church”, I’ve thoughtfully considered and adopted the goals of the Restoration Movement for my own.  I think it’s right that disciples of Jesus should seek to throw off the shackles of religious institution and tradition, taking the Bible for their only guide, desiring only to imitate the practices of the first-century church.  If the Bible is the word of God, and He speaks in no other way, clearly we should put the Bible first in every matter of faith and practice.

That’s a noble goal, I think.  However, in some ways, it runs counter to human nature.  After all, the history of God’s people has never been one of consistent obedience to His word.  The primary story arc of the Old Testament is one of spiritual failure culminating in the Babylonian captivity.  Within a few hundred years, the vibrant church of the New Testament fell away from following the pattern that had been handed down to it.


There’s no reason to believe that we are any less prone to apostasy now than people then were.  Human wisdom has not become any less appealing in the ensuing 1500 years.  Historically, brethren in the most conservative corners of the Restoration have recognized this danger.  Christians today, especially older Christians, often regard any change in practice with suspicion, and they are wise to do so.  Sometimes, change is nothing more than adopting a practice that has become expedient, but at other times, it represents a movement beyond the bounds that God has set.

However, innovation is not the only possible way that we can depart from the order of the New Testament.  In fact, it may not even be the most dangerous way.  There’s a reason why we decry the traditions of men rather than the innovations of men.  From the Pharisees on down, well-meaning followers of God have surrounded the teachings of Scripture with their own traditional practices, and then, over time, elevated those traditions to the level of Scripture.

The first half of this process is inevitable.  Human beings don’t like constant change and will sooner or later fall into a routine of religious observance.  Just imagine what a hassle it would be to have to decide on different service times every single week!  Just imagine having a debate every week about whether this week, we’re going to use song leaders and hymnals!  No church that refused to allow the formation of tradition could survive.  It would collapse from sheer exhaustion.

However, when we treat these routines that we have developed with the same reverence as the word of God, we have apostasized.  In a church where tradition is not elevated in this way, no tradition is beyond question.  Every routine is subject to revision and even replacement.  If three-songs-and-a-prayer is less effective than some other order of services, it needs to go.  If the traditional repertoire of hymns has grown stale, it’s time to freshen it up.  Obedience to Scripture must never change, but an “expedient” that isn’t the best answer anymore is no longer expedient and should be dealt with accordingly.  Otherwise, our usefulness to God must inevitably suffer.

Nearly every Christian would agree that this is how things should go.  However, the actual practice of churches often proves quite different.  The truth is that we like our traditions.  They’re comfortable!  They’re familiar!  They’re safe!  We don’t have to think about them too hard!

As a result, it is tempting for us to make our traditions rather than the Scripture into our spiritual sa
fe haven.  It doesn’t matter whether this safe haven is the most useful way of doing things.  We will regard a threat to it as a threat to us.  Maybe we know it’s not in the Bible, but we sure act as though it is.

Sometimes, the results of this can be comical.  I’m reminded of a preacher friend of mine who made the dire (?) mistake of passing around the collection plate after the second serving of the Lord’s Supper one Sunday evening.  After services were over, he was given the third degree by an irate older sister in Christ.  Incandescent with rage, she told him that she had been a member there since time out of mind, and before he died, her husband had been an elder there, and she had never, never seen anyone dare to provide a second opportunity to give to the Lord’s work!  One wonders what she would have said if he had actually done something wrong.

Similar stories abound.  The underlying philosophy, though, even more frequently takes the form of raising plausible objections to every single proposed change.  We might insist that we do not teach as doctrine the commandments of men, but if in practice, we behave as though we do, what’s the difference?

This gives rise to two problems.  The first is obvious.  When we elevate our preferences to the level of divine commandment, we are usurping the authority of Christ and subverting His kingdom for our comfort.  Ironically, what seems safe to us is actually very perilous.

The second, though, is more subtle.  While claiming to follow the Bible and the Bible only, we have actually begun to do something else.  What’s more, others, particularly younger Christians, will recognize that we have begun to do something else.  They see us setting up an ideal but not following it in practice, and they will conclude that we’re a bunch of hypocrites.

Of course, not all cries of hypocrisy are warranted.  Very few young people have the Scriptural grounding necessary to understand the Biblical pattern and correctly judge whether something is a departure from it.  It takes long years of study to reach that point, and humility from those who haven’t studied for long years is never out of order.

However, when brethren insist that something is right or wrong yet conspicuously fail to provide book, chapter, and verse in support, you don’t have to be able to see through a brick wall to see the problem.  In such cases, actions can speak even louder than words, and words and actions alike have the capacity to alienate those who are not just the future of the church, but its present.  The Bible should be our only guide.  We must fearlessly follow where it leads, whether seeking the fullness of God’s glory requires us to change almost nothing or almost everything.
