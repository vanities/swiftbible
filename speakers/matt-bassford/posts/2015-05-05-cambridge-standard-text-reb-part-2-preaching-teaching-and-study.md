Once I got the REB, off it went
to perform the usual tour of duty at the church building.  The REB’s 
was a little longer than normal. 
I used it to preach three sermons, all of which were out-of-the-Bible
rather than off-the-PowerPoint, it helped me teach several middle-school Bible
classes, and it spent a little over two weeks with me in my office.  Here is how I thought it did:

**PREACHING**

At 8 ¼” x 5 ¼”, the Standard Text
is right in the sweet spot for preacher-Bible size:  small enough to be easily portable, but large
enough that my sermon outline doesn’t stick out of my Bible like an untucked
undershirt.  Here too, I return to my
suspicion that there is more room for just-a-Bible Bibles than most publishers
seem to think.  The Standard text doesn’t
need to be a thinline for pulpit use; it merely needs to be of moderate length
and width.

In layout, the REB is another
double-column, paragraphed, text-only Bible. 
I’ve been developing a preference for these, and even though I commonly
hold that single-column, verse-by-verse Bibles are the most effective for
preaching, I have to admit that the REB’s layout performed well.  I didn’t struggle to find citations or
mis-cite anything.

This may have as much to do with
changes in me, though, as with changes in the layout of the Bible.  A verse-by-verse setting prioritizes *every* verse.  In the classic Foundation SCR, a verse in the
middle of a “paragraph” is set out in the same way as a verse at the
beginning.  Only a boldfaced verse number
indicates where the “paragraph” begins. 
If I’m preaching a topical sermon, which commonly use a lot of verses
from mid-context, I need the layout to emphasize those verses too so that I can
locate them quickly.

However, I’ve been preaching a
lot of textual, not topical, sermons recently. 
I’m not looking at individual verses; I’m considering entire contexts,
often reading a third of a chapter or more at a time, and making points and
applications from that.  For a textual
sermon, a paragraphed Bible is every bit as useful as a verse-by-verse Bible
because the paragraphed breaks highlight where I want to start reading
anyway.

Realistically, this is all that
matters, because if I’m preaching a topical sermon with verses from a dozen
different locations, I’m going to preach that one off the PowerPoint.  It breaks the flow of a sermon when I wait
for the congregation to turn to a number of passages in their Bibles, and if I
don’t wait (by cheating ahead when I’m still talking about the previous point),
they give up on trying to keep up with me after a passage or two. The REB might
not have worked well in a topical sermon, but I never asked it to perform in
that setting anyway.

Finally, it may be that it was
useful to read from an unfamiliar translation at the pulpit.  I’m certain that every member of my
congregation uses an NASB, an ESV, a KJV, an NKJV, or an NIV, and the Standard
Text REB doesn’t share a lineage with any of those translations.  It sounds different, and even though this is
extremely subjective, I thought people listened more closely to the reading
because it sounded different.

**TEACHING**

During the REB’s stint at the church
building, I taught a small middle-school class rather than an auditorium adult
class.  This difference in routine also
made a number of differences in my requirements for a Bible.  I spend a fair amount of time roaming around
even when teaching in that setting, but I can always set my Bible on the
classroom table when I feel like it. 
Also, as clever and thoughtful as they are, my middle-schoolers aren’t
going to throw a question at me in the middle of Bible class that makes me
scramble for the cross references.  I’m
much more in control of a children’s class than I am of an adult class.

Even given those differences, I
was happy with the REB, and I found it well-suited for classroom use.  However, there was one exception, and I think
it highlights an interesting difference between the faculties of bright
10-12-year-olds and those of adults. 
Adults don’t have much problem seeing past differences in wording to
similarity in meaning.  Kids do.  My three regular students used an NASB, an
ESV, and an NIV, and I spent a lot of time explaining
translation-to-translation wording issues, that “repudiate” in the NASB carried
with it the same meaning as “reject” in the REB.  It might have been more useful to adopt a
translation closer to what they were using.

**STUDY**

Even when I was alone in my
office, I enjoyed the handy size of the REB. 
The Bible is typeset in 8/9 Swift, and I didn’t have any problem reading
it (with the exception of the gutter issues I mentioned last week).  I don’t remember having any call to look up
anything using cross-references, so that issue never came up either.  The layout was easy to use.  I didn’t notice it or think about it, which
is the sign of a good layout.  I did miss
one verse citation in my handwritten notes, but I noticed and corrected it once
I transferred them to the computer. 
That’s a problem, but only a small one.

Here too, the predominant
impression that the REB left on me concerned translation.  It didn’t read quite like I was used to
either, and that made me think. 
Throughout most of the REB’s stint, I also had my standard Foundation
SCR in the office.  I found myself
constantly reaching for the SCR to check its wording (the SCR is an NASB)
against the wording of the Standard Text.

I don’t think that’s a bug.  I think it’s a feature.  I’m mostly an NASB guy, so when I use only
the NASB, I take the NASB perspective for granted.  When I use a translation as different as the
REB, the difference forces me to acknowledge that the NASB take on the text is
not the only possible one.  By looking at
things from both the NASB perspective and the REB perspective, I can triangulate
in on the truth, and that is extremely valuable.  Rock-brained conservative that I am, I
probably wouldn’t use an REB as my one-and-only for study, but it has a lot to
offer as a complementary take on the text.

Overall, I was pleased with the
REB for preaching, teaching, and study. 
I rate it at 9/10.
