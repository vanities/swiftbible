There are
plenty of people out there who want to use the same Bible for everything.  If you do, that’s fine; for more visual
learners, it helps them to remember where passages are located.  However, I don’t find that to be one of my
memorization cues, so I prefer to use different Bibles with different
attributes for different purposes.  For
example, I look for different things in a Bible for reading than I do in a
Bible for preaching, teaching, and study. 
A reading Bible needs to be optimized for doing a daily reading of a few
chapters at a time, or even for a one-sitting read of a whole book before I
write a class workbook on it.  Here are
the characteristics of my ideal reading Bible:

**SIZE.** Many people prefer to do
their Bible readings using a larger Bible, with the Bible resting on a desk or
a reading stand.  I’m not one of those
people.  I do the bulk of my Bible
reading leaning back in my desk chair at home, with my feet propped up on the
desk.  If I’m feeling really indulgent, I
might even retire to the couch and sprawl there.  I want to be able to one-hand the Bible, like
I would a paperback, and not have to think about it.



As a result,
I don’t really like larger Bibles for readings. Practically, I’m going to be
happiest with a Bible that’s at most 5 ½” x 8 ½”, with a weight of a pound and
a half or under.  Smaller is better, to
the extent that it doesn’t lead to ridiculously small font.

**LAYOUT.** Many authorities in the
Bible-blog world will insist that single-column layout is the way to go.  They point to its similarity to the novels
that we buy and read recreationally, and they also highlight the way that
double-column settings mangle the line length of poetry in the Old Testament.

Both of these
points are valid.  However, my
endorsement of single-column reading Bibles must be qualified at best.  For me, the issue is line length, not line
length in terms of number of characters (though that is a concern too), but line
length in terms of inches.  I find that
when I am reading, I can easily scan a line that is at most 3 ¼ inches
long.  3 ¾-inch lines are a little
annoying.  4-inch lines are downright
distracting, because I have to continually flick my eyes to the end of each
line to catch the last three words.

This has unfortunate
consequences for my use of single-column Bibles as reading Bibles.  I don’t suppose there’s some hard-and-fast
rule that says a single-column Bible must have a column wider than 3 ¼ inches,
but in practice, most of them do.  In
fact, many of the most highly touted “reading editions” have four-inch columns,
which keep me from immersing myself in the text and make me feel like a bad
Christian.

As a result,
I do most of my reading out of double-column Bibles.  Though the indents in the poetry sections can
be annoying, they are not as annoying as an overly-long line.  My ideal reading Bible would probably have
double-column prose and single-column poetry, but as far as I know, no such
chimera exists.

Even in the
double-column world, though, I don’t like to use Bibles that have overly narrow
columns.  Unless the average number of
characters in a line is around 40 or above, a Bible will read too choppily for
me to be happy with it.

However, I do
fall into line with conventional wisdom in my preference for reading Bibles in
paragraphed rather than verse-by-verse format. 
This is a no-brainer.  In a
verse-by-verse Bible, the big numbers at the beginning of and the hard returns
after every verse interfere with comprehension. 
Verse-by-verse Bibles are great for reference works; they’re lousy for
actual reading.

Finally, in a
reading Bible, I don’t want cross-references. 
I’m not looking passages up; I’m only reading.  If I do want to look something up, my
computer is waiting hopefully right next to me. 
On the other hand, I do want chapter and verse numbers, as long as
they’re unobtrusive.  Most daily reading
plans are chapter-based, so without chapter breaks of some kind, it’s tough to
figure out where a reading begins or ends. 
Similarly, it may be that some particular sentence in the reading piques
my interest, and the verse number helps me remember where it is for future
reference.  As long as the verse numbers
are superscript, they do very little harm and a lot of good.

**READABILITY.**My eyes are pretty good for
close work, so readability concerns aren’t a big deal here.  However, so far as is possible, I like to see
the usual readability factors present: 
larger font, opaque paper, and even line-matching.  8-point font seems to be a sweet spot for me.
