In my Bible-blogging, I don’t have a great deal to say about
translations.  I certainly don’t have the
Greek to critique the faithfulness of a given translation, and I accept both
formal equivalence and dynamic equivalence as defensible methods of
translation.  Indeed, I believe that if
we were to apply such terms to translations of secular works, those
translations would tend much more toward dynamic than formal equivalence.

Along with the translators of the King James Version, I
affirm that any translation produced in good faith by those with sufficient
understanding of the original languages is the word of God.  No such translation will ever be perfect, but
all are useful.  However, their
differences mean that they are not all equally good at everything.

This became particularly evident during my review of the
Pitt Minion NLT.  At times, the “NLT”
part of that became as significant to the way I used the Bible as the “Pitt
Minion” part.  The NLT is designed to be
readily comprehensible to the modern reader, but this ease of reading comes at
the expense of word-to-word correspondence with the original texts.  It’s a “forest” translation rather than a
“tree” translation.  It goes down
smoothly even in large chunks, but it doesn’t lend itself as well to nuanced
study of every word in the text.

Those in my religious tradition generally love nothing more
than nuanced study of every word in the text. 
As a result, the NLT is a translation that I’ve almost never seen my
brethren use.  They prefer instead the
conservative trinity of the NKJV, the ESV, and (especially) the NASB, with some
grizzled KJV holdouts.

This meant that my Pitt didn’t read like the Bibles
everybody else in the congregation was using, and even though nobody objected,
several commented.  When I read the first
eight chapters of Romans out loud, one of my friends commented that when he
tried to follow along in his NKJV, he found himself cataloguing differences in
translation rather than trying to figure out the meaning of the text.  Likewise, after I preached out of it, an older
gentleman and I had a cordial discussion about differences in translation.  In the course of this discussion, he repeatedly
referred to the NLT as a paraphrase, even though I tried to explain that it
actually wasn’t.

I also found that my comfort in using the NLT in study
varied from sermon to sermon.  The first
sermon I used it to write followed the next week after my reading of Romans
1-8, and it was a “here are the high points of what I just read” sermon.  My approach to the text was not rigorously
analytical.  Instead, I was merely after
a few verses that adequately summarized the main points of Paul’s
argument.  It was a broad-strokes sermon,
so the precise words the NLT used weren’t really an issue.

My second sermon, however, was on Calvinism, and that is a
subject that calls for *extreme*
analytical rigor!  Many of the arguments
both for and against Calvinism rely on specific words in specific passages, and
the more I studied, the more I felt the need to know what the text said,
instead of what the NLT translators thought the text said.  By the time I finished my study, I had
stopped referring to my Pitt NLT entirely and returned to Old Faithful, a
Foundation SCR NASB.

The moral of the story is not “NLT bad; NASB good”.  Instead, the moral of the story is “NLT
different”, and usually, that’s a good thing. 
I don’t think most Christians realize how much their habits of spiritual
thought are shaped by the translation they customarily use.  There have been many times in my preaching
career when I’ve based an argument on a particular word in one translation,
then gone to a second translation and found that I can’t make the same argument
from it.  If we stick to Old Faithful and
never switch translations, those doctrinal blind spots never become
apparent.

The more extreme the difference in translation, the more
dramatic the difference in perspective becomes. 
My friend’s experience of checking the NLT against his NKJV, though
perhaps not the best way to listen to a public reading, is actually a pretty
good way of studying out of the NLT or NKJV either one.  “Why does this Bible say it differently than
my Bible does?” is a valuable question to ask. 
NLT users (or NIV11 users or REB users or. . .) may—and probably will—find
that they don’t always agree with their favorite translation’s take on the
text.

Conversely, as one who primarily uses stodgy
formal-equivalence translations, I find that a dynamic-equivalence Bible will
often force me to re-evaluate my preferred readings.  I may not agree with a particular NLT rendering,
but the translators took that position for a reason, and their reasoning is
generally well worth considering.

This is not necessarily a comfortable process, but as a
lifelong rabble-rouser and disturber of the peace, I believe that a little bit
of discomfort in Bible study is important. 
If we stop looking at the Scripture in different ways, we stop learning,
and if we stop learning, we stop growing. 
Even if study of a different translation leaves us with exactly the same
positions we held before, at least we had to think about them first.
