Back in college, one of my professors remarked, “The lottery
is a tax on people who are bad at math.” 
In other words, it’s a sucker’s game that only the house is going to
win.  The other day, I ran across [an article](http://www.msn.com/en-us/news/us/the-powerball-jackpot-just-hit-dollar261-million-%E2%80%94-heres-the-best-time-to-buy-a-ticket-according-to-math/ar-AApkada) that examined this intuitive assumption. 
Is there ever a point at which it makes mathematical sense to buy a
Powerball ticket?

Those of you who enjoy math will doubtless relish the
opportunity to follow the author through his various regression analyses.  Those of you who do not can skip to the last
paragraph.  The end of the matter, when
all has been heard, is this:  If you
anticipate taking your lottery payout in a lump sum, there is literally never a
point at which it is a good idea to buy a Powerball Ticket.

If you are going to take your winnings in the form of a
30-payment annuity, it would make sense to play Powerball when the jackpot is
somewhere in the neighborhood of $360 million. 
However, this analysis does not take the impact of taxes into account,
and taxes on lottery winnings are notoriously savage.  When Uncle Sam’s cut is factored in, nobody
ever has a reason to play the lottery.



Of course, even if this were not the case, I still would
never play the lottery because of its connection to covetousness.  The dictionary tells us that covetousness is
a wrongful or extreme desire for gain. 
In Biblical terms, I think of covetousness as a desire for gain at
somebody else’s expense.  It’s not enough
for you to succeed; you have to intend or desire for somebody else to fail too.  Note, by the way, that this evil desire, and
not the presence of risk or chance, is the true problem with the lottery.  Taking a risk is morally neutral (generally
speaking); covetousness is not.

Most financial transactions are not covetous in nature.  When I go to Wal-Mart and buy a golf shirt
for $5, there are no losers.  I wanted
the golf shirt more than I wanted $5, and the Walton family wanted my $5 more
than the golf shirt.  Everybody walks
away happy.  Of course, if Wal-Mart
knowingly sold me a golf shirt that would disintegrate the first time I washed
it, that would be an expression of covetousness.  Their fraudulent gain would be a consequence
of my loss.

Playing the lottery is an example of this kind of zero-sum
game.  Everybody who plays the lottery
can’t win; otherwise, the lottery would soon go out of business.  Indeed, two things follow from the purchase
of a lottery ticket.  First, you have to
want to win.  Second, you have to want
everybody else to lose (because other winners diminish the value of your
winnings).

That’s covetousness plain and simple, and it is no less
covetous because of the presence of Powerball as a middleman.  If my wife were to hire somebody to kill me
(at times, an understandable course of action), she would be just as culpable,
both legally and morally, as if her finger had pulled the trigger.  If I desire and seek somebody else’s
financial harm, I’m still responsible for my evil desires even if I use the
lottery as my instrument.

However, the lottery is not merely a clear example of
covetousness.  It is a clear example of
the problems with covetousness.  As we
saw above, playing the lottery doesn’t work out the way that ticket buyers
think it will.  Generally speaking,
neither do other forms of greed.

The problem is that when we are covetous, it blinds us to
the long-term consequences of the short-term goal of financial gain by any
means necessary.  Much like lying,
covetousness typically sacrifices the future for the present.  If I’m a dishonest mechanic who consistently
overcharges my customers and makes unnecessary repairs, sooner or later, that’s
going to catch up to me.  Somebody who
knows cars is going to figure out that I’m lying and put the word out that I’m
not to be trusted.  Others will realize
that they can get the same work done for cheaper elsewhere.  Maybe I can trade on my reputation for a
little while, but eventually, I’ll end up with the financial failure that I
forfeited my integrity to try to avoid.

It’s also true that covetousness is more likely to expose us
to the covetousness of others.  Again,
the lottery is Exhibit A here.  The vast
majority of those who hope to profit by playing the lottery are going to end up
losers instead.  Con artists say that it’s
comparatively hard to cheat an honest man, but a man who can be led around by
his greed is easy meat.  An awful lot of
people who think they’re card sharps and pool hustlers end up learning that
they were actually the marks.

Being honest and godly in our financial dealings might seem
like a slow way to get ahead, but over time, it proves to be much more
certain.  When we treat others well and
are fair with them, whether they are customers, employees, or employers, they
will generally notice and appreciate it. 
When we approach a financial transaction looking for a fair deal rather
than hoping to make a score, the immediate results might not be as gratifying,
but over time, the practice will work to our benefit.

Covetousness in all its forms is a sin, and there are
numerous passages in Scripture that testify to this point.   It’s no more righteous to behave covetously
than it is to seek to injure others in some different way (and the evil desire
is the true problem, whether we succeed in achieving our greedy goal or
not).   We must recognize, though, that
God condemns this attitude not only for the good of others, but for our
own.  As we see in the lottery, if we
live our lives looking to make others lose, we will more than likely end up as
the losers ourselves.
