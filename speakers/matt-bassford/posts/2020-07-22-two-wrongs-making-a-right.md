Human beings are malleable creatures.  When we see someone doing something
(purchasing a Peloton, wearing high-waisted jeans), their example influences us
to do the same.  I suppose that we should
expect the same thing to be true of logical fallacy.  When people in our society make a bad
argument, we well may find ourselves adopting the form of the bad argument to
promote our pet cause.

Recently, a particular bad argument has caught my
attention.  It goes like this:

A.  X is wrong.
B.  However, Wrong Y has occurred.
C.  Therefore, X is acceptable.

Interestingly, when this argument is advanced, A and B are
made explicit, but C is implied (presumably because the people making the
argument sense that if made explicit, C will be attacked).  In fact, they often will disclaim C.  However, C must be the point, because unless
it is, B is irrelevant.  We can identify
this as the “Two wrongs make a right” logical fallacy, and it appears on all
sides of the political spectrum.

Consider, for instance, the defense made by many
progressives of rioting and looting during the recent racial unrest.  They have said, “Rioting and looting is wrong
[A], but the rioters and looters are reacting to the killing of George Floyd
and a history of racial injustice [B].” 
The implied conclusion is that racial injustice makes rioting and
looting acceptable [C].

However, political conservatives can be observed doing the
same thing.  Recently, it appeared most
prominently in the Candace Owens video response to the Floyd killing.  She said, “Police brutality is wrong [A], but
George Floyd was a career petty criminal [B].” 
The implied conclusion is that police brutality against career criminals
isn’t a significant problem [C].

Finally, I’ve seen the same logic from opponents of
vaccination when I complained about online bullying by the same.  They said, “Online bullying is wrong [A], but
vaccination opponents have had to deal with the effects of vaccine injury in
their families, and they often are mistreated because of their views [B].”  The implied conclusion is that we should condone
cyber-bullying by vaccination opponents because of what they have suffered [C].

The problems with adopting this line of reasoning cannot be
overstated.  Human wickedness is boundless.  As a result, anyone who wants to use
wickedness to justify more wickedness never will lack for an excuse. 

No limitations can be placed on its scope either.  Using wrong to justify wrong is more
dangerous than the *lex talionis* because vengeance at least is directed
toward the original wrongdoer.  Not so in
any of the above cases.  The victims of
rioting and looting were not chosen because they had inflicted racial
injustice.  George Floyd was not killed
by the woman he robbed.  I have not administered
any vaccines, nor have I treated any vaccine opponents unfairly.  If a wrong inflicted on you gives you the
right to inflict further wrong on whomever you choose, there is no limit to the
wrongs you can commit.

Ultimately, though, this style of argument is problematic
because it takes the right of judgment away from the Lord and bestows it on
humankind.  A commandment to which we can
append an asterisk whenever we feel like it is no commandment at all.  We feel so self-righteous when we do, but in
truth, we are not righteous.  Instead, we
merely walk the broad path of Judges 21:25.
