All of us have our good days, but sometimes we have the
other kind too.  In days like these, it
seems like everything is going wrong. 
Everything we touch at work turns to mud, either our spouse woke up with
a nasty-bad temper or we did, and in our walk with God, the devil won’t leave
us alone.  Worse still is when those bad
days start stringing together into weeks or months.

In times like these, it helps us to remember the bad days
and weeks and even months that our brethren 2000 years ago had.  We’re familiar with the stories of the trials
of the faithful, but we often don’t consider what it would have been like to be
in the middle of those trials.  This
morning, though, let’s look at a first-person description of what being in the
middle was like.  Let’s take heart from
Paul’s struggles in 2 Corinthians 1-3, and what he learned about consolation in
Christ.



**Reliance on God**

The first chapter of this context concerns itself with
Paul’s reliance on God.  It begins with
Paul praising **THE GOD OF ALL COMFORT.** Let’s read here from 2 Corinthians
1:1-11.  We might think that we have
problems, but Paul had capital-P Problems. 
Any student of the New Testament knows that Paul was a pretty tough
dude, but here he reports that he was utterly burdened beyond his strength.  He was so crushed by despair that he
considered himself as good as dead. 
Right here is where the skeptics and the atheists start smirking.  “If God is so powerful and good,” they ask,
“how come He let His faithful servant Paul go through something like
that?”  For that matter, why does God
allow us to go through trial that seems to stretch on and on?

Paul provides three reasons. 
First, he says, the experience of unbearable trial forced him to rely on
God, so that his deliverance felt to him like God raising the dead.  So too for us.  If we feel like the problems we’ve got are
too much for us, that we can’t go on like this any longer, maybe that’s the
point.  Maybe God is trying to get us to
let go of our pride and entrust ourselves to Him.

Second, he tells us that his trials were so dire so that
they would prompt others to pray for him. 
That way, when he was delivered, it would prompt all those who had been
praying to see his deliverance as the work of God.  I think this applies to us with equal force.  When we’re crying out, “Why me, Lord?”, part
of God’s work in that is to use us to teach others to pray.  God is giving them the opportunity through us
to learn to be more compassionate, trusting, and thankful.

Third, in what is actually the first point of the context,
Paul reveals that God allowed him to undergo trial so that he could receive
comfort from God and then share that comfort with others.  Paul’s experience of being comforted by God
made him more useful to the Corinthians and any other Christians he
encountered, because now he could pass that comfort along to them.

This holds true for us as well.  When we mourn and are comforted, that
experience equips us to help others in a way we could not before.  I’ve found this to be personally true.  One of the great tragedies of my life has
been the stillbirth of my daughter Macy. 
With the Lord’s help, I made it through that, but because I have been
through it, I’m much better equipped to talk to those who are going through
it.  Anytime we seek the Lord’s comfort
through trial, we learn something that will help us help others.  There’s another answer to “Why me?”  It may well be that God sees somebody in your
future who will need help, so now He is training you to help them then.

From here, Paul turns his attention to **CONFUSION IN CORINTH.** Let’s
continue our reading in 2 Corinthians 1:12-24. 
Here, we learn that Paul doesn’t only have problems with persecution.  He has problems with brethren who are
determined to give him a hard time. 
Reading between the lines, it sounds like Paul had told the Corinthians
that he was going to come and visit them. 
However, his relationship with the church there had deteriorated so much
that he decided it was better for them that he stay away.  Some of Paul’s enemies there jumped on this
and started accusing him of having deceived them in the first place.

Even though this is an offensive accusation, Paul doesn’t
lose his temper over it.  Instead, he
continues to address the Corinthians in good faith.  He points out that his conduct among them has
given them no excuse to accuse him of misconduct.  Instead, his whole pattern of behavior has
shown that they are his foremost concern. 
Furthermore, it was Paul who first taught them about the God and the
Savior they believe in.  How can the
proclaimer of a gospel of truth be a liar?

Sadly, it’s quite common for Christians to be unjustly
accused today, even by other Christians. When this happens to us, we need to
keep our cool, be open rather than defensive, and stick to the facts.  This is where what ought to be a track record
of proven character should come into play. 
If everybody knows we’re honest and trying to do the right thing, an
accusation of dishonesty won’t get much traction.  Hopefully, if we patiently explain what we’re
doing, that will persuade even our accusers. 
It’s not much fun to be put on the spot by somebody, but even there, we
need to work on shining with the light of Christ.

**Triumph in Christ**

The second chapter of 2 Corinthians explains what it means
for Paul to triumph in Christ.  The
chapter begins, though, with a story of **REBUKE
AND SORROW.** Look at 2 Corinthians
2:1-11.  Here, we see the explanation of
why Paul decided not to return immediately to Corinth.  At some point, in a story not covered in the
book of Acts, Paul did go back.  This
probably took place during his two-year stay in Ephesus, which is only a couple
hundred miles by boat away from Corinth. 
However, Paul’s visit didn’t go well. 
Apparently, one of the members was particularly obnoxious to him,
causing him great pain and wrecking the entire trip.

Once Paul returned home, though, he wrote a letter we don’t
have, that he calls “the letter of tears” to the church there, and the tables
turned.  The whole church, even including
the offender, became stricken with remorse at the way they had treated
Paul.  Even though the member repented,
the church continued to shun him, and Paul is writing now to get them to
forgive him.  Continued rejection will
only help the devil.  Paul then generously
says that the forgiveness of the majority is enough to count as his forgiveness
too.

As I read this, I can’t help but be impressed by the
apostle’s personal warmth, even to someone who had been offensive to him.  Considering all the other accusations that
Paul fields in 2 Corinthians, and how even-tempered Paul is about all of them,
whatever this guy said, it must have been truly awful.  However, now that the situation has been
resolved, Paul doesn’t hold a grudge.

Today, we can learn a lot from him about the art of
forgiveness, particularly the art of forgiving brethren.  It is sad but true that for many Christians,
the bitterest enemies they will ever have are other Christians.  All of us have seen or at least heard stories
about how ugly and nasty brethren can be to one another.  That kind of treatment can embitter us and
even drive us out of the church entirely. 
However, God doesn’t want us to be embittered.  He wants us to be ennobled.  He wants us to forgive others who wrong us as
generously as He through Christ has forgiven us.

Paul goes on, though, to describe himself as **A FRAGRANCE OF CHRIST.** Let’s consider here 2 Corinthians 2:12-17.  Paul begins by describing his confusion.  He went to Troas to preach but was upset
because he didn’t find Titus there. 
However, what to him seemed like aimless wandering was actually part of
the triumphal procession of Christ.

From here, Paul launches into a complicated metaphor.  This would have made perfect sense to the
Corinthians, but it makes much less sense to us.  Here’s what’s going on.  When a Roman general won a notable victory
over the enemies of Rome, he was granted a victory parade, called a triumph.  During this triumph, the general would lead
his victorious army through the streets of Rome.  After the victorious army would follow the
men of the defeated enemy army, along with their king or general.  Once the triumphal parade was over, the
captives would be sold into slavery and their commander would be ritually
executed.

Also, all along the route of the procession were servants
carrying censers.  They filled the air
with clouds of perfumed smoke.  To the
victorious Roman army, that smell carried with it the promise of wealth and
celebration.  To the defeated army, it
was the smell of humiliation and death.

Here, then, is what Paul is saying.  He’s saying that whatever our lives may look
like, what they actually are is part of the triumphal procession of our
victorious King, Christ.  The gospel that
we proclaim is like the clouds of incense along the triumphal route.  We like the gospel because it reminds us of
our salvation and promises us eternal life. 
It’s a fragrance from life to life.

However, we’re not the only ones in Christ’s triumphal
procession.  All the people of the world
are a part of it too.  After all, we’re
all going to the same place—the throne of God on the Day of Judgment.  They are exposed to the gospel too, but it
doesn’t have anything good to say to them. 
It reminds them of the spiritual death they are currently in and promises
them an eternal death and separation from God. 
As Paul says, to the perishing, it is a fragrance from death to death.

That’s a pretty cool metaphor, but here’s the so-what.  When Paul is talking about Christ leading him
in triumphal procession, he’s talking about his own life.  He’s talking about the life we’ve just been
studying about.  He’s talking about the
times when he is in such great danger that he’s not even sure he’s going to
live to see the next day.  He’s talking
about the experience of having the very people he taught the gospel to accusing
him of being a liar.  He’s talking about
humiliation and suffering.  Because of
Christ and his gospel, even a life like Paul’s is a triumphal procession.  That sure puts our lives in perspective in a
couple of different ways, doesn’t it?

Before the Lord’s Supper, then, let’s reflect on our
triumphal procession.  Let’s remember the
titanic struggle of our Lord on the cross and in the tomb, before He rose
victorious from the dead.  The sacred
meal before us is not a funeral supper. 
It’s a victory feast.  Let’s
remember too that no matter what is going on in our lives, it means triumph for
us.  Let’s think on these things as we
partake.

**The Covenant of
Christ**

The third chapter of 2 Corinthians, and the last one we’re
going to look at, is about the implications of the covenant of Christ.  However, it begins with Paul’s description of
the Corinthians as **A LETTER FROM
CHRIST.** Here, let’s consider 2
Corinthians 3:1-6.  Once again, we see
here that some of Paul’s biggest problems aren’t outside the church.  They’re in it.  This time, apparently some of the
Corinthians, or some religious leaders, are insisting that Paul is a bogus
apostle because he and his followers don’t have letters of recommendation.

It’s evident from Paul’s reply that he’s dumbfounded by this
accusation.  The charge that he’s not a
genuine apostle is coming from the very people whom he led to Christ in the
first place!  They’ve heard his preaching.  They’ve seen the miracles. If indeed Paul
needs a letter of recommendation, the people he saved are a letter of
recommendation all by themselves.

Furthermore, Paul goes on to argue, this insistence on
letters seems to belong philosophically with an insistence on the letter of the
Law of Moses.  This is the kind of
argument that ex-Pharisees would make, the people who had reduced the Old Law
to a series of external check-the-box rules instead of the heartfelt obedience
God wanted.  Paul isn’t sufficient as a
preacher because he has checked everybody’s boxes.  He’s sufficient because God made him so.

Today, brethren, we must be careful not to reduce the law of
liberty under which we live to a dead letter. 
The more we study the Scripture, the more evident it becomes to us that
it doesn’t spell everything out.  The
Bible tells us that we’re supposed to teach others, but it leaves the how of
teaching to our best judgment.  It tells
us that we’re supposed to assemble on the first day of the week, but it doesn’t
specify meeting place, meeting time, or order of services.  It teaches that women are to be modestly
dressed, but it doesn’t provide measurements for skirt length and neckline
depth.  Basically, in our study of nearly
any topic, we’re going to be filling in a lot of blanks.

There’s nothing wrong with that.  God expects us to make wise applications of
Scriptural principles.  However, we must
always be careful to distinguish between our judgments and the judgments of the
Lord.  While we should regard the
collective wisdom of the congregation, or even of tradition, with deference,
human wisdom and tradition are not the same thing as divine revelation.  Let’s take care lest, like Paul’s enemies, we
mistake our conclusions for God’s.

The final context in 2 Corinthians 3 concerns the transition
**FROM GLORY TO GLORY.** Look at 2 Corinthians 3:7-18.  In this section, Paul explains why coming to
the new covenant with an old-covenant attitude is such a big problem.  He builds his argument here around an unusual
historical event.  When Moses received
the Law from God at the top of Mt. Sinai, whenever he returned from the
mountain peak to the camp of the Israelites, because he had been in contact
with the glory of God, his face literally shone.  However, as time passed before he returned to
the presence of God, the glory faded from his face.

Paul says that this fading glory is an appropriate way to
understand the Law of Moses itself.  It
was glorious for a while, but with the coming of Christ it faded.  By contrast, what Paul calls the ministry of
the spirit, our service to Christ, comes with a glory that does not fade away.

However, the Exodus story has another wrinkle.  When Moses came down from Mt. Sinai, he put a
veil over his face.  However, he didn’t
do this to conceal the glory.  He did it
to conceal the fading of the glory.  He
didn’t want the Israelites to know that his face had stopped shining.

This, Paul said, is the way that the Jews of his day looked
at the Law of Moses.  They had a veil
over their hearts, so that they couldn’t recognize that the Law was fading and
passing away.  However, someone who
turned to Christ removed that veil, so that they could see the passing of the
Law and the enduring glory of Christ. 
This beholding changed them from Jews to disciples of Jesus.

Today, we must remember that the Law of Moses is not the only veil that can hide the glory of Christ from our hearts.  Our spiritual vision can be obscured by having been brought up listening to false teaching about the Bible, by thinking that we know what the Bible says instead of studying it for ourselves, and by plain old worldliness.  All of these things will keep us from seeing the truth about what God wants us to be.  We must constantly work to unveil our hearts, so that nothing about us is keeping the glory of Christ from transforming us into His image.
