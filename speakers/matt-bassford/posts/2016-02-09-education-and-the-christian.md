I don’t even know whether the above sign is real.  The Internet is full of Photoshopped
church-building signs.  Somebody with a
grudge could whip up such a sign in five minutes.

Whether it is real or not, though, it does not represent the
views of any of the seven churches with which I’ve ever been associated.  In fact, the Joliet church, for which I
currently preach, is much better educated than the national average.  At least 35 percent of the members old enough
to have completed college have bachelor’s degrees or better, usually one of
those STEM degrees that are so highly sought after these days.  The Joliet church is a pro-education church,
and strongly so.

It’s entirely possible, though, that there are other
churches with strong views in the other direction.  It’s a big country, and it’s a big
brotherhood.  Generally, people who make
confident claims about "the Church of Christ this" and "the Church of Christ that" are extrapolating on the basis of personal experience that cannot possibly be
representative.  Congregational autonomy
leaves room for plenty of odd folks out on the margins.  Perhaps I’m one of them!

Regardless of what Christians do believe, it strikes me that
one can arrive at wise, Biblical conclusions about what they ought to
believe.  Here’s my take.



**Education is
important.** This starts with
economics.  There are plenty of passages
in the New Testament that emphasize the responsibility of Christians to work in
order to provide for themselves and share generously with others.  This is not limited to male Christians
only.  Indeed, 1 Timothy 5:16 anticipates
one of many situation in which a Christian woman may find herself called upon
to provide for others.  More education is
positively correlated with higher earnings and lower unemployment, which leaves
the educated Christian better able (as a rule) to accomplish this.

Second, education is useful for its effect on broadening the
mind.  We cannot read the letters of Paul
and consider his missionary work without being struck not only by his
godliness, but by his education.  He
learned the Law of Moses by sitting at the feet of Gamaliel, the first-century
Jewish equivalent of a law degree from Yale.  He was familiar enough with the learning of
the Gentiles to be able to quote Greek poetry on the Areopagus.  Throughout his epistles, his reasoning
ability reveals a mind honed to razor sharpness.  Paul would not have been Paul without the
benefits of education, and Christians who choose to follow in his footsteps can
similarly be a blessing to the kingdom.

Of course, none of this implies that the educated Christian
is godlier than the one who is not.  Paul
was a scholar, but Peter was a fisherman, and the Lord was able to use them
both.  We all have different roles in the
body, and there are plenty of Christians who delight God but aren’t college
material.  Perhaps the best word on the
subject comes from my brother and friend Curtis Pope, who is wont to observe,
“Everybody should get as much education as they can stand.”  I’ve definitely hit my limit!

**Education does not
mean sharing the assumptions of the secular elite.** Nearly any discussion of the importance
of education will praise the benefits of inquiry and skeptical criticism.  There are many in our country who believe for
this reason that education and faith cannot coexist.  To be frank, I find this contention
ludicrous, and always have.  Everybody
believes in something, and the skeptics who are so quick to attack the
assumptions of religion are slow to critique their own assumptions, to the
point of failing even to recognize that they are making assumptions.

Take, for instance, the assumption of naturalism
itself.  Naturalism posits that events
can have only natural causes, excluding *a
priori* the miraculous and supernatural. 
This is not a scientifically testable theory.  It is an article of faith.  However, the scientists who are determined to
write God out of the universe and the religious scholars who are determined to
write Him out of the Bible generally operate as though it were established
truth.

When faced with adversaries like these, the believer
benefits greatly from education.  I’m not
terribly impressed when some tweed-jacketed expert with a string of letters
after his name shows up on The History Channel and tells me what I can actually
know about the “historical” Jesus.  Big
deal.  I’ve got a string of letters after
my name too, and I have the intellectual tools I need to consider the evidence
myself and discover that it doesn’t justify his conclusions.  Most of these skeptics would benefit greatly
from turning some skepticism on themselves!

**Education is not what
is most important.** American society,
at least middle-class white American society, is obsessed with education.  All parents want their little Johnnies and
Janies to have every educational opportunity available, so that when they
graduate from college (and when I was growing up, it was assumed that I would
graduate from college) they’ll be able to embark on stable, prosperous careers
that provide them with social status and (not coincidentally) reflect glory on
their wonderful, dutiful parents.

However, that must not be the primary goal for godly
parents.  I won’t deny that I would like
to see my children follow in my educational footsteps.  I have a great appreciation for the benefits
of education.

In the final analysis, though, education is not what matters
most.  Godliness matters most.  Faithful Christians who never so much as
graduate high school aren’t shunted to a trailer park on the outskirts of
heaven.  Ungodly Ph.D.’s will not find
the torments of hell more tolerable. 
Instead, either you hear “Well done,” or you don’t, and education does
not make hearing “Well done” more likely.

In fact, an overly zealous pursuit of education, whether for
ourselves or for our children, can have the opposite effect.  If education is first, God can’t be.  If the most important thing to us is to rush
our kids around to every activity under heaven, they will surely pick up on
that.  If they see that the Lord is less
important to us than scholastic attainment, that is the set of values they will
internalize, with eternally dire consequences.

Education is one of the good things in life, like family,
friends, work, and even moments of leisure. 
However, as is true with all of those things, we must remember to keep
it in its proper place.  Not even the
most impressive diploma can compare in value to our souls.
