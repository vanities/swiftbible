Two days ago, I ran a post entitled “Why the American Church
Fell”, which, not surprisingly, generated a great deal of conversation.  One particularly interesting point came from
my brother Jeremy LaValley, who opined that to the deadly trio I listed
(Scriptural ignorance, worldliness, and lack of spiritual leadership), we
should add lack of emotion.  Jeremy is
concerned that too many brethren go through the motions of a hollow ritual in the
assembly without the zeal and emotion that leads them to change their lives and
be a light for Christ in the community.

I agree that this is a problem, though I think it shares
some features with the three that I identified. 
Underneath them all, apathy is doing its usual evil thing.  However, I think it’s also worth exploring
what the Bible has to say about the role that emotion should have in our
spiritual lives.



When we consider the religious world around us, we don’t
have to look very hard to find churches that appear to rely not only on
emotion, but on emotionalism.  To them,
the assembly is a place where you come to feel a spiritual high, so their
worship services are constructed to manufacture that high.

Traditionally, those in the churches of Christ have been
suspicious of this model of “doing church”. 
For one thing, it emphasizes the importance of subjective experience
rather than objective rightness.  The
most important thing about a worship service becomes whether I got the feels or
not, not whether God was glorified in the way He has prescribed.  Second, it tends to be unsustainable.  All of us can think of mountaintop worship
experiences, but most of us don’t spend our spiritual lives on the
mountaintop.  Is worship that we have to
struggle through because of work problems and family problems and general
real-life yuck somehow less valid than worship where we effortlessly soar?  The conclusion that many in the world draw is
that worship without the high isn’t worthwhile, so they leave the
high-manufacturing churches, either for a church that offers a better high or
for nothing at all.

I think there’s something to these critiques, but I also
believe they can be carried too far.  I
think there’s a problem with a faith that is founded on emotionalism, but I
also think there’s a problem with a faith from which all emotion has been
stripped out.  The latter can certainly
be a problem in churches of Christ.  Our
suspicion of emotionalism can lead us to suspicion of emotion generally, so
that the ideal becomes a grim, repressive assembly in which every checklist act
of worship is carried out with clenched-jaw precision, and even something as
wonderful as a baptism cannot be greeted with joyful spontaneity.  If that’s where we have arrived, it’s quite
understandable that many want to swing the pendulum back the other way!  And so it continues.

The only way to stop the pendulum swings is to ignore what
others are doing and focus on what the word says we ought to do.  Once we do this, our struggles start making
more sense, because it becomes evident that the Bible uses a different
emotional map than we do.  Our society,
like most/all Western societies, uses a mind/heart duality.  Thought and emotion are two different things,
so you can have either thoughtfulness or emotion, but not both.  Given that duality, members of the churches
of Christ will generally emphasize thoughtfulness for fear of abandoning the
truth.  If those are your choices, I
guess you pick the least bad one.

The Bible, though, like most non-Western societies, rejects
this segregation of thought and emotion. 
The idea of pure emotion does exist in Scripture.  We see it in the Greek *splagchnon*, the bowels, which were regarded as the seat of reactive
fellow-feeling toward another.  In
English, the imagery doesn’t quite work, which is why we giggle at the Isaac
Watts hymn, “Blest Is the Man Whose Bowels Move”.  With compassion, people!  With compassion!

Similarly, the Greek *kardia*is not the same thing as the English heart, which we regard as the seat of
emotion.  Instead, the Biblical *kardia* is something that can understand
(Matthew 13:15), reason (Mark 2:8) and have thoughts and intentions (Hebrews
4:12).  However, the *kardia* can also love (Luke 10:27), burn (Luke 24:32), be troubled
(John 14:1), and be broken (Acts 21:13). 
In short, there isn’t really a good English-language equivalent for such
a flexible concept.  If anything, we
ought to understand *kardia* as
mind-and-heart, something that is neither wholly rational nor wholly emotional,
but partakes of both.

Experientially, this seems right to me.  I find that my thoughts frequently influence
my emotions, and vice versa.  Our society
either argues that this shouldn’t happen (“Be rational!”) or regards it as a
problem when it does.  I don’t think it’s
a problem.  I think it’s the way that
human beings are, and when we refuse to acknowledge this truth about ourselves
(as with legions of “objective” scientists who cannot see the extent to which
their findings are driven by their beliefs), we are certain to stray from the
truth we claim to cherish.

The Scriptural portrayal of Christianity is certainly an
emotional one, but we have to recognize what kind of emotion it is.  *Splagchnon*only appears 11 times in Scripture, and in every case, it’s directed toward
people rather than toward God.  There are
times when Christians ought to be impulsively emotional, but that kind of
reaction only plays a minor role in our walk with God.

By contrast, the New Testament contains 157 instances of *kardia*. 
Mind-and-heart is the dominant emotional paradigm of the Bible, and it’s
a paradigm that lines up perfectly with one of our cultural blind spots!  Paul’s instruction in Ephesians 5:19 to make
melody in our hearts to the Lord, then, is not an appeal for an emotional
outburst.  Instead, it is an appeal to be
both thoughtful and emotional in worship.

These two things shouldn’t fight against each other.  Instead, they should work together and
reinforce one another.  When we sing, we
should think about what we’re singing, whether the greatness of God, the
suffering and love of Jesus on the cross, or our community with one
another.  These thoughts, if we
understand their importance, will inevitably produce an emotional reaction
within us.  They’re supposed to!  When we listen to our brethren singing them,
it helps us to understand them better, not merely on an intellectual level, but
on an emotional one too.  Our worship is
supposed to be a time when we get it.  We
are participating in a Scriptural pattern that is designed and intended to
provoke the mind-and-heart.

So too with everything else we do.  Study of the word isn’t supposed to be a dry,
detached, emotionless exercise.  “Oh, how
I love Your law!” cries the psalmist in Psalm 119:97.  Our reflections on the Scripture are supposed
to be a mind-and-heart experience, as emotionally rich as they are
intellectually profound.  If the Bible
does not engage our yearnings and our will, we have not engaged the Bible.  This mind-and-heart understanding of our
discipleship should pervade every aspect of our service to God.

The Biblical model of spiritual emotion will not look much
like the frenzied assemblies we sometimes encounter on YouTube and
public-access television.  It will not
satisfy those who opine that “real” emotion must be detached from intellect.  However, if we pursue it, it will lead us to
spiritual maturity and help us to give our God the glory He deserves.
