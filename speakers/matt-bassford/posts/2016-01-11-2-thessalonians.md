It’s axiomatic that everybody’s got problems.  If we don’t at the moment, just wait a day or
two, and a problem is bound to show up! 
When we’re focusing on our own problems, it’s easy to let those problems
get to us.  Why am I facing this?  It’s not fair!  However, once we start considering the
problems of others, they put our own in perspective.  A couple weeks ago, I was stuck in the St.
Louis train station for eight hours because the train I was supposed to be on
hit a car that tried to beat it across the tracks.  I wasn’t happy, but I consoled myself with
the reflection that my day was still going better than the day of the guy who
was driving that car!

Similarly, when we compare our problems to the problems of
the church in Thessalonica, we start recognizing how good we’ve actually got
it.  They had to deal with neighbors who
rejected the truth, outside persecution, and even brethren who tried to sponge off
of other Christians rather than working to support themselves.  In response, Paul reminds the Thessalonians
that they aren’t the answer to their problems, but Jesus is.  Let’s consider this point as we explore 2
Thessalonians.



**2 Thessalonians 1**

2 Thessalonians has three chapters, which obviously suits my
purposes quite nicely, so let’s begin by pondering 2 Thessalonians 1.  It opens in typical Pauline fashion, with
**GREETINGS AND THANKSGIVING**.  Here, let’s
read from 2 Thessalonians 1:1-5.  Note
first of all that with the exception of one word, the first verse of 2
Thessalonians is the same as the first verse of 1 Thessalonians.  It contains the same three people—Paul,
Silas, and Timothy—writing to the same congregation.  Silas vanishes from the narrative of Acts
with the conclusion of Paul’s second missionary journey, so it’s likely that
this letter is written about the same time as 1 Thessalonians, in 51 or 52 AD,
during the 18 months that Paul spends in Corinth.

Paul’s description of the church in Thessalonica is sadly
familiar.  On the plus side, they are
growing in faith toward God and love for one another.  On the minus side, they are suffering from
affliction and persecution.  At a guess,
the Jews who began the persecution in Thessalonica in Acts 17 haven’t let up
even now.

Some might take this as evidence that God wasn’t with the
Thessalonian Christians.  Paul, however,
argues the opposite.  He says that the
persecution of the Thessalonians, when combined with their steadfastness
despite that persecution, shows that they are worthy of the kingdom of God.

There’s a twofold lesson here.  First, being faithful to God does not mean
that God is going to insulate us from earthly trouble.  If nothing else, the example of Job should
clue us into that!

However, that earthly suffering gives us the opportunity to
prove that we do belong to God.  All
kinds of people lose their faith because Mama died or they got sick or they
lost their job.  Their falling away,
however, only shows that they became Christians for the wrong reason.  We don’t follow Christ because discipleship
is a force field or a meal ticket.  We
follow Him because we hope for eternal life, and nothing that happens to us
here can diminish that hope.

In fact, Paul next points to the hope of the Thessalonians
by reminding them about **THE JUDGMENT OF GOD**. 
Consider 2 Thessalonians 1:6-12. 
As always, whenever we encounter a passage about the day of the Lord, we
have to ask “Which day?”  I think this
one is about the final judgment, and I base that on a couple of different
things.  First, in writing to the same
group in 1 Thessalonians, Paul was definitely talking about the final judgment,
with the resurrection of the dead and so forth, and it doesn’t seem likely that
he would switch contexts here without warning. 
Second, many of the elements in the text here, like the wicked being
punished with eternal destruction, seem more like the final judgment than the
70-AD destruction of Jerusalem.

Having said that, Paul’s message to the Thessalonians is
that they should not confuse the delay of justice with the denial of
justice.  Even though the righteous and
the wicked don’t seem to be getting what they deserve right now, sooner or
later, God is going to balance the scales. 
Jesus is going to return in power and deal out vengeance to the
wicked.  The response of the
Thessalonians to the wickedness around them, then, shouldn’t be to strike back
against their tormentors.  Instead, all
they have to do is remain faithful and glorify God, and then God will glorify
them.

In a few moments, we’re going to celebrate the Lord’s
Supper.  As we do, let’s remember that it
is a memorial, not only of the death, burial, and resurrection of the Lord, but
of His future coming in judgment too. 
After all, as Paul says in 1 Corinthians 11, when we do this, we are
proclaiming His death until He comes.  We
are declaring our faithfulness to Him until He returns and makes faith
unnecessary.  Let’s consider, then, our
Lord in all His guises:  suffering on the
cross to purchase forgiveness for our sins, but also awesome, powerful,
returning in the skies to rescue His own. 
His story is still going on, and we’re a part of it.  Let’s reflect on this as we partake.

**2 Thessalonians 2**

Now, let’s venture into the second chapter of 2 Thessalonians,
which is traditionally where all the problems lurk.  Let’s begin with what Paul writes about the
timing of **THE DAY OF THE LORD**.  Read with
me from 2 Thessalonians 2:1-7.  The first
thing to note here is that Paul’s words nail down which day of the Lord we’re
talking about.  This is first of all a
real event.  It isn’t Jesus coming in the
hearts of the Thessalonians or anything like that.  Second, it’s going to be preceded by a series
of events, some of which may not have happened even now, and none of which had
happened by 70 AD and the destruction of Jerusalem.  Everything in 2 Thessalonians continues to
look more and more like a prophecy about the end of the world.

Before that end comes, though, Paul says three events will
precede it.  The first is what the ESV
describes as “the rebellion”.  To be
honest, this is not my favorite translation here.  The Greek word in question is apostasia, from
which we get our English “apostasy”. 
This isn’t a rebellion by the Jews against the Romans.  This is a rebellion by God’s people against
God, in which those who used to follow Him fall away.  Sadly, we’ve already seen this great
apostasy.  It hadn’t happened yet in the
time of Paul, but it has certainly happened by now.  There are probably hundreds of churches in
the Joliet area, but only a tiny handful of them serve God according to His
word.  The rest, while claiming to follow
God, actually follow rules they’ve made up themselves.

Second, we see mention of a shadowy figure called “he who
restrains”.  Because the removal of “he
who restrains” will unleash the man of lawlessness, I think it’s safe to
identify “he who restrains” as a good guy, most likely God.  As I read this, I can’t help but be reminded
of Revelation 20, in which Satan is bound in prison for 1000 years (not
literal), only to be released for one last hurrah at the end of time.  I think we’re at some point in that
thousand-year period, a time in which Satan is not actively using world powers
to try to obliterate the church.

If I’m correct in that reading, we can’t identify the man of
lawlessness, because he hasn’t shown up yet. 
In previous visits to 2 Thessalonians 2, I’ve identified the man of
lawlessness with past or even present religious figures, but for the moment, at
least, I don’t think that’s right. 
Interpreting unfulfilled prophecy is a perilous business, but I think
this man of lawlessness has not yet been revealed and will only appear before
the very end.

However, Jesus will end this dark period of history with **THE
APPEARANCE OF HIS COMING**.  Here, consider
2 Thessalonians 2:8-12.  Once again, all
of this has a very end-of-Revelation-y feel to it, in a context where Paul does
not appear to be speaking apocalyptically. 
The man of lawlessness will only be undone when he is killed by Jesus
Himself, but before that end, he is going to deceive many with false signs and
wonders.

However, even if there isn’t some obvious religious figure
who is extending the realm of the devil with lying wonders, this is a text that
we should consider soberly even now.  You
don’t need to believe a lying miracle to fall into the condemnation of vs.
10-12.  Instead, the only qualification
for being in this grim group is to refuse to love the truth.

The truth is not an easy thing to love.  There’s a reason why three different Biblical
writers compare the word of God to a sword. 
It cuts.  It requires us to
believe things that to ordinary human experience are impossible.  It demands that we change our behavior in the
most fundamental way imaginable.  It
tells us that salvation is only possible through obedience to the truth, even
if obedience will cost us our lives. 
It’s hardly surprising, then, that people for thousands of years have
been finding excuses not to believe or obey the truth.  It’s hardly surprising when all too many
Christians pick which parts of the word they’re going to listen to and think
they’re OK in doing so.

Brethren, that is nothing more than the strong delusion that
God allows to come upon us if we don’t have a heart for Him.  If we’re determined to reject the truth
because it’s too harsh, we will always find a reason.  If we want to hang ourselves, there will
always be enough rope.  Discipleship is
an all-or-nothing proposition.  If we
aren’t willing to give Jesus everything we have, we’re in the same boat as
those who are willing to give Him nothing.

After this stern warning, Paul commends the Thessalonians to
**THE COMFORT OF CHRIST**.  Let’s look now at
2 Thessalonians 2:13-17.  The first thing
that’s worth noting here is Paul’s take on the process of salvation.  It looks like he’s handing the Calvinists a
gift in v. 13, but he takes it back in the next verse.  Yes, it is true that God chooses His people,
but He chooses them by calling them through the gospel.  Only those who believe and are sanctified are
chosen.

It’s like this.  Let’s
say that I want to put together a mariachi band, so I put an ad on Craigslist
inviting people to become part of my band. 
I specify that if they want to join up, they need to call me so we can
set up a meeting.  I get a handful of
phone calls, have a handful of meetings, and by the end of this process, I have
assembled my mariachi band.  I have
chosen them, yes, but they have also chosen me. 
That’s how it is with the gospel. 
God chooses us, but we choose Him too.

From here, Paul moves on to the solution to apostasy.  It’s simple. 
Don’t give up, hold to the truth you’ve been taught, and ask God and
Christ to establish you.  There’s a sense
in which every Christian is on a journey, but there’s also sense in which we
inherit eternal life by standing still. 
If we have the truth already, then any movement can only be a movement
away from truth.

**2 Thessalonians 3**

Naturally, we’re going to round out our study of 2
Thessalonians by looking at the third chapter. 
Within this chapter, the first context concerns **RECIPROCAL PRAYER**.  This appears in 2 Thessalonians 3:1-5.  One of the things that most stands out about
Paul in the epistles is his humility, and we see it on display again here.  He’s an apostle, the church in Thessalonica
is young and struggling, but he wants them to pray for him.

This should remind us that everybody needs prayers.  This is true most obviously when we’re
dealing with people who have disappointed us or otherwise don’t seem to be
headed in the right direction.  All too
often, I find myself grumbling inwardly about somebody who is disappointing
me.  I’ll get all indignant and
self-righteous, but then I remember to pray for them.  It’s funny how even a brief prayer can
completely change your attitude toward somebody.  It also does them a whole lot more good than
grumbling!

However, it’s not only struggling Christians who need our
help.  The pillars of the church need us
praying for them too.  It may look like a
brother or a sister has this discipleship thing figured out, but in reality,
they’re just barely holding it together. 
Even if not, the devil has a way of piling obstacles in our path.  There is no Christian too mature to benefit
from the prayers of others.

Next, we come to a discussion of **IDLENESS**.  Look here at 2 Thessalonians 3:6-12.  Much like Paul’s description of the day of
the Lord, this is a text that appears to relate back to 1 Thessalonians.  In 1 Thessalonians 4, Paul instructs the
Thessalonians to work with their own hands so that they can keep a good
reputation before outsiders and not be dependent on anybody.  Even this, apparently, was a repetition of
something that Paul had said while he was there.

However, evidently none of these positive exhortations had
had the effect they should have.  Rather
than working hard to support themselves, some of the Thessalonians were
apparently sitting around idly and stirring up trouble instead.  These troublemakers weren’t starving to
death, so presumably they were being supported in their idleness by other
members of the church there.

From this, I think there are two lessons we should
take.  The first is that Christians ought
to be hard workers.  It’s been years
since I had any co-workers, but back when I did, I can certainly remember guys
who were concerned with impressing the boss rather than doing any actual
work.  That shouldn’t be our
attitude.  We serve another Master first,
but one of the ways that we serve Him is by giving 100 percent to our earthly
masters too.  Christians should never be
malingerers.

Second, we must take care not to become enablers of sinful
brethren.  2 Thessalonians was written
long before the rise of the welfare state. 
These idle Christians would have been driven by their hunger to work
unless other Christians were helping them out. 
I’m sure those other brethren thought they were being helpful and
kindhearted, but they were really encouraging ungodliness.

Obviously, we also shouldn’t let any brethren who don’t want
to work ride the church gravy train indefinitely, or the individual gravy train
indefinitely, for that matter.  However,
it’s equally damaging when we’re tolerant of other spiritual problems.  When we see a brother or sister engaged in
sin, it’s our responsibility to go to them about it.  If we don’t, maybe because we don’t want to
hurt their feelings, we’re not doing them any favors.  We’re letting them destroy themselves.

The last section of the epistle contains, as is often the
case of Paul’s letters, some **CLOSING ADMONITIONS**.  Let’s read here from 2 Thessalonians 3:13-18.  Let’s note here first of all what Paul tells
us about church discipline.  He applies
this specifically to what he says in this particular epistle, but it’s equally
appropriate for us to handle any spiritual failing in this way.  The first element of this is social
sanction.  As Paul tells the
Thessalonians to keep away from the idle, here he tells them to have nothing to
do with the disobedient.  We can’t be
buddy-buddy with Christians who aren’t right with the Lord.

However, it’s obvious that this prohibition is not
absolute.  We can’t admonish people we
don’t talk to, nor can we show our brotherly love for them.  Like Jesus, we must always be ready to take
opportunities to call the sinner to repentance.

Finally, we see Paul doing something he also does at the end
of 1 Corinthians, Colossians, and Philemon. 
He includes a statement in his own handwriting to attest to the
authenticity of the epistle.  This is
important for a couple of reasons. 
First, it shows that Paul wasn’t the primary scribe for at least those
epistles.  Rather than writing his
letters, he dictated them, which is why they often have the feel of a speech
instead of a written document.



Second, even though obviously we don’t have the greeting in
Paul’s own handwriting anymore, and couldn’t recognize it if we did, this shows
the basic authenticity of these texts. 
Paul is not a myth, and his letters are not the product of forgers who
lived hundreds of years later.  They
contain the genuine truth that was revealed to him by God.
