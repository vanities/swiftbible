My decision to start writing about money matters from a
Biblical perspective actually came from a conversation I had with a friend
about my parents’ finances.  He and I
were riding together on our way to help a sister move, and he asked me how the
disposition of my father’s estate was going (my father having passed on a
couple of months ago).  From there, the
conversation shifted to a discussion of my parents’ financial
personalities.

Thinking about the discussion later, I realized that
regardless of the size of my father’s estate, the most significant legacy he
left me was what I had learned from him about how to handle money.  I think this is true for everybody.  It’s probably impossible for any of us to
think about our finances without being influenced by the people who raised
us.



Usually, this influence moves us to be like them.  It’s most natural for us to fall into the
patterns we absorbed as children.  If we
saw our parents fighting about money all the time, we will probably import that
conflict into our own marriages, one way or another.

Sometimes, though, the influence is exactly opposite.  I know people who have alcoholic parents who
will never, *ever* touch the
stuff.  Others have such a negative
perspective on their parents’ financial practices that they will do whatever
they can to avoid going down the same road.

This legacy is never going to be all good or all bad, and
this is certainly true in the case of my own parents.  My father was a frugal man, and my mother was
an extremely frugal woman.  Together,
they did the Dave Ramsey thing before Dave Ramsey was a thing.

Both of them were excellent long-term financial
planners.  For decades, their goal was to
save enough money for my dad to retire at 55, and they did.  They put three kids through college and had
enough left over to smooth their children’s lives after that.  I spent most of my childhood living in a
paid-for house.  From laying floor to
reupholstering furniture, they never hired anybody to do anything they could
learn to do themselves.  For vacations,
we went camping instead of to Disneyworld.

My father’s only financial vice was that for a decade or
two, he went through a phase of buying a new car every three years, and even
then, he (being an engineer by trade) drew out a series of carefully plotted graphs
“proving” that it was actually cheaper to buy a new car than it was to pay the
repair bills on an out-of-warranty older car. 
I’m not sure that my mother found the graphs persuasive, but she went
along with it because she loved him.

All of this sounds like an anecdote from a personal-finance
book, but looking back, I think it had its dark side.  My parents never talked about money in front
of the kids (though we did listen to Bob Brinker’s Money Talk on the way to
church), but after my mother passed on, my dad started having some of those
conversations with me.  Particularly, he
was concerned about the (from his perspective, unjustly) low interest rates
that banks were then offering on CD’s, etc., so he wanted to know where I
thought he should invest.  In retrospect,
I should have told him, “Spend it!  Enjoy
it!” but I didn’t.

The problem is that after a lifetime of saving, the habit of
saving can become so ingrained that you don’t even know what you’re saving for
anymore.  I don’t think that’s wrong, but
I do think it’s sad.  As Solomon observes
in Ecclesiastes 5:19, rejoicing in the fruit of one’s labors is the gift of
God.  My father certainly knew how to be
generous to others, but I’m not convinced he ever picked up the knack of being
generous to himself.

With parents like those, plus the evil memory of an extended
period of unemployment after I graduated from law school, it’s not hard to
figure where I end up.  When it comes to
money, I’m very good at living in the future, at calculating and storing up and
(most dangerously) seeking to protect myself from misfortune (more on that
later, probably).  I’m not very good at
living in the present.

Here, as everywhere else, we need to know ourselves.  We need to understand where we come from,
whether our inherited financial culture looks like my parents’ or its
opposite.  No matter what it is, our
parental legacy is going to come with strengths and weaknesses.  As human beings, we all tend to extremes,
which is why the Bible so consistently calls us to balance and peace.

Perhaps most of all, though, we need to consider the
legacies we are leaving for our children. 
Our parents powerfully influenced our financial thinking in ways that we
appreciate or wrestle with or resent, and as parents, we are exerting the same
level of influence.  Unless we work on
shaping that influence for good, we will faithfully export our money problems
for the next generation to grapple with. 
20 years from now, our money arguments with our spouses will be their
money arguments with their spouses.

Being part of a family means being part of something far
larger than ourselves.  We inherit
patterns of thinking from people we never knew and pass them on to people we
will never know, and these things define us as much as our DNA does.

However, simply because we live in the shadow of the past
doesn’t make us its prisoner.  We shape
our financial legacy as much as we are shaped by it.  We can squander a carefully preserved inheritance
of financial wisdom and leave our children with none of it, or we can plant
seeds of prudence that will only mature decades from now, so that what is a
struggle for us becomes habitual for our great-grandchildren.  We may not leave money to our heirs, but with
care, even in the financial realm alone, we can leave them something that is
far more valuable.
