**OVERVIEW:** The LPVT is a kind reader, though it’s
not great in the poetic sections of Scripture and has less-than-opaque paper.

I used the LPVT as my daily reader for a little over two
weeks.  Here’s what I thought of it:

**SIZE:**The size here is fine.  The
LPVT is slightly larger and heavier than a normal thinline, but only
slightly.  I didn’t have any problem
one-handing it or holding it however I wanted. 
I think it helps the LPVT that it has unusually large print for its size
and weight.  Even with my blind-as-a-bat
vision, I didn’t have to hold it under my nose to read it with my glasses
off.  Basically, the LPVT feels about
like a book you’re reading ought to.

**LAYOUT:** The LPVT has one of the lower characters-per-line
counts that I’ve seen in a text Bible. 
Normally, reference columns are what drives down CPL count the
most.  Here, though, the issue was the
comparatively large print in a comparatively small trim size.  However, even with the larger print, the LPVT
came in with a CPL count of just about exactly 40.  That’s the lower bound of what I like to see
in a reading Bible, but it’s still in the optimal range.

As a result, in prose, the LPVT reads smoothly.  In what is for me the key sign of a good reader,
there were several points during the review period when I forgot that I was
reviewing an unfamiliar Bible and simply got lost in the text.  One morning, I even kept reading past the end
of the reading because I wasn’t paying attention to where I was.

However, it’s also worth noting that I was reading out of
the LPVT at its best.  Like all other
Crossway Bibles, the layout of the LPVT makes use of frequent indentations of
varying lengths in the poetic sections of the Bible.  In single-column Bibles, this causes no
problems.  In double-column Bibles,
however, it makes many poetic lines shorter than they must be, thereby creating
a crop of one-word widows that take me out of the flow of my reading.  Even though I never read out of Psalms, I
glanced through the book, and it was obvious to me that the combination of
fairly-short lines and frequent indentation made more widows than the battle of
Gettysburg did.  This is a problem in
almost all double-column layouts, and it’s a worse problem for the LPVT than it
is in the great majority of text Bibles.

On the bright side, I thought the gutter margins in the LPVT
were fine.  I’d guess that they’re about ⅜”, which is extremely
generous in a thinline.  Generally, you
only need wider gutter margins on a thicker Bible.  The thicker the book block, the more of the
page falls into the gutter when the Bible is opened to the middle.  In my estimation, the inner margins in the
LPVT are actually a little larger than they need to be.  Perhaps Crossway intends to print the same
layout on better paper, which would increase the thickness of the block and
require a larger gutter margin.

Similarly, the LPVT is black-letter.  I can’t complain about the problems with the
execution of the words of Christ in red because the words of Christ aren’t in
red.  Generally, every red-letter Bible
manages to mess up something, so I’m glad to see this.

I was also glad to see that the LPVT is line-matched.  How far we’ve come in this!  Line-matching was first trumpeted as a
feature of the high-end Cambridge Clarions; now, it’s showing up in economy
Bibles like the LPVT.  However, laying
out the text so that the lines sort themselves into gray bars instead of
blending into a gray blob is every bit as important here as it is in the
Clarion (and for similar reasons, alas). 
Line-matching is one of the most significant reasons why the LPVT is as
readable as it is.

Finally, 10-point Lexion No. 1 is something of a luxury in a
smaller Bible.  Lexicon is one of the
most readable typefaces on the planet, and at 10 points, even if I wouldn’t
describe it as “large print”, exactly, it is something that most people will be
able to read.  I enjoyed it.  To tell you the truth, after the review
period was over, I found it difficult to go back to the 8-point thinlines that
I tend to prefer as readers.  I’ve been
sneaking readings out of a hardback Foundation In Touch.

**PRODUCTION
QUALITY.** I didn’t notice anything
out of the ordinary about the print quality in the LPVT.  Maybe the letters aren’t as finely formed as
you would expect to see in a high-end Jongbloed Bible, but the differences are
slight enough that only a Bible geek would notice or care.

Sadly, the same can’t be said for the paper.  During the review period, I happened to be
studying with a married couple that had only been attending services for a
couple of weeks.  Neither husband nor
wife had yet obeyed the gospel.  He was
curious about what sort of Bible he ought to be using, so I handed him the
LPVT, intending to give it to him if he liked it.  He didn’t. 
He asked, “How come I can see all those other words through the
paper?”

Admittedly, this gentleman’s eyes aren’t the greatest, but
his words are evidence that even a non-geek can see the LPVT’s issues with
paper opacity.  Here, we see that its
slimness and light weight come at a cost. 
The only way you can have a Bible with 1327 pages that’s three-quarters
of an inch thick is if you’re using some skinny, skinny paper.

By contrast, the famously opaque In Touch has only a couple
hundred more pages (1568 versus 1327), yet has a text block that’s almost twice
as thick.  The difference is in the
paper.  Thicker paper means a heavier
Bible but also less ghosting.  It’s true
that the line-matching of the LPVT mitigates what would otherwise be dreadful
ghosting issues.  However, as I usually
do, I find myself thinking that this would have been a better Bible if Crossway
had made it thicker with thicker paper.

**CONCLUSION:** Yeah, the paper’s not much good.  Yeah, the formatting in the Psalms is unhelpful.  However, I can’t overlook the pleasant times
I had reading from this Bible.  I think
it has earned an 8/10 as a reader, with an overall rating of 24.5/30, a solid
rating for a humble Bible.
