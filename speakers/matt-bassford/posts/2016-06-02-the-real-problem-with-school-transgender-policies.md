I don’t like howling with outrage about things I don’t
understand.  I much prefer to howl with
outrage about things I do understand. 
Most conservative Christians have heard about the recent guidance issued
by the Department of Education concerning transgender children in public
schools.  The ED wants school
administrators to understand that Title IX law, which prohibits sex
discrimination in schools, applies to the transgender.  I didn’t like the sound of that, so I sat
down and actually read through the guidance and cover letter myself.  You can find the letter [here](http://www2.ed.gov/about/offices/list/ocr/letters/colleague-201605-title-ix-transgender.pdf) and the guidance [here](https://www.justice.gov/opa/file/850991/download).



There’s some good news in this. 
For one thing, this isn’t the kind of regulation that is going to admit
a steady stream of perverts to locker rooms. 
All school restrooms are not going to go coed because of transgender
students.  In order to be admitted to a
facility set apart for a different biological sex, a student must first notify
the school of his/her determination to assert a different gender identity.  Administrators are supposed to grant such
requests only in cases where they believe such a determination is sincere.  That should be enough to weed out voyeurs and
sex offenders.

However, there is an obvious problem, and it has to do with
parental notification and consent.  The
cover letter states, “The Departments interpret Title IX to require that when a
student or the student’s parent or guardian, as appropriate, notifies the
school administration that the student will assert a gender identity that
differs from previous representations or records, the school will begin
treating the student consistent with the student’s gender identity.”

Any hymnist will tell you that conjunctions are important,
and some of the conjunctions in here are extremely important.  Quoth the cover letter, “when a student OR
the student’s parent or guardian, as appropriate, notifies the school
administration. . .”  Or, or, or.  In other words, when a student, without his
parents’ knowledge or consent, informs school administrators that he wishes to
assert a different gender identity, they will act on that when they deem it
appropriate and do not feel bound even to inform the parents.

That’s enough to make both my eyebrows go up.  When, exactly does this start?  Can I be confident that if my
middle-school-aged daughter tells the district that she wants to be treated as
a he, the district will at least let me know?

Apparently not.  Page
2 of the policies-and-practices document asks the question, “How do schools
communicate with the parents of younger students compared to older transgender
students?”  The document goes on to
summarize and quote from guidance from half a dozen educational entities, but
none of the guidance lays down the bright-line rule that parents of younger
children will be consulted.  Indeed,
guidelines from the Massachusetts DOE say, “Some transgender and

gender nonconforming students are not openly so at home for
reasons such as safety concerns or lack of acceptance. School personnel should
speak with the student first before discussing a student’s gender
nonconformity. . .”

I think it’s fair to say that any child of mine would,
indeed, encounter a certain lack of acceptance from me about a decision to go
transgender, and the younger the child, the less acceptance they would
find.  My resistance would have two
bases.  First, on the basis of my reading
of the first half of 1 Corinthians 11, I believe that presenting oneself as
transgender is sinful and morally wrong. 
God expects males to act like males and females to act like
females.  Second, I believe that humoring
such a request from a younger child is a good way to inflict lasting
psychological harm on that child.  I
cringe every time I see parents online proudly announcing that their
five-year-old is transgender.

I would never hurt or abuse one of my children who came out
as transgender, but I would do my strenuous best to talk them out of following
that course of action.  In fact, I would
consider myself responsible before God for doing so.  Furthermore, it is my right as a parent to
raise my children in the manner I deem best.

However, it is precisely parents like me, parents with a
traditional view of morality who feel strongly about such issues, who are least
likely to be informed.  All my kid has to
say is, “I don’t think my dad will accept me as I am,” and I’m out of the
loop.  I am quite certain that this will
start happening to parents very shortly, if indeed it has not already begun to
happen.  In practice, schools will involve parents with liberal convictions in these processes while excluding parents with conservative convictions.

I’m not entirely unsympathetic to the federal position
here.  Even I don’t want schools
informing parents if that will endanger their transgender children.  However, such student-safety exceptions
should be narrowly tailored and used with great care, only in instances where
harm is likely.

By contrast, what I see in the guidance as it is currently
written is a broad grant of discretion to administrators to act in what they
see as the best interest of transgender students.  I object. 
I believe that discretion belongs to parents, not bureaucrats.  Until my children turn 18, my wife and I
should be the primary participants in their major life decisions.

The government may well believe that children need to be
protected from their parents.  Who,
though, is going to protect children from the government?
