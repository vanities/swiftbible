A week or two ago, my wife was poking around on Facebook
when she encountered a status update containing the following question:  “Is it better to sing a hymn with the pitch
given by the song writer, or not use a pitch and ‘focus more on the heart of
the words’....is it possible to sing on pitch and focus on the words?”

Down the thread, the original poster added this
clarification:  “Some people are of the
belief that once you know a song, it should be ‘like a camp song’ and close the
book, ignore the notes and just sing the words....I believe that you can truly
focus on the words of the song while still singing the right notes....”

It is true that the question was not directed at me, but as
far as I’m concerned, anything on the Internet is fair game for a blog post, so
here goes!



Unlike most people who write hymns, my own gifts are heavily
skewed toward the lyric-writing side of things. 
When I first began to write hymns in 1997, I could not read so much as a
note of music, which made those early hymn-review sessions utterly
miserable.  Even today, my interest in
music is pretty much limited to what I need to know to write good hymns.  In short, I’m a word guy.  If there were a conflict between correct
pitch and other mechanics on the one hand and paying attention to the words on
the other, I would be the first to complain about it!

However, my perception is that precisely the opposite is the
case.  Pitching a hymn correctly and using
good mechanics while singing it will promote worship, not hinder it.  Let’s start by looking at the effect of
proper pitch when beginning a hymn.  Most
song leaders (myself emphatically included) don’t have perfect pitch, or even
near-perfect pitch.  As a result, we
benefit from using a pitch pipe (or smartphone, or tuning fork, or. . .) and
locating the correct pitch from there.

If we do this, at least two very important good things
happen.  First, the congregation gets the
pitch before the hymn begins, rather than from the first few notes the song
leader sings.  As a result, everybody
starts singing at the same time, instead of the song leader singing a
two-measure solo.  When everybody is
worshiping from the beginning instead of pitch-hunting, the overall meaning of
the hymn becomes clearer.

Second, the pitch pipe spares the song leader from the
Goldilocks dilemma of how to pitch the hymn just right.  This is important.  Good hymns are written so that the range for
each part is comfortable for those who sing that part.  Correct pitch makes sure that the comfortable
range on the page is actually comfortable for singing.  Sopranos don’t squeak when the pitch is
right.  Basses don’t croak.  Instead, everybody can sing naturally, look
past the music, and focus on worship. 
Similar arguments could be made about beating time or any other
mechanical element of song leading.

Attention to mechanics only causes a problem with a hymn or
praise song that is not written for the congregation.  Good hymn tunes have intuitive rhythms.  Sometimes, older hymns have tunes that don’t
“follow the bounce”, and praise songs originally written for use by a semipro
worship band are often rhythmically complex. 
A song leader who tries to adhere to these complicated rhythms will
generally confuse the congregation. 
However, the problem here really lies with the hymn, not with the
mechanics of its presentation.

There’s a deeper theme, though, that underlies the asking of
this question in the first place.  It’s
hard for a man to learn to blow pitch, beat time, and so on, especially if he
doesn’t have much musical background. 
It’s hard for a worshiper in the pew to learn to read music.  I’m not going to deny that I had a terrible
time learning all of the above!

Because these things are effortful and hard and require the
development of skill, people from our society tend to be suspicious of
them.  Americans are egalitarian.  We like to believe that Average Joe is just
as good as anybody, and anything that Average Joe can’t do easily must have
something wrong with it.  Usually, a
false dichotomy is drawn between Average Joe on the one hand, who doesn’t blow
pitch and isn’t beating time, but is “singing from his heart”, and Slick Sam on
the other, who blows pitch and beats time, but has reduced worship to a mere
choral performance.

I see things differently. 
Whether in song leading or any other aspect of serving God, I don’t
believe that sincerity can be offered as an excuse for defiant mediocrity.  God is great; God is holy.  He deserves our best, and beyond our
best.  Anybody who is truly sincere about
worshiping God is going to want not only to worship God to the fullest extent
of his abilities but also to extend those abilities as far as God will allow him.

Sometimes, that’s not very far.  I know wonderful Christians who can’t carry a
tune in a bucket.  The question is not
what they have.  It’s what they do with
what they’ve been given.

The effort we put into worship matters.  Effort is not the opposite of sincerity.  Indeed, effort and sincerity often walk hand
in hand.  It’s much easier for me to put
my mind on autopilot and forget to worship when I’m singing the melody line and
not reading music than when I am singing bass and reading every note.  The effort of (mostly) hitting the right note
keeps my mind engaged.

I don’t show that God matters to me by what I’m not willing
to do.  I show it by what I am willing to
do.  Learning correct mechanics, and
learning them so thoroughly that they become internalized, is hard.  There’s no question about that.

I believe God is worth it.
