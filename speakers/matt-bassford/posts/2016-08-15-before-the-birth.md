Several weeks ago, a brother in Christ who composes hymn
tunes approached me with an offer.  He
suggested that we collaborate on a hymn about the birth of Jesus.  I’m not one to say no to a hymn project, but
I have to say that the prospect filled me with some concern.  After all, thanks to Christmas, there have
been many, many spiritual songs written about Jesus’ birth.  Luke 2, which contains the birth narrative,
has to be one of the most heavily written-about texts in history.

As a result, I decided that if I wanted to say something
fresh, I’d have to stray from the hymn-as-nativity-scene path.  I couldn’t be writing about Mary and Joseph
and the shepherds and the wise men and the angels.  It’s been done.  Instead, I turned my attention not to Luke 2,
but to Luke 1, which is much less familiar territory.  Despite this, though, the account of Luke 1
may actually have much more to say that’s spiritually important.  Let’s look, then, at what happened, and
especially at what was said, before the birth of Jesus.



**John’s Birth Foretold**

The first main section of this chapter foretells the birth
of John the Baptist.  It begins, though,
with **LUKE’S PROLOGUE** to the entire
gospel.  It shows up in Luke 1:1-4.  This is extremely important because it
reveals Luke’s composition process.  He’s
aware that others have written accounts of the story of the gospel, and there’s
evidence in Luke that he used one of those accounts, the gospel of Mark, as a
source.

However, Luke perceived an opportunity to do something that
nobody else had done yet—tell the story of the life of Christ in consecutive
order.  In order to accomplish this, he
investigated everything carefully.  This
probably happened during the last part of Acts 24, so that when Paul was then
imprisoned in Caesarea for two years, Luke was traveling around Palestine
interviewing witnesses.  At the end of
this time, Luke was able to produce a narrative that would confirm for
Christians the exact truth of what they had been taught.

At first blush, this seems almost like a weakness.  After all, Luke isn’t claiming that the Holy
Spirit just injected all this information into his head.  However, it’s actually a strength.  If Luke says, “The Holy Spirit told me
everything I needed to know,” then only people who already believe in the
inspiration of the Bible will believe what he says.  However, Luke is holding himself forth as a
historian, and his gospel as a historical document.  We don’t have to start out in faith.  Instead, we can come to his narrative as we
would to any historical document and be persuaded by what it says.

The first part of Luke’s narrative concerns the interactions
between **ZACHARIAS AND THE ANGEL.** Let’s read here from Luke 1:5-17.  Luke tells us that Zacharias is a priest, and
both he and his wife, Elizabeth, are righteous people.  However, they have a problem.  She’s barren, and she has now passed the age
of childbearing anyway.  This is
serious.  It means that when Zacharias
and Elizabeth become unable to care for themselves, which is going to happen
shortly because they’re old, they aren’t going to have any children to take
care of them.

However, when Zacharias is performing priestly duties in the
temple, an angel appears to him and informs him that despite the physical
impossibility of it, he and Elizabeth are going to have a boy.  What’s more, this isn’t going to be any
ordinary boy.  He is, in fact, going to
be the fulfillment of the prophecy of Malachi 4, which predicts that God is
going to send Elijah the prophet to prepare the children of Israel for the day
of His coming.

In context, this is astonishing.  For hundreds of years, there have been no
Jewish prophets.  The Jewish nation
itself is in a strange in-between place, living in the promised land, but
subject to Roman domination.  The Jews
have been crying out to God to deliver them from their oppressors, and just
like in the beginning of the book of Exodus, God has heard them and is going to
act.  The birth of John is a big deal,
but it’s going to pave the way for something even bigger.

However, even in the midst of earthshaking events, **ZACHARIAS’ REACTION** proves that people
are still people.  The story continues in
Luke 1:18-25.  The angel tells Zacharias
that all his dreams are going to come true, and Zacharias promptly starts
giving the angel reasons why it’s not going to happen.  He asks for a sign to prove the truth of what
the angel is saying, and as is often the case when God’s message is questioned,
Zacharias gets a less-than-pleasant sign. 
Because he dared to question the angel, he’s going to be mute until the
baby is born.

Zacharias emerges from the temple, pantomimes what has just
happened, and returns home to his wife. 
There, he gets a second sign.  His
elderly wife becomes pregnant.

We might be inclined to get frustrated with Zacharias and
his fellow skeptics throughout the pages of Scripture, but before we do, we
need to remember that they’re doing us an important service.  They show us that people 2000 years ago
weren’t any more inclined to believe in supernatural events than we are
today.  These are not a bunch of
credulous fools who are so predisposed to accept a miracle that they hear a
bird one morning and think it’s an angel talking to them.  Instead, it is their incredulity that stands
out.  Even when an actual angel appears
to Zacharias, Zacharias doesn’t believe him because the angel’s word
contradicts his understanding of the way the world works.  These aren’t people who are manufacturing
signs out of nothing.  Instead, they are so
skeptical of signs that only a massive weight of evidence can convince
them.

**Jesus’ Birth Foretold**

After the appetizer, we come to the main course:  the foretelling of the birth of Jesus.  This starts with a conversation between **MARY AND GABRIEL**.  It’s recorded for us in Luke 1:26-38.  Before we plunge into the story, let’s
consider the female protagonist.  We
don’t learn much about Mary here other than that she is a virgin engaged to a
man named Joseph.  However, based on
Jewish marriage customs, this likely makes her between 14-16 years old, a
teenager.

However, this teenager receives an angelic visitation.  He informs her that she is going to conceive
by the power of the Holy Spirit and give birth to a Child who will be the Son
of God and reign forever on the throne of David.  This all sounds very impressive, but from
Mary’s perspective, there’s a catch. 
Even though she will know that she has done nothing wrong, everybody
else, including her husband-to-be, is going to look at her pregnant belly and
assume she has.  We think of the Word
becoming flesh as a glorious event, but for Mary, it is wrapped in layers of
shame.

Nonetheless, the only answer she gives Gabriel is, “Let it
be done to me according to your word.” 
What faith she displays here!  What
willingness to put God first and herself second!  Mary herself would be the first to say that
she’s not worthy of our veneration, but we see clearly here that like so many
other men and women of faith, she is worthy of our imitation.

Next up, we see an encounter between **MARY AND ELIZABETH**.  It’s
recorded in Luke 1:39-45.  More than
anything else, this shows that Mary isn’t serenely comfortable with what’s
about to happen to her.  As young women
have done since time immemorial, when she finds herself in a serious situation,
she runs to an older female relative she can trust.  There, even before she has a chance to
explain the situation, Elizabeth is filled with the Holy Spirit, and even the
in-utero John the Baptist leaps in the womb. 
Apparently Mary is already pregnant by this time, and both of them are
reacting to the presence of Jesus, even before He is born.

There are a couple of things worth noting here.  First of all, this story provides part of the
evidence for the Biblical case against abortion.  This story clearly treats John as John and
Jesus as Jesus, even before either one of them has been born.  It is not the passage from the womb that
makes any of us alive.  Instead, it is
God, who endows us with a soul while we are within it.

Second, I think there’s something to learn from Elizabeth
here that the text doesn’t even talk about. 
Mary doesn’t come see her, and ultimately end up staying with her for
three months, because she picked Elizabeth’s name out of a hat.  Instead, she came because she trusted
her.  In the same way, we need to work on
being trustworthy, so that when our young people or others who are spiritually
vulnerable have a problem, they feel comfortable taking it to us.

This section concludes with **MARY’S SONG.** We find it in Luke
1:46-56.  Even though the text doesn’t
say for sure, this sounds like a conversation between a couple of inspired
women chatting back and forth with each other. 
Either that, or our Jewish teenager just happened to be a skilled
extemporaneous poet!  Obviously, I think
the first is more likely.

Interestingly, even though Luke is a Gentile, the song that
he records, along with the song of Zacharias in a few verses, looks exactly
like the Jewish poetry of the Old Testament, especially like the song of Hannah
in 1 Samuel 2.  Nothing else later on in
the New Testament has quite this feel to it. 
There’s a sense in which this is exactly appropriate.  Jesus hasn’t died on the cross yet, everybody
is still under the Law of Moses, so God provides for one last flowering of
Hebrew poetry.

I think the content is equally fascinating.  Remember; this is Mary the young, single
pregnant woman talking.  Right now, she
is exactly at the bottom of her society’s hierarchy.  In the eyes of folks like the chief priests,
Mary is dirt.  What is she singing about,
then?  She’s singing about how God has
regard for the humble and is going to overthrow the proud.  God is going to feed the hungry but send the
rich away without anything.  God is still
this way today.  He has no regard for
those of high standing in the eyes of men, but He delights in the humble who
seek Him.

**The Birth of John**

The final section of our story before the birth of Jesus
concerns the birth of His older relative, John. 
This begins with **THE BIRTH
ITSELF.** Let’s read here from Luke
1:57-66.  Even though we don’t have
anything splashy like a virgin birth here, it’s still evident that God is at
work.  First of all, we see that
Elizabeth continues to be inspired.  She
knows that the baby’s name is supposed to be John, even though Zacharias could
hardly have told her, and she is extremely unlikely to be literate so as to
read a note from him.  Nobody else seems
to know this, so the most likely candidate for her understanding is the Holy
Spirit.

Naturally, the family isn’t going to take a woman’s word for
what the name of the baby is supposed to be, so they go to the mute
father.  He is literate, as we would
expect from a priest, so he writes for them that his name will be John, rather
than Zacharias Jr.

Interestingly, it is at this moment, not at the actual
moment of the birth, that Gabriel’s promise to him is fulfilled.  Only when Zacharias’ endorsement of God’s
name for the baby shows his faith is his tongue released from being mute.  Not surprisingly, this creates a lot of
discussion and even fear among those who hear of it.

Next, though apparently some time elapses before this
happens, we see Zacharias prophesy **ABOUT
JESUS.** This section of his song
appears in Luke 1:67-75.  Like Mary,
Zacharias is in fine Old-Testament form here, and it’s striking to me how differently
his original audience would have heard this from the way that we read it
today.  We look at this, we know it’s
about Jesus, and we see words like “redemption”, “salvation”, and “rescue”.  Without even thinking about it, we read these
things, and correctly so, as being about Jesus’ saving work on the cross.

However, when we impose that reading, we forget that we’re
on a different side of the mystery than Zacharias’ original audience.  At this point in the time, absolutely nobody
outside of God and Jesus understood that He was going to die for the sins of
mankind.  All these birth narratives are
most likely taking place around 4 B.C., and at this time, when the Jews hear
“salvation”, they almost certainly add “from the Romans” to the end of it.  They weren’t interested in Jesus putting away
sin for all time by the sacrifice of Himself. 
Instead, they wanted out from under foreign domination, with maybe an
opportunity to do a little dominating themselves.

Even today, God gives us what we need, not what we want, and
we need to be careful about imposing our wants on His will.  We must read the Scripture for what it says,
not for reinforcing our pre-existing beliefs about what it ought to say.  What matters is the truth.

Our context concludes with Zacharias’ prophecy **ABOUT JOHN.**This appears in Luke 1:76-80. 
The distinction is clear.  Jesus
is going to be the deliverer, but John is going to be the prophet who prepares
the way for Him.  We might read this and
think, “Surely here is where the Holy Spirit is giving the game away!”  After all, Zacharias talks about John
bringing the knowledge of salvation that leads to forgiveness of sins.  To us, that sounds like it’s one of the five
steps of the plan of salvation.

To them, though, it would have sounded like something
else.  In the Old Testament, before God
shows up to deliver them from the Midianites or the Philistines or the
Assyrians or the whoevers, they always have to repent and give up on their
idolatry first.  This too would have been
understood as a prelude to physical, earthly victory.

We know, of course, that that’s not how things worked
out.  The Jews’ relentless focus on
deliverance from the Romans kept them from recognizing the Messiah when He did
come.  They rejected Him and in so doing
doomed themselves to destruction by the legions just 40 years later.  Their expectations kept them from seeing the
truth.

Today, we see the truth about some things.  We see that the enemy Jesus came to defeat
was not the Romans, but the devil.  He
didn’t win His victory by killing His enemy but by dying Himself.  And yet, this paradoxical triumph is one we
still celebrate 2000 years later.  Now we
must remember Him, but we must also consider ourselves.  Is there anywhere that our preconceptions are
blinding us to the truth about what God wants from us?  Let’s ponder as we partake.
