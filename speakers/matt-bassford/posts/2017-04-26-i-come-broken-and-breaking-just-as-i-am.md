Yesterday, I saw a discussion on Facebook about “I Come
Broken”, [this modern reworking](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TGXDfxWM2r0) of
the classic hymn “Just As I Am”.  The
discussion revolved around the doctrinal suitability of “I Come Broken”.  Is the hymn a smug reaffirmation of our own
sinfulness and a demand that God accept us this way?  I don’t think so; both old and new versions
of the hymn are pretty clear that the singer is coming to God for cleansing and
healing, which implies an understanding that we are not acceptable just as we
are.

However, I observed that even though I didn’t have content
issues with “I Come Broken”, I did have other problems with it.  This seems to be as good a time as any to
explore them.



My issues begin with the original hymn.  It’s not that I have problems with “Just As I
Am”; indeed, the opposite is true!  [I've written recently](http://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2017/04/why-are-good-hymns-so-hard-to-write.html) about how hard it is to write a good hymn, and “Just As I Am” is undeniably not
only a good hymn, but a great hymn.  It’s
a moon shot.

Charlotte Elliot was a skilled writer, and “Just As I Am” is
her masterwork.  Every hymn has its weak
spot (if I had been her editor, I would have fussed at her about forcing “With
many a conflict, many a doubt” into eight syllables), but overall the writing
here is superb.  It exhibits phenomenal
structure (technically demanding aaab rhyme scheme, beginning and ending each
verse with the same repeated phrases), strong Biblical backing (I cannot read 2
Corinthians 7:5 without singing the hymn in my head), and clear, simple
writing.  Worshipers comprehend it
easily, they identify with it, and they sing it from the heart.

It doesn’t hurt either that the usual tune for “Just As I
Am”, WOODWORTH, was written by William Bradbury.  The world of classical music has its Bachs
and Beethovens and Mozarts, but nobody has ever done a better job of writing tunes
for the ordinary singers of the congregation than Bradbury.  WOODWORTH is only one of many home-run hymn
tunes on his resume.  It’s simple, easy
to sing, and highlights the emotional contours of the text perfectly
(protip:  writing with strong structure,
as Elliot did, helps composers craft evocative music).

Together, text and tune provoke powerful worship.  They’ve been used widely and regularly ever
since WOODWORTH was written (it postdates the lyrics by 14 years), and
deservedly so.  “Just As I Am” is one of
the gems of English hymnody.  All of us
should thank God for allowing us to sing it.

So.  We’ve got this
musical Mona Lisa, and about 2011, along comes contemporary Christian music
artist Travis Cottrell and draws a mustache on it.  Honestly, I don’t think that the mustache is
disastrously executed.  As CCM writing
goes, “I Come Broken” is better than most (though perhaps in the same sense that
Ho Chi Minh was better than most totalitarian dictators).  However, that doesn’t make it any less a
mustache.

Lyrically, the bridge and extra verse that he added aren’t
terrible, but they certainly aren’t up to the quality of the original.  First of all, they don’t really add to the
meaning of the hymn.  They’re a longer
and less elegant restatement of Elliott’s existing content.  Cottrell didn’t have anything new to
say.  Why say it?  (Though admittedly, “Because I want to sell
CD’s!” does provide a practical answer here.)

Technically speaking, Elliott’s rhymes are clean and
strong:  plea/me/Thee, not/blot/spot,
about/doubt/without, and so on.  Cottrell’s
rhymes, on the other hand, are shaky at best. 
In the bridge, he adopts a looser rhyme scheme than the original
(rhyming only every other line) and still ends up rhyming healed/filled, which
doesn’t work anyplace north of the Mason-Dixon Line.

However, his lack of technical skill really gets exposed in
his additional verse, where he has to match Elliott’s original rhyme
scheme.  It’s demanding, no doubt, but
she nails it except for one approximate rhyme in the often-omitted final
verse.  By contrast, Cottrell in his
single verse offers up lost/bought/cross, none of which are more than
approximate rhymes with each other. 
Elliott goes 14/15; Cottrell, 0/3.

The effect is kind of like when you’re watching the Olympics
on TV, and a figure skater goes up for a triple axel, but she whiffs it and
falls sprawling instead.  She will get up
and gallantly pretend that nothing went wrong, but everybody knows better.  Similarly, Cottrell tries to imitate Elliott,
but he ends up with ice shavings all over his backside.

The additional music for “I Come Broken” fares no
better.  Bradbury was a genius.  Cottrell may be a genius of CCM, but in the
congregation, his reworking doesn’t work. 
My experience with singing “I Come Broken” has been bizarre.  Everybody begins, locked in on the familiar
harmony of “Just As I Am”, and the worship is wonderful.

Then, the singing runs into Cottrell’s bridge like the *Titanic* into an iceberg.  The harmony isn’t intuitive, so you end up
with mass confusion and people singing in three different keys.  Those who are able to worship through that
would also be able to worship through a construction crew trooping into the
auditorium in mid-hymn and jackhammering up the floor.  Sad to say, I am not among them.

The real problem here, though, is not that Travis Cottrell
isn’t skilled enough to rewrite “Just As I Am”. 
It’s that he tried.  Bluntly, he
did what he did because Elliott and Bradbury are dead and couldn’t defend
themselves from him.  Make no mistake;
they would not be pleased, nor would they permit his adaptation if they were
able to prevent it.  If a journeyman like
me can see the problems with Cottrell’s efforts, what would a master think?

“Just As I Am” is Elliott and Bradbury’s masterpiece.  It is their memorial before the Lord.  “I Come Broken” is a desecration of that
memorial by somebody without the ability or discernment to recognize that his
additions don’t measure up.  I’m a better
technician than Cottrell, but no way in the world would I touch “Just As I Am”.  It doesn’t need fixing, and fundamentally, it’s
not my hymn to fix anyway.

Let’s show some respect for the dead.  Let’s leave “Just As I Am” just as it was.
