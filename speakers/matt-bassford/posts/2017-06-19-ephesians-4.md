A few weeks ago, we went through the entirety of Ephesians
1-3 in one sermon.  I could have spent
far more time on the text than 45 minutes. 
It is, after all, an extremely dense portion of Scripture!  However, I thought it was valuable to take
the whole thing at a run so that we could see the overall point:  God has given us such a great salvation that
we should anticipate the end of our earthly lives and the beginning of eternity
with hope and joy, not uncertainty and fear.

However, the magnificent grace of God shouldn’t merely move
us to awe.  It should move us to action
too.  When God has done so much for us,
it’s only right for us to offer our lives in service to Him, not because we
have to in order to earn our salvation, but because that’s what thankful people
do.  It pleases God when we imitate His
Son.  Why not do that?

This morning, then, let’s consider the actions that Paul
says we ought to be moved to.  Let’s
consider the applications that he makes from our great salvation in Ephesians
4.



**Gifts and the Body**

Paul’s exploration of this topic begins with his discussion
of gifts and the body.  Within this, his
first subtopic is **THE SEVEN ONES.** He lays them out in Ephesians 4:1-6.  Interestingly, Paul doesn’t take his
applications in the direction that I, at least, would anticipate.  He’s not talking about how we need to be holy
and stand strong against sin, at least not here.  Instead, he’s making points that have to do
with the way we treat other Christians. 
If we really understand how much God has blessed us, he tells us, we’re
going to be humble, gentle, and patient with one another.  Those are the hallmarks of somebody who really
understands this whole grace thing.

We do this, Paul says, so that we can maintain our spiritual
unity with one another.  However, that
only leads us to another question.  Who
are the people with whom we ought to be unified?  Paul’s answer is that the unity of the spirit
is based on seven beliefs.  We are
supposed to be united in spirit with those who acknowledge one body, one
Spirit, one hope, one Lord, one faith, one baptism, and one God.  These are the famous Seven Ones of
Christianity, and anybody who holds to these things is a brother or sister in
Christ.

Sadly, it’s not hard to see how these things make it
impossible for us to be united with many who claim to be Christians.  Anybody who belongs to a denomination, for
instance, cannot share our convictions on the one body, because denominations
are about division and not unity. 
Similarly, people who don’t accept the one baptism of Scripture aren’t
Christians either.

However, that still leaves a more expansive definition of
“brother” than we might be comfortable with. 
Not all the folks who are right on the Seven Ones worship at a building
that says “Church of Christ” on the sign. 
I don’t mean to imply that all such people are right with God.  We all know that you can believe the right
things and not be doing them.  However,
they are Christians.

After this, Paul examines the role of **GIFTS** in the church.  Let’s
read together from Ephesians 4:7-12. 
Paul starts things off by quoting from Psalm 68, which he applies
Messianically.  It is Christ who has
ascended on high after His resurrection from the dead, bringing with Him all
the captives He has rescued from the devil. 
As part of His triumphal return to heaven, though, He distributed gifts
to His people.

Ordinarily, this would lead us to expect a discussion of the
miraculous spiritual gifts of Scripture: 
tongues, healing, and so on. 
However, the gifts of Ephesians 4 are people, specifically, apostles,
prophets, evangelists, and shepherds and teachers.  Today, the apostles and prophets bless us only
through the writings they left behind, but we still have preachers and elders.

At this point, though, Paul says something critical about
what all of these men do.  They don’t do
the work of the church.  They don’t do
the building up.  Instead, they equip the
ordinary saints of the congregation so that they are able to serve and edify
the church.

This, brethren, is something that we must internalize if we
want our congregation to be all it should be. 
God’s vision for the church is not that it should have a few workers
with a whole bunch of admiring spectators. 
We aren’t supposed to be watching the boxers in the ring.  We’re supposed to be the boxers.  To switch metaphors, we are supposed to be
the soldiers on the front lines of the army of the Lord.  Preachers and elders are there to keep the
saints equipped, but it is the saints who are supposed to do the fighting.

This means that we must be very suspicious of efforts to
outsource any of our spiritual responsibilities to someone else.  It’s not the elders’ job to look after
struggling saints.  It’s the job of every
Christian.  It’s not the preacher’s job
to evangelize the community.  That too is
the job of every Christian.  We must not
imagine that we have discharged our responsibility to do either one because we
have elders or a preacher.  God wants the
whole church to do the work of the church, and that includes every member of
it.  Either we’re working according to
His vision, or we aren’t.

From here, Paul proceeds logically to **THE GOAL OF GIFTS.** He
describes it in Ephesians 4:13-16.  Paul
starts things off by explaining how long the work of the gifted men must
continue.  They have to keep equipping
the saints for the work of service until every saint knows as much as and has
the maturity of Christ, until the spiritual stature of every Christian in the
congregation is indistinguishable from His. 
As far as I can tell, this is a flowery way of saying, “The work of
equipping the saints never stops!”  It’s
true for everybody else, and it’s true for us.

Paul identifies the key element in this process as speaking
the truth in love.  This is a remarkable
concept, both for its simplicity in thought and its difficulty in
application.  There are few things in
Scripture that are more difficult than getting the balance of speaking truth in
love right.

I think we easily see the problems with truth without
love.  This is the stereotypical example
of the confrontational Christian of earlier generations who went around whaling
on people with the club of the Spirit. 
Today, though, I think we struggle much more with love without
truth.  This is when we see spiritual
problems in people we love, both inside and outside the church, but rather than
sitting down with them and sharing our concerns, we say nothing to them in
order to avoid an unpleasant situation. 
Brethren, love without truth is no more righteous than truth without
love!

Let’s also consider the connection between truth, love, and
usefulness.  Every one of us can think of
things we aren’t doing for the Lord, even things that we’re Scripturally
allowed to do.  Why is that?  Why am I, for instance, not the personal
worker I could be?  Often, I think we’re
tempted to say, “That’s not my gift,” but I fear that many times, what we
prefer to believe is an absence of giftedness is actually an absence of
love.  We don’t love enough to do what we
should.  Only when we devote ourselves to
fostering the love of Christ in our hearts will our work be what it should be.

Before the Lord’s Supper, then, let’s think first about our
great Head, about the way that He surrendered Himself for us and gave us His
perfect example of love.  We honor these
things each first day of the week in the bread and the cup, and we should.  However, He also wants us to honor Him by
carrying out His work as members of His body. 
Are we?  Let’s ponder as we
partake.

**Spiritual Renewal**

Next, let’s consider Paul’s thoughts on spiritual
renewal.  This begins with his scathing
description of **THE WALK OF THE
GENTILES.** Here, look at Ephesians
4:17-20.  As character portraits in the
Bible go, this isn’t a pretty one!  It
starts with hardness of heart.  These are
people who are indifferent to the grace and love of God and the truth of the
gospel.  Because their hearts are hard,
they are ignorant and without understanding. 
They don’t care, so they don’t bother to know.  This alienates them from the life of
God.  If you’re ignorant of God’s will,
you’re not going to do it except by accident. 
Instead, they throw themselves into every kind of evil.

There are two unpleasant truths we need to own about this
description.  First, this used to be
us.  Indeed, if you are of age and not a
Christian, this is who you are.  This is
where you find yourself in the Bible. 
Either we are living for God or we are living for ourselves, and as Paul
makes clear, this is an ugly way of life. 
The mere fact that we have come to the church building this morning, or
that we were “raised in the church” does not change the ugly truth.  There is no spiritual glow that envelops us
because we are in the presence of people who are righteous and dedicated to the
Lord.  Parking a Port-a-Potty next to a
flowerbed doesn’t make the Port-a-Potty stink less, and the proximity of
righteousness does not make sin more appealing to God.

Second, and even more alarming, this could still be us even
though we have obeyed the gospel.  Just
because we’ve gotten wet doesn’t mean that we’ve escaped this spiritual
trap.  In particular, we must beware the
hardheartedness that leads to ignorance. 
If we choose not to make time for the assemblies of the saints, if we
can find room in the schedule for Candy Crush but not reading the word of God
on our own, we’ve got a heart problem. 
We are cutting ourselves off from the truth that will make us free, and
sooner or later, the sins of the Gentiles are going to start cropping up in our
lives as a result.  The life of God
starts in the word.  There’s no other
way.

Instead, we are
called to **SPIRITUAL RENEWAL.** Consider Ephesians 4:21-24.  Paul could not be clearer here.  The Lord’s expectation for all of us,
Christian or no, is that we put off the old, corrupt, deceitful life, we renew
our minds and hearts, and we put on our new selves, which partake of the righteousness
and holiness of God.

This is a process that begins with baptism, but it certainly
doesn’t end there.  Instead, this kind of
spiritual renewal is a continual process. 
We all came to the Lord with our lives filled with a variety of corrupt
practices.  They could be anything from
lying to sexual sin to disobedience to parents. 
As different as they are, we adopted them for the same reason:  we cared more about making ourselves happy
than we did about anyone else, even God.

When we obey the gospel, even though the guilt of our sins
is washed away, the waters of baptism do not erase all of those evil
habits.  Trying to remove those sins from
our lives, then, is like trying to turn a weedbed into a garden.  Even if we pull all the weeds up, the seeds
are still there in the soil, and they will sprout again just as soon as our
back is turned.

None of us can solve our sin problems overnight.  It requires not just the change of our
outward behavior, but an inward spiritual renewal.  All these good habits that I’m constantly
encouraging--regular personal Bible study, prayer, good works and all the
rest—aren’t things that we should do just because.  We should do them because they renew our
hearts.  They dig up the roots and filter
out the seeds so that only God’s goodness will sprout and bear fruit in our
lives.

If you find yourself locked in a struggle with some sin,
then, don’t just attack it at the behavior level.  Attack it at the heart level.  Get rid of the things in your mind that make
you want to sin, and replace them with the things that make you want to glorify
God.

**The Renewed Life**

Finally this morning, let’s consider what Paul tells us
about the renewed life.  This is found in
Ephesians 4:25-32, and I want to read the text and then go through and talk
applications.

The first application that Paul makes concerns **LYING.**Here, he’s particularly concerned with lying to other
Christians.  His logic is that we
shouldn’t lie to one another because we’re members of one another.  Think about how much chaos it would cause if
our body parts lied to us!  It’s bad when
we put our hand on a red-hot stove element, but it would be even worse if our
hand insisted to us, “Nope, everything’s fine over here!”  The nose says, “Hand, I smell burning
flesh.  Are you’re sure you’re all
right?”, but the hand continues hiding the truth.  The result would likely be the crippling or
even the loss of the body part that wasn’t being forthright.

In the same way, friends, we need to be forthright with one
another.  If we’re struggling
spiritually, we need to own it.  This
doesn’t mean that we have to come forward and announce it to the whole church,
but we should involve somebody else. 
Even more to the point, when somebody approaches us with an offer of
help, we shouldn’t tell them we don’t need it. 
As with the hand on the hot stovetop, that could result in the loss of a
body part, except this time the body part is us!

Second, Paul warns us against **SIN.** In my opinion, this is
one of the most misunderstood passages in the Bible.  I hear people all the time saying, “I can be
angry, as long as my anger doesn’t make me sin.”  That’s not what the text is saying, brethren,
and we need look no further than the blanket condemnation of anger in v. 31 to
see the truth here.  Paul did not tell us
we could be angry in v. 26 and then contradict himself five verses later!

Instead, as we see when we look at Psalm 4, which this verse
is quoting, Paul is actually saying, “Be angry so that you do not sin.”  “Be angry that the devil is tempting you, and
don’t give him the chance to get you to give in.”  Our anger about sin is often directed at
others, but one of the most useful places we can point it is at ourselves.  After all, ours is the only behavior we can
change.

Third, Paul discourages **THEFT.** We know we shouldn’t steal.  We probably also know that we shouldn’t
engage in dishonest business practices, cheat on our taxes, copy copyrighted
works without permission, and engage in a host of other behaviors that amount
to stealing.

What’s interesting, though, is what Paul reveals about our
motivations.  He says that we should
instead work with our hands so that we can not only provide for ourselves, but
for others.  Theft, like all other sins, is
an expression of selfishness.  We will
only steal if we regard our money selfishly. 
If, however, we consider our financial blessings as something to
contribute to the Lord’s work and to use to help the needy, there’s no more
selfishness and no more motivation for theft.

Next, we come to **CORRUPTING
SPEECH.** Look here at Ephesians
4:29-30.  This has always been one of the
toughest parts of the tail end of Ephesians 4 for me to wrap my mind
around.  The key is the word that is
translated in the ESV as “corrupting”. 
It’s the Greek word *sapros*,
and it’s used elsewhere in the New Testament mainly in the parables of Jesus,
where it describes rotten fruit and spoiled fish.  Basically, we’re being warned against speech
that stinks, words that are unprofitable, unpleasant, and make you wrinkle your
nose up in disgust.  In context, words like
these are among the things that grieve the Holy Spirit.

Instead, we are supposed to use words that give grace, words
that smell good instead of stinking. 
This doesn’t mean that we only tell people what they want to hear;
instead, we have a responsibility to build up with our speech.  Even then, though, even when we’re conveying
an unpleasant truth, our words should smell like Pine Sol instead of last
week’s garbage.  They should be the kind
of words that honest people will thank us for saying.

Finally, we come to **ANGER.** Let’s read from Ephesians 4:31-32.  Here is where we encounter the actual
Biblical teaching about anger instead of in “Be angry and do not sin,” a few
verses up.  Obviously, there were times
when Jesus was angry, but His anger was never personal.  Instead, it was anger on His Father’s behalf
when His law was violated.

Usually, though, we aren’t mad because somebody is
sinning.  We’re mad because they’ve
offended us in some way, because they haven’t shown us the respect to which we
believe we’re entitled.  That kind of
anger is rooted in selfishness, and it’s never acceptable.

Rather than putting on a heart of anger and bitterness,
Jesus calls us to put on a heart of kindness, tenderness, and forgiveness.  How can we do that?  How can we stand to let other people get away
with hurting and diminishing us?  Paul’s
answer is simple.  We can forgive them
because God in Christ has forgiven us. 
By His mercy, we are freed to be merciful.
