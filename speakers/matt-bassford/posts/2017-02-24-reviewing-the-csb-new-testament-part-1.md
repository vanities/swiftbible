As part of my review of the Holman CSB Large-Print UltraThin
Reference Bible, I decided that I also wanted to review the translation.  My somewhat doubtful credentials are
these:  I’m not a Greek scholar, but I
have spent a considerable amount of time reading and studying the Bible in a
number of translations, with a particular focus on the formal-equivalent trio
of the NKJV, ESV, and especially NASB.  I
have a pretty good memory, and I have a sense of how the logic of the Bible
works and how it is supposed to sound.



To test the CSB as a translation, I read the New Testament
and noted every passage I came to that didn’t seem to read right.  In this, I wasn’t concerned with style.  There are places where the CSB reads
strangely but accurately, as with the CSB rendition of 1 Corinthians 10:7,
which reads in part, “The people sat down to eat and drink, and got up to
party.”  That might not be how I’d put
it, but it is what the Israelites did.  Indeed,
it’s actually clearer than the formal-equivalent “got up to play”.

Similarly, I wasn’t interested in places where I thought the
CSB had a particularly apt or precise rendering.  For instance, I think the CSB is closer to
the Greek original in Hebrews 5:13 than the revered NASB is.  Also, when the CSB describes Gentiles in
Ephesians 3:6 as “partners in the promise” (contra the NASB’s “fellow partakers
of the promise”), that’s simply strong English style.  There’s little meaning lost, and it’s vivid
and memorable in a way that the NASB isn’t. 
There are actually quite a few spots like these, to the point where
analyzing them all would be tedious.

Instead, I focused on places where the CSB appeared to me to
have a different meaning than the formal-equivalent translations, or to miss an
important nuance.  In real life, I’m not
going to decide whether to use a translation, or not, because of the things I
like about it.  The NLT has lots of
lovable features, but I’m never going to preach an expository sermon out of
it.

In real life, I’m going to adopt a translation based on the
presence or absence of translation-killers, passages where I simply can’t
accept the translation’s rendering.  “Sin
nature” in the NIV84 is a translation-killer. 
For that matter, so is Acts 22:16 in the NLT and HCSB.  In these places, the translation distorts the
meaning of a doctrinally significant text so much that it “tilts the playing
field” toward a particular theological position.  This is a danger for all translations, and as
translator freedom increases, so does the danger.

Also, I didn’t delve into the Old Testament at all, so I’m
not going to be commenting at length on, for instance, the CSB translation of
Psalm 51:5 (short version:  I’m not
thrilled with it, but I don’t find it to be a translation-killer because I
believe the verse is hyperbolic anyway). 
Similarly, this was one reading, not months of prolonged study.  I don’t claim to have spotted every
translation problem even in the NT. 
However, these are the ones I did notice.

**Matthew 27:11**

*Now Jesus stood before
the governor.  “Are you the King of the
Jews?” the governor asked him.*

*Jesus answered, “You
say so.”*

Ironically, this is a place where I think the CSB is a
little too literal for its own good. 
According to my interlinear, the Greek there literally is, “You
say.”  However, I think that the Greek
idiom and English idiom don’t line up here. 
In English, “If you say so,” carries with it a sense of doubt and
disagreement, as though the speaker is saying, “I understand you, but I don’t
think I agree with you.”

In context, that’s not at all Jesus’ intent.  He’s not dubiously and noncommittally
replying to Pilate’s assertion.  He’s
agreeing with it.  In fact, He’s agreeing
with it in the full knowledge that His enemies are going to use His agreement
to have Him executed.  I think the
English, “You’ve said it,” would be a much better contextual fit while not
straying far from the Greek.  Note that
similar problems emerge in Mark 15:2, Luke 22:70, 23:3, and John 18:37.  Interestingly, the reading of Matthew 26:64
is much clearer (and closer to what I suggest), despite the Greek being
similar.

**1 Corinthians 4:6**

*Now, brothers and
sisters, I have applied these things to myself and Apollos for your benefit, so
that you may learn from us the meaning of the saying, “Nothing beyond what is
written.”  The purpose is that none of
you will be arrogant, favoring one person above another.*

I don’t mind the beginning and the end of this rendering,
but the middle baffles me.  In context,
with “what has been written,” Paul isn’t referring to some kind of saying.  He’s referring to his written argument in 1
Corinthians 3 that he and Apollos as human teachers are unimportant, but God is
all-important.  In 4:6, Paul is urging
the Corinthians not to “go beyond what has been written” by placing more
emphasis on their teachers than he does. 
In the past, they *have* gone
beyond what has been written, and it’s been the cause of disputes and
factionalism in the church.

Unhappily, rather than making this connection, the CSB
leaves us to ponder a rather gnomic made-up saying.  In the context of 1 Corinthians, I have no
idea what “Nothing beyond what is written” even means.

**2 Timothy 2:19**

*Nevertheless, God’s
solid foundation stands firm, bearing this inscription:  **The
Lord knows who are his,** and let everyone who calls on the name of the Lord
turn away from wickedness.*

I think the CSB has all the right words, but I find the
result unclear.  It would have been
clearer had the entire inscription (through “wickedness”) been placed in
quotation marks.  Otherwise, the effect
of the boldfacing is to make readers think that the quotation ends with
“his”.
