In 1964, Marshal McLuhan famously opined, “The medium is the
message.”  In other words, the content of
a given communication is influenced by the context in which it is
presented.  A news story in an evening
newspaper is different than the same story on the 6-o’clock news is different
than the same story read on a website.

This is familiar ground to every communications major for
the past 50 years, but rarely do we consider its impact in the realm of the
sacred.  In particular, the context in
which a given hymn or spiritual song is used will shape that hymn’s very
nature.



We can identify two extremes of context, and every worship
service is going to fall somewhere on the spectrum between these two
extremes.  The first pole is a church
that adopts a **PERFORMER-AUDIENCE** model
of worship.  According to this vision,
attending a worship service should be like going to a concert.  The organist, praise band, choir, or praise
team makes the music, while the congregants sit passively and listen.  In *The
Purpose-Driven Church*, the bible of the megachurch movement, Rick Warren
endorses this pattern of worship. 
Americans, he says, are uncomfortable singing in public, and they are
much more at ease in a worship environment where they are not expected to sing.

At the other extreme, some churches follow a **CONGREGATION-PARTICIPANT** model of
worship.  In this mode, the church
doesn’t listen to the music.  The church
makes the music.  Sometimes, this occurs
spontaneously, without any kind of direction or instruction.  In other, slightly less pure instances, it is
directed by a song leader whose aim is to encourage the congregation to worship
rather than to edify them with his soloist skills.  This vision of worship is advocated by Paul in
Colossians 3:16, where he describes “teaching and admonishing one another with
psalms and hymns and spiritual songs.” 
In the performer-audience mode, communication is one-way, from the stage
to the seats.  In this mode,
communication is reciprocal, from pew to pew and back again.

The more a church adopts the performer-audience model, the
more the sacred songs it uses will have these characteristics:

1.      
**DIFFICULT.** The performers on the stage are generally
at least semi-professional, and indeed, they may be full-time sacred
musicians.  They have musical skills and
abilities above the norm, and they generally meet at least a couple of times a
week to rehearse.  As a result, they can
handle more difficult music.  Challenging
melodic lines, tricky rhythms, and counter-intuitive structure are all well
within their capacities.

2.      
**NOVEL.**  Everybody loves going to a concert, but only
groupies go to hear the same band every week. 
Most bands have a limited repertoire, and a limited repertoire quickly
exhausts the audience’s attention. 
Church performers, however, DO have people coming to hear them every
single week.  As a result, they face
tremendous pressure to “sing a new song”. 
If they do not, congregants are likely to seek more interesting fare
elsewhere.

3.      
**EPHEMERAL.** The audience appetite for novelty will
create a tremendous “repertoire churn”. 
Spiritual songs in this context are quickly written, quickly introduced,
and quickly discarded.  As a result, the
quality of a given song is not going to be particularly high.  Why bother? 
Nobody tries to make literary art out of a blog post about last night’s
baseball game either.  In both cases, it
is much less important that the product be enduring than that it be
timely.  A praise band that is able to
keep a step or two ahead of the trend will be a successful praise band.
