Normally, I’m not terribly surprised by the chatter that results from my various blog posts.  Last Wednesday was an exception.  I wrote, as I often do on Wednesdays, about an aspect of hymnody that interests me.  However, my recent posts about losing my daughter Macy had attracted more readers than usual to the blog, and many of them, not being used to what I normally have to say on the subject, reacted with varying degrees of horror.

In particular, they seemed to be bothered by the notion that a hymn might be, as I said in that post, “spiritually pointless”.  I talked pretty bluntly about hymns being good or bad, and many of my readers seemed to find that language harsh and judgmental.  Instead, they opined, all evaluations of hymns are taste-based and subjective.  I might like a certain hymn, you might dislike it, but both of those evaluations are based on personal preference, and none is better than another.

Before I go on, let me hasten to acknowledge that these commenters had and have a point.  There is an important subjective component to the way each one of us considers any hymn.  For instance, I enjoy the musical style of hymn tunes written a little more than a hundred years ago, like “In the Garden” and “One Day”.  My wife can’t stand it.

Neither one of us is “right”.  The harmony in both of those hymns is technically sound, and she doesn’t argue that there’s something wrong with it.  She just doesn’t like hymn tunes from that period, even when they’re executed well.

Taste is a thing.  Taste is especially a thing when it comes to song leaders.  Few indeed are the song leaders who will lead a hymn they personally don’t like, unless somebody else puts them up to it.  If enough song leaders don’t like a hymn, it will never be sung, whatever its other merits may be.

However, simply because subjective evaluation of hymns is important doesn’t mean that objective evaluation isn’t.  In fact, of the two, the latter is most significant.  I think we see this when it comes to the bare command to sing.  From time the time, I encounter the story of a Christian (usually an adult convert) who simply can’t find anything in our hymn repertoire that he likes.  Maybe he listens to death metal on his own time.  Maybe he prefers rap music.  Regardless, there is nothing in our body of hymns that appeals to him.  What’s he supposed to do?

I think most of us would say what I say.  Even though I sympathize with his lack of connection with our hymn repertoire, he still has to sing anyway, even if he doesn’t like a single hymn on the song list.  God has commanded us to sing, and the divine commandment is more important than our personal preferences.  Ideally, we will be able to like what we are given to sing, but in the event of a conflict, the will of God trumps our will.

It’s important, then, to recognize that God doesn’t merely tell us to sing.  He tells us what to sing.  He sets out goals for our song worship, and a hymn is good to the extent that it enables us to achieve those goals.  Conversely, if a hymn hinders us in achieving those goals, how can it be anything other than bad?

Of course, all this requires judgment on the part of song leaders and other Christians.  There is no divinely inspired list of good and bad hymns.  However, it’s far from the only area of spiritual importance where judgment is required.  Modesty isn’t an area of personal taste.  Our choices in entertainment are not fundamentally subjective.  There too, we’re supposed to base our judgments first on what God likes, second on what we like.  We compare a given song or movie to the divine standard, and even if we don’t all agree on whether it’s acceptable or not, neither do we refuse to acknowledge that the standard exists.  Sometimes, a song or movie so obviously falls short that anybody who champions it can’t honestly be referring to the standard.

As there is such a thing as immodest dress or an evil, corrupting movie, so too there is such a thing as a bad hymn.  In fact, the contours of the latter aren’t too hard to discern.  The New Testament has considerably more to say about song worship than it does about modesty (Where, you ask?  Stay tuned next week!).

For whatever reason, we generally haven’t read those passages with the care that we’ve lavished on the likes of Acts 20:7.  Nonetheless, they should affect our decisions about what we sing as much as the practice of the church in Troas affects our decision to partake of the Lord’s Supper on the first day of the week.  It’s not about personal taste.  It’s about doing God’s will to glorify Him.
