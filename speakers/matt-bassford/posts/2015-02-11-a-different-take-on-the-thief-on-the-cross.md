On Monday, I spent some time on Amazon reading the negative
reviews of the book *Muscle and a
Shovel.* There were several dozen
such reviews, and one recurrent complaint was with the book’s treatment of the
thief on the cross.

There is, of course, a segment of the denominational world
that really likes the story of the thief on the cross because it offers an
example of salvation without baptism, from which these people contend that we
don’t have to be baptized today to be saved either.  Apparently (I haven’t read the book yet,
though I’m likely to), the author of *Muscle
and a Shovel* responded with the familiar-to-me argument that the thief on
the cross died under the old covenant, so he doesn’t provide us with a pattern
for behavior under the new covenant.

Most of the negative reviewers answered this with indignant spluttering
along the lines of, “The thief on the cross does too count!”  However, one pointed out that if we’re going
to be technical about such things, the thief on the cross actually died after
Jesus did.   Thus, according to him, the
thief died under the new covenant and so does provide us with a pattern for
salvation.

I’m a little skeptical about that argument.  So far as I know, the Bible doesn’t give a
clear start date for the new covenant. 
Did the new covenant begin when Jesus died?  What about when He entered the holy place of
heaven by means of His own blood, securing eternal redemption?  What about when He was proclaimed the Son of
God with power by His resurrection from the dead?  What about the day of Pentecost, when men
were first commanded to call upon the name of the Lord in order to be saved?  A reasonable case, I think, can be made for any of those
options.

However, the uncertainty cuts both ways.  Though the Amazon commenter can’t prove that
the new covenant began when Jesus died, I can’t prove the opposite, either.  This argument, then, only leads to a tedious
round of did-not-did-too, which accomplishes nothing.

Besides, I can’t help but wonder if in making a covenantal
argument about the thief on the cross, members of the churches of Christ are
going to the well once too often.  It’s
certainly an important argument when it comes to issues of instrumental music
and tithing, but it seems like an awkward fit here.

After all, the exchange between Jesus and the thief doesn’t
fit the Old-Testament pattern either.  Under
the Law of Moses, forgiveness was a matter of making the proper sacrifices in
the tabernacle/temple.  Leviticus devotes
whole chapters to the subject.  Nobody
was forgiven because some religious authority figure said so.  The story of the thief on the cross doesn’t
look any more like that than it looks like “Arise, and be baptized, and wash
away your sins.”

Instead, we need to recognize that the story of the thief on
the cross belongs to a special period of time that isn’t defined by
covenants—the time when God Himself walked the earth.  During this time, things worked differently
than they ever had before or ever have since. 
In the book of Acts, Paul cast out demons in the name of Jesus.  In the gospels, Jesus cast out demons without
any appeal to external authority at all. 
He was the Son of God.  He could
do that.

A similar set of rules applied to forgiveness.  Jesus spelled it out explicitly in Mark
2:5-11

*And
Jesus seeing their faith said to the paralytic, "Son, your sins are
forgiven."*

*But
some of the scribes were sitting there and reasoning in their hearts, "Why
does this man speak that way? He is blaspheming; who can forgive sins but God
alone?"*

*Immediately
Jesus, aware in His spirit that they were reasoning that way within themselves,
said to them, "Why are you reasoning about these things in your hearts? Which
is easier, to say to the paralytic, 'Your sins are forgiven'; or to say, 'Get
up, and pick up your pallet and walk'? But so that you may know that the Son of
Man has authority on earth to forgive sins"--He said to the paralytic,
"I say to you, get up, pick up your pallet and go home."* (NASB)

To everyone who saw this, the lesson was plain.  Jesus had authority on earth to forgive sins,
an audacious assertion confirmed by the healing of the paralytic.

The story of the thief on the cross doesn’t fit with the
Mosaic pattern of sacrifice.  It doesn’t
fit with the pattern of baptism in Acts. 
However, it dovetails perfectly with the story of the paralytic.  Why did Jesus tell the thief that he would be
with Him in paradise?  Because the Son of
Man had authority on earth to forgive sins. 
Even while hanging on the cross, He could tell the penitent thief he was
going to paradise, and it would happen.

As impressive as this is, it isn’t exactly a pattern we can
follow.  There are still plenty of sinners
on earth, but the Word who became flesh isn’t dwelling among us anymore.  None of us can go to Him and seek forgiveness
directly from His lips.  Instead, if we
want to be saved, we must find salvation according to the pattern given in His
name:  belief, repentance, confession,
and baptism.
