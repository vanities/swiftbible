**OVERVIEW.**  This is a great big Bible with an unusual
cover and a rugged aesthetic.  I do have
some concerns about durability, but I like it.

This is another review Bible that I got *gratis* from Crossway.  I
asked them for a review copy of the six-volume Reader’s Bible, but they were
fresh out, so they took pity on me and sent me this instead.  As always, there were no conditions placed on
the review, other than, I guess, that I review it.  Besides, once I’m done reviewing the Bible,
it goes to my photographer, so I don’t have a whole lot of incentive to lie.



**VITAL STATISTICS.**

**Translation:**  English Standard Version (2016)

**Cover:**  Natural Leather

**Binding:**  Sewn

**Trim Size:**  6 ½” x 
9 ¼”

**Text Block Width:**  1 ½”

**Weight:**  3 pounds, 2 ounces

**Column:**  Single

**Layout:**  Paragraphed

**Print Size:**  9.5 Lexicon No. 1

**Words of Christ:**  Black

**Cross-References:**  None

**Ribbon Marker:**  1

**SIZE.** As Bibles go, the Large-Print
Single-Column Journaling Bible (henceforth the LPSCJB) is about the upper size
limit of anything I’d care to review.  In
its dimensions and weight, it reminds me of the 2002 edition of the Foundation
Side-Column Reference (subsequent SCR’s aren’t quite as hefty).  There’s no way that anybody is fitting this
into their glove box, unless they drive a tank. 
Likewise for purses, unless you carry one of those massive grandma
purses that can double for use as a ball-and-chain flail.  A backpack? 
Sure.

**COVER.** The LPSCJB has easily the most
distinctive cover of any Bible I’ve ever reviewed.  Crossway calls this trim “Cowhide”, but that
doesn’t even begin to capture its uniqueness. 
First of all, it has a flap.  No
bigs; plenty of other Bibles have flaps too. 
However, this flap secures the Bible not with one of those girly clasps,
but with a yard-long strip of rawhide that I promptly dubbed “the tail”.  The tail doesn’t work well in the pulpit, not
unless you want this giant string hanging down from your Bible the whole time
you’re preaching, but I think it’s pretty cool anyway.  For those who don’t intend to use the LPSCJB
while standing up, I think the flap-and-thong combination will work well.  A tail coiled neatly on the desk next to you
isn’t much of a problem.  Plus, if you
are cramming the LPSCJB into a backpack, the flap will protect the pages.

After the flap and the tail, the most noticeable thing about
the cowhide LPSCJB is the treatment of the leather.  This cowhide isn’t glossy and folded neatly
over the bookboard, with a poly liner to cover everything up and make it look
neat.  Instead, it’s much thicker than
the cowhide in Bibles normally is. 
Rather than being folded over, it’s simply cut, with some kind of darker
band around the perimeter to keep the leather from tearing and fraying.  I would describe the finish on the leather as
a natural finish.  It doesn’t have the
glossy, durable coat on top.  As a
result, it will scuff up about like a bomber jacket or a pair of leather work
boots would, which I suspect is the point.

Inside the flap and the cover of the Bible, the sueded side
of the cowhide is fully exposed, which is another first for me in a Bible.  The endpapers are glued directly to the
bookboard, so that you can see the side of the bookboard if you look.

I find all this extremely appealing.  In fact, if I weren’t a preacher, I could see
myself wanting a Bible like this to have in the pews.  If ever a Bible cover could seem rugged, this
is it.  Overall, it reminds me of a
moderately sized leather satchel.

However, when it comes to the durability of the Bible, my
impressions are mixed.  On the plus side,
the LPSCJB has extremely stiff tabs, tabs like the ones that people complain
about in the Schuyler Quentels.  Rather
than lying flat in Genesis, this is a Bible that will literally spring closed
anywhere from Genesis 35 forward.  I know
that this will not make the Bible popular with Bible geeks, but the tabs do
valuable work in protecting the endpapers.

To be perfectly honest, this is important because the
endpapers need protecting.  This is a big
Bible, big Bibles put more stress on endpapers, and these endpapers are stiff
paper rather than vinyl-coated leatherette. 
If some clever soul flexes those stiff hinges back and forth until the
Bible starts lying flat, it probably won’t be long until the endpapers start
tearing.  In other words, Do Not Do That.

**TEXT BLOCK.** Not surprisingly, given that the
publisher is Crossway, the text block is sewn. 
When I peek under the headband, the signatures are as clear as they
could possibly be.  On the one hand, the
stiff hinges mean that the Bible won’t lie flat, even though it’s sewn.  On the other hand (and much more
importantly), the sewn binding means the Bible won’t have pages start falling
out.  Plus, the factory in China did a
good job with the sewing.  There’s no
cockling in the Bible, so that when I open it, it opens in perfect silence.

Like all the other Crossway journaling Bibles I’ve ever
encountered, this one doesn’t have edge gilding.  Instead, the edges of the text block are the
same off-white color as the paper. 
Crossway’s been doing this with a lot of its text blocks recently, and I
like the more austere look.  The fact
that it makes the Bibles slightly cheaper to produce doesn’t hurt either!

**VALUE.** There are a couple of things to consider
here:  The way the LPSCJB compares to its
competitors, and the way that it compares to the same Crossway text block in a
less expensive edition.  The LPSCJB
retails for $140.  That’s a lot of money
for a mass-market Bible, but it’s the same price as the trio of premium-leather
large-print journaling Bibles from the various arms of HarperCollins
(Zondervan, Nelson, etc.).  In real life,
you can buy one of these from Christian Book Distributors for around $75.  That’s about what you’re going to pay for a
large-print journaling Bible with a flap and a tail (Though interestingly, the
HarperCollins offerings sell for slightly less. 
Not selling as well, maybe?)

However, you can get a hardbound or imitation-leather LPSCJB
from CBD for $30.  That poses an
interesting question.  Is it worth $45 to
get a Bible with a cover made from the skin of a dead animal instead of the
skin of a dead polyurethane beast?  I
think that for a preacher, the answer is no. 
If I decide that I want to use one of these for my main wide-margin
Bible, I’m going to buy one of the imitation-leather Bibles and have it
rebound.  It’s more expensive, but it
gives me the durability that I like without the things that aren’t useful to me,
like the tail.

On the other hand, for someone who is sitting in the pews, I
think the calculus may be different. 
Paper endpapers and all, I think a leather cover on a LPSCJB will
outlast a poly cover.  It’s not as
vulnerable to heat and sunlight, and it will flex better over time.  The tail isn’t a problem and may even prove
to be an asset.  If you’re writing notes
in a Bible, you want it to last for a long time, and it’s this version that
offers the best hope of that.

**CONCLUSION.** This is a big Bible, which will always
cost points.  It has some weird traits,
and either the stiff hinges or the weak endpapers are going to be a
problem.  However, I still really enjoy
the aesthetics of the Bible.  I’m willing
to award it a 7.5/10 for Physical Form.
