**OVERVIEW.** To me, this one is right in the sweet
spot for reading Bibles.  I wish it were
smaller, but that would be dreaming the impossible dream.

I’m not really following a daily Bible-reading plan at the
moment.  Instead, I’m reading as much as
I feel like when I feel like it, progressing canonically through Old and New
Testaments simultaneously.  As a result
of this, I did read the majority of Numbers and Luke in the SCLPJB.  Here’s how I liked it:



**SIZE.** Normally, I say that size in a reading
Bible isn’t any big deal.  However, for
whatever reason, the LPSCJB bothered me. 
It could be the season.  My office
in the church building isn’t heated independently from the rest of the building,
and we keep the building at 55 or 60 during the week, so I spend the winter
huddled over a space heater with my coat on. 
That does make it harder to brace a large, heavy Bible against my chest.  It could also be, though, that the
combination of full-sized Bible plus a bulky leather flap was enough to be
annoying under any circumstances. 
Regardless, the size of the Bible didn’t drive me crazy, but I noticed
it.

**LAYOUT.** The LPSCJB is a single-column Bible with
60-65 characters per line.  Normally, I
would say that a Bible with so wide a text column isn’t right for me, but in
practice, I proved to be perfectly happy with the LPSCJB.  I still think this has to do with field of
vision, though.  I can hold Bibles with
larger, darker print farther away from my face, which means that my eye can
take in a wider distance without having to move.  The LPSCJB has to be right on the border of
what I can read.  In testing, I found
that I couldn’t focus on the rightmost word in an LPSCJB line, but my brain
could figure out what the word was, even when it was out-of-focus.

If a single-column Bible works for me in prose, it will also
work in poetry, and indeed, the setting of the LPSCJB handles poetry
beautifully.  I still don’t like the ESV
editorial decision to indent alternating poetic lines, but the cpl count is
high enough in the LPSCJB that this only rarely causes problems.

Even though the inner margins of the LPSCJB aren’t for
writing in, they’re still half an inch wide. 
That’s plenty big enough to keep text from falling into the gutter.  This isn’t even an issue in Job 42, near the
middle of the Bible.  The inner margins
pose no obstacle to reading.

Neither does Crossway’s decision to print the LPSCJB in
blackletter.  Red lettering in Bibles
inclines people to believe that a statement is more significant because Jesus
said it (contra 2 Timothy 3:16-17), and it’s nearly impossible to execute
perfectly.  Even my Jongbloed-printed
red-letter NASB Pitt Minion has registration errors in some places.  That’s to say nothing of the manifold perils
of Words Of Christ In Orange and Words Of Christ In Pink!  By contrast, no red lettering means no
problems, and that’s much to be preferred in a reader.

I’m equally glad that Crossway line-matched the LPSCJB.  The recent Crossway six-volume Reader’s Bible
isn’t line-matched, and it gets away with that because the paper is so
thick.  However, the LPSCJB couldn’t do
the same.  9.5-point Lexicon is dark
enough that it creates plenty of ghosting even with the fairly opaque paper,
and having all that ghosting bundled into lines makes reading the Bible much
easier.

Finally, the print in the LPSCJB is well above the minimum
that I need to see in order for a Bible to be an ideal reader for me.  There’s literally nothing about the layout of
this Bible that I don’t like when I’m reading it.  I had no trouble getting lost in the text.

**PRODUCTION
QUALITY.** The LPSCJB is another
China-print offering from Crossway, and it does nothing to injure the steadily
improving reputation of text blocks from across the Pacific.  When I look at print quality in a Bible, I
like to look at the footnotes and marginal references.  The LPSCJB doesn’t have very many of those,
but the print on those that exist is clear and fine.  I haven’t seen any random ink splatters in
the text block either.  At this point,
there’s honestly not much difference between a China-print Crossway text block
and a Jongbloed text block (China-print Bibles from other publishers aren’t
necessarily as good).  This probably
isn’t good news for Jongbloed, but it’s good news for Bible-buyers.  If you don’t care about luxurious goatskin
covers and art-gilding, and the text blocks are just as good, why not go
mass-market?

As I’ve already discussed, the paper in the LPSCJB isn’t the
equal of the paper in golden-age Foundation Bibles, but it’s certainly much
more opaque than the paper you’ll find in most other Bibles.  There really aren’t other mass-market Bibles
with print of equal or greater size and less ghosting.

**CONCLUSION.**Yeah, the LPSCJB is big, but as a reader,
it gets everything else right.  People
with rotten eyes may be well advised to steer clear of modern wide-margin
Bibles altogether, but for the rest of us, this one should work.  I give it a 9/10 as a reader, which nets it
an overall score of 24.5/30.  Not bad!
