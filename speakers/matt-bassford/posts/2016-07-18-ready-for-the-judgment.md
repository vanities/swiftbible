Human beings generally have some huge blind spots in their
thinking, and a blind spot that almost all of us share is the tendency to
believe that our lives aren’t going to change. 
We assume that things are going to continue as they are
indefinitely.  The reality, of course, is
that nothing about life on earth is unchanging, including life itself.  The earthly existence that seems so permanent
to us in reality is anything but.

Because this is true, the single most important thing we can
do with our lives is to prepare for when those lives are over.  They might end when the Lord returns, they
might end when we die, but one way or another, they’re going to end.  When they do, we will be called to account
for what we have done in the body.  If we
can’t give a good answer for the way we lived, the consequences will be more
dire than we can imagine.  Let’s
consider, then, what our Lord says about how we must get ready for the
judgment.



**Preparedness**

This morning, we’re going to be looking at a context that
runs from the end of Matthew 24 through Matthew 25, and the first of the three
main subheadings in it concerns the subject of preparedness.  In context, the Lord has just answered
questions from His disciples about the fall of Jerusalem and the end of the
world.  Rather than focusing on the
first, which He encourages His disciples to anticipate and predict, now, He’s
talking about a coming that is entirely unpredictable.

As a result, He commends **ALERTNESS** to His audience. 
Read with me here from Matthew 24:42-51. 
In this section, he makes two main arguments.  The first is that because the return of the
Lord is unpredictable, the only way we can be ready for it is to be ready all
the time.  Like a thief breaks into a
house at a time when he is not expected, so too Jesus will return when He is
not being looked for.  This makes perfect
sense.  After all, if the Bible said that
Jesus would return on July 31st, 2016, I guarantee you that on July
30th, we’d have a line around the block of people wanting to be
baptized.  It would be too easy for
people who didn’t really love Jesus to game the system.  God prevents this kind of crafty behavior by
keeping the hour of His coming secret.

Second, Jesus points out the consequences of being found
prepared, or not.  He does this by using
a parable about a master returning at a time when no one is expecting him.  The servant whom he finds being faithful will
be rewarded, but the servant whom he finds living it up will be punished.

Interestingly, this is not a parable about outsiders.  This is a parable about Christians.  When the Lord returns, He will find some of
us faithful and some of us not.  We might
be able to hide our lack of faithfulness from one another, but we won’t be able
to hide it from Jesus.  If He finds that
we’ve been claiming discipleship but not following Him, we’ll end up with the
hypocrites too.

When we hear this, we might say, “Man!  I’ve got to shape up my life!”, and that’s
certainly the right reaction.  However,
it’s not enough to start being faithful. 
We have to continue being faithful, and that’s the point of the parable
of **TEN VIRGINS.** Consider Matthew 25:1-5.  There are many parables in the gospels about
Jewish wedding customs, and apparently this is one of them.  As part of the wedding festivities, a bunch
of young women would wait outside the house where the wedding feast would be
held to meet the bridegroom and escort him in.

This one, it seems, is supposed to be an evening wedding, so
the young women are going to stage a lamplit procession.  Some of them bring extra olive oil for their
lamps, some of them don’t, and this decision starts looking more and more
important as the bridegroom doesn’t show up.

In this parable, I think the women, wise and foolish alike,
represent Christians, and the lamp oil represents our commitment to the
Lord.  The sad truth is that not all
Christians are equally committed, and some have such a lack of commitment that
they choose not to stay faithful. 
They’re excited about serving God when they’re baptized, but as the
months and years pass, they lose interest, attend services less and less, and
ultimately fall away.  It’s a sad story
I’ve seen play out far too many times, and if we don’t keep watch over
ourselves, it will surely happen to us.

The problem with lack of commitment is highlighted by **THE BRIDEGROOM’S RETURN.** Look at Matthew 25:6-13.  The foolish virgins run out of oil, they have
to go off and try to buy some more, and while they’re gone, the bridegroom shows
up and goes into the wedding feast with the wise virgins.  As a result, the foolish ones are left out in
the cold.

On the Day of Judgment, that’s how it’s going to be.  The Lord will return at a time when even the
best Christians on the planet won’t be expecting Him, just like even the wise
virgins started nodding off because of the absence of the bridegroom.  On that day, it is only the disciples whom
the Lord finds prepared to meet Him, even though they weren’t expecting Him,
who will be admitted into the joys of heaven. 
By contrast, unfaithful Christians are going to be shut out.

This should be a sobering warning for all of us.  It demonstrates first of all that those who
teach the doctrine of once-saved-always-saved are dead wrong.  Even the foolish virgins started out with lit
lamps.  The problem is that they didn’t
stay that way.  This is a problem to
which none of us are immune.  Either we
make sure we’re ready to meet the Lord, or we’ll have eternity to regret it.

**The Parable of the
Talents**

However, commitment is not enough.  The Lord expects productivity from us too,
and He makes this point in the parable of the talents.  The parable begins when we meet **ONE MASTER AND THREE SERVANTS.** We read about them in Matthew
25:14-18.  Notice that Jesus’ words make
it seem like the master is entrusting the entirety of his property to his three
servants, and that’s about right.  Back
then, a talent wasn’t an ability or gift. 
Instead, it was a unit of money, an amount of precious metal equal to
what one man could carry.  Generally, a
talent is considered to be about 75 pounds, so the master here is distributing
75-pound increments, probably of silver.

Today, of course, not everything that our Lord entrusts to
us is money, though He certainly does that. 
Instead, we should think of a talent as “stuff we can use to serve God”,
be it money, time, energy, or ability. 
As in the parable, not all Christians have equal opportunities for
service.  A poor homebound widow simply
doesn’t have as much to offer as a man in the prime of life with a good job and
many abilities.  We’re not responsible
for what we have to give.

Instead, we’re responsible for what we do with it.  In the parable, the five-talent man and the
two-talent man take their money and double their initial stake.  Similarly, there are some Christians who take
what they are given, be that amount great or small, and serve God
accordingly.  Sadly, though, like the
one-talent servant who takes his money and buries it, there are some Christians
who bury what they have been given by God. 
They have some ability, but they do no useful work.

We see what Jesus expects from the master’s conversations
with **THE FIRST TWO SERVANTS.** Let’s keep going with our reading in
Matthew 25:19-23.  The important thing to
note here is that each man works, and is commended for working, in accordance
with what he has been given.  The
two-talent man only presents the master with two extra talents, but the
master’s fine with that.  He only expects
two talents from the one to whom he gave two talents.

In the same way, we need to remember that God is going to
evaluate our service to Him according to the opportunities He has given
us.  It’s not just a question of what we
have done.  It’s a question of what we
have done with what we had to offer.

God expects more out of the millionaire than he does from
the man who is sick and unable to earn money. 
He expects more from the Christian who has decades of study under his
belt than from the new convert who hasn’t had time to learn much yet.  That’s only fair, isn’t it?  When I’m looking at my son’s math workbook, I
don’t expect him to be able to master pre-calculus yet.  I only care whether he works hard to learn at
the level where he is.  It’s the same
with God and us.

This tells us, then, that we had better be able to do two
things.  First, we must be able to
accurately assess what we have to offer. 
Second, we need to be working according to our potential.

We learn what will happen if we don’t do this in the story
of **THE ONE-TALENT SERVANT.** We see him talking to the master in
Matthew 25:24-30.  What’s interesting to
me is that even though it was the servant’s own decision not to do anything
useful with his one talent, he tries to pin the blame on the master.  It’s the master’s fault because his
expectations are too high!

Not surprisingly, the master doesn’t buy in to the servant’s
blame-shifting.  Instead, he replies that
the obvious thing to do for a master with high expectations is to work to live
up to those expectations.  The servant doesn’t
get a pass for being lazy.  Instead, he
gets thrown out.

This is important, friends, because there are all too many
Christians who are walking the path of the one-talent servant.  We know what the Bible teaches us about
working for the Lord and helping other Christians and saving the lost, but we
whine that God’s expectations are too high, and we’re afraid to try, so we
never actually do anything about any of it.

That, brethren, is a one-way ticket to disaster.  God doesn’t expect us to live up to the
example of Jesus, but He does expect us to try. 
We can’t refuse even to attempt to serve Him and then entrust ourselves
to His grace.  Grace doesn’t cover
willful sin, and when we consider a commandment of God and say, “Nope; not even
gonna try,” willful sin is exactly what we’re engaging in.  Discipleship isn’t about not doing.  It’s about doing.  We must make sure we’re doers.

**Before the Throne**

The final section of Matthew 25 takes us out of parables and
directly to that awesome scene before the throne of God on the Day of
Judgment.  The text begins with a
description of **THE SON OF MAN IN
GLORY.** Here, let’s consider Matthew
25:31-33.  I read a fair amount of blog
posts and whatnot online about religion and religious issues, and one of the
things that strikes me about posts from a more liberal perspective is their
habit of writing judgment out of the Bible. 
They say that we need to get away from that harsh heaven-or hell
perspective and get back to Jesus.

As this text reveals, along with many more like it, when we
do get back to Jesus, what we find there is judgment.  This is a universal judgment.  Everyone is invited; no one may decline.  It is not a judgment of shaded boundaries or
blurred lines.  Nobody is going to be
left standing in the middle.  Every soul
is going to end up either to the left or to the right.  The consequences of ending up to the right
are joyful beyond description; the consequences of ending up to the left are
dreadful beyond imagination.  Our time
before the throne is the most important moment of our existence.

After painting this picture, the Lord explains what He will
say to **THE SHEEP ON THE RIGHT.** Our reading continues in Matthew
25:34-40.  The Lord will inform the
righteous that they have cared for Him in all sorts of different situations and
different ways.  Bewildered, they will
ask Him when this happened, and He will tell them that in caring for the least
of His brethren, they cared for Him.  As
a result, He will reward them and admit them into eternal life.

Before we get to the application of this text, I think
there’s a doctrinal issue here to discuss. 
At first glance, this text looks like it’s presenting an alternate path
to salvation.  No more believe, repent,
confess, and be baptized; as long as you care for the needy, you’re OK.

I don’t think that’s what’s going on here, though.  First of all, Jesus is talking to an audience
of Jews, all of whom at this time are part of God’s covenant people.  He’s not telling a group of outsiders how
they can backdoor into heaven.  Second,
even though caring for the needy is enough to establish us as good and faithful
servants, it’s not enough to show our perfection, and without the grace of
Christ, we would have to be perfect to inherit eternal life.  This is not about a path to heaven apart from
Jesus.  It’s about how those who are
already in Christ can please Him.

Having said that, this text must impress on us the importance
of good works, particularly good works directed toward our brothers and sisters
in Christ.  When we help the least
significant Christian out there, in Jesus’ eyes, we’re helping Him.  The mark of genuine discipleship is not that
we show up here on Sundays.  It’s that we
live lives that are filled with service and love.

Next, of course, Jesus addresses **THE GOATS ON THE LEFT.** We
read about their fate in Matthew 25:41-46. 
Here too, I think the Lord is talking to people who are nominally
Christians.  What’s interesting, though,
is that their sin isn’t lust or greed or anything like that.  Instead, it’s apathy.  These are people who saw brothers and sisters
in need of help and didn’t help them.  If
we want to go to hell, it’s really easy. 
All we have to do is nothing.

Obviously, we need to pay attention to the needs of our
brother and secondarily our neighbor in the specific areas that Jesus
mentions.  We need to provide food and
water and clothing and shelter on demand. 
We also need to be careful, though, that we don’t treat this list as
exhaustive.  There are shut-ins who are
members here who are just as imprisoned by their bodies as they would be if
they were behind bars.  Are we visiting
them?  When a brother puts out the call
for people to help him move, are we there to help?  When we see a Christian standing around
friendless and uncertain after services, do we go up to him and prove that
we’re his friend?  When we do those
things, we’re doing them for Jesus too, and when we ignore these needs, we’re
also ignoring Jesus.

This is a sobering passage. 
It is the opposite of checklist Christianity, in which as long as we
keep out of the thou-shalt-not’s, we’re good, and the rest of our lives are our
own.  Really, Jesus is treating keeping
out of the thou-shalt-not’s as a given, and he’s treating the rest of our lives
as belonging to Him.  We must understand
that unless we are devoted to following in His footsteps, we aren’t truly His
disciples.

Before the Lord’s Supper, this gives us a lot to think about.  Let’s consider first of all Jesus’ standard
of selflessness and love as it applies to Him. 
He was perfectly selfless and perfectly loving, even though we are much
less worthy objects of selflessness and love than He is.  He was so willing to care for us in our need
that He died for us.  Let’s first of all
remember that.

Second, let’s remember also the moral obligation that the
death of Jesus lays on us.  Because Jesus
died for us, we don’t get to live for ourselves anymore.  Instead, we must live for Him.  We devote our lives to a higher calling
because it’s His higher calling.  That’s
what He saved us for.  How are we doing
at that?  Let’s examine our hearts and
lives as we partake.
