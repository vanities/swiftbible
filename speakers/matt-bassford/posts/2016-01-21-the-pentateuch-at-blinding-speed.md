Well. . . maybe not “blinding”, exactly.  I probably could have read it in a sitting,
but instead I read it in 15 days, as part of my read-the-Bible-in-90-days
adventure (Schuyler Caxton ftw!).  I’ve
read the Pentateuch before, but certainly never so quickly.

It was a much different experience than my normal
readthrough of the Bible in a year.  For
one thing, my usual Bible-reading plan has daily Old-Testament and
New-Testament readings, so that the Pentateuch is mixed up with the Synoptic gospels.  Reading about animal sacrifices and Jesus at
the same time certainly has meditative value, but reading about animal
sacrifices without reading about Jesus makes the point of the former shine
through.

And the point of the Pentateuch is pretty grim.



Flying through the story as quickly as I did gave me a very
different sense of what it was about.  We
spend a lot of time talking about Genesis, but in the context of the whole
Pentateuch, Genesis pretty much functions as backstory.  You need to know who God is, who the
Israelites are, and what they’re doing in Egypt in order to understand what
happens next.  Genesis presents the
backstory in cursory fashion:  Thousands
of years in 50 chapters, which I read in four days.

Once you hit Exodus 3, though, the speed of your journey
through history slows way, way down.  The
story of the next 40 years takes 135 chapters to tell.  Other than the ministry of Jesus Himself, no
other story in the Bible receives such attention to detail.

The details are not pleasant.  Fundamentally, over and over again, the story
of the Pentateuch is about God’s people failing God.  They complain when Pharaoh’s overseers punish
them for Moses’ demands.  Nearly every
miracle in the 40 years in the wilderness, from the crossing of the Red Sea to
water from the rock to manna from heaven, is preceded by complaint from the
people.  While Moses is up on Mt. Sinai
receiving the law, they’re busy worshiping the golden calf.  When God tells them to enter the land, they
refuse.  When God tells them not to enter
the land, they make the attempt anyway. 
They complain, murmur, and disobey until God strikes nearly all of them
down.

Even at the end, when the Israelites finally seem to have
gotten their act together, when they’re about to enter the land under Joshua,
Moses reveals in his final speech that they haven’t really changed.  The blessings of obedience are described
briefly, but the curses of disobedience are presented in hideous detail,
because Moses knows where the Israelites are going to end up.  Once they conquer Canaan, he warns them, they
are going to continue to disobey, until God finally gets so fed up with them
that He is going to erase them from the land He has given them.  On that uplifting note, the Pentateuch ends.

As national narratives go, this one is a strange one.  No matter whether you’re American, British,
German, or Chinese, you want to believe that your nation is the best.  The Pentateuch, by contrast, is at pains to
emphasize that the only thing that’s special about the Israelites has nothing
to do with them at all.  They aren’t the
most numerous, they aren’t the fiercest, and they certainly aren’t the most
righteous!  Their only claim to fame is
that God has chosen them.

However, even that choosing is in doubt.  They are a stubborn and rebellious people, a
complicated system of sacrifices for sin can only paper over the cracks in
their relationship with their God, and their history and future are both marked
by catastrophic failure to keep faith.

In Galatians 3:24, Paul notes that the Law was intended as a
tutor to lead us to Christ.  Usually, we
understand this passage in terms of the laws and prophecies that lay the
foundation for the emergence of Christianity. 
It may be, though, that the most important teaching in the Pentateuch is
about human inadequacy.  As Jesus
observes in Matthew 23:29-31, the more we deny our kinship with the
disobedient, the more we establish how like them we really are.

The gap between where we are and where we need to be to
maintain a relationship with a holy God is more of a chasm than a gap.  The story of our efforts to bridge that chasm
on our own can only be the story of failure. 
The relationship between the Israelites and God in the Pentateuch is the
relationship between man and God in miniature. 
The two cannot be brought together while the world remains the
same.  We are helpless, unless existence
itself can be transformed by a single dazzling act of grace.
