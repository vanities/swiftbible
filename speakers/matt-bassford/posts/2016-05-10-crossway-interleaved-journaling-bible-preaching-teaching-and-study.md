**OVERVIEW.** You could write a novel in all the white
space in the IJB, and the print, though small, is quite readable for me.  However, the Bible is too heavy to make for
comfortable holding out of the pulpit, and the lack of book and chapter
headings makes locating passages more difficult.

During the course of the review period, I used the IJB to
preach two sermons (really one sermon twice), teach two adult Bible classes,
and prepare a sermon, a eulogy, and a variety of blog posts at my desk.  Here are my impressions:



**PREACHING.** I’m a loud advocate for wide-margin
Bibles.  Now that I’ve started preaching
expository sermons from marginal notes, I wouldn’t want to go back to doing it any
other way.  For somebody like me, then,
the IJB would seem to be a dream come true. 
Every other page is blank, and even the pages with text have a
substantial one-inch outer margin.  This
is a clever touch on the part of the Crossway designers because it gives you
room to write notes on the verses in the outer column next to those verses.

As it turned out, though, I didn’t actually write in the IJB
at all.  There were three reasons behind
this.  First, the sermon I used the Bible
to preach was topical, not expository, and my topical sermon notes go in my
sermon notebook.

Second, the Bible was a victim of its own beautiful
design.  I knew that when I was done with
it, I wanted to give it to somebody special. 
I eventually decided on my mother-in-law, who’s into Bible journaling,
but for now is only journaling in a notebook. 
She is a genuinely wonderful disciple of Christ who was my friend before
I ever started dating her daughter, a woman who has never done me anything but
good for 15 years.  I couldn’t give a
woman like that a marked-up Bible, could I?

Besides, there’s a sense in which testing out the IJB by
writing in it is ridiculous.  I’ve never
used a Bible with heavier paper, and if my handwriting weren’t horrible, I
could just about copy out an expository sermon longhand on the blank
pages!  If the IJB isn’t enough for your
note-taking/journaling needs, no Bible would be.  Let’s just stipulate that the IJB is wonderful
for writing in.

In fact (and I can’t believe I’m saying this), it might be a
little too wonderful.  It has far more
space for writing than I would ever want to use, and the white space comes at
the cost of greatly increased weight. 
The Crossway Single-Column Journaling Bible has print exactly the same
size and all the space I need, but it weighs only 60 percent as much.

In practice, that weight makes a difference.  I find that when I’m using a Bible that
weighs more than three pounds, I won’t carry it with me.  I’ll leave it on the pulpit instead, or
simply not leave the pulpit.  The IJB
weighs more than four pounds.

As a result, I noticed myself not even picking it up, but
rather leaving it on the pulpit to read out of it.  The 7.5-point Lexicon No. 1 is surprisingly
readable, so that didn’t pose a problem for me, but it did represent a marked
departure from what I normally want to do with a Bible when I’m preaching.

In addition to this substantial weight, the IJB has a
significant wingspan.  In the small
pulpit that my home congregation has, this definitely led to some maneuvering
as I tried to arrange it alongside my sermon notebook.  In the larger pulpit at a different
congregation, it wasn’t an issue.

As I said, I preached the same sermon twice, and it was a
topical sermon, so I did lots of Scripture location.  I got every citation right (I think the
generous leading plus markedly superscript numbers made the difference here),
but I noticed something else that I didn’t like.  Apparently, when I’m thumbing through a Bible
looking for a passage, I only use the book and chapter headers on one side of
the Bible.  If I’m turning left, I’ll
look at the left header.  If I’m turning
right, I’ll look at the right header.

However, because every other page in the IJB is blank, half
the time, when I glanced at my chosen corner, it had nothing to tell me.  I had to flick my eyes back in the opposite
direction to read the header there.  This
slowed me down considerably, especially at first, and I think it’s the only
true design flaw I noticed in the IJB. 
Even if Crossway puts nothing else on those blank pages, they should put
the book-and-chapter header.  That would
make turning to a given passage significantly easier.

**TEACHING.** Many of the same issues showed up
here.  I was not about to spend 45
minutes roving the front of the auditorium with a four-pound Bible, so it
pretty much stayed on the lectern unless I wanted to look something up.

The lack-of-headers issue also showed up.  During one of the classes, a student asked a
question about the origin of the Samaritans, but it took me so long to find 2
Kings 17 that somebody else got the reference before I did.  Humility is good for the soul, I guess.  It is true, though, that I didn’t miss any
citations in class either.

It might have made a difference had the IJB been a reference
Bible.  I will acknowledge that as I was
turning, I was debating about whether I was looking for 2 Kings 17 or 2 Kings
18.  However, the last thing that I want
in a ginormous brick of a Bible is a reference column to add even more
weight!  Plus, because the IJB is a
tweener Bible, it did offer me Old-Testament prophecy citations, etc., down at
the bottom.  None of the Crossway ESV’s
are true text Bibles.

**STUDY.** Of my three professional uses for the
IJB, I was actually happiest with it here. 
I wasn’t in any particular hurry to find any passages, so the header
problem never appeared.  The weight made
it a little awkward to use, but I solved the problem by leaning back in my desk
chair and propping the Bible on my breastbone. 
The text was comfortable to read, even with my glasses off.  I did miss a citation, though; I originally
wrote 1 John 2:17 when I meant 1 John 2:16. 
That may have been my fault rather than the Bible’s, though; there were
plenty of other errors in the sermon. 
It’s likely that I was simply unusually distracted during my writing and
preparation.

**CONCLUSION.** When I was a teenager, I learned that
though there were many beautiful young women in the world, most of them weren’t
meant for me.  Similarly, the IJB is a
beautiful Bible, but I don’t think it’s meant for me.  There are probably journalers out there who
would kill to get their hands on a Bible with that much room.  Given a couple decades, a skilled artist
could turn an IJB into a near rival to the St. John’s Bible.  I hope somebody does that with one of
them.  I’d love to see it.

However, I don’t want to journal.  I badly want a little bit of room in the
margins of my preaching Bibles, but that little bit of room is all I want.  As long as I can jot a few notes and
underline some key phrases, I’m happy. 
For me, then, the IJB is an embarrassment of riches, and all the white
space that doesn’t matter to me comes at the cost of things that do matter to
me, like smaller size and quick-turn ability. 
As much as I hate to do it, I have to award the IJB a 6.5/10 here.
