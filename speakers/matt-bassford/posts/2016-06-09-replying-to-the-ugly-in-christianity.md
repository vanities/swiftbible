It’s fairly rare to see the churches of Christ mentioned on
some major online news platform, even on said platform’s blog network.  As a result, when [this article](http://www.huffingtonpost.com/entry/the-ugly-in-christianity_us_57588f08e4b053e219786f6b) popped up on HuffPo recently, it got my attention.  The story here is a sad one that most
long-time Christians have seen before. 
Young person is “raised in the church” but is driven away by something
that she perceives as hypocritical or evil. 
However, I think there are some valuable take-homes here that are worth considering:



**Politics has no place
in our assemblies.** The story at the
heart of the HuffPo blogger’s narrative is of a woman standing up in Bible
class in a church of Christ someplace, proclaiming “Praise the Lord!  Ted Kennedy’s dead!”, and proceeding, to
applause, to pretend to dance a jig on his grave.  At that point, she says, she lost her faith entirely.  I have no reason to doubt that the story
happened, and if it did, there’s a whole lot of wrong here.

On a number of occasions, I’ve expressed my deep ambivalence
about being politically involved, even as an individual.  It requires me to make compromises with my
conscience, it tempts me to ungodly behavior, and it may well put a stumbling
block in the path of people with different political convictions than
mine.  It’s also true, though, that there
are plenty of good, sincere brethren out there whose faith leads them to be
politically active.  I acknowledge that
it’s a complex issue, and there’s plenty of room here for honest disagreement.

By contrast, I believe that there is no room whatsoever for
political expression in our assemblies and Bible classes.  Talk about moral issues?  Sure. 
I have no compunctions about preaching against abortion, even though
it’s also become a political football. 
The similarities between abortion and the worship of Molech are too much
for my conscience to ignore.

However, when I (or any other Christian) cross the line from
moral advocacy to political advocacy in a church setting, that’s going too
far.  First of all, it’s contrary to the
purposes of the gospel.  My work in the
pulpit is to promote Christ as King, not anybody for president.  Second, it invites all the harms that
accompany thoughtless political participation at an individual level.  Pretending to dance on Ted Kennedy’s grave is
at the very least inconsistent with Matthew 5:44.  If God takes no pleasure in the death of even
the wicked, why should we take pleasure in the death of anybody?  Third, political advocacy in an assembly can
cost a church its non-profit 501(c)(3) status and subject it to federal
taxation.

Finally, as it has evidently done in the mind of this
blogger, it creates confusion in the minds of those present as to what
Christianity is even about.  Those with
differing political convictions may be repulsed and driven away, and even those
who agree may fall prey to the delusion that being a Republican and being a
Christian are the same thing.  We are
called to be disciples, not voters and debaters.

**The churches of
Christ are extremely diverse.** As the
blogger was, I was raised in the church. 
If the church-house doors were open, my parents saw to it that I was
there.  I’ve been a Christian for 25
years, and I’ve been a member of seven different churches in four very
different states.  I’ve worshiped with
dozens more.  Never once have I seen
anything in an assembly that remotely approaches what she claims was a common
experience for her.  There are a couple
of Trump fans among my Facebook friends, but most of the Christians who have
shared their opinions of him with me can’t stand the guy.

On that basis, I’m tempted to claim that she’s exaggerating
or otherwise distorted the truth, but the reality is that my experience simply
isn’t wide enough to make such a claim. 
Congregational autonomy as we practice it means that churches are going
to be different, sometimes very, very different.  I can’t discount the possibility that there’s
a whole segment of the brotherhood out there that behaves exactly as she
describes.

Of course, that’s a knife that cuts both ways.  Whenever I see anybody, no matter their
sincerity, make blanket statements about how “the Church” is like this or like
that, I immediately begin to question whether they have the knowledge necessary
to make such a statement.  All of us are
tempted to generalize on the basis of our experience, but we must first
remember the limitations of that experience.

Usually, the best we can do is to re-enact the parable of
the blind men and the elephant.  We might
be holding an ear or a tail, but we haven’t gotten a handle on the whole
pachyderm.  There may well be churches of
Christ like the author describes. 
However, I can say with certainty that at least some of them are
different, and it may well be true that most of them are.  Only God knows for sure.

**Human imperfection is
a constant.** From time to time, I
have been known to observe that the church would be a truly wonderful
organization if only it didn’t have people in it.  People just mess everything up.

Of course, the messiness isn’t only the problem.  It’s also the point.  There are no perfect people, but if there
were, they wouldn’t have any need for the church.  Only the people who know they are imperfect
will seek for perfection through Christ.

To the blogger, then, I would say this:  I’m sorry that your experiences with the
church were bad.  Every Christian, one
way or another, will have similarly bad experiences with the church or its
members.  Preachers are by no means
exempt from this, either!

I’m sorry that your expectations were disappointed.  However, I fear that you will find that no
group of people will ever meet them.  There
are imperfect Republicans, Democrats, conservatives, liberals, believers, and
atheists.  In fact, every member of every
one of those groups is flawed, often grievously so.  If you expect better than that from anyone,
you can only find further disappointment.

By nature, I’m a pretty cynical guy.  However, I find that my cynicism drives me
toward the church rather than away from it. 
I don’t want to go through life alone, and if I’m going to be part of a
group, I want to be part of a group that owns its imperfections rather than
denies them.  It has at least the
potential to lead away from shrill self-righteousness (another human constant,
I fear) to humility and grace.  I’ve
noticed that on the left-hand side of things, grace for sin is an alien concept,
and grace (whether in the extending or the accepting) is not the kind of thing
that any of us can afford to lose.

I’ll be praying for the church.  I often do, especially when its members
strike me as particularly imperfect. 
I’ll be praying for you too. 
“Never again,” you say, but I know that life is long, and the road
before each of us has many twists and turns beyond our power to predict.  May the grace of Christ be with you, and with
me, and with all of us as we try to find our way home!
