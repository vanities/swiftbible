To those who’ve read much of my writing about hymns, it’s
obvious that I generally prefer a formal approach to the discipline.  I’m a writer to the depths of my soul, so bad
writing offends me (are we sure that bad writing isn’t a sin?).  I think it’s important to insist that hymns
be written well.

However, there’s a vast difference between what “should” be
sung and what is sung.  It’s a difference
that formal critique can’t capture or explain. 
If I spend my time arguing that Hymn X shouldn’t be sung rather than
seeking to understand why it is sung, I’m missing the point of Hymn X.  A hymn that is formally good but is never
sung is like a car that is never driven. 
It isn’t fulfilling its purpose.

If we want to understand the genre, then, we need a complementary
system of evaluation that is based on reality, not theory.  This evidence-based approach suggests a
division of hymns into four hierarchical categories.



**Failed Hymns**

The first of these categories is that of the failed
hymn.  A failed hymn is a hymn that is
not sung.  Such a hymn could be new or
old, published or unpublished.  Whatever
it is, wherever it comes from, if it isn’t sung, it doesn’t work.

There are several reasons for a hymn not to be sung.  The first of these is technical ineptitude.  It’s possible to have a good lyrical or
musical idea, yet execute that idea so badly that the idea is defeated by the
execution.  Hymns with unclear writing
and badly broken meter fall into this category. 
So too do hymn tunes that are overly complicated or have arrangements
with serious harmonic flaws.

Second, a hymn will not be sung if it does not resonate with
the singers.  This can be true of bad
hymns, but it can be true of technically proficient hymns too.  The latter are like the girl with the nice
personality.  They have a list of
positive attributes a mile long, but nobody’s interested.  A hymn that is a sparkling technical
achievement but gets ignored is still a failure.

This flaw can exist in the core thought of the hymn.  Maybe the hymn doesn’t have an identifiable
core thought at all.  Maybe the core
thought is buried beneath mounds of verbiage. 
Maybe the core thought simply doesn’t interest most worshipers.  As a rule of thumb, if the hymn isn’t about
something that would make a good Internet meme, the core thought is probably
not good enough.  For whatever reason,
hymns like this are sung politely rather than enthusiastically and soon
discarded.

The same thing can happen with music.  A hymn with boring music will not be sung. A
hymn with music that strikes worshipers as hokey or dated won’t be sung
either.  It does not have to be the case
that the music of the hymn matches the text emotionally.  “He Bore It All” was sung (and to a
diminishing extent is still sung) despite a dreadful text-tune mismatch.  However, a weak tune will doom a hymn.

**Successful Hymns**

On the next level up are the successful hymns.  These are hymns that are sung.  Something about them has captured the
attention of Christians so that they appear in worship.

These hymns do not have to be good.  In fact, most of the new hymns that are sung
in any era are not good.  However, all of
them have some quality that makes them stand out from the mass of failed hymns.

This quality could be lyrical.  The hymn could say something that Christians
want to say.  The central core thought
can be surrounded with bad writing like a diamond in a dungheap, but if it
resonates powerfully enough, worshipers will sing it anyway.

It could be musical. 
Some hymn tunes are earworms like a Top 40 smash hit.  Nobody listens to Top 40 radio for the
lyrics.  It’s about the music.  These hymns function the same way, often
using so much repetition that the lyrics contain little but the core thought.

Some hymns are sung because of good marketing.  This became possible with the advent of
electronic mass media.  The first hymns
to benefit from marketing were the Stamps-Baxter hymns of the radio era,
starting in the 1920’s.  Gilbert
Alexander (a hymnist and good friend of mine who has since passed on) once told
me that during his boyhood in Alabama, when he went outside on summer evenings,
he could hear the same Stamps-Baxter song playing from the open windows of
every house on the block.  Everybody was
listening to the same radio station.

When it comes to hymns, familiarity breeds fondness, not
contempt.  It’s hardly surprising that
Christians from that era imported many of their radio favorites into their
worship, even in cases where the music was technically demanding and
difficult.  They wanted to sing what they
listened to.

To a lesser extent, the same phenomenon operates today.  Even though there is no musical genre with
the same cohesion, reach, and power of the Southern gospel music of 75 years
ago among Southern evangelicals, marketing is still important.  A recording of an unremarkable song by a
prominent contemporary Christian group can vault it into use.

**Good Hymns**

Third, we come to good hymns.  A good hymn is a hymn that is sung outside
its own time.  Generally, it takes about 100-150
years for a hymn to be proven good (or not). 
Time is a ruthless winnower of hymns. 
The vast majority of hymns from any era will fall into disuse before the
first century elapses.  Today, it is the
repertoire of Southern gospel hymns that is being winnowed.  In about 50 years, the chaff of the
contemporary era will begin to be separated from the wheat.

With few exceptions, any hymn that survives this process
will be good.  Charles Wesley wrote
thousands of hymns.  We sing the dozen
best.  Fanny J. Crosby likewise wrote
thousands of hymns.  Perhaps a couple
dozen of her hymns remain in use today. 
Many more of those hymns were sung in their own time, but once they
ceased to be trendy, they lost their appeal and were forgotten.

For a hymn to ascend to the pantheon of the good, it’s not
enough for it to have one good thought or an appealing tune.  The content has to be good, the writing has
to be smooth, and the text and tune have to match.  It has to work as a hymn, not merely as a
cultural expression.

**Great Hymns**

Finally, there are hymns that are great.  These hymns are sung widely, not only outside
their own time, but outside their own religious/musical tradition.  Hymns like this have such compelling
thoughts, such strong content, and such striking music that they have a
universal appeal to believers.  These are
the hymns that everybody knows:  “Holy,
Holy, Holy”, “Amazing Grace”, “The Church’s One Foundation”, and so on.

No writer ever has shown the ability to consistently produce
great hymns, and only William Bradbury among composers consistently produced
great hymn tunes.  No era of hymnody has
contributed more than a hundred or so to the list.  Such a repertoire can only be developed by
the efforts of thousands over the course of centuries.

Here, then, is the single biggest problem with the push to
replace the traditional body of hymns. 
It is the loss of the great hymns. 
Older hymns are not inherently better than newer hymns, but the older
hymns that worshipers still sing are. 
They’ve been tested and refined in a way that no contemporary praise
song yet has been.  They rely not on
trendiness, but on spiritual strength.

Certainly, we should sing hymn written in the past few
decades.  Every era does.  However, we must not believe that our lyrical
and musical tastes represent a quantum leap in human understanding.  The electric guitar has not unlocked the
secrets of worship, and to the extent that it has driven worshipers from
actually singing, it has done great harm.

Wisdom teaches us that our approach to worship should be
evolutionary, not revolutionary.  As we
carefully add the best of the new to the enduring old, God will be best
glorified.
