*And his brothers said
to him, "Shall you indeed reign over us? Or shall you indeed have dominion
over us?" So they hated him even more for his dreams and for his
words.*

--Genesis 37:8

As we saw last week, the household of Jacob was not an oasis
of tranquility and harmony.  Joseph was
Jacob’s favorite son, and he made sure that everybody else in the family knew
it.  Understandably, the rest of Joseph’s
brothers resented this favoritism.  Clearly, the situation is bad enough without
Joseph adding anything else to it, but he fails to realize this.



One night he has a dream. 
In this dream, he and his brothers are in the field binding sheaves of
grain, and his sheaf stands upright while the other sheaves bow down to
it.  The interpretation of this dream is
obvious:  someday, Joseph’s older
brothers are going to bow down to him. 
Joseph, however, fails to realize how his brothers are going to take
this announcement, and he tells them about it. 
Predictably, they ridicule this dream and hate him even more because of
it.  However, Joseph still doesn’t learn
his lesson.  Next, he has a dream about
the sun, the moon, and eleven stars bowing down to him.  Obviously, his father is included in this one,
so this time, he tells his whole family about his dream.  Jacob keeps an open mind about the dream, but
Joseph’s brothers just want to wring his neck.

The problem with Joseph’s behavior wasn’t that he was
wrong.  In fact, the later course of
Joseph’s life proves that this was a legitimate vision sent by God.  The problem was that chose to tell his
brothers about this dream in a way that appeared to be self-glorifying.  This only added to their pre-existing
hostility toward him.

As Christians, it’s easy for us to make the same
mistake.  Several years ago, when I was
still living in Houston, I mentioned to a non-Christian co-worker that I
worshiped with a church of Christ.  He
instantly responded, “Oh, you’re the ones who think that you’re the only ones
going to heaven.”  The Lord’s church has
precisely that reputation in many places, particularly in the South, and it
doubtless acquired it because of all of the brethren in years past who have
told their denominational neighbors, “We’re going to heaven, and you’re not.”

Even though we rightfully fear for the souls of those who
are not baptized for the forgiveness of sins, loud proclamations of our
righteousness and everybody else’s wickedness do more harm than good.  If we announce to someone that they’re going
to hell, but they won’t see us there, that’s not an invitation to Bible
study.  It’s an insult, and most will
perceive it as such.  When we speak to
outsiders, we must remember that the goal is to persuade, to gently lead others
to draw correct conclusions about their salvation, not to force our conclusion
on them without the underlying logic. 
Otherwise, like Joseph, we will be speaking the truth in a way that only
generates resentment.
