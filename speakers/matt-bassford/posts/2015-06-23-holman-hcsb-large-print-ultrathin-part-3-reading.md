**OVERVIEW.** Generally, the Large-Print Ultrathin
fares well as a reader.  Its lovely
hand-feel, high characters-per-line count, line matching, and high-quality
print all commend it.  My only real objection
is to the design decision to make columns of poetry narrower than columns of
prose, which only accentuates one of the usual design problems with
double-column Bibles.



\*             \*             \*

As is my invariable practice, I used the LPUT as my daily
reading Bible for two weeks, fourteen sessions in all (though I think I may
have doubled up on my reading once after skipping a day).  Here are my impressions:

**SIZE.** Once again, the LPUT is a little bit
outside my comfort zone here.  I normally
don’t prefer to read from Bibles that are larger than 6” x 9” in trim
size.  However, I really liked the size
of the LPUT as a reader.

Most of the time, when I’m reading from a larger Bible, I
will prop it against my breastbone to support it.  Not so with the LPUT.  At less than 2 pounds, it’s a light enough
Bible that I could one-hand it throughout a reading session, and its flexible,
touchable cover meant that I wanted to. 
They say that you shouldn’t judge a book by its cover, but in the case
of Bibles, cover quality really does matter a lot.

**LAYOUT.** Generally, I was impressed with the
design of the LPUT.  As soon as I opened
it, I said to myself, “That’s a 2K design, isn’t it?” and quickly confirmed my
impression with an e-mail.  From
everything I’ve seen, our friends in Denmark do good work, and the LPUT brings
no shame to the firm.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEg6VbZYsraoGLDj5DLO7Sjbfd4nX17DE6GXek4PV_GDcjVH1R-Cb0-1ZG5xX5ado8oqN2Y16i3P9uT1X3vJd1y6SXnTh_MmPhCKjqkG1ytkIsojaBJ7t4KxwCB69wJ5PkA849-7M1gIgg/s640/R_general.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEg6VbZYsraoGLDj5DLO7Sjbfd4nX17DE6GXek4PV_GDcjVH1R-Cb0-1ZG5xX5ado8oqN2Y16i3P9uT1X3vJd1y6SXnTh_MmPhCKjqkG1ytkIsojaBJ7t4KxwCB69wJ5PkA849-7M1gIgg/s1600/R_general.jpg)



First of all, the characters-per-line count in the prose
sections of the LPUT is good.  I make it
out to be somewhere in the 40-42 cpl range. 
This is unusually good for a center-column-reference Bible.  In fact, the only other such Bibles with a
decent cpl count that I can think of are Cambridge’s tribe of Pitt
Minions.  The Pitt Minion accomplishes
its magic by being a relatively wide Bible for its height.  The LPUT, however, appears to get there with
a combination of fairly narrow typeface and narrow reference column.  It’s an interesting design choice that works
for me.



2K also made an interesting design choice in dealing with
the poetic sections of the Bible.  The
HCSB has more of these than most other translations, versifying even some
sections of the New Testament. 
Generally, double-column Bibles have trouble with poetry.  Because the poetic lines are often longer
than the width of the column, they create all sorts of one-word overhangs
(called “widows” by typesetters) that are unsightly and interfere with reading
comprehension.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEjgzzW9sySWxYeV2LEKOLuAn1fXeQj-B87olfr1I8BlX2yBZyUMnjkOKjEwRR_PnZqg-dXVxRU9L3iZokZLSie-noYYfGUPGbxhnfiUY_CGpDoW0UQka2EWZVKKVvFnJO8sIzhnxdQing/s640/R_no+widows.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEjgzzW9sySWxYeV2LEKOLuAn1fXeQj-B87olfr1I8BlX2yBZyUMnjkOKjEwRR_PnZqg-dXVxRU9L3iZokZLSie-noYYfGUPGbxhnfiUY_CGpDoW0UQka2EWZVKKVvFnJO8sIzhnxdQing/s1600/R_no+widows.jpg)

In dealing with this problem, the design of the LPUT both
gives and takes away.  On the plus side,
so far as I can tell, the volume is widow-free. 
The typesetters chose to break overly long lines into phrases, so that
every line in the poetry of the Bible has at least two words and they make some
kind of grammatical sense together.  This
is good.  Every Bible design should do
likewise.

However, the LPUT has another design feature that doesn’t
work nearly as well for me.  The left
side of poetic text is indented over nearly half an inch from where a column of
prose text would begin.  This costs the
poetry 7-8 precious characters per line.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEjl7b9rWrukdgk2lg17VSeAQ_0NLrK3TVe0KlWFNShN1VhpnYDdMP2J_PNXhJJDxhoGKnd3EfPymPsDqi2CXOsHa0yk8N41Ckqpl2hEBCx2GQPwFnoldvFlk1Vd0JRQRQXysXYh5x4kfA/s640/R_mixed.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEjl7b9rWrukdgk2lg17VSeAQ_0NLrK3TVe0KlWFNShN1VhpnYDdMP2J_PNXhJJDxhoGKnd3EfPymPsDqi2CXOsHa0yk8N41Ckqpl2hEBCx2GQPwFnoldvFlk1Vd0JRQRQXysXYh5x4kfA/s1600/R_mixed.jpg)



In most Bibles, this would result in an epidemic of
widows.  In the LPUT, it creates a lot of
two- and three-word lines, which also has an unfortunate effect on the flow of
the text.  In fact, it feels kind of like
a dramatic reading by William Shatner: 
“In the beginning was.  The
Word.  And the Word was with God.  And the Word was God.  He was with God.  In the beginning.”

I appreciate the aesthetic effect of setting the poetry
columns off like that, but I think that it loses in utility what it gains in
beauty.  Instead, I believe that a Bible
design works better when the designers take advantage of all the space
available for a poetic line.

As I mentioned in the “Preaching, Teaching, and Study”
portion of the review, the LPUT has half-inch inner margins.  They aren’t much good for writing in, but
they do a marvelous job of keeping the text out of the gutter, especially in
such a thin volume.  It makes me wonder,
in fact, if the LPUT was originally designed to be printed on thicker paper
(thicker Bibles need larger gutters), but someone along the way decided to
print it on thinner paper instead so that they could advertise it as Less Than
An Inch Thick.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEjgPxGQ5E7Cmp6p9jWx58eQdSRowXC_xT3bWevDODRoYVMRkIObVfO2blFd2qOfgrfo_EMruxplVlADNuDCPTdiHsLrLw_TsxAV5E3FcGTIyMBKv26cwjHYEtU3FEpVBOXN7wGztJkEmA/s640/R_prose.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEjgPxGQ5E7Cmp6p9jWx58eQdSRowXC_xT3bWevDODRoYVMRkIObVfO2blFd2qOfgrfo_EMruxplVlADNuDCPTdiHsLrLw_TsxAV5E3FcGTIyMBKv26cwjHYEtU3FEpVBOXN7wGztJkEmA/s1600/R_prose.jpg)



The LPUT is a red-letter Bible, but it’s one of the good
ones.  The red ink is a nice dark brick
red, and there don’t even appear to be any registration problems.  As far as I’m concerned, it reads as smoothly
as a black-letter Bible would.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEhP5H6kSep9PQT_9Op05Nrn7eANisWnoS9l1gjppUzOcWnW7uwKJYUNfOjjdNcL-5mWKpZ20L-XIi7pY6G4lcoqa3l6py9PrZdTfso2TOa4o5B_jNVg1KVEEl-7qY9eJHw-5vHaMtPOxA/s640/R_redletter.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEhP5H6kSep9PQT_9Op05Nrn7eANisWnoS9l1gjppUzOcWnW7uwKJYUNfOjjdNcL-5mWKpZ20L-XIi7pY6G4lcoqa3l6py9PrZdTfso2TOa4o5B_jNVg1KVEEl-7qY9eJHw-5vHaMtPOxA/s1600/R_redletter.jpg)



Like most settings from the past few years (and the LPUT was
first published in 2013), this is a line-matched Bible.  As always, there are a few hiccups with the
line-matching here and there, but they aren’t a big deal, and the line-matching
does a great deal to mitigate the ghosting problems posed by the thin paper
used.

Finally, the print is plenty big enough for me to read.  The advertising copy for the LPUT claims that
it has 10.5-point font.  When I’m just
looking at the Bible by itself, I say, “That can’t be right!”  I would eyeball it at more of a 9-point
font.  However, once I started getting
other Bibles down and comparing print size, it became apparent that yes, the
print size is as advertised.

I think the culprit here is the sans-serif typeface used
(probably another 2K design).  As a rule
of thumb, I prefer serif typefaces for printed material and sans-serif
typefaces for online text.  Pixellated
computer screens have trouble displaying serifs correctly, but in print, the
fine lines of serifs make a typeface more readable.  As a result, the sans-serif font in the LPUT
makes it read a little bit smaller than it actually is.  Maybe Holman opted for the sans-serif design
because they wanted a Bible that looked trendy and modern.  It’s not really a big deal, though.

**PRODUCTION
QUALITY.** My LPUT, at least, is
unusual because of its origin in the USA. 
Most of the Bibles I review are either printed in the Netherlands by
Jongbloed or in China by who-knows-who. 
As a rule, Jongbloed Bibles have crisper print than any of the
China-print Bibles, though it is certainly true that the quality of the latter
has improved greatly in the past 10 years.

However, I had trouble comparing the LPUT to any of my
Jongbloed Bibles.  The Jongbloed print
quality shows up most in serifs, particularly the serifs in the
cross-references, and without serifs to examine, I can’t really get as good a
sense for the USA print.  All the
sans-serif text in the LPUT, though, looks as crisp as it could be.  This, combined with the execution of the
red-letter text, leads me to think that these printers know their business.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEhPih1x5SWRvhB_jxo6S5KUziuWABfsj10jln82ATyNGMGP35SG1ylfMofEFMU8ScIrhB3mBG3EEqEczw9f_cSC-mvn1nfbt4c-G-TxhxX-lPBD8uJJHlt6tAwtM8QcTe4lTLnviB86aA/s640/R_poetry.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEhPih1x5SWRvhB_jxo6S5KUziuWABfsj10jln82ATyNGMGP35SG1ylfMofEFMU8ScIrhB3mBG3EEqEczw9f_cSC-mvn1nfbt4c-G-TxhxX-lPBD8uJJHlt6tAwtM8QcTe4lTLnviB86aA/s1600/R_poetry.jpg)



That paper, though. 
I’ve spent the whole review complaining about it, so even though I’m
still not happy with it, I want to say a word in its defense.  Bible design is the product of
compromises.  It’s simply not possible
for a Bible to offer every feature that anyone might want.  Even while I clamor for more opaque paper, I
still find myself praising characteristics of the LPUT that are the direct
result of the choice to use thinner paper. 
An LPUT with more opaque paper wouldn’t be as light and easy to one-hand.  It wouldn’t be as thin.  It wouldn’t be as flexible.  It wouldn’t have such a nice wide
gutter.  I like all of those things, so
maybe I just need to shut up about paper opacity.



**CONCLUSION.** I thought the LPUT was a fine
reader.  The only thing that really
bugged me about it was the choice to use narrower columns when the text is set
as poetry.  Really, though, the cpl
problems even there aren’t any worse than they would be in most
center-column-reference Bibles.  I
therefore rate the LPUT at 9/10 in reading. 
Overall, it scores a 23/30, mainly because of my unhappiness at paper
rippling in a premium Bible.
