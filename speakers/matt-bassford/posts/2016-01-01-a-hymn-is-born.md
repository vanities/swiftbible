Much like the Spanish Inquisition, nobody ever expects a
hymn to show up.  At least, I don’t.  I never sit down intending to write; instead,
I get an idea from who-knows-where and go with it.  This happened to me yesterday, and I thought
y’all might be interested in a glimpse into the initial process.



It started over my Christmas vacation, when I was hanging
out with my brother, and he showed me a YouTube of a song called “Palästinalied”.  The hymn tune to “Fairest Lord Jesus” is
called CRUSADER’S HYMN because somebody started spreading the story that the
tune was originally sung by knights on their way to the Holy Land.  Not true; CRUSADER’S HYMN actually began life
as a Silesian peasant song.

However, “Palästinalied” is the real deal.  It was written in the early part of the
thirteenth century by Walther von der Vogelweide to gin up support for the
Fifth Crusade.  I thought that was pretty
cool, and I liked the tune besides, so I filed it away for future reference.

Yesterday, I decided while I was eating breakfast (at about
8:30; I’m sick, so I’m slacking) that it would be fun to watch the “Palästinalied”
YouTube again before getting ready to go to my office at the church building.  I watched it and decided that I could enter
the melody into Finale (a music-notation program) before I went to the office.

In real life, entering only a melody into Finale is like
opening a bag of Cool Ranch Doritos and eating one chip.  It is not something that I will actually do.  Once I’ve gotten that far, I will start
arranging the tune.  So it happened
yesterday.  I started wondering what a
particular melody line would sound like with harmony, so I filled it in, and
once you start harmonizing, all the empty measures to either side beckon
invitingly.

I threw down a quickie arrangement and started looking
around for other versions of “Palastinalied” (any tune that is 800 years old is
going to have them).  I didn’t like the
ornamentation in the melody, and I could tell that it was tending toward a
meter (lyrical, not musical) that wouldn’t give singers room to breathe.  I found another YouTube, this time by a
singer named Annwn (I think that’s missing some vowels).  If you like harp-accompanied medieval songs
in dead languages, you will like Annwn. 
Her take on “Palästinalied” sounds [like this.](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3_rbio4-RDk)

Annwn’s version cleared up the metrical issues in the other
versions I’d found, so I decided to use it as the basis for my arrangement,
entered it into Finale, and put a little more care into my harmonization.  It was about 11:30 by this point, and I still
hadn’t gotten to the shower, much less to the office.

At this point, I started to think about what kind of a tune
I had.  Folk-song writers break meter all
over the place, so when I’m working with a folk tune, I look for what I think
of as the “native meter”, the meter the tune would have without all the extra
notes added to accommodate broken meter. 
I also looked at the original meter and rhyme scheme of “Palästinalied”.  I decided that the rhythms of the version
that I was using implied a different meter and rhyme scheme than the von der
Vogelweide original; to be exact, a hymn in 7.8.7.8.77.8, with masculine rhymes
on the 7-syllable lines and feminine rhymes on the 8-syllable lines.

That’s a weird meter. 
The Cyber Hymnal contains more than 10,000 hymns, but none in
7.8.7.8.77.8.  However, I had a hymn in a
similar meter.  After nearly 20 years of
writing hymns, I have an awful lot of hymns and tunes sitting around in limbo,
waiting to be used for spare parts.  One
such hymn, which had been giving real live composers fits for about six years
now, is called “Light Your Love Within My Heart”.  It’s written in 7.8.7.8.77.88, so I would
have to amputate some lines and change up some rhyme schemes (a 7.8.7.8.77.8
calls for a triple rhyme on the feminine rhymed-lines), but I thought I could
make it work.  It was now about 1:30, so
I decided to take a shower and think about it.

I emerged from the shower convinced that “Light Your Love”
would fit with some surgery.  I spent the
next couple of hours performing the surgery, amputating a couplet here, adding
a new rhyme there.  The majority of all
of the original verses survived the operation, which in my book counts as minor
repair.

It was now 3:30, and I hadn’t eaten lunch yet.  I’m hypoglycemic, so this was bad.  I decided to eat lunch (at the computer, as I
continue to tweak things) and go to the grocery store so I will have things to
eat in future.  I also needed to buy a
mailbox to replace the one the plow truck slew a few days back.  As I did my shopping, I continued to rework
the hymn in my head.  This is
particularly dangerous when I’m driving, but I managed to make it home without
hitting anybody, though I did shut the garage door on the bumper of my car,
scratching it up pretty impressively.

I spent the next several hours doing more fine-tuning (a
hymn is most fluid during the first 24 hours after writing) until the hymn
stopped making sense to me.  At that
point, I gave up, watched part of a movie (*The
Two Towers*, in keeping with the medieval theme of the day), and went to
bed.  Here’s the result of my day of
labor:

Light Your Love Within My Heart

Light Your love within my heart;

Jesus, fill me with its burning;

Breathe upon it at the start;

Fan to life its godly yearning.

From the ashes, wake a flame

That will glorify your name,

Keeping darkness from returning.

Feed Your love within my heart,

Casting in the sins I treasure;

Lest it dwindle and depart,

Offer up each passing pleasure,

While refining in the fire

Holy action and desire;

Make me Yours in fullest measure.

Show Your love within my heart

That the world may be enlightened;

May its radiance impart

Comfort to the lost and frightened.

By the kindness you have willed,

May their souls be warmed and filled

And this darkling life be brightened.

However, the hymn certainly isn’t anywhere done.  Please don’t take it and try to use it
yet!  I probably spent 10-12 hours on it
yesterday, which means that it will take about 90 more hours of fine-tuning  and considering critique from other writers
before I arrive at a finished hymn.  In
the late stages of editing, if I spend an hour and change a word, I consider
that a productive session.

Of course, this hymn may not get that far; if I get a sense
that the idea of the hymn is not innately good enough to be sung, I’ll stop
working on it.  Regardless, this is how
far it’s come, and that’s how I spent the last day of 2015.
