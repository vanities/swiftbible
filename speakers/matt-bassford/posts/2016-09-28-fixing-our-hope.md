Yesterday, one of my Facebook friends posted 1 Peter 1:12 as
his status update.  He made no indication
one way or the other, but I suspect he may have had the recent presidential
debate in mind.  If so, he’s certainly
right.  Though the passage may seem to
have more relevance in this election cycle, it’s equally applicable to every
election.  As Christians, we’re not
supposed to put our trust in princes. 
Only those who trust in Jesus will not be disappointed.



However, that strikes me as a for-you-but-not-for-me
application.  I’ve had a cynical
perspective on politics for years, and if this election is any kind of
barometer, increasing numbers of believers are joining me in cynicism.  I’m not at all sure that’s entirely healthy,
but it’s certainly better than confusing voting with discipleship.

Nonetheless, my life does not allow me the luxury of wagging
my finger at all those poor deluded souls who have these great expectations for
the political process.  I may not fix my
hope on a particular candidate, but that does not mean that I’m fixing my hope
on Jesus either.  Indeed, Peter specifies
that our hope must be *completely* fixed
on the revelation of the Lord, which leaves no room for anything else.

I suspect that few if any Christians manage to make their
hope in Christ exclusive.  I certainly
struggle with it.  By nature, I’m a
planner, and it’s hard to plan unless you have a goal in mind.  I hope to continue working effectively for
the kingdom.  I hope to save enough to
ensure a comfortable retirement.  I hope
to raise children who are faithful Christians and productive members of
society.  For that matter, I hope to
write a great hymn.

I think those are all good and worthwhile goals, but fixing
my hope on any of those things is problematic. 
First of all, all of those things are uncertain.  Noah preached for 120 years and got
nowhere.  The government may confiscate
every dime I own if I refuse to quit preaching against the practice of
homosexuality.  My kids may turn out
worthless, and my writing may never rise above the mediocre.  If I’ve built my life on those things, what
then?

Second, if I allow something to become more important to me
than preparing for the Lord’s return, the devil will certainly find that
weakness and exploit it.  How many
preachers through the years have been led astray from the gospel by the desire
to draw a crowd?  How many pew-sitters
have lost their souls because they love money more than God?  How many Christians make idols of their
families?  All of these things, good
though they are, can easily have a distracting and distorting effect.

Instead, we must be careful to attach the proper amount of
importance to them.  Our vision of the
future must be dominated by one fact. 
The day will come when we must give an account of our lives to God.  On that day, our only hope will be
Jesus.  Therefore, every day of our lives
must be about Him.

When I was in law school, my professors adopted an elegant approach
to determining my grade.  100 percent of
the grade was in the final:  no homework,
no quizzes, no required attendance, nothing. 
My time was my own.  I could spend
every day of the semester drinking myself into an alcoholic coma if I really
wanted to.  However, what I couldn’t do
was avoid the final, not and remain enrolled.

Similarly, we cannot avoid our need for Jesus.  Like my law-school professors, He doesn’t
baby us.  He doesn’t make us maintain our
relationships with Him.  Our decisions
are up to us.  However, unless we make
those decisions with the goal of seeking Him first and only, we won’t find Him
when we need Him.  There is no other
sensible way to live our lives than to go all-in on Jesus.
