Every time a church is presented with a new hymn, it (often
unconsciously) engages in a cost-benefit analysis.  It asks, “Do we like this hymn well enough
that we want to go to the trouble of learning and introducing it?”  This is a sliding scale.  The more difficult a hymn is, the better the
church has to like it to add it to the repertoire.

Thus, it’s helpful for writers, composers, and
hymn-introducers to think of hymns as having a difficulty budget.  You can only include a certain amount of
difficulty in your hymn if you want it to be sung. Concerning this, there are
two things to keep in mind:

- Your difficulty budget is always lower than you think it
is, because your hymn is not as good as you think it is.

- If you overspend your difficulty budget, your hymn will
not be sung.



However, there are several factors that increase the
difficulty budget of a hymn.  The more
these factors are present, the more difficulty a hymn can get away with.  These factors include:

**Compelling Content.** There is something about the subject or
expression of a hymn that grabs its target audience and makes them want to sing
it.  The more profound a hymn and the
more emotionally involved its writer, the more difficulty a church is willing
to accept in it.

Sometimes, the compelling nature of a hymn is evident even
outside its target audience.  Recently, a
friend sent me a copy of the Mormon hymn “O My Father”.  I am not a Mormon, and I do not agree with
the doctrine the hymn teaches (it’s about the preexistence of the human soul
before conception), but I’d be lying if I said the hymn didn’t affect me
emotionally.  I found it both sincere and
beautiful, and there was a part of me that wanted to sing it despite my
intellectual objections.  If a hymn like
this can move even those who don’t agree with it, those who do agree with it
will find/make a way to sing it.

**Strong Rhyme.** At least for English speakers, rhyme is
the lyrical equivalent of a cadence in music. 
After a hymn introduces the first word of a rhyme pair, the congregation
finds it emotionally satisfying to sing the second.  People like to sing rhymes, so the better a
hymn rhymes, the more likely they are to sing it.  I don’t know why this is, but I’m certain
that it is.

Sometimes, rhyme is even enough to carry a hymn in the
absence of other redeeming qualities. 
Even though I (thankfully) don’t see it much these days, I grew up
singing the gospel standard “Let Me Live Close to Thee”.  Its first stanza reads:

In the field I would wield sickles brave and true;

In the fight for the right I would dare and do,

Spend my days in Thy praise all the journey through;

Let me live close to Thee each day!

As content goes, the hymn is quite frankly incoherent.  Is it about agriculture?  Warfare? 
Spiritual pilgrimage?  It does not
merely mix metaphors; it churns them. 
The tune, though sprightly, isn’t anything special.  This is a hymn that is sung, when it is sung,
because of its intricate internal rhyme scheme.

Ironically, the rhyme scheme is also the source of its
content flaws.  The author didn’t have
the technical skill needed to hit a rhyme every three syllables while staying
on topic (which, honestly, may be impossible in English), so, like the sermon
with no outline, he went everywhere preaching the gospel.  However, the rhymes were enjoyable enough
that congregations adopted his work despite its schizoid message.

I’m certainly not holding “Let Me Live Close to Thee” up as
a model for emulation!  However, it does
demonstrate the appeal of strong rhyme, and writers and song leaders are
foolish if they don’t pay attention.

**Compelling Melody**.  Christians like to sing hymns with pretty
melodies.  Duh.  Here, it’s particularly important to remember
that a given melody is not as good as you think it is.  However, a melody that believers find
themselves humming or whistling throughout the week is likely to bring the hymn
to which it is attached into the repertoire. 
If the melody is good enough, they will do so even if the hymn puts
other obstacles in their way.

There are also several congregation-specific factors that affect
the difficulty budget, namely:

**Attitude Toward New Hymns.** Some congregations love to learn and
incorporate new hymns; others do not. 
Some say, “Sing to the Lord a new song!” 
Others reply, “What’s wrong with all these hymns in the hymnal we’ve
been using for 35 years?”  As always, my
preference is for good hymns, not new or old hymns.  However, it is certainly true that a
congregation that inclines toward that latter will have to be presented with a
new hymn that is truly compelling before it will adopt it.  It has a much smaller difficulty budget than
an Athenian-spirited church that will sing any “something new”, so it will balk
at the slightest obstacle to incorporation.

**Singer/Performer
Skill.** When it comes to ability to
learn a new hymn, not all congregations are created equal.  Some, by dint of talent, practice, or pure
dumb luck, have members who are good enough sightreaders that they’re able to
tackle just about anything.  There are
congregations out there who sing hymns of mine that I would never have dared to
write today (where angels feared to tread, there I rushed).  Others have a more modest capacity for
mastering unfamiliar songs, even with the best will in the world.

When there is a mismatch between the musical ability of a
congregation and the difficulty level of material, the congregation is likely
to move from participatory to performance worship.  In my own religious tradition, congregations
that wanted to adopt a praise-song repertoire for a-cappella use have often
found themselves driven to form praise teams. 
These are groups of skilled singers at the front who in theory guide the
congregation through the hymn but in practice can end up supplanting the
singing of the congregation with their performance.  These congregations have been able to
increase their difficulty budgets, but at the (in my opinion, dire) cost of
limiting congregational participation.

**Cultural
Context.** Any spiritual song, no
matter how difficult, has a much greater chance of being accepted if it has
already become a Christian contemporary radio hit.  Most people find it much easier to learn a
song by listening to it repeatedly than by reading the music, and if they’ve
been hearing it on their morning commute for the past two months, much of the
work of learning it has already been done. 
Similarly, most people develop an emotional attachment to the
familiar.  They like the song because
they’ve heard it performed by professional musicians, even if Sunday-morning
renditions never reach that level.

In my own religious tradition, this has been going on for
decades.  It began with the gospel
quartets of the 1920s through the 1950s. 
Many Southern-gospel hymns are musically difficult, especially in the
choruses.  I shudder to think what it
would be like to try to sightread one of those lickety-split syncopated
two-pagers (the bass line for “He Bore It All”, anyone?), and I’m probably a
better sightreader than most.  We sing
them not because people learned them from the sheet music, but because they
learned them from the radio, and we have known how those hymns are supposed to
sound ever since.

This sorting process is not something that Christians
consciously have to decide to do.  It’s
something that will happen automatically. 
Instead, it’s something that writers and introducers of hymns need to take
into account.  Only then can we make sure
that our efforts aren’t self-defeating.
