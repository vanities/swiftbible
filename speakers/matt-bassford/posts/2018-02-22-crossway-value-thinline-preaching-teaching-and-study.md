**OVERVIEW:** Not terrible to use, but the
lower-quality paper and lack of a ribbon marker do create some issues.

This has probably been the strangest review period for any
Bible I’ve ever reviewed.  It was much
longer than normal, but it was also filled with travel and distraction.  I’m pretty sure that along the way, I
preached at least three sermons out of it, maybe more.  I wasn’t tasked with teaching a Bible class
during the review period, but I did lead several smaller studies.  Finally, I used it for various study
purposes, including writing my father’s eulogy (though I used my father’s Bible
to deliver it).  Here’s how I thought it
did.



**PREACHING.** I think that my tastes have changed again
here.  For the past couple of years, I’ve
been trumpeting the value of wide-margin Bibles in the pulpit, but recently,
I’ve not been excited about them as I used to be.  I’ve become increasingly comfortable
preaching away from the pulpit without notes of any kind, so I’m not getting as
much out of marking up a wide-margin Bible beforehand.  In case my mind goes blank, I’ve got a sermon
notebook with a moderately detailed outline sitting on the pulpit.

As a result, my primary concerns in a preaching Bible are
now threefold:  Can I turn pages quickly
in it, can I cite verses correctly in it, and can I read it?  Sad to say, I had considerable trouble
turning pages in the Value Thinline.  I
chalk this up to the kind of paper used. 
To me, it feels almost as much like newspaper as it does like Bible
paper.  It’s rougher, and I think that
keeps the pages from sliding smoothly against one another.  As a result, I had many more fumbly moments
while preaching than I’m used to, which wasn’t my favorite.

By contrast, finding a citation wasn’t a problem.  I don’t remember any problems doing this once
I fumbled to the correct page.  Also,
7.5-point Lexicon No. 1 is small print by anybody’s definition, but when I have
my glasses on while preaching, I didn’t have any problem making it out.

I did miss the absent ribbon marker, though.  In the pulpit, I use the marker when I’m
citing multiple passages in the same context. 
I’ll mark my place, set the Bible down, explain what I’ve just read,
then come back and resume the reading. 
In times past, I’ve used a visitor card as a bookmark in Bibles that
didn’t come with one, but I didn’t like that solution with the Value
Thinline.  The text block is so thin that
the visitor card affected the way I opened the Bible.  As a result, I found myself leaving the Value
Thinline open upside-down on the pulpit like a paperback book.  It got the job done, I guess, but a ribbon
marker would have been considerably more elegant.  Note, of course, that the Value Thinline’s
more upscale brother, the Crossway Ultrathin, does have a ribbon marker.

**TEACHING.** When I’m teaching a class, I care about
small size and cross-references (if I’m teaching a reference-heavy subject like
the book of Revelation).  In less formal
studies, I’m invariably sitting down, so small size stops mattering quite so
much.  However, cross-references are
still nice to have sometimes.

The Value Thinline is certainly small.  It weighs less than a pound, which means I
can carry it all day and not notice.  As
far as cross-references go, it’s the typical Crossway tweener Bible.  Most Crossway products, even those that are
nominally text Bibles, will have cross-references for Old-Testament passages
cited in the New Testament and parallel passages in the Synoptic Gospels.  Interestingly, they don’t contain
cross-references for the times when the Old Testament quotes the Old Testament
(like Nahum 1:15 quoting Isaiah 52:7) and the New Testament quotes the New
Testament (like 1 Timothy 4:18 quoting Luke 10:7).  Not sure why not.  Regardless, even at that level of references,
the Value Thinline will give me most of what I need.  My most frustrating moment during its trial
period came not when I was teaching, but when I was listening.  The teacher mentioned that the Israelites had
put a pot of manna in the ark of the covenant, I couldn’t remember where that
was, and the cross-references weren’t helpful (Exodus 16:32-34 and Hebrews 9:4,
by the way).  As a rule, I find that I
care more about cross-references when I’m a student because I’m more prone to
bunny-trailing then.

**STUDY.** My expanded review period was a pretty
low-effort season of preparing for sermons and kitchen-table studies.  However, the Value Thinline did throughout
it.  It wasn’t a problem to hold or read,
and I don’t remember any trips to the bookcase for reference volumes that a
Bible with no cross-references would have spared me.  For instance, I wanted to see whether the
Hebrews writer is citing the Septuagint in Hebrews 10:5-7 (he is), but no Bible
that is not a Septuagint could have helped me with that one.

**CONCLUSION.** This isn’t a bad Bible for teaching an
informal class or studying by oneself, but I didn’t care for it in the
pulpit.  I’m not one of those guys who
goes gaga over Berisford ribbons, but a ribbon marker at all is a nice thing to
have!  Overall, the Value Thinline
deserves about a 7/10 for preaching, teaching and study.
