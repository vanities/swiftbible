**OVERVIEW.** The Large-Print Compact does all kinds of
things right here.  Its paperback-like
size is nearly ideal.  It’s a text Bible
with columns of just the right width to make for optimal reading for me.  There are no gutter problems, and the red
letters for the words of Christ are nice and dark.  The Bible is line-matched, and the text is
standard size, larger than one would expect from a small Bible.  However, the paper and print quality leave
something to be desired.

**SIZE.** Sure, I can lose myself reading the Bible
even when I’m hefting some black-leather behemoth, but I’d rather not.  It’s still much more pleasant to me to kick
back in my chair, one-hand a Bible, and read that way, just as though I were
reading some brain-candy fantasy novel. 
The LPC allows me to do exactly that, which makes my daily reading flow
as smoothly as it possibly can.  Gold
star here.

**LAYOUT.** There is a lot to like about the layout
of the LPC.  In fact, it’s one of the
most impressive pieces of design I’ve seen from a mass-market Bible.  Much to my delight, it bucks the
single-column trend and presents the text in two sturdy columns per page, free
from interference by a center column of reference hieroglyphics.

This is important for a couple of reasons.  First, because double-column Bibles are
smaller than single-column Bibles (think of all that white space in a
single-column setting of Psalms or Lamentations), the LPC can be a smaller
volume, which makes me happy.  Second,
and more importantly, the two text columns have a characters-per-line count
right around the sweet spot of 40 cpl.  I
read most easily from Bibles with about 40 cpl. 
I’ve found that lines that are much longer or shorter than that will
slow me down and take me out of the text. 
By contrast, the line length of the LPC allowed me to immerse myself in
the word.

Many of the other details in the LPC are right too.  My LPC is a TruTone Bible, and TruTone
bindings take a little while to break in, but the Bible’s inner margins are
generous enough that I never had any gutter issues.  Yes, the words of Christ in the LPC are in
red, but they are actually red, rather than orange or pink, and that’s plenty
good enough for me.  Indeed, it’s nice to
see a mass-market Bible get the color right there.

It’s also nice to see line-matching in a mass-market
Bible.  The Cambridge Clarion was the
first Bible that really thumped its chest about being line-matched, but since
the publication of the Clarion, most mass-market publishers have picked up the
trick too.  As a 2013 design, the LPC is
certainly recent enough to benefit from the line-matching revolution, and
benefit it does.

Finally, even though the LPC certainly does not deserve the
title of *Large*-Print Compact, its
print is still relatively large for the size of the text block.  8-point Lexicon No. 1 certainly qualifies as
standard print for a Bible, and it’s more than large enough for me to read
without noticing the font size.

**PRODUCTION
QUALITY.** Here, though, we come to my
one big gripe with the LPC as a reader, and indeed my one big gripe with the
LPC, period.  It’s the paper.  The paper isn’t just thin for paper; it’s
thin for the paper in Bibles.  It makes
the paper in most thinlines look thick.

I understand why Crossway did this.  As Bibles go, the LPC has a pretty high page
count, somewhere a little short of 1400 pp. with all the front matter
included.  1400 pages normally makes for
a fairly thick text block.  A Foundation
Large-Print Pew Bible, for instance, has about 1300 pages, and its text block
is more than an inch thick.  Clearly, the
Crosswegians didn’t want to try to sell a supposedly compact Bible with that
kind of girth, so they chose paper with a comparatively low GSM count.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEhHHVgI3E1erdksEP4RA1b5eboN1H-4YXW0kKXWP0zLOkbTNsJGI16-f8jAPgPlrcJR8r_6Q1nauBQtS_IchIXSge3DJE_HYBrq5fJPp9IwFfhxDP4GcztlEn7nQGtHEUI1SE0sjeR_2A/s640/R3.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEhHHVgI3E1erdksEP4RA1b5eboN1H-4YXW0kKXWP0zLOkbTNsJGI16-f8jAPgPlrcJR8r_6Q1nauBQtS_IchIXSge3DJE_HYBrq5fJPp9IwFfhxDP4GcztlEn7nQGtHEUI1SE0sjeR_2A/s1600/R3.jpg)



This caused two main problems.  The first is the predictable problem with
opacity.  The paper in Cambridge Pitt
Minions isn’t exactly renowned for its opacity, but to my eye, the paper in the
LPC is even less opaque than what my NASB Pitt offers.  As a result, there’s enough ghosting to populate
a horror movie.  Line-matching or not,
Bible buyers who don’t want to be able to read Revelation while the Bible’s
open to Genesis had better steer clear of the LPC.

Second, though, my copy of the LPC offers something I’ve
never noticed in a Bible before:  not
merely ghosting, but actual ink bleed-through. 
The paper is so thin that the printer’s ink has bled all the way through
and made little spots on the opposite side. 
Sometimes, a whole half-letter has soaked through.

Even by the rather low standards we apply to Bible paper,
this is a touch extreme.  After noticing
the problem in the LPC, I went back and examined several of the thinlines and
other thin-papered Bibles I own.  Some of
them have a touch of bleedthrough too, but I didn’t see any where the problem
was as obvious as it is in the LPC.  I
even used the Beth-Rhodes piece-of-black-paper-underneath trick, which will
hide ghosting that is only ghosting, and the ink dots were still plain.  When there’s text on the other side, the
line-matching hides the dots to a great extent, but they’re quite noticeable
when they’re opposed only by white space.

This didn’t interfere with my use of the Bible, but it
doesn’t look good, and I think the LPC setting deserves better.  It’s my favorite of all of Crossway’s small
Bibles; for instance, I like it better than the Crossway Thinline, which isn’t
line-matched and (to my eye, at least) is at least a little over-leaded.  I fully intend to hang on to the LPC as my physical
edition of the ESV, bleedthrough and all. 
Even as it is, the LPC is a great little Bible, but without that
bleedthrough, it would be a true gem.

In the future, I’d like to see Crossway trade some slimness
for better paper, even in the mass-market poly editions.  I also believe that the LPC setting is a
great candidate for the Heirloom treatment. 
If Crossway is willing to let this Bible be an inch and a quarter thick
instead of an inch thick, it would be a great competitor for the Pitt Minion in
a market environment that doesn’t offer much in the way of small premium-grade
Bibles.  As it is, the best way I can
describe the LPC is as a Bible I like well enough to use as a daily reader, but
not well enough to rebind.



**CONCLUSION:** So many things right; one thing very
wrong.  I’ll give the LPC a 7/10 as a
reader.  Overall, it achieves a
respectable score of 24/30, and it would probably have gotten a 28/30 with
decent paper.
