My greatest regret about becoming a preacher is that I no
longer have the opportunity to lead singing regularly (My second greatest
regret is that I have to grapple with the chimera that is the American
health-insurance system, but that’s a whole ‘nother story.).  Even now, though, issues of worship and
leading worship continue to preoccupy me.

I believe the subject is worth exploring because of the
confusion both within my religious tradition and outside of it about how a
worship leader does his work.  My friends
with high-church backgrounds have never seen a worship leader in action, so
they have trouble understanding how a man up front with a microphone can be
anything but a soloist.

I believe too that many members of churches of Christ don’t
recognize the difference between a song leader and a worship leader.  A song leader is the guy who gets up front,
calls out the number, and starts singing. 
He may or may not blow pitch and/or wave his hand in the air.  A worship leader is something far more
subtle, but the difference between worship led by a song leader and worship led
by a worship leader is profound.  Here
are three things that song leaders can work on outside of the assembly to
become worship leaders.



**Become mechanically
fluent.** A few weeks ago, I posted
about [the importance of mechanics in worship.](http://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2016/01/mechanics-and-worship.html) The man who wants to become a worship leader
will practice the mechanical aspects of his craft until they become second
nature.

There are lots of ways to do this.  If you aren’t comfortable giving the starting
pitch, practice your DO-MI-SOL arpeggios in the shower until you can find the
pitch without thinking about it.  Practice
beating time whenever you’re by yourself, until you feel the rhythmic pulse of
a hymn as easily as you comprehend the words. 
If you’re going to lead a less familiar hymn, sing it over and over
again until it flows from your lips as smoothly as “Jesus Loves Me”.  Mechanical proficiency is not the same thing
as worship-leading proficiency, but it does free you to focus on more important
things as you are leading.

**Understand the hymns
you lead.** Some hymns have a simple
meaning that is easily comprehended. 
Such hymns have a place in a worship service, provided that they are
presented as the appetizer instead of as the main course.

Great hymns, however, are possessed of subtlety and depth
that make them fitting subjects for meditation. 
In order to lead them correctly, you must understand them first, and in
order to understand them, you have to think about them.

This begins, of course, with the text of the hymn.  Craig Roberts recommends that Christians keep
a hymnal on their bedside tables, so that before they turn in for the night,
they can read a hymn or two and close their days by reflecting on spiritual
things.  This practice is particularly
useful for the worship leader.  It will
help him to make the strongest hymns in the repertoire his own.

Consider, for instance, the second verse of “Sun of My
Soul”.

*When the soft dews of
kindly sleep*

*My wearied eyelids
gently steep,*

*Be my last thought,
“How sweet to rest*

*“Forever on my
Savior’s breast!”*

This isn’t something that most 21st-century
Westerners (most 21st-century Christians?) would say, and even if
they did, this isn’t how they would express it. 
The NLT rendering of this verse would be something like, “When I go to
sleep this evening, may I joyfully anticipate the sleep of death, because death
will unite me forever with Jesus!”

Not exactly “your best life now”, is it?  However, I believe that “Sun of My Soul”
correctly represents the Biblical perspective on life and death.  This sentiment is a pearl of great
price.  Thinking on such things will
benefit every Christian, and understanding and internalizing them will help the
song leader lead “Sun of My Soul” in worshipful fashion.

**Understand the Music
You Lead.** Though it’s of lesser
importance, the worship leader will also understand the tunes of the hymns he
leads, as well as the way they interact with the text.  Every good hymn tune has a particular
emotional feel, and as the music runs its course, it will take the worshiper on
an emotional journey with peaks and valleys. 
Few good hymns maintain the same emotional intensity throughout.

The worship leader will seek to understand this emotional
journey before he interprets the hymn for the congregation.  For instance, HURSLEY, the usual tune for
“Sun of My Soul”, has the following dynamics, which tell the worship leader how
he should lead.  Here’s how I understand
the music and would lead it:

*When the soft dews of
kindly sleep* (Soft, gentle, slow. 
Rein the congregation in for a devotional feel, but don’t let them drag.)

*My wearied eyelids
gently steep,* (Not quite as soft and gentle here.  Loosen the reins on the congregation a little
bit; encourage them to sing in slightly fuller voice.  Let them sense the climax that is coming.)

*Be my last thought,
“How sweet to rest* (The tune is building to its climax here.  Start the phrase at the same level of
intensity as the end of the second phrase. 
Encourage the congregation to build in intensity until they’ve reached
perhaps three-quarters voice by the end of the phrase.  Help them anticipate the high point of the
hymn)

*Forever on. . .* (This
is the climax of the tune.  Lead the
congregation to reach full voice here, not shouting, but tuneful.)

*. . .my Savior’s
breast.* (Here, the tune
resolves.  Back the congregation down
from full voice for a musically and spiritually satisfying conclusion.)

This is not mere musical showmanship.  This is fulfilling the divine intent for
worship.  Few things operate more
powerfully on the heart than music, and emotional expression is inherent in the
command to sing.  A great hymn is a
precision instrument.  The better a
worship leader understands its nuances and helps the congregation understand
them, the more effective he will be.
