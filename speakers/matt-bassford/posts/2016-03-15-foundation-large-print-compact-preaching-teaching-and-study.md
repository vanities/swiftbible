**OVERVIEW.**  It doesn’t offer me margins filled with
glorious white space, but the LPC did everything else I want a work Bible to
do.  It’s small, handy, and possessed of
distinct verse numbers.

Using the LPC, I preached two sermons, both of which were at
least partially topical and involved lots of page-turning.  I used the Bible to prepare everything I
wrote in my office for two weeks.  I did
not, however, teach a Bible class with it. 
I’m still in preschool-class land, and the guy I’ve been studying with
recently has been sick.  Regardless, here
are my thoughts:



**PREACHING.** The LPC is a fine carry Bible.  That fact alone pretty well keeps it from
being one of the Bibles I most want to use in the pulpit.  By preference, I wield a wide-margin Bible
when I preach, and no grab-and-go edition of the Good Book is going to have
margins wide enough for me to put a sermon in.

Of course, this is only an issue when it comes to exegetical
sermons (which most of my sermons are). 
When I preach topical sermons, I don’t copy my notes into the
margins.  I use a sermon notebook.

For such sermons, I’ve started abandoning my Cambridge
Wide-Margin for my matching Cambridge Pitt Minion anyway.  We have a fairly small pulpit these days, so
when I have a larger Bible open at the same time as my sermon notebook on the
pulpit face, one or the other gets at least partially covered up.  It takes a Bible with a small wingspan, like
the Pitt, to fit neatly beside my notebook.

The LPC is almost, but not quite, as good at this as the
Pitt is.  Because it’s a thicker Bible
than a Pitt, its wingspan is slightly wider, so my sermon notebook overlaps
about a quarter-inch of page.  That’s not
as tidy as I like my pulpit to be, but I can still have both volumes open and
readable at the same time.

However, the LPC does have significantly larger print than
the Pitt does (9-point Weidemann versus 6.75-point Lexicon), which certainly
does no harm when I’m trying to read a passage. 
Also, because its pages are similarly sized to those of a Pitt, they’re
very easy to turn.  I can remember a time
or two in practice sessions, though, when I had trouble separating pages
because of the thin paper.

It is true that the LPC is a single-column paragraphed
Bible, and I’ve historically had trouble with verse citation in single-column
paragraphed Bibles.  However, I don’t
recall missing a citation in the LPC. 
This may be due to the unusual arrangement of the verse numbers.  Instead of being superscript, the verse
numbers are either set on the baseline (in the case of boldfaced numbers at the
beginning of paragraphs) or hovering in the middle of the line (for
non-boldfaced numbers).

In both cases, they are also considerably larger than quotation
marks.  This makes it difficult to
confuse the two.  In any event, I had no
trouble locating verses while preaching. 
In short, I couldn’t write in the LPC, but it allowed me to do
everything else I wanted.

**TEACHING.** As I said, I didn’t actually use the LPC
to do any of this, but I have a pretty good idea of what would have
happened.  The LPC doesn’t weigh any more
than a thinline, and especially when I’m teaching an auditorium class, I prefer
light Bibles rather than heavy ones.  The
LPC certainly qualifies here.

However, the LPC doesn’t have cross-references.  Indeed, the only marginalia in the LPC are
translator’s notes at the bottom. 
Generally, I don’t need cross-references when I have them, but I do need
them when I don’t have them.  It’s likely,
then, that the LPC’s lack of references would have disappointed me at some
time.

**STUDY.** Truth be told, I really liked the LPC
here.  It’s a great Bible to have sitting
off to the side.  You can easily pick it
up and flip to whatever passage you happen to be thinking about.  Likewise, it’s a great Bible for resting in
your lap while typing.  With my glasses
on, I can easily read the text even when it’s in my lap, and it doesn’t push me
farther away from the keyboard than I want to be.

If I missed a citation in anything I wrote using the LPC, I
haven’t found it yet.  It could be that
the layout is clear enough that I avoided errors, or it could be that I’m
getting more careful in my old age.

I don’t recall being bothered by the LPC’s lack of cross-references.  I think that on the rare occasions when I
wanted a reference, I simply ran a word search on blueletterbible.  Is it possible for me to be getting both more
careful and lazier at the same time?

**CONCLUSION.** No wide margins.  No cross-references.  However, the LPC did everything else that I
want a preaching Bible to do.  Sounds
like it merits about an 8.5/10.
