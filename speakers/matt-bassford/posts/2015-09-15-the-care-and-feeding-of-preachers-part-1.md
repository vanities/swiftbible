Like most of the preachers I know, I love what I do.  If a heretofore-unknown rich uncle died and
left me $50 million, I would keep on doing the same thing.  I count myself truly blessed to be able to do
exactly what I most want to do and support my family at the same time.

However, there’s a price to be paid for such a high level of
emotional involvement in our work.  For
most people, their job is just a job. 
They aren’t passionately invested in the well-being of their
employer.  They (hopefully) offer a solid
day’s work all day long, then punch out mentally as well as physically, come
home, and do what they want to do.



By contrast, I’m never really off the clock.  Pretty much my whole life revolves around
God, His word, and the brethren. 
Whenever I’m not thinking about something else, I’m thinking about one
of those three things.  I write hymns when
I’m showering.  Probably half the
conversations I have with my wife are church-related.  I regularly wake up in the middle of the
night worrying about or at least thinking about somebody I worship with.  Even when I’m on vacation, I generally look
for someplace to preach.  If preaching
were removed from my life, I wouldn’t have much of a life left.

I don’t think I’m unusual in this.  I think it’s true for most gospel preachers,
certainly for any who are worthy of the name. 
Not surprisingly, such a high-intensity lifestyle carries with it the
risk of burnout, particularly when things aren’t going smoothly.   Take, for instance, the words of the apostle
Paul in 2 Corinthians 11:28-29 (NLT): 
“Then, besides all this, I have the daily burden of my concern for all
the churches.  Who is weak without my
feeling that weakness?  Who is led
astray, and I do not burn with anger?”

The severity of this “daily burden” is made plain by the
context of 2 Corinthians 11.  This comes
at the end of Paul’s breathtaking catalogue of the trials he has endured for
the Lord.  In other words, Paul considers
the mental stress of being a preacher to be equal to the physical trauma of
being beaten, stoned, and shipwrecked! 
Modern-day disciples would do well to take his words to heart.

However, because the problems associated with stress are
gradual and subtle in their development, it’s easy for preachers to overlook
them until they become serious.  Indeed,
this blog post was prompted by a Facebook post from a preacher friend of mine
who was looking for help in his battle with burnout.

Many of the posts and articles that I’ve seen on this subject
are directed toward other Christians and urge them to be sympathetic to the
trials of the poor, poor preacher.  I
suppose there’s some value in that, but ultimately, we preachers have the
responsibility to care for ourselves rather than expect somebody else to do it
for us.  In this spirit, I offer the
following four recommendations to my pulpited brethren in the hopes that they
will prove useful:

**Balance.** For me, one of the most difficult
passages to understand in the Bible has always been Ecclesiastes 7:16.  In the NASB, it reads, “Do not be excessively
righteous and do not be overly wise.  Why
should you ruin yourself?”  This one gets
a funny look from me.  If I’m supposed to
imitate Christ, how is it possible to be excessively righteous and overly wise?

Perhaps the issue that Solomon is pointing to here is one of
balance.  Too much, even of a good thing,
is still too much.  Although I love just
about every aspect of preaching, even doing things that I love takes a
physical, mental, and emotional toll on me. 
If I don’t acknowledge that toll and make myself go easy, I’m likely to
get sick, or, worse still, get sick of what I’m doing.

To be perfectly honest, this is hard for me to admit.  In my head, I think I should be working 8 to
5 weekdays like everybody else at church, running Bible studies in the
evenings, prepping on Saturday, preaching on Sunday, and then inviting visitors
over for Sunday dinner!

That, I think, is exactly what being overly righteous looks
like.  I feel guilty when I don’t hit all
those marks (even though I’ve never gotten a negative comment from
anybody else about how hard I work), but that’s not a pace that even Jesus
could keep.  All through the gospels, we
find Him slipping off to pray (Mark 1:35), trying to dodge the crowds so he
could have some down time with His disciples (Mark 6:31), and even going on the
ancient equivalent of a vacation (Mark 7:24). 
If Jesus Himself couldn’t be all-gospel-all-the-time, who are we to even
make the attempt?

Of course, it’s also true that Jesus was a single man, and
I’m not.  Lauren is a super-extrovert who
loves being a preacher’s wife about as much as I love being a preacher, but
even devoted preachers’ wives need husbands who are willing to invest time and
effort in being husbands and fathers too.

We often joke that she is a “pew widow” because when I take
the pulpit every Sunday, I abandon her to the tender mercies of my two
rambunctious children.  I, and indeed all
married preachers, need to make sure that the joke doesn’t become a grim
reality.  In 1 Corinthians 9:5, Paul
argues that preachers have the right to the financial support they need to
provide for a wife.  It’s no less wrong
for a preacher to deprive his wife of the emotional and spiritual support she
needs as well.

All this means that it’s OK for me to take a half hour in
the morning to exercise.  It’s OK for me
to take a whole day in which I do no work to spend that day with my family (in
real life, this almost never happens). 
It’s even OK for me to take a whole week of vacation with my family and
do nothing useful with it (in real life, this literally never happens.  I need to practice what I preach!).

My martyr complex to
the contrary, none of these things constitute robbing God.   Instead,
they constitute a recognition that any earthly life must include elements of
balance.  As Solomon observes in
Ecclesiastes 3:1, “There is an appointed time for everything.”  We preachers need to appoint time to renew
ourselves too.
