New-Testament Christianity is not a religion for the
majority.  It begins with the premise
that Jesus was raised from the dead, which scientists tell us is
impossible.  It doesn’t cater to the lust
of the flesh, the lust of the eye, or the pride of life.  It demands that we change our lives in
dramatic ways.  In exchange for all this,
it offers us rewards that primarily emerge in the life to come.  As paths go, this is clearly not the one that
most people are going to pick!

This frustrates us today when we try to tell others about
the gospel.  We work and work, but nobody
seems to be interested.  Perhaps we even
long for the easy times of 2000 years ago.

In reality, though, New-Testament Christianity didn’t have
any more mass appeal in the time of the New Testament.  The story of the gospel is not one of a world
being won for Christ, but of a comparative few here and there who
believed.  First-century Christians
succeeded not because they had such an easy time of it, but because they
persevered through difficulty.  Nowhere
is this more evident than in the life of the apostle Paul.  Let’s look this morning at a section of
Scripture that describes His work among the Gentiles.



**Thessalonica and
Berea**

The first portion of our text, which comes from Acts 17 and
18, tells the story of Paul’s work in Thessalonica and Berea.  Let’s start with the first city first:  **THESSALONICA.** Acts 17:1-9 tells the tale here.  This is the story of a promising beginning
that goes rapidly downhill.  It begins
with Paul arriving in the city and preaching the risen Christ for three weeks.  During those three weeks, Paul makes a number
of converts:  some of his fellow Jews,
but a large number of God-fearing Greeks. 
Remember that here as elsewhere in Acts, the God-fearers are Gentiles
who believe in the God of Israel and worship in the synagogue, but aren’t
willing to become proselytes and adopt all the Jewish customs.  They’re some of the most enthusiastic hearers
of the gospel because it tells them that through Christ, they can become part
of God’s people without becoming Jewish.

However, even though Paul made some converts, he appears to
have made more enemies.  The majority of
the Jews don’t appreciate Paul preaching in their synagogue and teaching the
second-class Gentiles that they can become God’s people too.  As a result, they incite a riot, drag all the
Christian leaders they can find into court, and accuse them of rebelling
against Caesar, which, of course, was the last thing they were doing.  Even though they haven’t done anything wrong,
Jason and the other accused Christians have to post bond to be released, and
Paul has to flee by night.  As he leaves,
he is filled with concern about whether the Thessalonian church will survive at
all.

This points to an unpalatable truth about teaching the
lost.  Sometimes it’s going to be
disappointing.  Somebody will appear open
to the gospel, then all of a sudden not be. 
In a way, this can be even worse than if they weren’t ever interested in
Christ at all.  If we want to follow in
the footsteps of Jesus and Paul, we have to be prepared to face disappointment
too.  Only if we keep going despite
discouraging results will we eventually see the fruit they saw.

Paul’s flight from Thessalonica takes him to **BEREA.**Acts 17:10-15 explains what happens next.  Berea was and is a small city in the interior
of Greece about 50 miles from Thessalonica. 
Paul goes there, enters the synagogue, and begins to preach as usual.  However, the reaction he meets with here is
very different.  In Thessalonica, most of
the Jews had refused to listen.  Here,
many of them accept what he says and believe in Jesus.

We’ve all heard sermons on the noble-minded Bereans before,
but let’s pause for a moment to appreciate what this means in context.  Paul’s message can’t have been any more
palatable to the Jews in Berea than the Jews in Thessalonica.  I don’t think any Jews appreciated hearing
that through Jesus anybody could become part of God’s special people.  However, even though the gospel wasn’t
something that the Bereans easily and naturally heard, it was something that
they accepted, and even something they obeyed, because it was the truth.

Today, if we are truly noble-minded like the Bereans, we
will receive the truth in the same way. 
We will hear and obey God even when His word leads us in the opposite
direction from our inclinations.  Let’s
apply this to our subject of evangelism. 
In my experience, most Christians are not naturally inclined to
evangelism.  Many of us find it awkward
and frightening.  What do we do, then,
when we are confronted by passages that show that the New-Testament church is
supposed to be evangelistic?  Are we like
the Jews in Thessalonica, who brush off and dismiss the difficult message, or
are we like the Jews in Berea, who accept the truth and obey it?  It’s not our self-endorsement that reveals
our noble mind.  It’s our willingness to
do what’s hard.

Sadly, despite Paul’s success among the Jews and even the
Gentiles in Berea, his work there comes to an end when Jews from Thessalonica
show up and once again start stirring up riots. 
This really shows you how much they hated the gospel.  They were willing to walk 50 miles one way just
to cause trouble for Paul and his companions. 
Once again, Paul has to leave.

**Athens**

The next stories from Paul’s second missionary journey come
from his stay in Athens.  This begins
with his **PREACHING IN THE
MARKETPLACE.** Let’s read here from
Acts 17:16-21.  I see a couple of things
in this section that are worth noting. 
Let’s take them in reverse order. 
The second is that Athens is a very different place than Thessalonica or
Berea either one.  For that matter, it’s
a very different place than Paul has ever been before.  Unlike any of those other cities, Athens
prides itself on being an intellectual center. 
There are people who are so starved for intellectual stimulation that
they will listen to anybody who has something new to say.  There are philosophers wandering around in
the marketplace looking for somebody to argue with.  In its sophistication and intellectual
pretension, Athens is something of a cross between New York City and a college
town, and the Athenians are people who think Paul is a hick.

The first thing in this text that is worth noting, though,
is that Athens isn’t somewhere that Paul has even come intending to
preach.  All he’s doing is hanging out
and waiting for Silas and Timothy so that he can continue on his journey.  However, Athens has many idols in it, and
they bother Paul so much that he can’t leave them alone.  It’s obvious from his later words that he’s
been examining them closely, and even though arguing with Athenian philosophers
is probably about as profitable as arguing on the Internet, Paul is so provoked
that he argues with them anyway.

Paul’s example should remind us that our faith too shouldn’t
lie very far below the surface.  It would
have been easy for Paul to remain silent in Athens, just as it is often easy for
us to remain silent in our various situations. 
However, Paul had such a love for the Lord and His gospel that he
couldn’t remain silent.  So too, friends,
the people who know us shouldn’t have to talk to us for very long before we
start talking about Jesus because we believe He’s too important to stay silent
about.

Now that Paul has been invited to speak on the Areopagus,
which is the local philosophers’ hangout, he begins by proclaiming **AN UNKNOWN GOD.** This section of the Mars Hill sermon
includes Acts 17:22-28.  This is
completely unlike anything Paul has said to any of his Jewish and God-fearing
audiences.  However, in it, Paul uses an
interesting mix of familiar and unfamiliar ideas.  Some of the things he says are concepts that
every member of his audience would have known. 
All of them would have seen the altar dedicated to an unknown God, which
was a pagan way of covering all your bases. 
Many of them would have recognized Paul’s quotation of the Greek poet
Aratus of Tarsus, who originally said “We ourselves are his offspring,” about
Zeus.  If not, they would have been
familiar with similar concepts from the lips of other poets.

From this comfortable starting point, though, Paul moves on
to say things that would have made his audience uncomfortable.  He makes the unknown God known, and this
newly revealed God is so big that He doesn’t leave any room for anybody
else.  Instead of being a sky god like
Zeus, He is God of everywhere, everything, and everyone.  He’s so much greater than we are that, unlike
the gods of the Greeks, He needs nothing from us.  Instead, He is the one who gives.  He supplies us with all good things so that
we will learn from them to seek Him.

There’s a critical lesson here for all of us as we’re trying
to teach the lost.  We need to start with
common ground, but we can’t stay there. 
Particularly in our dealings with people who are religious as the
Athenians were religious, it’s awfully easy to avoid conflict and just stay on
the common ground forever.  I believe in
God, you believe in God, so we’re all really on the same team, right?  Well. . . that depends.  Is the God we both believe in the God of the
Bible, who has told us what to do and expects us to do it?  If that conception of God is correct, people
who obey the truth can’t be on the same team as people who don’t, and we can’t
pretend to them like we are, either.

Paul really parts company with his audience, though, when he
brings up **THE RESURRECTION.** Here, consider Acts 17:29-34.  This is where we start seeing the difference
between what we think preaching the gospel in the first century was like and
what it really was like.  From 2000 years
later, we think that everybody who lived back then was a superstitious fool, so
that Paul could talk about the resurrection of the dead and have that easily
accepted.

That’s about 180 degrees from the truth.  Acts 17 mentions two philosophical schools in
particular as being part of Paul’s audience, the Stoics and the
Epicureans.  The Stoics were pantheists
who didn’t believe in personal immortality. 
The Epicureans were materialists who didn’t believe in any kind of life
after death at all.  Neither one of them
believed in anything resembling the resurrection from the dead.  They knew just as well as we do that dead
bodies don’t get up and start wandering around again!

For Paul to preach that Jesus had been raised from the dead
to a bunch of Epicureans and Stoics, then, is about like us going into the
biology department of a major university and talking about creationism.  These are the best and brightest minds of the
first-century Roman Empire, and their whole worldview does not allow them to
accept what Paul is saying.  Paul, of
course, knows this going in, but he proclaims the resurrection anyway.  The wonder of this text is not that some
people sneer at Paul and others treat him like a diversion.  The wonder is that anybody listens at all.

Before the Lord’s Supper, then, let’s reflect on His
resurrection.  If the resurrection seems
like foolishness to many in our time, how much more did it seem like
foolishness 2000 years ago!  The gospel
was not an easy sell either to the Jews or to the Gentiles.  It succeeded for one reason and one reason
only:  the evidence for the resurrection
was so strong that people believed it.

Today, we can take comfort from the strength of that same
evidence.  If Jesus was raised from the
dead, then it follows that He did not die in vain, and also that if we believe
in Him, we will have life in His name. 
Let’s remember His death, burial, and resurrection as we partake.

**Corinth**

Paul’s final main stop on the second missionary journey was
in the city of Corinth.  This begins with
**EIGHTEEN MONTHS** of preaching.  We learn about it from Acts 18:1-11.  A couple of things stand out about this
text.  First, it looks like Paul has come
to the end of his resources.  Rather than
preaching all the time as he would prefer to do, he finds himself spending his
weeks making tents with Aquila and Priscilla. 
This state of affairs continues until Silas and Timothy arrive from
Macedonia.  Though Luke doesn’t report
it, we know from Philippians that they bring with them some support from the
church in Philippi, and that allows Paul to preach full-time again.

Second, Paul’s exchange with the Jews in Corinth is noteworthy
both for its similarities and its differences with his previous
experience.  As was the case in
Thessalonica, it seems like most of the Jews don’t believe him when he says
that Jesus is the Christ.  In addition to
having problems with the inclusiveness of the gospel, the Jews also struggled
with the idea of a crucified Messiah. 
They were expecting Jesus to drive out the Romans, not die on a
cross.  However, reading between the
lines, it looks like a substantial minority of Jews believe Paul, as do many
Gentiles.

At the point where Paul leaves the synagogue to go to the
Gentiles, if we’ve read this far in Acts, we think we have a pretty good idea
of what’s going to happen next.  The Jews
are going to get jealous, they’re going to raise a mob, and they’re going to
drive Paul out of the city and on to his next destination, maybe stoning him in
the meantime.  However, that’s not what
happens.  Instead, Paul is allowed an
unprecedented 18 months of peace in which to preach the gospel.  For those of us who have lived for years or
decades in the same place, let’s remember how good we have it!

In a dream, Jesus explains that Paul has been left in peace
because Jesus has many people in the city of Corinth.  This is an awfully strange thing to say about
a city that we can think of as the Las Vegas of the Mediterranean.  These were not moral people, yet Jesus says
they belong to Him!  Perhaps more than
any other passage of Scripture, this one should remind us not to judge hearts
by appearances.  We’re to be seed-sowers,
not soil analysts.

Finally, we come to a last bout of **LEGAL TROUBLE** that holds to form for Corinth in not following the
script.  Here, let’s read from Acts
18:12-22.  The first thing that this text
offers us is one of the best clues to chronology in the whole New
Testament.  Luke tells us that this whole
incident happened while Gallio was proconsul of Achaia.  We know from an archaeological inscription in
Delphi that Gallio was in office in 52 AD, so his encounter with Paul must have
happened in 51 or 52.  From this date, a
whole lot of other dates for New-Testament events and epistles can be
extrapolated.  Even if you’re not a
history geek like me, this is still extremely important.  It reminds us once again that what we have in
the Bible is a historical record.

However significant this may be to us, Gallio is significant
in the story because of the way he handles this complaint made by the Jews
against Paul.  In Thessalonica, the city
fathers believed the wild accusations of the mob, but Gallio isn’t so
credulous.  He listens to the complaint,
realizes that it has to do with religious rather than legal questions, and
dismisses it.  When the Jews take out
their anger at the failure of their plot by beating the synagogue leader who
presumably instigated it, Gallio ignores that too.  It’s strange to describe governmental
non-involvement as a happy result for early Christians, but that’s exactly what
it was, and it allows the gospel to flourish in peace.  Eventually, though, Paul moves on, stopping
briefly in Ephesus before returning to Antioch and concluding the second
missionary journey, emerging unscathed from many dangers.
