**OVERVIEW.** This Bible is extraordinarily small and portable
for a wide-margin Bible.  Generally, it
seems sturdily constructed despite its use of bonded leather in the cover.  I like the paper too.  The only fly in the ointment is that I would
like to see sturdier endpapers.

I acquired the NKJV Notetaking Bible the way that I get most
Bibles these days.  I asked for it, and
Holman sent me a copy.  As always, there
were no conditions placed on the review. 
Here’s what I think of its form and appearance:



**VITAL STATISTICS**

**Translation:**  New King James Version (1982)

**Cover:**  Bonded-Leather Hardcover

**Binding:**  Sewn

**Trim Size:**  6 ⅛”
x 8”

**Text Block Width:**  1 ⅜”

**Weight:**  2 pounds, 4 ounces

**Column:**  Single

**Layout:**  Paragraphed

**Print Size**:  8/8 Bible Slab

**Words of Christ:**  Red

**Cross-References:**  No

**Ribbon Markers:**  1

**SIZE.** Much to my surprise, I can glove-box this
one.  Admittedly, the glove box in
question belongs to my Toyota Corolla, which means that it doesn’t have
anything in it but the owners’ manuals. 
However, I was able to put the Bible in on top of the owners’ manuals
and close the compartment door without hitting Bible, though the cover did get
stuck on the lip of the plastic once.

This emphasizes, I think, the portability of this class of
Bibles.  Generally, wide-margin Bibles
are behemoths.  I primarily preach out of
a Cambridge NASB Wide-Margin Reference because it’s the only NASB wide-margin
Bible I like that weighs less than three pounds.  The Notetaking Bible has the Cambridge WM
form beat by half a pound.  It gets there
by using fairly small print and getting rid of cross-references, but if you
want a wide-margin Bible that travels well, you need to look at the Notetaking
Bible and the other, similar Bibles produced by Holman and Crossway.

**COVER.** I’ve spent the past two years complaining
about bonded-leather covers, but I don’t mind this one.  It makes all the difference that the Notetaking
Bible is hardcover rather than softcover. 
Softcover bonded-leather Bibles are vulnerable to flexing, crinkling,
and crumpling (and I think dog-eared Bible covers look awful), but the thick
bookboard inside the cover of the Notetaking Bible does a great job of protecting
the bonded leather from abuse.  In fact,
I probably would not have guessed that the cover was made out of bonded leather
if I had not known beforehand, and even now, its nature is only obvious when I
examine the spine.

The hardcover also serves another useful function.  This is a Bible for writing/drawing in, and
the sturdy cover means that you’ll always have a hard surface to support your
writing, even if the Bible is in your lap in church.  I think this is an important component of the
journaling-Bible package.

I think the appearance of the cover is reasonably
attractive.  The brown bonded leather
that predominates has an attractive pebbled grain, and I think the stitching
around the edge is equally attractive. 
The spine is elegant and uncluttered, bearing only translation, Bible
name, publisher’s logo, and four sets of double line bars.  It would look right at home on the shelves of
an old-money study.

My least favorite thing about the cover is the
endpapers.  In my experience, hardback
Bibles wear most at the corners and endpapers. 
The corners of the Notetaking Bible are reasonably sturdy, but the
endpapers are only thick paper.  It helps
that the Notetaking Bible is only about half the size of an old-time Foundation
Side-Column Reference, so the book block itself doesn’t put as much stress on
the endpapers, but over time I suspect those endpapers would shear and
separate.

In terms of lovability, there’s nothing here to compare with
edge-lined goatskin, but a journaling Bible is primarily a utilitarian
Bible.  It’s hard to print a hardcover
that’s more lovable than this one.

**TEXT BLOCK.** Thankfully, this bonded-leather Bible
doesn’t have the glued binding that often accompanies bonded-leather
Bibles.  The signatures are obvious when
one inspects the spine, and I didn’t have any trouble finding thread in the
gutter either.  The endpapers in this one
will fail long before the binding does.

The paper in the Bible is of the fairly thick, fairly opaque
variety that you’d expect in a wide-margin Bible.  It’s not quite as opaque as the 38-gsm
Tervakowski Thinopaque in my Cambridge WM, but it’s not far off.  I’d guess it’s probably about 36 gsm.  Also, it’s worth noting that although the
edges of the text block are rounded, they aren’t gilded.  This should amuse all of the Bible artists
who want to draw/paint on the edges of their Bibles too.

**VALUE.** This is a relatively new class of Bible,
and the retail values are pretty closely clustered.  The Notetaking Bible retails for $44.99, and
you can find a Crossway (ESV) Single-Column Journaling Bible in hardback with
an imitation-leather cover that retails for. . . $44.99.  Of course, prices online will be lower.  For those who are interested in Bible
journaling, or for those who want a Bible that’s good for taking notes on the
sermon, I’d say that’s a decent value for money.

There are lots of things to like here:  small size, sturdy cover, and paper that will
take ink about as well as the paper in any Bible will.  Only the endpapers are less than ideal.  However, the whole Bible deserves a 9/10 in
Physical Form.
