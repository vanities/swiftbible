The Internet is famous for its arguments, but sometimes, and
more rarely, it produces an unexpected agreement.  The other day, I found myself commiserating
online with a Unitarian Universalist pastor. 
She and I don’t agree on a whole lot doctrinally, but we’re both pretty
sure that we live in a world with too much spite, venom, and fear, and not
enough love and grace.  When people with
two such different perspectives agree on a conclusion, there’s probably
something there.

So too it is with this article [this article](https://christianrenaissancemovement.com/2016/07/30/sing-to-the-lord-a-new-song-in-an-old-way/).  The author is a Catholic deacon, and his
interest is with a particular aspect of 2000 years of Church tradition.  My corner of the religious world, by
contrast, is militantly opposed to “the traditions of men.”  As far as we’re concerned, the only thing
that comes after Revelation 22 is the concordance and the map section.



However, because the author writes about a-cappella worship,
what he quotes from his tradition resonates with me deeply.  Like the earliest Christians, my worship
today is a-cappella.  “Ain’t no
Christians using instruments in the New Testament,” we reason, so we don’t use
them.

To most who haven’t experienced it, this sounds like the
death knell for any hope of beauty in a worship service.  A bunch of untrained singers led by an
untrained song leader?  That’s going to
sound like a heap of tomcats dumped in a mosh pit!

In practice, though, that’s not the way it works.  The singing itself has, I think, an objective
beauty of its own, but to those who participate in it, the experience is one of
nearly inexpressible joy.  I’ve heard new
converts grope for words to explain why they enjoy it so much (even though they
probably can’t carry a tune in a bucket), but even though I’ve been doing this
for much longer, I can’t explain it any better myself.  There’s nothing like praising God with only
the ordinary church folks singing along with you.  It’s so important to me that I literally
don’t want to think about what my life would be like without it.

1800 years ago, the early church was also a-cappella.  Their big concern was not imitating the
worship of pagan temples, all of which employed instruments in their
worship.  Pretty austere too, right?

That’s not how it comes across in the writings of the church
fathers.  Instead, I see in their writing
the same inexpressible joy in worship that I feel.  The author does a great job of capturing this
with his pull quotes.  Consider this
quotation from Augustine:

*For he that sings praise, not only
praises, but also praises with gladness; he that sings praise, not only sings,
but also loves him of whom he sings. In praise, there is the speaking forth of
one confessing; in singing, the affection of one loving.*

And this from Clement of Alexandria:

*The Word of God, scorning the lyre and
cithara as lifeless instruments, and having rendered harmonious by the Holy
Spirit both this cosmos and even man the microcosm, made up of body and soul –
he sings to God on his many-voiced instrument and he sings to man, himself an
instrument: “You are my cithara, my aulos and my temple,” a cithara because of
harmony, and aulos because of spirit, and a temple because of the word, so that
the first might strum, the second might breathe, and the third might encompass
the Lord… The Lord made man a beautiful breathing instrument after his own
image; certainly he is himself an all harmonious instrument of God, well-tuned
and holy, the transcendental wisdom, the heavenly Word.*

I’m not even sure what-all that means, but I can’t read it
without tears coming to my eyes because it so perfectly captures the experience
of a-cappella worship—not using an instrument, but being an instrument, together
with a whole congregation of beautiful breathing instruments that resonate with
the song of the Lord.

Yes.  From 1800 years
apart, Clement and I arrive at the same place. 
That’s still exactly what it feels like, this luminous, shimmering experience
of unaccompanied song worship.  I don’t
think he would have traded anything for it either.
