**OVERVIEW.** This is a pretty hefty hand-sized Bible,
larger than I normally prefer.  The
double-column paragraphed layout with inline verse numbers is a little clunky,
but it’s functional.  There are abundant
cross-references, but they don’t seem particularly useful.  However, I fell in love with the translation.



\*             \*             `\*

As is somewhat frequently the case, the ASV got more of a
workout than normal.  Due to my
appointment-preaching schedule, it appeared in the pulpit for four sermons
instead of two.  Interestingly enough,
three of the four appearances involved the same sermon—on Psalm 103—but my
opportunity to try the Bible out in the pulpit was nonetheless ample.  I also used it to continue teaching a
young-adult class on Revelation and to prepare for all my various doings for
three weeks.

**PREACHING.** The Teacher’s Edition is pretty squarely
in just-a-Bible territory.  It will hide
a half-sheet of paper, but it isn’t much larger than that.  However, it is a Bible of some heft.  Part of this is due to the ferocious 234-page
concordance in the back (which, for instance, contains about two pages of
small-print entries on “God”).  Part of
it, though, is due to the more opaque paper used throughout.  The Old and New Testaments together are only
1066 pages long, which is certainly thinline territory, but together, they’re
about an inch and a quarter thick—definitely not thinline territory.  I suppose the point is that over the past 50
years, Bibles, like supermodels, have gotten skinnier.

Regardless, the result of it all is that the Bible has a satisfyingly
weighty hand feel, which is only accentuated by the feel of the spine ribs from
the Leonard’s rebind.  It’s a pleasant
Bible to hold.

The format of the Bible is weird, but it works fine for
preaching.  It’s a paragraphed Bible,
though this often doesn’t count for much. 
The translators of the ASV generally regarded paragraph breaks about the
way my three-year-old son regards baths:  to be avoided as much as possible.  In any event, the result is about a mile away
from verse-by-verse.  However, unlike
most modern layouts, rather than using superscript verse numbers, the Nelson
uses inline verse numbers in the same typeface as the body text.  I guess that superscript verse numbers hadn’t
occurred to anybody back in 1901 when this Bible was originally set.  Of course, strange though they appear to the
modern eye, the inline numbers actually make a given verse easier, not harder,
to locate.  They certainly weren’t a
problem in the pulpit.

There is one other point worth mentioning here, though, and
it’s a translation-related point.  After
my third sermon with the ASV, I found myself “cleaning up” the text as I read,
replacing archaic words, pronouns, verb forms, etc., with modern
equivalents.  I’m very comfortable with
public reading, so I was able to do this consistently though a sermon with only
one stumble (a “ye” slipped out), but those who might not be as glib will
probably be stuck with the ASV in all its Elizabethan glory.

**TEACHING.** As I observed above, the Teacher’s
Edition is a fairly portly Bible.  I
prefer Bibles that are two pounds or less; this one is slightly more.  It’s not terrible to hold for long periods of
time, but it’s not a fairy wand or a flyswatter either.  However, I’ve been teaching my Revelation
class sitting down rather than standing up, so the weight hasn’t had a chance
to bother me.  Besides, I enjoy holding
this Bible more than most.

However, I do have some issues with layout and format
here.  In principle, I like the
Say-No-To-Paragraphs approach to the ASV. 
I even like the typeface used in this Bible, whatever it is.  For some reason, though, the combination of
limited paragraphing, typeface, and possibly even inline verse numbers made it
difficult for me to locate passages.  I’d
be scanning a page and simply not recognize the verse I meant to find.

Likewise, the cross-referencing system proved not to be my
favorite.  I have no idea how many
hundreds of thousands of references it contains, but many (most?) of them refer
back to passages in the same book.  The
connections they make to other books aren’t as thorough as I would like.  When prepping for my Revelation class, I
found myself using the references I’d written in the margins of another text
Bible, because the references in the ASV wouldn’t reliably give me what I
wanted.  The cross-reference suites of
both the NASB and the ESV are much stronger.

**STUDY.** The Teacher’s Edition ASV was at its
strongest in my office.  This starts with
translation.  I normally try to avoid
translation issues, and I’m certainly not going to opine that a given
translation is “the best”, but the ASV certainly has its place.  I don’t know Greek (and may never trouble to
learn it), and of all the English translations available, the ASV gives me the
best picture of what the Greek looks like. 
Its translators had little concern for anything but word-for-word
faithfulness to the Greek texts they were using, so it is probably the most
strictly formal-equivalent translation ever made in English.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgETpVf2DOtN5bLvvYZV6l6E8VmcFt6fKxGJ_ypxqf8d_A1t7f3OCQOHuft0gg3goVYUY75cXehz-moVSLdVRjxXefY0ySBbwluDb7ECyOdCLZHQdSRxfa_LB2FpES0zoeJuuyRkpagcw/s640/PTS+copyright+date.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgETpVf2DOtN5bLvvYZV6l6E8VmcFt6fKxGJ_ypxqf8d_A1t7f3OCQOHuft0gg3goVYUY75cXehz-moVSLdVRjxXefY0ySBbwluDb7ECyOdCLZHQdSRxfa_LB2FpES0zoeJuuyRkpagcw/s1600/PTS+copyright+date.jpg)



For instance, due to its use of archaic pronouns (“thou” and
“thee” for second-person singular; “ye” and “you” for second-person plural),
the ASV does a better job of capturing the differences between second-person
singular and second-person plural in Greek than any modern translation can
do.  Likewise, the translators tried to
adhere to a policy of using the same English word to translate the same Greek
word whenever possible, so that, for instance, both Philippians 1:5 and 4:14
use “fellowship” for the Greek *koinonia*,
where the NASB (hardly a loose translation) uses “participation” and
“share”  respectively.

Now, even the ASV translators freely acknowledged that it
doesn’t have much to offer in the way of readability or beauty in English, but
it does an excellent job of capturing the nuances of the original.  It probably deserves a place in every
preacher’s library, as a reference volume if nothing more.

My particular volume of the ASV served me well in the time I
used it for study.  The paper is opaque,
the size is fine, and the text is readable. 
I still didn’t love the cross-references, but that’s a much easier
deficiency to remedy in private study than while teaching a class.  In study, I actually liked the
paragraphing.  In many instances, the
more frequent paragraphing of modern translations, to say nothing of section
headings (my personal bête noire) interferes with the flow of the text.  The ASV’s layout lets me see the connections.



**CONCLUSION.** I believe that this particular setting
was first used in 1901, so this is a venerable design for a Bible.  In many ways, it feels like a venerable
design and is not as easy to use as a more modern edition might be.  Bible publishers have learned a thing or two
in the past century-plus.  However, I
highly recommend the ASV as a study help for anybody who isn’t KJVO.  For that use alone, the Bible merits an 8/10.
