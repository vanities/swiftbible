**OVERVIEW.** A dedicated wide-margin Bible probably
isn’t going to be a great reading Bible. 
The same is true here.  The
Notetaking Bible isn’t the worst reader I’ve ever used, but it’s far from the
best.

As per usual, I did two weeks’ worth of daily Bible readings
out of the Notetaking Bible.  Here are my
impressions:



**SIZE.** Honestly, my impressions here surprise me
a little bit.  Even though the Notetaking
Bible isn’t particularly large, I found myself noticing its size and weight
much more than I was expecting.  Perhaps
this is due to its hardcover construction. 
I don’t do my readings with the Bible resting on a desk or table.  Instead, I hold the Bible I’m using up like a
book.

Softcover Bibles are better at this.  They conform to your hand instead of fighting
your grip.  The Notetaking Bible is just
big enough to become unwieldy during an extended reading.  Also, the print is small enough that I wasn’t
happy propping the Bible on my chest, as I’ve done with other hardcovers I’ve
reviewed (normally, unwieldy means larger print means I can read it from
farther away).  As a result, I find the
Bible to be in an unhappy middle ground in terms of size.

**LAYOUT.** The Notetaking Bible is a single-column
Bible without a reference column. 
Normally this is a sign of trouble for the characters-per-line count,
and so it is here.  At least according to
my count, the Notetaking Bible has something like 80 CPL.  Normally, I like my single-column Bibles to
have no more than 60 CPL.  There are
exceptions, such as the Tyndale Select/Schuyler Caxton, but that text block is
redeemed by its extraordinarily generous leading.

The Notetaking Bible, by contrast, doesn’t have much in the
way of leading at all, and its extremely high CPL count makes it hard to
read.  I frequently have trouble figuring
out what line I’m supposed to be on, and I have trouble making sense of the
text.  This is simply not a reading-optimized
layout.

On the plus side, the Notetaking Bible has spacious inner
margins, at least half an inch and possibly more.  Not even in the middle of the Bible are there
issues with the text sliding into the gutter. 
It’s nice to not have to manipulate the Bible to peer at those last and
first few words!

Contrary to my usual preferences, the Notetaking Bible is of
the red-letter variety.  Giving credit
where credit is due, the ink the printer used isn’t a problem.  It’s a dark red, almost a brown, rather than
an orange or a pink.  It’s slightly
harder to read than the black ink (the ivory-colored paper doesn’t offer as
sharp a contrast), but only slightly. 
However, an examination of the gospels reveals fairly frequent, albeit
minor, problems with registration, both up-and-down and side-to-side.  This isn’t a big deal, but none of these
problems would even exist if Holman had simply made the bible black-letter.

However, Holman (or more accurately 2K) showed
thoughtfulness in design in making the Bible line-matched.  Because the paper is fairly opaque already,
line-matching isn’t as important, but it still helps in making the text more
readable, and this is a Bible that needs the help.

According to my normal preferences, the font in the
Notetaking Bible should be large enough. Bible Slab is a readable typeface, and
8-point is standard for smaller Bibles. 
However, I was never completely comfortable with the text size.

I think the problem is line length.  A long line demands larger print.  I informally compared the readability of the
print in the Notetaking Bible to the print in my Cambridge NASB Pitt
Minion.  Even though the latter only has
6.75 Lexicon No. 1, I found it to be more readable because the shorter lines
were more accessible.

**PRODUCTION
QUALITY.** The Notetaking Bible is a
little bit outside the norm for a mass-market Bible.  Rather than being printed in China, it hails
from Korea.  I can’t recall having
reviewed a Korea-print Bible before.

The Notetaking Bible brings no discredit on its country of
origin.  Bible Slab isn’t a very subtle
typeface, but so far as I can tell, the printing is still executed well.  To me, it appears to be even and dark
throughout.  There are no splotches,
blobbed letters, or anything like that.

The paper is respectable too.  As I think I said earlier, I’d guesstimate it
at about 36 gsm, not quite up to Cambridge Wide-Margin Reference standard, but
far from terrible.  My only issue with
the text block is that some of the page edges opposite the spine are bubbled a
little bit, which affects the looks of the Bible slightly and its usefulness
not at all.  This isn’t a premium Bible,
so I’m inclined to be lenient about such things.

**CONCLUSION.** I’ve said repeatedly that Bible design is
a series of compromises, but the Notetaking Bible doesn’t do much
compromising.  Its aim is to provide a
single-column wide-margin Bible in as small a package as possible.  In doing that, though, its designers had to
sacrifice large print and line length.

Honestly, this doesn’t bother me much.  If I were to adopt the Notetaking Bible, I’d
be adopting it for one reason—to preach out of it.  If I’m preaching out of it, I’m writing in
it, and if I’m writing in it, I’m not using it for daily readings.  I’m fine with a Bible that sells out on
everything else to be a handy, useful wide-margin edition.

However, there are likely people out there who want to read
out of the same Bible they write in.  For
people like that, the Notetaking Bible may well pose some problems.  It’s simply not a very good reader.  I think it deserves a 6.5/10 score here,
which gives it an overall score of 24.5/30. 
For a Bible that’s meant to be a niche Bible and not an all-rounder,
that’s pretty good.
