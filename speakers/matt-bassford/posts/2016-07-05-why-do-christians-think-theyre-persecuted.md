These days, I and many like me are growing increasingly
frustrated with the double standard advanced by many liberal, secular
elites.  They proudly proclaim how
tolerant they are, yet they feel free to behave intolerantly toward religious
conservatives, insisting that the bigoted and hateful aren’t worthy of
tolerance.  Personally, I’m a little
skeptical of pretensions to tolerance that come with an asterisk attached.

So too it is with sympathy toward oppressed minorities.  Most folks to the left of the spectrum will
champion the rights of racial and sexual minorities, but when it comes to
religious minorities, they will often deny that a problem even exists.



Consider [this opinion piece](http://www.msn.com/en-us/news/opinion/most-american-christians-believe-they%E2%80%99re-being-persecuted/ar-AAhOG5n),
in which the author marvels that most American Christians are convinced that
they suffer persecution.  She explores a
number of alternative theories for this conviction (Muslims, immigration,
demographic trends) while dismissing the most obvious explanation—that there
actually are ways that American Christians are persecuted, and they know
it.

I can’t help but be reminded of various right-wing attempts
to deny the reality of American racism. 
The logic is similar:  there
haven’t been any headlines about it, I personally haven’t seen any of it, so it
must not be real.  Black people/religious
people who insist that it happens to them must be overly sensitive.  In both cases, I think these smug dismissals only
provide an excuse for continued bad behavior.

Let’s start with the obvious claim:  the United States is still a
majority-Christian nation, so clearly Christians can’t suffer persecution.  That’s about like saying that all African
Americans are people, and people make up 100 percent of U.S. citizens, so
clearly African Americans can’t be oppressed. 
We treat people so well here!  In
both cases, the demographic category is too broadly drawn to be useful.

These days, it’s entirely possible for two people with
dramatically different religious beliefs to shelter under the name
“Christian”.  There are people who
describe themselves as Christians who don’t believe that Jesus is truly the Son
of God.  Lumping me in with the likes of Episcopal
bishop John Spong (who isn’t even a theist) and creating some vague majority
out of all of us proves nothing.

Instead, if we want to truly define the problem, we need to
consider only those Christians whose conservative moral beliefs set them at
odds with the culture and so could expose them to persecution.  Start with the 25 percent of the population
that (according to [this study](http://www.pewforum.org/2015/05/12/americas-changing-religious-landscape/)) is evangelical Protestant, toss in the conservative half of American
Catholics for another 10 percent, and you’ve got a religiously conservative
Christian minority of 35 percent—significant certainly, but also not beyond the
reach of persecutors.

This is particularly true in places where the actual
percentage of conservative Christians is considerably smaller than average.  Nobody much messes with evangelicals in rural
Alabama, but in liberal enclaves on either coast and in college campuses across
the country, the reality may well be quite different.

In fact, I have reason to believe that it is different.  Consider the following hypothetical:  a young African-American woman takes a
college class on the Civil War at a Southern university.  The professor has a particular interest in
the exploits of Nathan Bedford Forrest. 
During one class period, he begins to show his students an admiring
documentary about Forrest entitled “The Wizard of the Saddle”.

Our hypothetical student knows her history, and she knows
that after the Civil War, Forrest founded the Ku Klux Klan, which caused untold
suffering for her people.  She is deeply
offended at this celebration of a man she considers a monster, gets up, and
leaves the classroom rather than continuing to watch the video.

At the beginning of the next class session, she insists that
the issue be discussed.  However, the
professor has no sympathy for her position. 
It’s his job to teach his students history, he says, and he has no place
in his classroom for people who don’t appreciate the nobility of the Lost
Cause.  If she has further concerns about
this, she can take them up with one of her own people.  She gets up to leave, again, he bellows at
her to remain, but she walks out anyway.

She brings her concerns to her faculty advisor, who advises
her that as much as he personally might be sympathetic, any complaint she files
will go nowhere.  The dean and the
provost are old friends of the professor. 
All of them are members of the same group of Civil-War reenactors.  If she doesn’t want to get thrown out of the
class, she’d better swallow her pride and her opinions, apologize, and be a
good little girl for the rest of the semester.

Outrageous, right? 
This is headline news!  There will
be protestors and TV cameras 24/7.  Heads
will roll throughout the history department over this one.

Now, consider the following non-hypothetical.  My wife takes a social-work class (one
required for graduation) at a large public university.  Her professor is gay and an extremely vocal
advocate of the gay-rights movement. 
During one class, he begins to show a graphic, explicit documentary
about the experience of being a gay man.

My wife believes the
practice of homosexuality is a sin, and she has deep moral objections to watching
a documentary that’s just this side of pornography.  She gets up and leaves the classroom.

At the beginning of the next class session, she insists that
the issue be discussed.  The professor
isn’t interested.  He tells her that it’s
his job to broaden her views of sexuality, and he doesn’t have a place in his
classroom for the narrow-minded.  If she
has further questions, she can take them up with her pastor.  She gets up to leave, again, he bellows at
her to remain, but she walks out anyway.

She takes her problems to her faculty advisor, who advises
her that as much as she personally might be sympathetic, any complaint she
files will go nowhere.  The dean is a
lesbian who is entirely in sympathy with the professor and goes to the church
where he preaches (that Christian diversity thing again).  If she doesn’t want to get thrown out of the
class, she’d better swallow her pride and her opinions, apologize, and be a
good little girl for the rest of the semester. 
There are no TV cameras, no outraged social-justice warriors, so my wife
does exactly that.

Persecution doesn’t have to involve buffets for the lions at
the Colosseum.  The persecution that
Jesus contemplates in Matthew 5:10-11 appears to be largely verbal, the abuse a
hostile society directs toward those with whom it disagrees.  I don’t have to go out from under my roof to
find people who have suffered from that. 
Do Christians have reason to fear being persecuted?  Increasing reason, even?  You tell me.
