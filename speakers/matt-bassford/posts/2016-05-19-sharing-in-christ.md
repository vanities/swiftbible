Of course, our most sacred observance doesn’t involve
fasting.  It involves eating.  Before we partake of the Lord’s Supper this
morning, though, let’s consider what Paul has to say about sharing in
Christ.  We often think of 1 Corinthians
11 as a chapter that has a lot to say about the Lord’s Supper, and it is, but
so too does 1 Corinthians 10.  Let’s look
there this morning.



The first section of 1 Corinthians 10 concerns **THE ISRAELITES.** Read with me here from 1 Corinthians
10:1-5.  Paul here is at pains to
emphasize the similarity between the children of Israel during the Exodus and
Christians in our time.  In passing
through the Red Sea, they were “baptized” like we are.  They shared in a spiritual meal of manna and
water from the rock; we celebrate the Lord’s Supper with bread and fruit of the
vine.

However, despite these similarities, there is one thing that
Paul points to that we hope is a significant difference.  Despite sharing in these blessings from God,
the Israelite nation cratered spiritually. 
They had the right rituals but the wrong heart, and all but two of them
died for it.

Today, every one of us needs to be on our guard against
exactly the same problem.  It’s
important, indeed necessary, that we carry out these holy activities.  We need to be baptized.  We need to share in the Lord’s Supper on the
first day of the week.

However, those things, though necessary, are not
enough.  We can’t buy God off with
crackers and grape juice once a week.  He
wants our lives too.  If we rebel against
Him instead, we too will pay the penalty of death in the wilderness, a death
not of the body, but of the soul.

Paul tells us, then, that we need to **LEARN FROM THE ISRAELITES.** Let’s
keep reading in 1 Corinthians 10:6-13. 
As I read this text, I can’t help but be impressed by the ingenuity of
Satan.  He knew that one sin wouldn’t fit
all, so he used different sins to get almost everybody.  Some Israelites were vulnerable to
idolatry.  He used that.  Others were susceptible to sexual sin.  He used that. 
Still others were arrogant and contentious.  He used that too.

Paul’s point is twofold. 
He tells us first that this grim story, which looks like a triumph for
Satan and a defeat for God, was recorded for us so that we could learn from the
Israelites’ example.  They were unwary
and were destroyed.  Today, if we’re
puffed up about our spiritual accomplishments and think we can’t get got,
that’s exactly what the devil needs to get us.

On the other hand, though Satan is more powerful than we
are, God is more powerful than he is, and ultimately, God is the one who is in
control.  Maybe people out in the world
are fair game, but God won’t allow the devil to overwhelm us.  It is possible for us to resist the
devil.  It is possible for us to offer
God not only the five acts of worship, but our faithful lives.  Where the Israelites failed, we can
succeed.  We have to be wary, we have to
be wise, but we can do it.

Finally, Paul warns against **IDOLATRY**, using the Lord’s Supper to advance his point.  Look at 1 Corinthians 10:14-22.  First of all, let’s remember that everything
in 1 Corinthians 10 appears in the larger context of meat sacrificed to
idols.  Paul here is distinguishing
between unwittingly eating meat offered to an idol and then sold in the
marketplace on the one hand, and eating that same meat as part of an idolatrous
festival on the other hand.  He begins by
pointing out that the Lord’s Supper is a sharing.  When we eat the bread and drink the cup
together, that observance joins us into one and joins us together with
God.  Paul says, though, that participating
in an idolatrous ritual has the same effect. 
It made Christians who did into sharers with demons.

Today before the Lord’s Supper, then, let’s reflect on just
what it is that we are sharing in.  As
Christians, we share in the sacrifice of Christ.  We share in the grace that flowed from His
wounds on Calvary.  We come together with
His church, which is the body of Christ.

However, we must also be wary.  Dining in an idol’s temple is not the only
way to become a sharer with idolaters. 
Colossians 3 explicitly describes covetousness as idolatry, but anything
that we elevate above God becomes an idol. 
It could be the lusts of our flesh. 
It could be our pride in ourselves or our accomplishments.  It could even be things that are good of
themselves, like our jobs or our families. 
When we put any of those things before God, we become sharers, not with
faithful Christians, but with all the people of the world who do the same.  Let’s consider ourselves, then, and whether
our hearts are directed to Christ and His sacrifice, or to an idol with no
power to save.  Let’s think about these
things as we partake.
