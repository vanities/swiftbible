Some stories make your soul shrivel.  Here is one. 
A friend of mine works as a labor-and-delivery nurse.  One day, she was directed to attend the birth
of a young single mother.  The child was
stillborn.

Afterward, no one came to visit:  not parents, not other relatives, not
friends, and certainly not the child’s father. 
No one.  “Alone” looks like an
absolute, but I think it’s relative, and it’s hard to imagine someone more
alone than that.



If you are a man whose wife has just lost a child, your
foremost goal must be to make sure that she isn’t alone.  This is a matter of metaphor, not of literal
truth.  It is possible for you to share a
bed with her, eat with her, ride in the car with her, and attend the funeral
with her, yet still be leaving her alone. 
Physical proximity is not the same thing as mental and emotional
intimacy.

The problem is that in the aftermath of loss, mental and
emotional intimacy are *hard*.  I don’t know whether it’s true elsewhere, but
it is certainly true in our society that grief turns inward.  Grief is self-absorbed.  When you yourself want nothing more than that
inward turning, reaching out to somebody else is hard.

It’s doubly hard when the somebody to whom you are reaching
out only wants to turn inward herself. 
You hear about couples who cling to each other in the face of tragedy
and assert that it made their marriage Stronger Than Ever.  That may be, but it wasn’t how things went
for us.  Losing Macy had a corrosive
effect on our marriage.  It eroded things
that I thought were solid, like turning a high-pressure nozzle on a lump of
clay.  Losing Macy hurt.  Trying to fill the marital breaches hurt too.

Lots of men never manage it. 
A good friend of mine also had a stillborn child.  His wife blamed him for it.  He started drinking.  His marriage went smash, followed shortly
afterward by everything else in his life. 
I don’t think he started living again until decades later, a year or two
before his death.

It’s hard.  It’s like
trying to carry a piece of furniture that’s too heavy for you to lift.  But it’s still your job to be the one
reaching out.

This is implied first of all by the structure of what the
Bible says about marriage.  Ephesians 5
compares Christ to the husband and the church to the wife.  As my brother and friend Andy Diestelkamp
likes to point out, it is not the church that reconciled Christ to
herself.  It is Christ who reconciled the
church to Himself.  Reconciliation, then,
is the husband’s responsibility.

Second, you have to do it because you’re the one who can do
it.  This doesn’t seem fair.  It’s like asking a seriously wounded soldier
to drag his critically wounded comrade to safety.  I can barely walk, and you want me to help
someone who can’t walk at all?  Well. . .
do you really want somebody else doing it? 
Do you really want your wife forming a strong emotional bond with
somebody else in a time of intense distress? 
I’ve heard that isn’t so good for marriages either.

In short, reach out. 
Keep reaching out.  Don’t believe
the, “I’m fine.”  It’s almost certainly
not true.  Reach out until you know
what’s going on in your wife’s head as well as she does.  It may literally be a matter of life and
death.

Let me explain.  About
two weeks after Macy died, I decided it was time for me to go back to
work.  Like most men, I process better
when I’m doing than when I’m doing nothing, and I didn’t think I was
accomplishing anything at home.

While I was off at the church building, my wife was at home,
lying in bed, contemplating suicide.  There
are three steps to suicide:  thinking
about suicide, talking about suicide, and planning suicide.  Lauren skipped Step Two—indeed, she never
told me or anybody else until years later—but she had Steps One and Three
down.  Because Macy had been a C-section
baby, Lauren had come home from the hospital with plenty of painkillers, and
she figured that if she took all the pills in the bottle at once, that would
probably kill her.  The only thing that
held her back was the sense that if she thought it would be wrong for her to kill
herself, it probably would be wrong, with undesirable consequences on the Day
of Judgment.  We’re also inclined to give
credit to the grace of God.

For those of you who don’t know my wife, this is not some
flighty, unstable diva type we’re talking about here.  Coming into Macy’s death, she had all the
mental-health boxes checked: 
white-picket-fence upbringing, strong faith, strong marriage, and no
history of mental illness.  After the
stillbirth, her doctor promptly put her on antidepressants (that never seemed
to me to do a lick of good, but that’s another story).

However, the cocktail of post-partum depression and grief
was enough to throw her for a loop.  I
remember her talking during the pregnancy about how proud she was that her body
was able to produce new life, and now (she thought) she had spectacularly
failed.  In retrospect, it’s clear that I
should have paid much better attention than I did.

Thankfully, the experience didn’t destroy her or destroy our
marriage.  We survived, we endured, and
eventually we flourished again.  While I
am glad for that, I wish I had been wise enough to do more to avert disaster.  It’s wonderful when God keeps your life from
going over the edge of a cliff; it’s better still when you have sense enough to
avoid the cliff in the first place, and I didn’t know enough or understand
enough to do that.

My story, I think, is another lesson on the dangers of
silence.  When men don’t talk about the
experience of child loss, we deny other men the opportunity to learn from our
lives.  We leave them without the wise
counsel that may mean the difference between healing and disaster.  I don’t think there’s anything unique about
what Lauren and I went through.  To the
contrary; I suspect that marital problems and mental-health issues in the
aftermath of child loss are as common as dirt. 
However, because this isn’t something that’s on the list of acceptable conversation
topics, we leave other men to face those things alone.

And alone is a
terrible thing to be.
