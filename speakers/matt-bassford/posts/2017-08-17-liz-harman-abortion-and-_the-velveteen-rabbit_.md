Yesterday, my brother Andy Diestelkamp posted [a video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=r5SQnQjryzI&feature=share) that purports to be a philosophical defense of abortion (Andy, of course, does
not agree with said video).  The defender
in question is Liz Harman, a philosophy professor at Princeton.  She argues that not only should abortion be
legal, it should be something that we don’t even feel bad about.  Aborted fetuses aren’t worthy of our regret.



Her first point in support of this contention is fetuses
aren’t worthy of being treated like human beings because they have never had
consciousness or experiences.  From an
atheistic perspective, I suppose this makes sense.  If you don’t believe that people have souls, the
next most obvious marker of humanity is consciousness (though there are logical
problems down that road too).

Note that Harman is very carefully choosing *past* consciousness as her standard
because she knows that if she doesn’t, it opens the door to further
enormity.  After all, many people with
advanced dementia and various other medical conditions no longer have consciousness.  Are they nonpersons too?  However, even people who are pro-abortion
generally don’t like the idea of snuffing out Granny without remorse or regret.  Keep this idea in mind; we’ll come back to
it.

Harman, then, wants to draw a bright line between those who
don’t have consciousness but will (fetuses) and those who used to have
consciousness but don’t anymore (many elderly people).  However, if we are to put one of those two
groups above the other on the basis of consciousness (which I wouldn’t; I
passionately affirm the sanctity of all life), it looks to me like the fetuses
would win.  The fetus will, in the
ordinary course of events, develop consciousness; the Alzheimer’s patient won’t.  All they’re going to do is die.  If it’s consciousness that’s valuable, beings
who will never have consciousness don’t seem very valuable to me.  They’re like dogs or cattle.

Beyond that, Harman’s got another problem, which she spends
the bulk of the video discussing.  If
fetuses aren’t valuable because they’ve never had consciousness, then no fetus
is valuable.  All those expectant mothers
who want their babies, who dutifully take their vitamins and schedule their
prenatal doctor visits and spend hours daydreaming about how life with Baby
will be, all those expectant mothers are lavishing attention on a lump of
tissue with no special significance. 
That can’t be right either!

Here, Harmon introduces the idea of a contingent future
consciousness.  The fetuses that will be
born will have future consciousness, so they matter.  Fetuses that won’t be born (including ones
that won’t be born because of abortion) won’t have future consciousness, so
they don’t matter.  Side note:  my favorite part of the video was when she
told James Franco (who was “interviewing” her) that if his mother had chosen to
abort him, he wouldn’t have mattered.  He
didn’t look too happy about that.

I see two problems here. 
First, it fails to account for the value that expectant mothers who
suffer pregnancy loss place on their *in-utero*children even afterward.  According
to this logic, Harman ought to go up to a friend mourning a miscarriage and say,
“Buck up, buttercup!  Little Whoever
never developed consciousness, so they don’t matter!”  In my book, that makes her either inconsistent
or an awful human being.

Second, she fails to acknowledge the ultimate end of her
reasoning:  that in the case of abortion,
a child is valuable or not according to the mother’s decision to value it or
not.  She is, after all, the one who determines
whether it will live to develop consciousness.

After I finished watching the video the first time, I came
upstairs and said to my wife, “It’s *The
Velveteen Rabbit*!”  For those of you
who missed out on the children’s book, it’s about a child who loves his stuffed
bunny so much that he makes it real (and by the end of the story, really
real).  If the child had not loved the
bunny, it would never have become real.

So too, apparently, with fetuses.  Wanted fetuses will develop consciousness
(because they are wanted) and are therefore real; unwanted fetuses won’t and
therefore aren’t.  I think this also is
lurking underneath Harman’s refusal to extend her logic to euthanasia.  We love Granny, even though she has Alzheimer’s,
so she’s real too!

In other words, what Harman actually believes is that we are
valuable because another human being has decided to value us.  As long as some external entity thinks we matter,
we’re safe.  If some external entity
doesn’t, well. . .

Off the bat, this means that dementia patients without next
of kin or close friends are probably in as much trouble as the unwanted
fetus.  If nobody thinks you’re real, you
aren’t.  Just imagine the Medicaid
savings!

In practice, it will also lead to the erosion of the
protections offered by consciousness. 
Just *how* conscious does that
fetus or graybeard have to be to count as conscious?  If somebody really loves you, not at
all.  If somebody kinda loves you, maybe
a little bit.  On the other end of the scale,
though, what if everybody thinks the world would be a more convenient place
without you in it?  “Great-Aunt Ethel
lost her keys *again*!  Can we take her to the ‘doctor’ yet?”

I think it’s pretty simple. 
Either all human beings have intrinsic value, or they don’t.  If they do, it’s time to chain up the
abortion clinics’ doors.  If they don’t,
if we get to decide who counts and who doesn’t, then the disfavored are going to
start dying.  Americans may have grown
used to living with abortion, but it is only the first step down a long, dark
road.
