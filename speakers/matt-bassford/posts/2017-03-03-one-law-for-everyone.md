The other day, I watched a Facebook video posted by a
Singaporean friend in which the prime minister of Singapore was interviewed
about his country’s criminalization of consensual homosexual sex.  During this interview, the interviewer asked
if his feelings about the law would change if he found out that one of his sons
or grandsons were gay.  The prime
minister didn’t address the question squarely; instead, he talked about how
decriminalization of homosexual activity in the West has done nothing to
decrease societal tension about the issue. 
Surprisingly, the interviewer let him get away with it (although maybe
not surprisingly; there were probably a lot of cultural subtleties in the interview
that I didn’t pick up on, even though it was conducted in English).

It reminded me, though, of a similar interview I watched
many years ago, during the 1992 presidential campaign.  The interviewee was Dan Quayle, then the
Republican candidate for vice president, and the subject was his pro-life
views.  Predictably, the interviewer
asked him what he would do if his daughter wanted to get an abortion.  The vice president replied that he would
support his daughter in whatever decision she made, which my yellow-dog
Democrat parents jumped on as a sign of Republican hypocrisy on the issue.



Both of these interviews reveal a common human
intuition:  you might have strong
opinions about an issue, but your views on the subject will change once those
views start affecting somebody you love. 
Too often, this intuition proves correct.  I’ve never been closely involved in such a
situation, but I’ve watched from afar as a gospel preacher, when faced with a
daughter who wanted to get divorced and remarried without Scriptural warrant,
suddenly found new meanings in Matthew 19 that he’d never seen before.

However, this cannot be the way that the people of God
behave.  James devotes the first section
of James 2 to a discussion of partiality, and he identifies it as a sin.  It is a sin to treat others with partiality,
and it is a sin to apply the law of God unevenly to those to whom we are
partial.  “Be holy, for I am holy,” says
the Lord.  The call to holiness applies
to strangers, it applies to brethren I don’t know well, it applies to my
dearest friends, it applies to my family, and it applies to me.  If I deny that the law speaks with equal
force to everyone, I am convicted by it as a transgressor.

My son is my son, and he will always be my son.  I will always love him.  However, I will also always say to him, “Be
holy.”  He’s a charmer, utterly unlike
his charmless father.  I strongly suspect
that he will find it easy to get in trouble with women when he’s older.  Nonetheless, I will expect him to be holy.  I will love him no matter what he does, but I
cannot approve of sin in my son any more than I can approve of it in anyone
else.  As the nature of God Himself
reveals, love and approval are two very different things.

In the event that he finds himself inclined toward other
men, I will still expect him to be holy. 
Holiness, not sexual self-expression, is the highest call of
mankind.  Jesus never married, nor did He
ever sin.  He lived, died, and rose
without ever having been intimate with anyone. 
If that proves to be my son’s cross, he needs to pick it up and carry
it.  I honor those who do.

I will similarly expect my daughter to be holy.  If she chooses to sin and gets pregnant out
of wedlock, I will continue to love her, but she will have to make that right
with God.  Likewise, neither in her case
nor anyone else’s does sin provide justification for further sin.  If she wants to raise the baby on her own, I
will support her in every way that I can. 
If she wants to put the baby up for adoption, I will support her in that
too.

However, if she says to me that she wants to have an
abortion, we’ll have a talk about that. 
Convenience is never an excuse to end the life of another human being,
even when the one inconvenienced is my daughter.  Sin has consequences.  If it’s your sin, you own the consequences,
no matter who you are.

The above, by the way, is what Dan Quayle should have said.

There is one law for everyone to obey, one Example for
everyone to follow.  Yes, absolutely,
there is also forgiveness and grace, but grace does not and cannot extend to
rewriting the rules for anybody.  God
Himself didn’t solve the sin problem by adding loopholes and asterisks and
exemptions.  He solved it by sending His
own Son to pay our debt in blood.

Who am I to do what God could not?
