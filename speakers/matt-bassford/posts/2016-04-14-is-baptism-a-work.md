One of the members of the Joliet church is involved in a
study with several who worship with a denominational church.  They’ve been studying James together, and in
this study, a question arose about the necessity of baptism for forgiveness of
sins.  The other women insisted that
because baptism was a work, it wasn’t necessary, because salvation is by
faith.  She wanted to know what I thought
about the issue.  Here’s my take on it.



I see two main problems with the argument that baptism is
unnecessary for salvation because baptism is a work.  The first is that the characterization of baptism
as a work is mistaken.  The Bible does say
that faith is a human work (John 6:28-29). 
By contrast, nowhere does the Bible say that baptism is a human
work.  You can assert that it is, but you
can't prove it from Scripture, and assertions on their own prove nothing.  Instead, God Himself is the one whom the
Bible describes as working during baptism (Colossians 2:12).

Consider here too the essentially passive language of *being* baptized.  Baptism isn't something you do.  It's done to you.  Baptism isn't a human work.  It's the work of God, and surely everyone
must agree that God's working is essential to salvation!

Second, the whole "baptism is a human work" thing
arises from confusion about what Paul is talking about when he distinguishes
between faith and works.  Paul's
distinction is clearest in Romans 3-4.

In Romans 4:5, we see a pretty classically Pauline statement
about being justified by faith apart from works.  However, the context makes clear that Paul
doesn't simply mean works in the sense of "people doing stuff".  As we've already seen, even faith is a work
in that sense.  Instead, Paul defines the
works he's talking about in Galatians 3:28, where he says that "man is
justified by faith apart from works *of
the Law*." (italics mine) 
Usually, when Paul says "works", that's shorthand for
"works of the Law".

Paul's big concern in life is not people who are proclaiming
baptism for forgiveness of sins.  He *is* one of those people!  Consider his discussion of baptism in Romans
6:3-7.

Instead, he's preoccupied with Judaizing false
teachers.  These are the guys who want to
bind the Law of Moses on Gentile converts. 
Against that, Paul argues that salvation is by faith, not by keeping the
Law of Moses, so there is no reason for Christians to try to carry out the
works of the Law.  In fact, the attempt
is spiritually harmful.

Fast-forward 1500 years, and you come to Martin Luther,
who's really upset about the abuses of the Renaissance Catholic Church.  In particular, he hates the sale of
indulgences, the idea that something you do (giving money to the Church) can
purchase forgiveness of sins.

Luther, then, is looking for a stick to beat the Church
with, and he stumbles across the Scriptural language of justification by
faith.  Rather than reading it in its
cultural context and applying it to the spiritual battles of Paul's day, he reads
it in his own context and applies it to the spiritual battles of his day.  He misses completely that "works"
is about the Law of Moses and makes it mean instead "stuff you do",
because that gives him a reason to condemn the sale of indulgences.  Unfortunately, later thinkers used this idea
that we're saved by faith, not stuff we do, to deny that baptism is necessary
for forgiveness of sins.

In short, baptism is not a human work, and even if it were a
human work, the idea that it is unnecessary for salvation is based on a
dramatic misreading of one of the major themes of the New Testament.
