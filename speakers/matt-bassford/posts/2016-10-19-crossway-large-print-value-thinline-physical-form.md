**OVERVIEW:** This is an attractive poly-cover Bible at
an extremely low price.  What’s not to
like?

Unlike most of the Crossway Bibles I’ve been reviewing
recently, I did not buy this one.  I
requested one from Crossway’s marketing department, and they sent one.  Apparently, nobody else has bothered to
review this particular edition.  As
always, they put no conditions on my review.



**VITAL STATISTICS**

**Translation:**  English Standard Version (2011)

**Cover:**  Polyurethane

**Binding:**  Sewn

**Trim Size:**  5 ⅜” x 
8 ⅜”

**Text Block Width:**  ¾”

**Weight:**  1 pound, 7 ounces

**Column:**  Double

**Layout:**  Paragraphed

**Print Size:**  10-point Lexicon No. 1

**Words of Christ:**  Black

**Cross-References:**  None

**Ribbon Marker:**  0

**SIZE:** This is not at all the Bible I was
expecting to get.  I had thought that in
order to produce the Large-Print Value Thinline, Crossway had simply used an
existing setting, in much the same way that the Value Thinline shares a setting
with the regular Thinline.  Basically, I
was thinking that the LPVT was an economy version of the Large-Print Thinline
Reference.

In reality, though, this isn’t that Bible at all.  So far as I can tell, the LPVT doesn’t share
a setting with any Bible in the Crossway lineup.  It’s its own animal.  This has a number of consequences, but one of
them is that it is considerably smaller than the Large-Print Thinline
Reference.  In fact, despite its large
print, the LPVT isn’t much distinguishable in size from a normal thinline.  As a result, I could easily put the Bible in
my glove box.  You couldn’t put this one
in a pocket, but you could definitely stick it in a bag or a purse.

**COVER:** This is pretty typical low-end Crossway
poly.  However, I don’t mean that as an
insult in any way.  Low-end Crossway poly
is respectable stuff.  The cover is first
of all pretty.  My review copy is a nice
dappled russet color.  Unlike some of the
two-toned abominations of 10 years ago, I didn’t feel any qualms about using it
in the pulpit.  Crossway appears to have
learned the lesson that simply because you can make a Bible purple doesn’t mean
that you should.  Even a value Bible
deserves some dignity.

The ornamentation of the Bible is also dignified.  It’s all one piece, without any sewing to
distract or fail.  It has a burnished
line around the perimeter of the Bible (at a recent visit to R.R. Donnelley, I
learned that when poly is stamped with a darker ink instead of gilt, that’s
called burnishing) and a reasonably dignified burnished spine.  Here too, Crossway has learned from prior bad
behavior and only included two variations of “English Standard Version”.  Personally, I think they could just say “Holy
Bible”, along with the Crossway logo and the ESV logo, and have done, but they
weren’t willing to go that far with this one.

As one would expect from a Bible at this price point, the
endpapers are thick paper instead of leatherette.  It’s not as sturdy, and it’s more prone to
splitting.  I would guess that like most
poly covers, this one would fail as the poly starts to flake off along the
edges of the spine.  It could also
perish, like some poly covers, because it was left in the sun and melted.  There may be an endpaper split in its future
too.  However, there is no such thing as
“indestructible value thinline”, so I’m hardly outraged by this.

**TEXT BLOCK:** It’s time for another verse of my hymn of
praise to Crossway.  Other than the very
humblest Bibles they make (giveaway New Testaments, etc.), every Bible in their
lineup is sewn.  This one certainly
is.  I open it up and see thread.

I think that’s awesome. 
Because my regular kitchen-table Bibles are out for photos at the
moment, I’ve been using a matched set of glue-bound pew Bibles.  Both of them are shedding pages, and I grump
about it every time I open one.  No one
should have to put up with Bible pages drifting hither and yon like dandruff,
and Crossway makes sure you don’t have to. 
This thing will easily survive years of regular use.

It’s similarly worth noting that the edges of the LPVT are
plain (though corner-rounded) rather than gilded.  Crossway probably made this choice so that
the Bible would be slightly cheaper to produce. 
Also on my field trip to R. R. Donnelley, I learned that China-print
Bibles are hand-gilded with foil over there, and even at Chinese wages, that
still has to cost something.

I approve of cheaper Bibles that are still able to take a
beating, and I don’t mind the looks of the white page edges either.  It has a severity to it that I
appreciate.  Plus, it’s always possible
that sunlight glinting off your Bible gilding may attract the attention of
enemy snipers (I’ve been reading a book about World War I recently).

Finally, this is a Bible without a ribbon marker.  In practice, I find that I don’t use ribbon
markers much anymore, so I didn’t miss it terribly.  However, I still missed it some.  If the absence of a marker really bothers
you, I guess you can stick your own ribbon in or just use a bookmark, maybe
even one with an inspirational Bible verse on it.

**VALUE:** The LPVT retails for $20.  For a Bible with a sewn binding, regardless
of what other characteristics it may or may not possess, that’s pretty
wonderful.  It’s only $5 more than the
normal Value Thinline, and it may well make the Bible accessible to someone who
can’t read the former.  Plus, the LPVT,
like nearly every other Bible, is deeply discounted online.  As of the writing of this post, they were
available on the Internet for $12 plus shipping.  In short, if I’m buying a Bible for somebody
who can’t handle the smaller print in the normal Value Thinline, I’m buying
this one.

Basically, there’s not a lot to dislike here.  It’s a reasonably small, reasonably sturdy
Bible for cheap.  It earns a 9/10 for
Physical Form.
