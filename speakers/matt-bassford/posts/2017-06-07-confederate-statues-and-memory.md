I’m a Civil War buff of long standing, and I admit that I regard with some ambivalence the recent push to take down statues of Confederate leaders across the South.  This is not based on any Lost Cause romanticism.  Even though my relatives who fought all wore gray, I think the Southerners were wrong, not only because of their racism, but also because of their rejection of the democratic process.  Democracy can’t survive unless the losers accept the verdict of the polls.

When it comes to individual Confederate leaders, I think some of them deserve statues and some don’t, even from a Southern perspective.  For instance, I believe the Confederate general Patrick Cleburne is statue-worthy.  He was a magnificent combat commander (sometimes called “the Stonewall Jackson of the West”), and he’s the only significant Rebel I know of who called for the South to emancipate and arm its slaves.  It went nowhere because none of the other Confederate leaders shared Cleburne’s view of racial equality, but at least he tried.  He deserves to be remembered for his physical and moral courage.

On the other extreme is Jefferson Davis.  In addition to being a rebel, a racist, etc., he was a pretty terrible president.  His mishandling of the Confederate officer corps, putting his cronies in command instead of competent officers (Exhibit A:  Braxton Bragg), did more to cost the South the war than just about anything else.  For Lost Cause romantics to put up statues to Jeff Davis is about like *Titanic* fans putting up statues to the iceberg.

Regardless, I’m not sure that pulling Davis down from his pedestal, along with all the rest, is such a great idea.  The only people I can remember pulling down historical statues in my lifetime were fun folks like ISIS and the Taliban, and as a rule, whenever you find yourself wanting to do the same kinds of things that ISIS does, it might be time to reconsider.

 It’s not that I particularly approve of the things those statues commemorated either.  Most famously, the Taliban blew up the Buddhas of Bamiyan in 2001, and even though I’m not exactly an idolater, I mourn their destruction.  It’s important to remember, and dynamiting the statues represents a dynamiting of the past.

I worry about the same thing when it comes to statues of Confederate leaders.  I understand and sympathize with the argument that they are symbols of racism and slavery, but that’s not all that they stand for.  Whether intended or no, they are symbols of the Civil War itself.  They commemorate a time in our country’s history when citizens of the United States developed such suspicion and hatred of one another, when the political process broke down so badly, that hundreds of thousands of young American men had to die before the country could be reunited.

Surely the American people of our day don’t need any warnings about fearing and hating and not listening to other Americans who don’t agree with them, do they?

Approximately 3 percent of the entire population of the United States died because of the Civil War.  3 percent of the US’s current population is 9.78 million.

Just something to think about.

Of course, even people who don’t put up statues still need to remember history.  As a rule, the churches of Christ don’t go in for statues, not even of Jesus, much less modern-day leaders.  However, we still have a history, and history tell us that about every 60 years since the Restoration of the early 19th century, Restoration churches have big brotherhood-wide splits.

Some people forget how Christians did things in the first century.  Pretty much everybody forgets that Christians are supposed to treat each other with compassion, patience, and love.  The results are ugly.  Churches are torn in two, friendships shattered, souls lost.

Maybe history isn’t going to repeat itself.  Maybe Christians today are wiser and godlier and better than Christians in 1900 or 1960, so we don’t have to worry about the same thing happening again.

Or, maybe, we should remember that 1960 plus 60 is 2020.
