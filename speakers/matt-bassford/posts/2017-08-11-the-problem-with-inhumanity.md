The other day, a Bible-geek friend of mine posted the above meme
on Facebook.  I don’t believe I’ve read
anything by Thomas Merton (though I know who he is), but it struck me as even
more appropriate today than it was 50-60 years ago.  I think we do live in an inhuman age that
poses a threat to our humanity, and this is a problem for the godly as much as
for the worldly.

Of course, by “human” I don’t mean the same thing (and I
don’t think Merton did either) as does the unrepentant sinner who, when
confronted about his sin, replies sullenly, “I’m only human.”  I am not a Calvinist, and I do not believe
that sin defines human nature.  That’s
something we inflict on ourselves later, but it is not how we are created.  Sin does not make us human; it makes us
something less.

Instead, I like Merton’s definition for “humanity”, “the
image of man that reflects the image of God”. 
What makes us truly human, distinct from all the rest of the creation,
is that which we share with God.  It is
our soul; it is our consciousness; it is the eternity in our hearts.



As a result, there is no better example of what it means to
be truly human than the Lord Jesus.  The
gospel accounts are glorious with His humanity. 
Even 2000 years later, His portrait is so intense that it leaps off the
page.  How alive He was!

We see His humanity in His pity, in His willingness to
extend a hand to touch a leper in his loathsomeness.  When I read the account, I sometimes find
myself wondering how long it had been since anyone else had touched the
leper.  No one else would.  Jesus did.

We see it in His marveling, both at the faith of others and
their unbelief.  We see it in His anger
and His passion as He upbraids the disciples for their failure to
understand.  He is on a clock, and they
have got to get this!  We see it in His
love for sinners, tax collectors, and children, those whom the religious elite
didn’t have time for, but the Son of God did. 
We see it in His deep tenderness toward the repentant and His equally
deep contempt for the hypocritical and self-righteous.

We often remark on the sinlessness of Jesus.  However, I think His understanding of and His
submission to His Father went even beyond that. 
Jesus was also the first human being ever to live life the way that God
intended it to be lived.  He lived
fully.  He opened His heart to everyone
around Him, absorbed spiritual body blows of hatred and envy, and yet refused
to become scarred by cynicism and bitterness. 
He combined eternal wisdom with a childlike conviction that every day,
every person, and every conversation was important.  His humanity suffused His life with meaning.

The Contemporary English Bible is known as a translation
for, among other things, choosing to translate “Son of Man” as “the Human
One”.  I’m not a fan of that rendering
for a number of reasons, but it does capture the unique humanity of Jesus.

This, I think, is the humanity that we should guard within
ourselves, the divine spark that bears the likeness of God.  However, in practice, most of us find it
difficult to keep the spark from being dimmed or snuffed out.  Sin will do this, of course, but often, the
problem is subtler than the thou-shalt-not’s.

If to be truly human is to live like Jesus, than to be
inhuman is to live like Jesus didn’t. 
Inhumanity is to be confronted with the imploring leper and to turn away
with loathing and scorn.  Inhumanity is
to be so calloused by our experiences that we greet both faith and unbelief
with a shrug.  It is to have such
jaundiced expectations of others that we react to their failings with
indifference rather than passion.  Yup;
figured he’d blow it.  People like him
always do.

Inhumanity is all the more alluring because of its
appearance of wisdom.  It hurts to go
around being like Jesus all the time.  It
hurts to invest yourself in people who are probably going to fail you.  Why not cut to the chase and keep everybody
at arm’s length, where they’re going to end up anyway?

True wisdom, though, isn’t found in cynical detachment and
refusal to feel.  It is found in their
opposite.  It is found in the love that
hopes all things, believes all things, and endures all things, despite the
possibility, even likelihood, of disappointment.  Love is the only escape from the prison of
the self, but when we allow our scars to keep us from loving, we slam the cell door
on ourselves.

This is an inhuman age. 
Nobody believes in anything or anyone anymore.  Politics only reveals a much larger cultural
divide in which each half of the country has concluded that the other half
isn’t worth its time.

On a smaller scale, we have acquaintances, but we don’t
really have friends.  I remember reading
awhile back that the average American invites someone else into their home an
average of one time a year.  We abandon
real people for the sanctuary of screens and then complain when people on
Facebook behave like people too often behave. 
“I don’t have time for the drama,” we say.

We do have time for the drama.  Of course we do.  What is life, if not a continuing pageant of
joy and pain and love?  What is left of
life, if it does not consist of our interactions with others?  What is left of discipleship?

Jesus didn’t come to an empty planet.  He came to one that teemed with all kinds of
flawed people, just as flawed as those who surround us.  When He embraced those people anyway, He
revealed the greatness of which the human heart is capable.  The extent to which we are willing to do as
He did reveals who we are too.
