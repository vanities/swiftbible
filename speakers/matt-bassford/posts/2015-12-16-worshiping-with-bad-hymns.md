The critique of “Sanctuary” that I posted several weeks back
netted a number of interesting comments, but two in particular struck me as
being worthy of post-length replies.  I
addressed Dominic Venuso’s comment a couple weeks ago.  This week, I want to look at Stephen Rouse’s
reaction.



He wrote:

*Honest question: Is it possible to give our Lord A+ worship (or at least our
best efforts at it) when a sub-par hymn is selected by someone else? This is
important.*

*I believe it is possible. Or we
should at least strive for it.*

*I believe it’s possible to find the
good in a hymn, even if it does not meet our structural guidelines. Should we
choose better? Of course. Should we try to help others choose better? As gently
and helpfully as we can.*

*But the fact is, in almost any
given worship service, the song leader is going to pick something that I find
“sub-par.” And sometimes… often times I need to be honest about whose standard
I’m using.*

*So what should I focus on? Spend
those minutes of “worship” silently bemoaning that we’re singing \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_
(Sanctuary, etc.) agaaaain? Ok, so the song selected isn’t the greatest. What
do I do?*

*I struggle. Struggle. With this.
Because I don’t believe it’s pleasing to God to just sit there and judge. Even
with some righteous judgment, sometimes.*

*I’m really trying to be more of a
good-seeker in hymns. Is there a place to point out the bad? Yes. But maybe I
need to do it less for my own worship’s sake.*

Here, Stephen points out something that every hymn geek on
the planet has to acknowledge is true. 
You have your standards for hymns, but sometimes the song leader (who is
probably not a hymn geek) picks out a hymn that doesn’t live up to those standards.  In truth, this doesn’t happen very much in my
own congregation because the song leaders are thoughtful, responsible men, but
even in the Joliet church, it still happens occasionally.

What do you do about it? 
I think Stephen is right.  Except
in extreme cases, you do your best to worship with it.

This is not necessarily easy.  In fact, it’s something that I struggle with,
though I suspect that my struggle is different than Stephen’s.  The sad truth is that even though I write
about hymns and worship issues all the time, I’m not naturally very good at
worship.

There are some Christians (such as my wife) who are
naturally gifted worshipers.  Give them a
hymn to sing, and they get lost in it. 
For such people, hearing “Joy to the World” over the loudspeakers at the
mall during Christmastime may be a religious experience.  Often, people like this are very musically
attuned and emotionally expressive.

I’m neither, so worship for me is often a battle.  I have trouble keeping mentally focused, and
too often, I’m emotionally disengaged.  I
work at this, and I think I’m better at worship than I used to be, but I’m
still not very good at it.

In fact, it’s probably true that much of my critique of bad
hymns is driven by my difficulties with worship.  I’m like the guy who complains about immodest
dress because of his struggles with lust. 
“I already have a problem with this, and you’re not helping!!!”

Having said that, even as a C-level worshiper, I have to do my
best with what I’m given.  I have a
responsibility to glorify God and to edify my brethren.  It may be that trying to do either with a
particular hymn is like trying to type with a hammer, but that doesn’t change
the responsibility.

There are two big exceptions here.  The first is if I believe a hymn teaches
false doctrine, as in “You ask me how I know He lives?  He lives within my heart!”  I won’t sing that phrase, because that is *not* how I know Jesus lives.  I will instead long for a chorus that says,
“You ask me how I know He lives?  The
500-plus witnesses to His resurrection!” 
Subjective experience should never be allowed to replace Scriptural
truth.  I am not sad at all that “He
Lives” does not appear in the hymnal we use.

The second is the “Shut the doors!” exception.  The language here comes from Malachi
1:10.  In context, God is so upset with
the quality of offering that is being presented that He would rather have no
offering at all.  When Hebrews 13:15
describes our worship as a sacrifice, I think it’s reasonable to conclude that
God has similar expectations for our offerings of song.  If I believe that a hymn is of such poor
quality that it is an offense to God, I’m responsible for not singing it.

In practice, I can’t even remember the last time I refused
to sing a hymn for this reason.  I think
of this as the nuclear option, and I’m leery of exercising it.  My congregation sang “Sanctuary” a few months
back.  I sang too and did my best to
worship with it.

I made this decision in part because I was concerned about
the impact that my silence would have on others.  I sit toward the front of our auditorium, so
my refusal to participate would be a lot more obvious than if I sat on the back
row.  Also, our congregation has several
teens and tweens who often choose not to sing. 
I don’t want to get onto them about that—I can remember not wanting to
sing myself—but I do want to provide them with a good example.  I don’t want my silence to give them an
excuse for theirs.

In short, I certainly believe that there’s a time and place
for hymn critique.  However, I'm not at all sure
that the assembly is generally a good place for it.

Others’ mileage may vary here.  I have friends who conscientiously employ the
“Shut the doors!” exception much more frequently than I do.  If that’s what they need to do to feel right
in their relationship with God, far be it from me to criticize them!  I don’t mean here to argue for any particular
outcome, only that we should approach worship with thoughtfulness and
reverence.  Even if a hymn is not
thoughtful, it’s still up to us to do our best.
