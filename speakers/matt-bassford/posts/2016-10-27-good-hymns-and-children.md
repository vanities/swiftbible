A few days back, I ran across [this post](http://www.patheos.com/blogs/ponderanew/2016/10/21/20-hymns-your-kids-should-know/) by worship blogger Jonathan Aigner, entitled “20 Hymns Your Kids Should Know”.  Upon reading through it, my first thought
was, “My kids don’t know hardly any of those hymns!” Aigner’s list, though
worthy, is pretty high-church, and my congregation’s repertoire is pretty. . .
not.  Of the 20 on his list, we’ll sing
“All Hail the Power of Jesus’ Name” and “Holy, Holy, Holy”, maybe one or two
more.

My second thought was, “I wonder what hymns my kids *do* know.”  My children are 5 and 6, so I wasn’t
expecting too much, but I thought I’d check. 
As it happened, that very night we went to a singing at another area
congregation, so I passed the time during the drive by quizzing them about
their repertoire knowledge until they began to grumble.  However, I emerged from the discussion with a
list of at least 10 hymns.



During the singing itself, my 5-year-old son sat with me, often
in my lap.  Because of my normal
preaching duties, this is unusual.  It
did teach me, though, that even though he’s little more than an emergent
reader, he knew enough of the repertoire, even at a different congregation, to
sing along a respectable percentage of the time.  Since then, I’ve been paying attention, and from
our ordinary household interaction and chatter, it’s become obvious that they
are well on their way to learning dozens (if not hundreds) of hymns.

I am, to say the least, happy to see this.  In my experience, accidental parenting
successes are rare enough that they ought to be treasured.  In fact, I think there are several reasons
why any child will benefit from learning, and should be encouraged to learn,
good hymns:

**Good Hymns Are
Memorable.** There is something about
song that engages the memory.  How many
song lyrics do all of us remember from the then-current radio repertoire when
we were teenagers?  There are a number of
such songs that I can easily recall even though I don’t want to!

In addition, most good hymns have structural qualities that
make them even more memorable.  One such
quality is rhyme.  Many lines in
Shakespeare’s plays rhyme not because Shakespeare was attempting to be pretty,
but because he wanted to make them easier for actors to memorize.

Similarly, rhythmic, metered language engages the memory in
ways that ordinary writing does not. 
It’s a big part of the reason why *The
Iliad*, which was originally an oral composition, is in dactylic
hexameter.  The patterns helped Homer and
his contemporaries to remember it.

Though we don’t often consider the impact of all of this, it
is widespread and profound.  Unless a
church has fallen into the trap of changing out its repertoire every two years
to chase the praise-band flavor of the month, its worship services will
inevitably teach its members to memorize hymns. 
I’m certain that nearly all Christians know many more hymn verses by
heart than Bible verses.

What’s more, these memories are uniquely resistant to the
passage of time.  I haven’t done it so
much recently, but in years past, I often spent my Sunday afternoons visiting
older Christians in nursing homes to pray and sing with them.  Naturally, these impromptu worship services
were open to everyone, so we would be visited by a number of residents, many of
whom clearly suffered from advanced dementia. 
They would shuffle (or be wheeled) into the room where we were meeting
and sit staring blankly off into space until we began to sing.  Then, people who may not even have known
their own names anymore could sing “Amazing Grace” or “Take the Name of Jesus
with You” start to finish, without missing a single word or note.

There is nothing so deeply graven on our hearts as the hymns
we learn in worship.  When I see my
children beginning to learn them, I know they are acquiring a treasure they
will retain to the end of their lives and beyond.

**Good Hymns Are
True.** If hymns were content-neutral,
the ease with which we remember them wouldn’t matter.  However, as a spiritual guide, the hymns we
know by heart are second in importance only to the Bible.  Rare is the sermon that is as central to our
faith as a hymn!

In fact, precisely because we know them so well, the hymns
we sing are our first answer to the challenges of life.  A bad hymn may not be able to meet those
challenges, but a good hymn will be. 
When we encounter trial, “What a Friend We Have in Jesus” reminds us to
pray.  When we are uncertain, “Give Me
the Bible” directs us to the word.  When
grief overwhelms us, “Be Still, My Soul” reassures us that sorrow lasts for a
moment, but joy in Christ will last forever. 
And so on, through hymn after hymn. 
Unless we are a people of song, we are no better equipped to face these
things than the people of the world are.

Similarly, good hymns serve as a gateway to the Bible.  On the way home from that singing, my wife
and I were talking with our 6-year-old daughter, Zoë, about her favorite line
in her favorite hymn.  She replied that
her favorite was the line in “In Christ Alone” that reads, “No power of hell,
no scheme of man, can ever pluck me from His hand.”

“Did you know that that’s in the Bible too?” we asked her.

“Really?  Wow; that’s
amazing!” she exclaimed, and from there, we spent the next couple of minutes talking
about John 10:28 and Romans 8:38-39.

I suspect that every thoughtful Christian comes to the same
realization sooner or later.  Good hymns
aren’t good hymns simply because their authors were smart.  They’re good because those authors knew the
Bible so well that when they wrote, they wrote from the overflow of the word in
their hearts.  We may know hymns better
than the Bible, but in many cases, we know the Bible because we know
hymns.  If those hymns are teaching us
Bible from our earliest youth, so much the better!

**Good Hymns Are
Beautiful.** Time has a sorting effect
on all works of art.  After decades and
centuries have passed, generally it is only the good that we remember.  The mediocre and bad fade mercifully into
oblivion.  Good hymns survive not only
because they are good hymns, but because they are good art.  They are beautiful to read, and they are
beautiful to sing.

I think it’s important for our children to associate the
worship of God with beauty, particularly a simple beauty that is easy to
understand and access.  Most of the way
my religious tradition “does church” is not very accessible to children.  You sit still and listen to some guy talk
about things you don’t understand for half an hour, then you bow your head and
close your eyes (with likely peeking) while another guy talks about things you
don’t understand for what feels like another half an hour.  Singing, though, is something that children
can understand.  In my experience, children
enjoy singing and love to sing themselves until they get old enough that their
peers shame them out of it.  Honestly, I
think that singing with other people who are singing is much more accessible to
children than earsplitting concert-style praise songs are.  That kind of thing usually prompts my
children, at least, to cover their ears and wail!

By contrast, nobody wails at “Jesus Loves Me!”  It’s known and loved by children from a
bewildering array of different cultures all over the world.  Some might scoff at the notion that “Jesus
Loves Me!” is great art, but it has resonated with hundreds of millions of
souls in the way that only great art can. 
If my children love “Jesus Loves Me!”, it’s not too far from there to
loving Jesus.
