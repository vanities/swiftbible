Last week, I ran across an article from the Washington Post
that explained why pop hits usually have repetitive lyrics.  For those of you who are curious, you can
read the whole article [here](http://www.washingtonpost.com/local/why-do-you-keep-hearing-shut-up-and-dance-everywhere-you-go-heres-the-scientific-answer/2015/07/16/32c921f8-2655-11e5-b72c-2b7d516e1e0e_story.html).

For those of you who are less curious, the article cites a
psychological phenomenon called “processing fluency”.  Basically, the less we have to think about
something, the more we like it.  We don’t
elect our presidents on the basis of multi-volume economic studies.  We elect them on the basis of catchy
three-word slogans.  Because of this
bias, songs that say the same thing over and over again are more likely to become hits than songs that don’t.



**Repetition in
Contemporary Christian Music**

Because I am a bomb-thrower by nature, I linked to the
article in a couple of different places with the caption, “There might, MIGHT,
be applications to modern worship music here.” 
Of course, there are.  Much of
contemporary Christian music looks just like songs from the Top 40 with tweaks
to content.  They’re about Jesus rather than the hot boy/girl at the club, but the construction is the
same:  a catchy, repeated phrase directed
at the object of affection, surrounded by some other words that nobody pays
attention to.

The formula works. 
For instance, take the Hillsong United song “Oceans”, which was a
massive big smash hit on the Christian charts last year.  The refrain (“Spirit lead me where my trust
is without borders”, etc.) is repeated six times, back to back to back to back
to back to back.  You may not have known
the refrain when the song began, but by the time it’s over, you’ll be hearing
the refrain in your sleep.

I was amused to note that when I googled “Oceans”, one of
the suggested search phrases that popped up was “oceans hillsong chords”.  People don’t just want to listen to this
song.  They want to play it in worship.

**The Problem with
Repetition**

If our goal is to become (let’s be honest) a slightly less
cool version of the world, that’s fine, but if our goal is to conform to what
the Bible says about worship, it’s a problem. Songs with high processing
fluency are by definition thoughtless, but the writers of the New Testament
believed worship should be thoughtful. 
In 1 Corinthians 14:15, Paul declares, “I will sing with the spirit, and
I will sing with the mind also.” 
Likewise, Colossians 3:16 reads, “Let the word of Christ dwell richly
within you, with all wisdom teaching and admonishing one another with psalms
and hymns and spiritual songs.”

The point here is plain. 
Worship is to be mindful.  Worship
is to reveal a rich indwelling of the word of Christ.  Worship is to be an expression of
wisdom.  Rather than being fluent and
effortless, it is to be thoughtful and effortful, because unless we invest
effort in understanding our worship, we aren’t going to learn anything.

However, this is not to say that repetition is bad per
se.  If it were, the Holy Spirit would
not have used repetition in Psalm 136, in which every other line reads, “For
His lovingkindness is everlasting.” 
Besides, repetition in hymns with human authors is hardly a new
phenomenon.  Every verse of “Holy, Holy,
Holy!” begins with the eponymous phrase. 
If we tore out every hymn from our hymnals that used some form of repetition,
there wouldn’t be much of a hymnal left!

**What**

Instead, when it comes to repetition, we should ask,
“What?”, “Why?”, and “How much?”  Some
phrases are intrinsically worthy of being repeated more than others.  At the bottom of the heap, we must put the
repeated phrases in hymns such as the radio-hymn standard “It Won’t Be Very
Long”.

It won’t be very long? 
Really?  Are you sure?  Last I checked, we know neither the day nor
the hour.

By contrast, consider “Great Is Thy Faithfulness”.  In the case of that hymn (written only five
years before “It Won’t Be Very Long”, incidentally), the repeated phrase is
straight Scripture, taken from the KJV rendition of Lamentations 3:23.  What is more, it possesses extraordinary
logical and devotional power.  The faith
of the Christian is predicated upon the faithfulness of God, and the reflection
that His faithfulness is indeed great is a source of profound assurance.  Every believer can benefit from singing it
not merely once, but over and over.

**Why**

Also, we must ask how the repetition relates to the rest of
the hymn.  For instance, the Michael W.
Smith song, “Thy Word” repeats Psalm 119:105. 
So far, so wonderful.  However,
the rest of the song has nothing to do with the meaning of the Bible to the Christian.  Instead, it appears to be about the
believer’s experience of God in times of trouble.  That too is a worthy thought, but it doesn’t
belong in the same hymn as Psalm 119:105. 
It’s logically dissonant, and that strips the repeated phrase of meaning.

By contrast, “I Stand in Awe of You” (again, written only
three years after “Thy Word”) has additional content that reinforces its
repetition.  Why should we stand in awe
of God?  Because He is beautiful,
marvelous, wonderful, incomparable, infinitely wise, and profoundly
loving.  By that point in time, the hymn
has sold me.  I’m ready to repeat my awe
of God because the other content has primed me for it.

**How Much**

Finally, we should ask how much of the hymn or praise song
is repetitive.  Repetition in a hymn is
like a tail on a kite.  It may be
important and even necessary (depending on the kind of kite), but if you have
too much tail and too little kite, the whole thing will never get off the
ground.  Generally, if the amount of
repetition outweighs everything else in the spiritual song, you’ve got too much
of it.  Only the most powerful inspired
phrases can bear up under such heavy use.

I’m no enemy of repetition. 
Long before I read the *Washington
Post* article, I sensed that a repeated phrase focused a hymn and made it
accessible to worshipers.  As a result, nearly
every hymn that I’ve written for the past 10 years contains some repetitive
element.  I think of such elements as
signposts that get the thoughts of the congregation moving in the right
direction.  However, a hymn or praise
song that uses repetition wrongly, appealing though it may be, is only setting
up signposts that lead nowhere.
