The other night in Bible class, we were making our way
through 2 Chronicles 30 and the story of Hezekiah’s re-institution of the
Passover.  In this story, there are two
things in particular that are likely to alarm those associated with the
churches of Christ.  First, Hezekiah
decides to celebrate this Passover on the fifteenth day of the second month
instead of the fifteenth day of the first month, even though the Law of Moses
prescribed the latter as the appropriate day for celebration (vs. 2-4).  Second, a bunch of people from the nation of
Israel show up to celebrate the Passover even though they have not consecrated
themselves according to the ordinance. 
Hezekiah prays for God to accept them anyway, and He does (vs. 17-20).

From the discussion that ensued, it was obvious that many of
those in attendance hadn’t grappled with this textual issue before.  I feel kind of bad about that; after all,
I’ve been working with the church here for more than 11 years, but I’ve never
preached or taught on the text.  This
probably highlights a tendency in me and likely in others to focus on the
obscure stories from the Old Testament that allow us to easily make the point
we want to make (“Uzza got roasted, so you’d better do *everything* according to the ordinance!”) while ignoring others that
seem to point in the opposite direction (Hezekiah and the Israelites didn’t do
what the ordinance told them to, and they were fine).  However, if we are truly being honest with
the word, our understanding of it will be drawn from both kinds of stories.



**Hezekiah's Circumstances**

I’ve certainly seen people online use 2 Chronicles 30 to
attack the notion that we ought to “do all things according to the pattern”,
but I think that is taking the text farther than it will go.  After all, the God who condoned Hezekiah’s
date shift and healed the unconsecrated Israelites is the same God who brought
down fire to consume Nadab and Abihu. 
God is not capricious, nor does He show partiality.  We must conclude, then, that there is
something different about Hezekiah’s situation that led God to favor the Jewish
king instead of destroying him like the unhappy sons of the high priest.

First of all, we must remember that this story takes place
in the very beginning of Hezekiah’s reign. 
His father before him, Ahaz, was one of the most wicked of the kings of
Judah, and among his other misdeeds, he had allowed the temple to fall into
disrepair and its priesthood to become unsanctified.  2 Chronicles 30:3 notes that in the fifteenth
day of the first month, there weren’t enough priests who had consecrated
themselves to keep the Passover.  2
Chronicles 29:17 reveals that the temple itself wasn’t consecrated and ready
for use until the sixteenth day of the first month, a day after the festival
was supposed to begin.

In other words, Ahaz’s bad leadership had left his reforming
son in an impossible situation.  There
was no way that Hezekiah could have kept the Passover on the prescribed date
without violating another commandment of God. 
As a result, he chose to postpone the celebration until he could do it
right.

There was precedent for this.  According to Numbers 9:10-14, somebody who
was ceremonially unclean on the date of the Passover was supposed to keep it a
month later.  Admittedly, Numbers 9
doesn’t exactly fit Hezekiah’s circumstances, but he apparently concluded it
was the best he could do, and God endorsed his choice.

Similarly, 2 Chronicles 30:5 reveals that the keeping of the
Passover had generally ceased in Israel and Judah alike.  For most of these people, they first thought about
traveling to Jerusalem when the king’s messengers showed up and invited them to
come.  The Passover was already being
celebrated on an alternate date, so there was no provision in the Law for the
people who were unclean when they got the invitation.  They had a choice:  show up to celebrate the Passover even though
they were unclean, or not bother and stay home. 
The people who had a heart to seek God came anyway, so Hezekiah prayed
that God would receive them despite their uncleanness, and He did.

In both of these cases, the same thing happened.  There were opposing commandments that put a
follower of God in a position that was not merely difficult, but
impossible.  There was no way that either
the unconsecrated priests or the unconsecrated people could have avoided
violating the Law.  However, they chose
to seek God as best as they could, and God was gracious to them.

This is a far cry from concluding that 2 Chronicles 30 gives
us precedent for ignoring the commandments of God!  Hezekiah was very aware of those commandments
and equally aware that because of past failures, neither the priests nor the
people could abide by them.  He did not
claim that imperfect obedience was equally good; instead, he sought forgiveness
for it.  When he received that
forgiveness, he did not presume that God’s mercy in cases of impossibility gave
him the right to ignore God’s law in cases where obedience was not impossible.

**What the Story Means for Us**

All of these principles apply to us today.  There are times when it is not merely
difficult but impossible for us to fully obey the law of Christ.  The most obvious example of this, and one
that was brought up in class, is in the realm of church government.  We all know that God wills His churches to be
governed by elders.  However, most
churches don’t have enough qualified men to form an eldership.  As a result, Christians in those
congregations either disobey Him by forming an unqualified eldership (bad
idea!) or by having no eldership at all. 
Churches with the opportunity to install elders must do so if they want
to be pleasing to God, but congregations with no such opportunity can hope to
find forgiveness for their failure.

There are other cases where the impossible nature of
circumstances may lead us to deviate from following God’s commandments to seek
His mercy instead.  For instance, I
believe that one must be baptized to be saved. 
If somebody comes to me and wants to pray the sinner’s prayer with me in
order to be saved, I’m going to refuse his request and invite him to study 1
Peter 3:21 with me.  Those who can obey
the gospel must obey the gospel.

However, let’s suppose that I become a chaplain in the Army
and end up in a shooting war.  I come
upon a mortally wounded soldier who acknowledges his sins and wants to find his
way to God before he dies.  It is
impossible to baptize the man; indeed, he will be dead in minutes.  Under those circumstances, I will certainly
pray for God to have mercy on him even though he has not obeyed the gospel, and
I see no contradiction between that and my view of the sinner’s prayer
generally.

We must be careful, though, to confine our application of
this principle to cases where there is literally no way to fully obey God.  It does not, for instance, apply to
Christians in unscriptural marriages. 
Divorcing the husband or wife you love is extremely difficult, yes, but
it is not impossible, and the word gives us no reason to suppose that God will
show mercy to those who choose to continue in sin.

Nor is this the same issue as the ignorance with which new
Christians frequently come to the Lord. 
The Great Commission tells us to baptize first, then teach, so many
recent converts simply aren’t going to realize all the things in their lives
that have to change.  We need to teach
such brethren and then be patient with them in their struggles against sin, just
as we pray for God to be patient with our struggles.  However, the problem here isn’t
impossibility.  It’s the difficulty of
spiritual transformation, so it’s unwise to extract principles from the story
of Hezekiah and apply them to a situation that doesn’t line up.

Ultimately, though, it should be reassuring to all of us
that God does not expect us to do the impossible.  The feeble shut-in who hasn’t made a service
in two years can certainly still have a right relationship with Him.  God is mindful of our frame and knows our
limitations.  How comforting it is that
He will show us mercy when our hearts are fixed on Him!
