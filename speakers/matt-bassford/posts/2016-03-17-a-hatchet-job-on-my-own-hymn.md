Yesterday, I blogged about the lessons that bad hymns can
teach, particularly to aspiring hymnists (and every hymnist is an aspiring
hymnist).  Among other things, I argued
that bad hymns actually convert lack of content into an advantage because the
lack of content helps them avoid lack of focus. 
“Good” hymns (hymns that follow the rules) often have lots of Biblical
content that isn’t precisely on point and so distracts from the point of the
hymn.  As a result, these “good” hymns
don’t connect with worshipers and so are not sung.



This is particularly a problem in longer hymns, precisely
because longer hymns offer the writer more room to get lost in.  An eight-line verse might not seem very long
to an ordinary Christian, but if you’re used to measuring out your words by the
syllable, that’s an eternity of space! 
Toss in a bunch of parallels and Scriptural paraphrases, and pretty soon
you’ve got a verse that follows all the rules but has wandered through half the
Bible.

This is hard to spot because the verse will say things that
are kinda connected to the point but aren’t necessary to it.  The verse is full, but the writing isn’t
tight.  Even if worshipers aren’t able to
articulate the problem, they lose their connection to the hymn, and their eyes
start glazing over.

When a hymn like this is sung, it has a particular
sound.  It is the sound of, “I can find
my notes, but I don’t care about what I’m singing about.”  It is the sound of the absence of
buy-in.

Over my career as a hymnist, I’ve heard that sound much more
than I care to.  Most recently, I heard
it during a hymn tryout session I held a few weeks back.  Such tryout sessions are painful but
necessary.  They dispel all of the
illusions that you have been cherishing about your flawed hymns.  Ball don’t lie, and tryout group don’t lie
either.

One of my failed hymns last time was entitled “Surely God Is
in This Place”.  Now that I’ve thought
about it, I’ve decided that it is a classic example of a “good” hymn with
subtly unfocused content and a buried point. 
Here’s the version that we sang:

*Surely God is in this
place,*

*Though we cannot see
His face;*

*As the song of praise
ascends,*

*Here He joins His
faithful friends.*

*Though the earth
cannot contain*

*All the splendors of
His reign,*

*Still He joins us from
above,*

*Rich in mercy, great
in love.*

*Surely God is in this
place*

*In the fullness of His
grace;*

*By His bidding, let us
bring*

*All our sorrows to our
King.*

*He is sure to save and
bless*

*In His truth and
faithfulness;*

*When He hears us in
our plight,*

*None can stand against
His might.*

*Surely God is in this
place,*

*Bound by neither time
nor space;*

*When His chosen people
meet,*

*With the first, He takes
His seat.*

*Everywhere the
righteous dwell,*

*God is in their midst
as well,*

*Here, yet hidden from
our sight*

*Till we dwell with Him
in light.*

The point of each verse is relatively simple:

God is with us.

Because God is with us, He will hear our prayers.

God will continue to be with us.

I didn’t need eight lines to say any of those things, but I
took eight lines to say them anyway.  And
the people said, “Meh.”

I don’t think there’s anything wrong with the core thought
of the hymn.  “Surely God is in this
place” is a neat idea, and it’s a Scriptural near-quote besides (from Genesis
28:16).  However, the execution was
bad.  The lines scanned, the rhymes were
strong, and there weren’t any clichés, but every verse has an awful lot of
lines that aren’t pulling their own weight because they don’t have to.

I decided, then, that I was going to chop the hymn down to a
four-line hymn and see if it worked any better. 
Here is the mutilated and improved (?) version:

*Surely God is in this
place,*

*Though we cannot see
His face;*

*He has joined us from
above,*

*Rich in mercy, great
in love.*

*Surely God is in this
place*

*In the fullness of His
grace;*

*As our faithful prayer
ascends,*

*He will swiftly bless
His friends.*

*Surely God is in this
place,*

*Bound by neither time
nor space;*

*With His chosen, He
will meet*

*Till we gather at His
feet.*

I don’t think I lost any content, and I may have picked up
enough focus that the hymn resonates. 
Certainly, those odds are better than the “No chance” I got three weeks
ago!
