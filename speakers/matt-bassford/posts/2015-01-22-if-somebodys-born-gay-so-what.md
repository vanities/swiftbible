One of the great arguments advanced by advocates of the
practice of homosexuality is that homosexuals are, in the immortal words of
Lady Gaga, “born this way”.  They
conceive of a binary scheme of human sexuality, in which one is either born
straight or born gay, and in either case, is incapable of changing.  Because of this, they argue, anyone who does
not endorse and welcome the practice of homosexuality (“They can’t help it!”)
is a bigot.

I am, at the least, suspicious of the straight/gay
dichotomy.  I cannot imagine that
everyone who practices homosexuality does so for the same reason, nor that the
reason is a genetic predisposition toward homosexual behavior.  Human sexuality is a complicated, messy
thing, and any binary analysis of it is inevitably going to be fatally flawed
(even if it does make for good PR).  This
may well be why the Bible speaks of sexual behavior but never sexual
identity.  Action is relatively
straightforward, but motivation can vary dramatically from individual to
individual.



However, let’s say that’s not true.  Let’s say that everyone is indeed born
straight or gay.  You are locked into
your sexual inclinations from the moment of conception, those inclinations are
entirely one-sided, and you cannot change them. 
The dividing line between straight and gay is a chasm, as well-defined
as it can possibly be.

Even if all of this is true, it does not change in any way
the requirements of the law of God, nor does it change the responsibility of the
individual to obey those requirements. 
An inborn inclination to sin is not unique to those who want to practice
homosexuality.  Indeed, such inclinations
are universal, and God universally expects mankind to deny them.

For instance, I myself have an inborn sexual attraction to
women.  This is not something I chose or
learned.  Instead, I can trace it back to
Genesis 1, when God blessed the man and the woman and told them to be fruitful
and multiply.  Since then, the
procreative urge has been handed down through the generations to me.

This urge does not discriminate.  I am certainly attracted to my wife (I
married her, after all), but I was attracted to other women before I married
her, and I've been attracted to other women since.  If I allowed my urges to control my behavior,
I would have had sex with other women before I got married and continued to
have sex with other women afterward, as often as opportunity presented
itself.  That is what the inborn desire
of my flesh wants to do.

However, the fact that my desire is inborn does nothing to
make its expression legitimate.  My flesh
longed for women before I was married, but God said, “Don’t fornicate.”  It continues to desire other women now that I
am married, but God said, “Don’t commit adultery.”  It longs to dwell on the physical attributes
of women other than my wife, but God said, “Don’t lust.”  God knows very well that I was “born this
way,” but He still requires me to walk according to the Spirit, not the flesh.

Now, it is true that
I have something that the one inclined toward homosexuality does not.  I have a legitimate outlet for my
desires.  However, whether or not that
outlet exists does not change the divine commandment.  I had no right before I was married to say to
God, “Your requirement of purity is too much for me to bear, so I’m going to go
out and fornicate.”  The difficulty of
righteousness is no excuse for sin.

God’s requirements
can be even more demanding than that. 
Let’s say that Lauren decides to divorce me, not because she’s carrying
on an affair, but because she’s sick of living with me.  I can be kind of a jerk, after all.

According to Matthew 19:3-9, once the divorce is final,
unless she reconciles with me, my sex life is Over.  Other than her, I can’t have a lawful wife,
so my choices are either lifelong celibacy or sin.  I don’t get to say, “God, that’s too
hard.  I’m going to get remarried in
spite of what Your word says.”   There
are no asterisks for difficult situations. 
Either I obey, or I don’t.

God’s requirements for the would-be homosexual are the same
as His requirements for the divorcee.  If
there is no legitimate way for a desire to be fulfilled, then its fulfillment
is sin.  Christians who make the choice
to be celibate rather than evil are the “eunuchs for the kingdom of heaven’s
sake” of Matthew 19:12.  I don’t claim
that such a choice is either easy or pleasant. 
I do claim, though, that no one who denies himself, takes up his cross,
and follows Jesus will be disappointed in his reward.

Our society would react, and indeed does react, to such an
uncompromising position with shock and outrage. 
“How could you ask that?”  That’s
not fair!”  However, first-century
Christianity could be far less compromising even than that.

Consider, for instance, the ominous words of Hebrews 12:4,
“You have not yet resisted to the point of shedding blood in your striving
against sin.”  By modern standards, the
recipients of Hebrews had already shown incredible perseverance in following
Jesus.  Some of them had been thrown in
prison for their faith, and others had invited the same fate by caring for the
prisoners.  They all had seen their
property seized by the enemies of the gospel, but they rejoiced in their hope
of heaven.

Now, however, their lives as well as their property were at
stake.  Some of them were debating a
return to the Jewish faith of their fathers, which, though it didn’t have
Jesus, wasn’t attended by violent persecution either.  A modern American might sympathize.  A modern American might say, “Oh, that’s too
much to ask!  Run along and be
Jews.”  The writer of Hebrews said, “When
you die for Jesus as Jesus died for you, that will be enough.  Until then, if you turn aside for any reason,
you will lose your soul over it.”  If God
expected Christians in the first century to obey even at the cost of their
lives, there can be no doubt that He expects Christians today to obey even at
the cost of sexual fulfillment.
