In Romans 12:3, Paul writes, “By the grace given to me I say
to everyone among you not to think of himself more highly than he ought to
think, but to think with sober judgment.”  In the context of Romans 12, Paul is talking
about our self-understanding of the gifts that God has given us for service in
the church, but this is a principle that applies in any context.  It’s simply impossible for us to make wise
decisions without a sober judgment of ourselves and our position.

This certainly holds true when it comes to the decisions we
make about our finances.  In particular,
if we’re having financial problems, without some kind of financial benchmark,
we have no idea whether those problems are due to our own improvidence or to
the fact that we simply aren’t making enough money to keep our heads above
water.  We need some kind of baseline we
can use to evaluate ourselves.



**Introducing the
Living-Wage Calculator**

My favorite baseline-providing site isn’t even intended for
personal-finance use.  It’s [the MIT Living-Wage Calculator](http://livingwage.mit.edu/).  I recognize that the idea of a living wage is
freighted with all sorts of political baggage, but for those of us who are
actually trying to make a living in a particular area of the country, data
about what it takes to make that living are extremely valuable.

Basically, when you go exploring around in the Living-Wage
Calculator, what you will find is not a calculator, but a whole bunch of
tables—a table for every metropolitan statistical area and county in the
country, in fact (I would tend to look for county data because counties are smaller than or the same size as MSA’s). 
Navigating by state, you can easily find the table that best applies to
your location, and it will tell you what families of various sizes in your
location will need to make ends meet.

The Living-Wage Calculator arrives at this number by
estimating expenses in seven different categories:  Food, Child Care, Medical, Housing,
Transportation, Annual Taxes, and Other. 
You can find information about what comprises these categories and how
the relevant numbers were arrived at [here](http://livingwage.mit.edu/resources/Living-Wage-User-Guide-and-Technical-Notes-2016.pdf).

**The Living-Wage
Calculator and My Family**

Just to give you a for-instance, I live in the state of
Illinois, in Will County.  I am part of a
family of four containing two adults and two children, and I am the only adult
who earns income.  Here, then, are the
numbers in the column “2 Adults (1 Working), 2 Children”:

Food:  $9079

Child Care:  $0 (the
Living-Wage Calculator assumes that a non-working adult watches kids)

Medical:  $5543

Housing:  $14,112

Transportation: 
$11,460

Annual Taxes:  $8939

Other:  $6260

Required Annual Income Before Taxes:  $54,512

This is a frugal standard of living.  It doesn’t include categories for things like
vacations and restaurant dining.  It
doesn’t allow for saving for retirement. 
There is no space for charitable giving, especially, from a Christian
perspective, for giving to the Lord’s work. 
However, somebody who is bringing in that much income can, if they are
disciplined and don’t suffer a financial crisis (divorce, serious illness,
etc.), continue to subsist indefinitely.

**What We Can Learn**

Such a baseline is useful for a number of different
reasons.  First, it helps us to spot
places where our expenditures differ from the average—if we know what we’re
spending.  The “Housing” number above
assumes the cost of renting a two-bedroom apartment (for a family of four) plus
utilities.  Thanks to the intricacies of
preacher taxation, I have to track the money I spend on housing every year.  Not surprisingly, since I own a three-bedroom
house instead of renting a two-bedroom apartment, I spend considerably more on
housing than the baseline.  If I want my
budget to balance, I need more money than the required annual income too.

Second, it calls us to realism.  At the moment, I own two paid-for vehicles,
so my required yearly outlay on transportation is much less than $11,460.  However, the day will inevitably come when
both of my current vehicles expire. 
Unless I’ve been saving in the interim (which will bring my
transportation expenses much closer to $11,460), I’ll have to take on a car
payment, which is basically a finance company charging you for the privilege of
making you be disciplined about money.

Third, if we’re having financial problems, it tells us what
the problems actually are.  If a head of
a family of four in Will County, IL is bringing home $85,000 a year and still
lurches from crisis to crisis, he’s got a spending issue.  He can stabilize if he is willing to make
enough painful choices.

However, if the same head of a family of four in Will
County, IL is bringing in $40,000 a year, he can look at those numbers and see
that he’s got a different problem.  It’s
possible to trim them some, but not much.  For instance, somebody might say, “I just
won’t buy health insurance!”, but sooner or later, that’s going to be an
expensive decision.  You can’t really
dodge medical expenses; you can only defer them into a bankruptcy-inducing
catastrophe.  This is why insurance premiums
are what they are.

Instead, if he wants to make ends meet, Mr. Head of
Household is going to have to make some structural changes.  First, he might send his wife to work.  Note, though, that if he does, she’d better
be making more than $16,486.  Due mainly
to childcare costs, the living wage for a family of four with two working
parents in Will County, IL is $71,358 (this goes down considerably if you can
coerce Grandma or some other hapless family member into watching your kids for
free).

Second, he might recognize that he needs to start on a new
career, though often there are costs associated with this.  Third, he might decide that he needs to move
someplace where it’s cheaper to live.  Sadly, though, places with lower costs of
living also tend to offer lower-paying work.  In any event, people who aren’t bringing in enough money benefit simply
from seeing that they aren’t, so that they don’t spend the rest of their lives
in a series of vain attempts to rob Peter to pay Paul.

**Conclusion**

They call economics the dismal science, and for many of us,
reflecting on how much money we need to sustain ourselves in our society is a
dismal experience!  However, the
Living-Wage Calculator does us the very great service of freeing us from
uncertainty.  I know people who exist in
a financial fog.  Their money situation
never seems to be working for them, but they have no idea why.  If we’re willing to pay attention, a
statistical baseline will dispel the mist and allow us to confront the truth
about ourselves.
