During my time in the Bible-geek community, I’ve noticed
that most of the other members appear to have much more money available to
spend on Bibles than I do.  I certainly
don’t mean to complain about this in either direction.  I am comfortable in my own situation, and I
am glad the Lord has seen fit to bless them! 
However, it does mean that I have to weigh my Bible-purchasing decisions
with a little more care, and I’m always on the alert for ways to get what I
want for less.

I scented such an opportunity in the middle of last year
when Mark Bertrand posted on Bible Design Blog about his visit to a leather
store in Springfield, MO, which bore the less-than-astonishing name of
Springfield Leather.  He took advantage
of the trip to have a new cover made for one of his fancy notebooks.

I’m generally more of a back-of-old-sermons-as-scratch-paper
guy, so fancy notebooks don’t have a great deal of appeal for me.  However, in my many, many visits to the
Leonard’s Books site, I had noticed that they offered to rebind Bibles using
leather supplied by the customer for considerably less than a rebind using
Leonard’s leather would cost.  As of June
2015, they charge $55 for such an own-leather rebind.

Of course, I don’t have any leather suitable for Bible
rebinding just sitting around (unless I decide to skin one of my children), but
the thought crossed my mind that maybe Springfield Leather could help me out
with that.  I wandered over to
springfieldleather.com and had myself a looksee.

The first kind of leather that commended itself was
kidskin.  After all, if goatskin makes
such a great Bible cover, surely *baby* goatskin
would be even better, right?  I floated
the idea to Margie at Leonard’s, who in turn referred me to Eric.  He regretfully informed me that kidskin is
generally either too thin or too stiff to be useful in bookbinding.  Scratch that idea.

I returned to the Springfield Leather catalog, and the next
leather that caught my eye was some matte-black calfskin.  It was 2 ½ to 3 oz. in weight, and at the
time, it retailed for $4.99 a square foot (the price has since increased to
$5.49).  I asked the Leonard’s folks to
take a look at it for me, and they agreed that it looked suitable for a Bible
rebind.

Once I had accumulated the necessary money several months
later, I set my scheme in motion.  I
placed my order with Springfield Leather and had them ship the leather directly
to Leonard’s for me (Leonard’s was willing to take receipt of it so long as it
was identified as belonging to me).  I
mailed the ASV to Leonard’s likewise, and after the usual exchange of e-mails,
got my magnificently rebound Bible back. 
To be quite honest, I was astonished at how smoothly the process
went.  Generally, whenever I try anything
that complicated, it falls apart on me somewhere along the line.

My costs for the entire Bible restoration process were:

2 square feet of calfskin from Springfield Leather:              $10

Shipping the calfskin to Leonard’s:                                      $5

Own-leather rebind by Leonard’s:                                        $55

Torn-page repair by Leonard’s:                                            $3

New flyleaves, tightening the sewing, etc.:                          $4

Total:                                                                                     $77



This is, of course, in addition to the usual $20-ish cost of
shipping a Bible to and from Leonard’s. 
Even with shipping included, the whole project only cost me somewhere
around $100, and I’ve never been able to find a rebinder who would rebind for
me in his own calfskin that cheaply.  My
ASV went from being useless as a Bible to being useful *and* looking like a work of art, and in my book, that is money well
spent!
