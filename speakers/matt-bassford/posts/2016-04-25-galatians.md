A couple weeks back, I fielded a question about the argument
that because baptism is a work, it isn’t necessary for salvation.  In my reply, I noted that one of the problems
with that argument is that it represents a fundamental misreading of Scripture.  Because Luther wasn’t reading Romans and Galatians
with an eye to what they were actually about, he missed the point.

Today, if we focus too much on the individual verses of the
New Testament rather than its overall arguments, we can easily make the same
kind of mistake.  We can focus so much on
a sentence or a phrase that seems to say what we want it to that we fail to
appreciate its context, which may well point in a completely different
direction.  This morning, then, let’s
zoom out from the trees and spend some time pondering the forest.  Let’s consider what Paul was actually saying
in one of the books Luther misread, the book of Galatians.

 **The Gospel of Christ**

Galatians is written to the churches of, not surprisingly,
Galatia, a Roman province in the interior of modern-day Turkey.  I’m in the minority on this one, but I think
Galatians is the earliest of all Paul’s letters, written sometime in the late
40’s AD, less than 20 years after the crucifixion.

The first two chapters of the book are concerned with
different aspects of the gospel of Christ. 
Paul begins by describing **THE
REVELATION OF THE GOSPEL** to him. 
This description appears in Galatians 1. 
Once Paul moves out of the opening greetings of the book, we see him
pronouncing a strong condemnation.  He
says that some are luring the Galatians away with a different gospel than the
one that he had preached to them, and that these people who are preaching the
different gospel are to be accursed. 
Wow, that’s harsh!

However, Paul backs up this harsh language with the key
point of the first chapter, which appears in v. 12.  Rejecting Paul’s gospel in favor of a
different gospel is a huge problem because Paul was taught his gospel directly
by Jesus Christ, through revelation. 
When the Galatians are turning away from Paul, they’re really turning
away from Jesus Himself.

Paul supports his claim by describing his career.  Before he became a Christian, he was a famous
persecutor of the church, not exactly a situation in which he would have
received instruction in the gospel! 
Likewise, after his conversion, he never spent more than a couple weeks
in Jerusalem.  It was plainly impossible
for him to have learned everything he taught from the apostles in such a short
time.  Therefore, he had to have gotten
his information straight from the source. 
Even today, this is important to us. 
It shows that we’re relying on firsthand information, the truth about
Jesus, and not some hazy oral tradition.

Because Paul knew that he had the truth, he showed little
hesitation in **DEFENDING THE GOSPEL**.  Look at the shocking narrative of Galatians
2.  This begins with the story of Paul’s
second visit to Jerusalem, which I think we can identify as the famine relief
expedition of the last part of Acts 11. 
While he was there, he explained his divinely inspired plans to go
spread the gospel among the Gentiles, the plans that would lead to the first
missionary journey.  At this point,
Peter, James the Lord’s brother, and John endorse Paul’s plans.  They don’t say that the Christian converts
Paul hopes to make from the Gentiles have to become Jews too.

However, when Peter comes to Antioch, which I understand as
happening at the very beginning of Acts 15, suddenly the story becomes very
different.  Peter is concerned about the
party of the circumcision, those Jewish Christians who believe that Gentile
Christians must be circumcised and keep the entire Law of Moses.  He doesn’t want to offend them, so he refuses
to associate with Gentile Christians, just as ordinary Jews would refuse to associate
with ordinary Gentiles.

Paul lets him have it with both barrels in v. 14, the key
verse of this chapter.  Peter isn’t even
keeping the Law himself anymore, yet he is hypocritically demanding that
Gentile Christians keep it.  Worse still,
he does this even though it is faith in Christ, rather than the works of the
Law, that saves.  Here is the real
faith-versus-works debate in the New Testament. 
It’s not about mental assent versus doing stuff.  It’s about trust in Jesus versus keeping the
Law of Moses.

In just a few moments, we’re going to be partaking of the
Lord’s Supper, and before we do, let’s reflect on Paul’s words here.  He emphasizes the depth of the transformation
that he has undergone by describing himself as having been crucified with Christ.  The old Saul of Tarsus is dead.  Now he lives a new life, a life made possible
by the loving sacrifice of Jesus.  He has
slammed the door on what he used to be. 
Today, Jesus has done as much for us. 
Have we slammed the door on the worldly people we used to be, or do
those things keep creeping in?  Let’s
think as we partake.

**Faith in Christ**

The second main heading of Galatians addresses faith in
Christ.  It begins by outlining that key
Pauline doctrine, **JUSTIFICATION BY
FAITH.** Now, let’s read Galatians 3
together.  The main figure in this
chapter is the patriarch Abraham.  For
Paul’s purposes, Genesis says two important things about him.  The first is that according to the Scripture,
his faith was reckoned to him as righteousness. 
Even though he wasn’t righteous, because he believed in God’s power to
justify, God counted him as righteous. 
Second, God made a promise to Abraham that through his seed all the
nations of the earth would be blessed, a promise fulfilled in Christ.

Next, Paul describes the ineffectiveness of the Law of Moses.  It couldn’t bring a blessing.  It could only bring a curse.  Rather than justifying, it could only
condemn.  Also, because the Law came
after Abraham, it couldn’t nullify the promise that had been made to him, a
promise of blessing apart from the Law.  God
handed down the Law not to justify, but to serve as a stopgap until the time
when the promise to Abraham was fulfilled. 
As our key verse, v. 24, says, the Law was basically a glorified
crossing guard to keep God’s people safe until it could hand them off to
Christ.  After that, it had fulfilled its
function and ceased.

The Galatians, then, shouldn’t have worried about keeping
the dead letter of the Law of Moses. 
Today, we shouldn’t be worried about it either.  For instance, people often argue for the use
of instrumental music in worship by pointing to instrumental worship under the
Law of Moses, but brethren, if we’re not under the Law, we’re not under the
Law.  Moses has nothing to do with us.

Because all Christians are justified by faith in Christ,
Paul warns the Galatians against **DEPARTING
THE FAITH.** This shows up in
Galatians 4.  Here’s Paul’s concern:  Even though Paul brought Christ to the
Galatians, making them adopted children of God, the Galatians are showing a
deadly fascination with the outmoded crossing guard of the Law.  As Paul says in v. 9, they’re leaving God
behind for a series of rules that never saved anybody.  This is all the more distressing because of how
enthusiastic the Galatians had been when Paul preached the gospel to them the
first time, even though he had some kind of disgusting illness.  He simply doesn’t understand why they’re
turning away from him now.

He then tries to get them to understand what they’re doing
through an allegory about two of Abraham’s flesh-and blood sons.  Paul compares the Jews to Ishmael, the son of
the slave girl Hagar.  Christians, by
contrast, are like Isaac, the son of the free woman Sarah.  Even though Ishmael was older, just as
Judaism was an older religion, it was Isaac who inherited the promise.  In the same way, the promise today is through
Christ, not through Moses.  Basically, in
listening to Judaizing false teachers, the Galatians are exchanging heavenly
freedom for earthly slavery.

**Belonging to Christ**

Paul rounds out the epistle by discussing the implications
of belonging to Christ or not.  The first
part of this concerns **WALKING BY THE
SPIRIT.** Let’s see how this unfolds
in Galatians 5.  First, Paul points out
that if the Galatians choose to be circumcised, they are choosing slavery over
freedom.  They are bringing the entire
Law down on their heads, and it’s going to ruin them.

However, Paul then makes the countervailing point that
freedom from the Law of Moses does not mean lawlessness.  Instead, in our key verse for the chapter, v.
13, he notes that they’re supposed to use their freedom in Christ for service
and expressions of love.  God did not
free us so we could do evil with no consequences.  He freed us so we could be righteous.

He then compares the life of lawlessness to the life of
service to Christ.  We can be among those
who pursue the deeds of the flesh, or we can be among those who bear the fruit
of the spirit.  If we really belong to
Jesus, there can be no question as to which of those two groups we belong to.

Even though we don’t struggle with Judaizing false teachers,
precisely these same things are true of the genuine disciple of Jesus
today.  Christ does not call us to be
worldly.  He does not call us to run
secretly after all the pleasures of the flesh all week long and then show up
here looking fine in our Sunday best. 
That’s not grace.  That’s
hypocrisy.  Instead, He calls us to bear
the same fruit in our lives that He bore. 
We won’t be perfect like He was, but every one of us can be faithful.

The final section of Galatians is about **SOWING AND REAPING.** Look at
Galatians 6.  Paul begins here by
emphasizing the danger of self-deception. 
The devil is constantly at work. 
He may well succeed at luring a brother or sister away from Christ, and
if we try to yank them back into the fold, he will get us too.  We prove our spiritual worth by our
willingness to help one another, but in the end, every one of us is going to
stand in judgment without help from anyone but Jesus.

The basic principle here is simple.  You reap what you sow, which shows up in v.
8, our key verse for this chapter.  If we
aren’t thinking about tomorrow, if we aren’t thinking about the consequences of
our actions, any earthly happiness we enjoy is nothing more the devil fattening
us up for the slaughter.  A selfish life
will lead to an eternity spent by ourselves, without God.  Sure, it’s hard to push all the time.  It’s hard to live for others, but that’s the
only life that leads to heaven.

Paul explains the difference by comparing the Judaizing
false teachers to himself.  They’re
trying to lure the Galatians to follow the Law of Moses so that they can thump
their chests about what wonderful teachers they are.  However, Paul isn’t interested in promoting
himself.  He’s only interested in
promoting Jesus.  It’s striking that
today, none of us even know those proud false teachers’ names, but everybody
knows who Paul is.  In the end, God will
exalt His own.
