For the past several weeks, Zoë and Sam Meyer have been in
the same karate class on Monday evenings. 
Because parents are required to remain in the building during lessons,
both Brian and I have also been in attendance, and we’ve had an enjoyable time
visiting for the hour.

During the last session, though, Brian asked me a very
interesting question.  If you’ll recall,
last Sunday I preached, among other things, that a church that follows the
first-century pattern will be evangelistically dynamic.  Brian wanted to know what that looked like in
practice.  I suggested a few things in
the gym Monday night, but when I got home, I realized that I had much more to
say, and that the answer would be relevant to everybody here.  I now had a sermon topic!  Let’s look, then, at part of the Bible’s
answer, which we can find in its description of Acts 2 evangelism.



**Preparing the Soil**

There are, of course, many, many lessons to draw from Acts
2, but rather than delving into any of those worthy subjects, I want to begin
with one subject in particular.  What did
the early Christians do to prepare the soil for the first gospel sermon?

The first thing that we see the apostles doing is **STARTING WITH THE SPIRIT.** Here, look at Acts 2:1-4.  This, of course, is one of the most famous
events in the New Testament.  The
apostles are baptized with the Holy Spirit and begin to speak in foreign
languages that they had never learned.

Today, we cannot expect to receive any miraculous spiritual
gifts, but neither should we overlook the help that the Holy Spirit gives to
us.  This help begins with the word of
God.  Just as the Spirit spoke through
the apostles on the day of Pentecost 2000 years ago, today, God speaks through
His word.  We must be people who listen
intently to His message, not only by hearing it when we assemble here once or
twice a week, but by opening the book every day of our lives.  When we get up in the morning, why not get
things started with the Bible and a cup of coffee?  When we get home from work, why not flip open
the Scriptures instead of flipping on the TV? 
The Bible is God’s gift to us. 
Let’s make sure that we’re taking full advantage of it.

If we do so, we will find that through the word, the Spirit
begins to work in us.  We simply can’t
spend that much time with the Scriptures without having it change our lives and
our attitudes toward evangelism.  After
all, God’s love for the sinner is the great theme of the Bible.  The more time we spend with the Bible, the
more we will make that love for the lost our own.

Second, we see the apostles **SPEAKING OUT.** Consider the
account of Acts 2:5-11.  Remember,
brethren:  when the apostles received the
Spirit, it didn’t make them puppets.  They
could choose to use their gifts or not. 
They were out on the street on the day of Pentecost because they chose
to be there, because the good news about Jesus was too good to hide.

This is the nearly invariable pattern of the New
Testament.  The gospel is hardly ever
preached by a single Christian on his own. 
Instead, we almost always see a group of Christians working together to
proclaim the word.  Peter had the other
apostles.  Paul had his companions.  Sure, one guy often had the headline role, but
everybody on the team was involved in the work.

So too here, friends. 
For the gospel to have its fullest effect, we’re going to have to make
it a group effort.  As with the disciples
in Acts 1, if a substantial number of the brethren here will be committed to
and vocal about evangelism, that will lay the foundation for success.

OK, so who should be in that number?  To be honest, that question reminds me of
“Who is my neighbor?”  We become
someone’s neighbor when we decide to be, and we become fishers of men when we
decide to be.  Great knowledge is not
required.  The truth is that most
Christians here probably know more about the gospel than the apostles on
Pentecost did.  After all, we have the
completed revelation, and they didn’t. 
However, they did know that Christ had risen, and they told everybody
they could about that.  If we know the
same, we can do the same.

Third, let’s pay attention to the way that the other
apostles **SET THE STAGE** for
Peter.  Look at this in Acts 2:12-14.  Notice that by the early part of this
reading, even though the apostles had faithfully repeated the mighty deeds of
God, they hadn’t made any converts.  Some
of the multitude were confused; others thought the apostles were drunk.  However, the apostles had accomplished something
very important.  They had pulled together
a crowd that was going to be willing to listen to what Peter had to say.

This is where I come in. 
As Brian observed on Monday, the Joliet church invests in evangelism by
supporting an evangelist.  However, any
evangelist is going to be most effective when he is working with the church,
not working for the church, and that’s exactly what I want to do.  The power is always God’s and not mine, but
it is true that so far this year, I have helped sinners on to six baptisms and
one restoration.  However, I wish with
all my heart that those numbers were 60 and 10, not six and one, and I can’t
get there without all of your help.

In particular, there are three things that I’d love to see
Christians here doing to help me.  The
first is inviting friends to services.  I
urge you to do this not because I’m convinced that I’m an amazing speaker, but
because I am convinced that the gospel I preach is an amazing gospel.  It has the power to pierce hearts no matter
how limited the guy up front may be. 
When we bring our friends and neighbors to services, we give the gospel
the opportunity to do its work.

Second, in a little under a month here, we’re finally going
to get a community Bible study off the ground. 
Right now, we’re looking at holding that study at the Minooka library on
the last Tuesday of the month at 7 in the evening, and Lord willing, we’ll
start things off on October 25th. 
If you have a friend who is leery of the whole church thing but might be
willing to learn in a more relaxed setting, invite them to that.

Third, if neither of those options work, I’m willing to
study the Bible with anyone, anywhere, any time, and on any subject.  If you’ve got a neighbor or co-worker who
might be interested but lives far away from Joliet, set up a study in your
home.  I’ll come, and I’ll tell them
about whatever they want to know.  Of
course, this list is not exhaustive.  Any
way you think I can help you reach the lost, talk to me about it, and I’ll see
what I can do.

**Peter’s Sermon**

In the second part of our study together this morning, we’re
going to examine Peter’s sermon from the perspective both of its meaning for us
and its meaning for the lost.  After his
introduction, Peter really gets things going by turning to **JOEL’S PROPHECY**.  This is
recorded for us in Acts 2:15-21.  As we
saw a few moments earlier, the display of speaking in tongues by the apostles
left the crowds confused.  Peter
addresses this confusion by pointing out that the strange events of the day are
actually a fulfillment of something Joel had written hundreds of years
before.  God did in fact pour forth of
His Spirit on His people, and their prophesying proved the point.

However, the most important part of the quotation for
Peter’s argument isn’t the beginning. 
It’s the end.  Once his audience
accepts that the spectacle is actually a fulfillment of prophecy, there’s
something else that they must accept. 
According to Joel, God wouldn’t pour forth of His Spirit just for
fun.  Instead, He would do it to prove
something:  that anyone who called on the
name of the Lord would be saved.

That sounds really important, but its meaning is still
unclear.  In fact, it leaves three main
questions unanswered.  Who is the Lord,
what does it mean to call on Him, and from what will those who call on His name
be saved?  Peter is well aware of this,
and he’s going to spend the rest of his sermon answering these three questions,
especially the first one.  The bulk of
his sermon is going to be a logical examination of the evidence that the saving
Lord is Jesus.

Indeed, the next two pieces of evidence that Peter
introduces concern **THE RESURRECTION**
of Jesus.  They appear in Acts
2:22-28.  The first item is the fact of
the resurrection itself.  Peter here
gives a thumbnail sketch of the life of Jesus. 
As he observes, everybody in the crowd knows perfectly well that Jesus
was a prophet.  They also know perfectly
well that He was taken by His enemies and crucified.

However, from there, Peter gets provocative.  He reveals first of all that it was actually
according to God’s plan that His prophet was killed.  Yes, the Jews and the Romans did the deed,
but they were doing God’s will all along. 
Second, it wasn’t merely God’s plan that Jesus would die.  It was also His plan that Jesus would be
raised from the dead, which is exactly what He did.

This is shocking stuff! 
Too often, we think of people two thousand years ago as a bunch of rubes
who would believe whatever outlandish story they were told.  However, that’s not the picture that the
Bible paints of them.  Instead, we see
them reacting in much the same way as we would. 
When told of some supernatural event, they treat it with skepticism
until convinced otherwise.

They’re going to need some proof even of the idea of resurrection,
so in search of this proof, Peter turns to Psalm 16.  David is the author, and in the psalm, he
says some pretty strange things.  The
Jews knew as we do that sooner or later, all of our souls go to Hades, the
abode of the dead, and all of our bodies undergo decay.  That’s a grim fact of life.  However, David here clearly expresses
somebody’s confidence that he won’t be abandoned to Hades and that his body
won’t undergo decay.  David thought that
death wasn’t inevitable and that resurrection was possible.

Along with the earlier display of prophetic power, Peter
then uses these two pieces of evidence to construct **A TRIPLE PROOF** that Jesus is Lord. 
This emerges in Acts 2:29-36. 
Peter starts with Psalm 16.  He
argues that it can’t be about David himself because David did die and is still
dead.  Instead, David is predicting the
future.  He’s anticipating what will
happen to the Messiah, who will be one of his descendants.  As a result, if Jesus rose from the dead, His
claim to be the Christ is immeasurably strengthened because that’s what the
Christ is supposed to do.

The second part of the triple proof is the eyewitness
testimony of the apostles.  The closest
followers of Jesus, they abandoned Him to die and spent the Sabbath in deep
despair.  Now, though, they’re convinced
that Jesus is the Christ, and they’re convinced of this because they saw Him
after He rose from the dead.  What David
predicted, the apostles beheld.

The third proof that Jesus is Lord is the miraculous
spiritual gifts of the apostles.  If they
just said, “This dead guy came back to life,” the Jews would brush them off,
just as we would.  However, if the
apostles say, “This dead guy came back to life, and He gave me the ability to
speak in dozens of different languages,” and then the apostles do it, that’s a
whole lot harder to dismiss!

Our triple proof, then, is prophecy plus testimony plus
miracle, and together it leads to the conclusion of v. 36:  the Jesus whom the Jews crucified has now
been made both Lord and Christ by God. 
Now the Jews know who the Lord is, so they’ve got an awfully good idea
what they need to be saved from now too. 
They just killed the Son of God, and He’s not likely to be happy about
it!  In the space of about 15 verses,
Peter answers two of the three Joel 2 questions.

Before the Lord’s Supper, let’s reflect on all this.  The same triple proof that convinced the
crowds on the day of Pentecost ought to work on us too.  Prophecy plus testimony plus miracle,
faithfully recorded for us in the word, show us that Jesus is our Lord and
Christ too.  On the cross, He died for
us, but now He lives and reigns for us. 
Let’s remember this as we partake.

**Reaping the Harvest**

The last section of Acts 2 describes the way that Peter and
the early church reap the harvest the gospel had prepared.  This begins with Peter **PIERCING THE HEART** in Acts 2:37-41. 
In context, the Jews’ reaction is entirely understandable.  They are convinced they’ve killed the Christ,
so they want to know what they can do to get out from under divine
vengeance.  In response, Peter tells them
that they can be saved by repenting and being baptized, which is how they call
on the name of the Lord.  This will
rescue them not only from the blood guilt of killing Jesus, but also from the
fate about to befall the wicked people around them and even from all the sins
they have committed.  This is quite a
promise!

I suspect that almost everybody here has heard a sermon on
the importance of Acts 2:38 before.  In
the churches of Christ, we say, and rightly so, that Peter’s words here
establish a pattern that we must follow if we want to be saved from our
sins.  Of course, not everything in the
text squares up.  None of us are personally
responsible for killing Jesus, for instance. 
However, we still recognize that because Jesus is Lord, we must call on
Him if we want to be saved.

However, brethren, we can’t grab out v. 38 from Acts 2 and
quit there.  If it is right for us to
follow the command of Peter, it is also right for us to follow the example of
the apostles who so boldly taught the lost.

Again, not everything squares up.  They were apostles, for one thing, and we
aren’t.  However, we can’t let the
details obscure the basic truth.  If the
Bible teaches that calling on the name of the Lord is the only path to
salvation, we must make sure that as many people as possible hear the gospel,
even if they don’t obey it.

The final section of Acts 2 describes the **SHARED DEVOTION** of the church.  Look at Acts 2:42-47.  In this text, the emphasis is definitely on
the sharing.  It reports that the
Jerusalem church shared two things in particular:  their worship and their lives.

The application of this closeness to evangelism might not be
immediately obvious, but it is profound. 
After all, it doesn’t do the kingdom any good if we baptize people but
they all fall away immediately.  Nor is
this some hypothetical problem.  Of the
six people I’ve baptized this year, two of them are already gone, and one of
the two didn’t last a week.

The New-Testament way to solve this problem is twofold.  The first is an emphasis on assembly, and
this emphasis has to come not just from me, but from the whole church.  We need to emphasize to one another that
anytime it is physically possible for us to be here, here is where we need to
be, every Sunday, every Wednesday.  By
contrast, the less a Christian assembles, the more vulnerable he is going to be
to the deception of the devil.

Second, we need to associate with one another outside of the
assembly and take particular care to include new and struggling
Christians.  I’ve had more than one
person say to me recently that they think this is a weak spot for the
congregation, that we simply don’t associate with one another as much as we
used to, and I have to agree.  I’m afraid
that many of us are so wrapped up on our own lives that we don’t have time for
one another.  Friends, that has to
change.  Let’s consider our homes, our
calendars, and our hearts to figure out how we can include one another.
