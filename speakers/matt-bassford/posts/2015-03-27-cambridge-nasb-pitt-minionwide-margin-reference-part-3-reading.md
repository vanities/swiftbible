**OVERVIEW.** Both the Pitt Minion and the Wide-Margin
Reference have quite a lot to offer as readers. 
I prefer their double-column paragraphed layout in a reading Bible, and
their red-letter format is well executed.  
The setting is largely, though not completely, line-matched, and a
Jongbloed-printed Bible is always a pleasure to read.



\*             \*             \*

As always, both of these Bibles spent two weeks on my desk
at home, where I go early in the morning to carry out my Bible reading.  The reading light there isn’t ideal, but it’s
good enough to determine a Bible’s quality as a reader.  Here’s what I thought:

**SIZE.** These are two verrry different Bibles in
the hand.  As always, the Pitt feels
paperback-like, which makes reading it an effortless experience.  The Wide-Margin has considerably more heft,
like one of those ponderous history books I like to read (you can tell how
smart the author is by how many pounds the book weighs!).  However, it doesn’t read at all like one of
them.  Even after two weeks of use, the
calf-split cover droops down significantly, so that as I read, I turn the Bible
in my hand to consider each column in turn. 
This isn’t a problem, though; it’s just different.

**LAYOUT.** Normally, I don’t care for reading from
center-column-reference Bibles.  The
problem is the reference column.  It
steals space from the text column on either side of it, so that both are
narrower than they would be in a pure text Bible.

Usually, the narrow drives the characters-per-line count
down into the thirties, and this creates two problems.  First, I read most quickly and easily from
Bibles with a cpl of around 40, so a 32-cpl Bible will slow me down, even in
prose.  Second, the narrow columns really
do a number on versified poetry in the Old Testament.  Because the poetic lines can’t fit on a
single line of text, the layout inevitably contains a number of one-word
“widow” overhangs.  Those are ugly, slow
down reading, and interfere with comprehension.

However, the Cambridge settings for all Pitt Minion and
Wide-Margin Reference Bibles are a glorious exception to the rule.  I don’t know whether Cambridge or Blue Heron
Bookcraft (the typesetter for the series) came up with the idea, but some
clever soul solved the center-column-reference idea by widening the
setting.  Most Bibles share the
proportions of a half-sheet of copier paper: 
5 ½“ x 8 ½“.  That’s the classic
“book shape”, with a length-width ratio of about 1.55.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEjFC1whDUmvZ1EFoBJ4uzrX_AgbP9TljxYwfVNDhC_WPLNvE7si929bBf3tzD3RQU4yUsWPvcP5fRA8_Lfa1AK5CWjPJ9S1fs8vUqhNuJQZxCYRePq1Fpsk5ifGhIfU-3zJbHnj2QvtRw/s1600/R3.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEjFC1whDUmvZ1EFoBJ4uzrX_AgbP9TljxYwfVNDhC_WPLNvE7si929bBf3tzD3RQU4yUsWPvcP5fRA8_Lfa1AK5CWjPJ9S1fs8vUqhNuJQZxCYRePq1Fpsk5ifGhIfU-3zJbHnj2QvtRw/s1600/R3.jpg)



Not so with the Pitt Minion. 
At 4 ¾“ x 6 ⅞“, it is much wider for its height than the norm, with a
length-width ratio of 1.45.  This means
that it has room for text columns as wide as they would be in a text Bible,
plus a column of references.  Even though
the Wide-Margin doesn’t share those dimensions (its dimensions being as much
determined by its acres of marginal white space), it still shares the setting
that they allow.  In the words of the
Pontiac ad campaign from about 20 years ago, wider IS better!

As a result of this, both the Pitt and the Wide-Margin read
as smoothly as any double-column text Bible, with cpl counts in the low
forties.

Speaking of typography, kudos to Blue Heron for actually
using a small-cap typeface for the small-cap Old-Testament quotations in the
New Testament!  This is almost never
done.  Most Bible settings use
smaller-print capital letters for small caps. 
Those smaller-print capital letters have a lighter weight than the
lower-case letters surrounding them, so they don’t look quite right.  Blue Heron, by contrast, used a dedicated
small-cap typeface, with the result that the small-cap letters have the same
weight as the rest of the text and blend in smoothly.

Sadly, Blue Heron did not do this with “Lord” in the Old Testament, but I takes
what I can gets.

Neither Bible has a gutter problem.  The inner margins on the Wide-Margin are an
inch wide, and the Pitt’s inner margins are a quarter-inch, which is fine for
such a skinny Bible.  Little-known
fact:  thicker Bibles need wider inner
margins.

Yes, both the Pitt Minion and the Wide-Margin are red-letter
Bibles.  This is not my favorite, but
it’s not a terrible problem here.  Most
importantly, the red ink in both Bibles is a dark brick red rather than pink or
orange, and it does not vary in intensity from page to page, so it’s never
difficult to read.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgpBSGwB1RTtXHEPFA1_jO6OF5d1Nt5EcuLB8HHFFo8GJpYztgHSMLMwG88fgdDxluvs3rRNoz5y6eJhP1Wro4ZM8MyBvNSb0kgcFKHmSZcZihA-lJgH4Oi7n8w7IaaVCn-X5IXMIUTew/s1600/R5.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgpBSGwB1RTtXHEPFA1_jO6OF5d1Nt5EcuLB8HHFFo8GJpYztgHSMLMwG88fgdDxluvs3rRNoz5y6eJhP1Wro4ZM8MyBvNSb0kgcFKHmSZcZihA-lJgH4Oi7n8w7IaaVCn-X5IXMIUTew/s1600/R5.jpg)

However, the second nemesis of the red-letter Bible,
registration problems, are present to some extent.  The worst example I was able to find was on
p. 875 in the Pitt Minion.  The red ink
is set higher on the page than the black ink, so that red ascenders almost
touch black descenders.  It’s not a
serious problem, but it does show that even Jongbloed can’t perfectly execute a
red-letter Bible.  Honestly, that makes
me ask, “Isn’t it about time to stop trying?”

I don’t think the setting that both Bibles share was
designed with line-matching in mind, but due to other design decisions, both
Bibles ended up largely line-matched anyway. 
This is almost irrelevant in the case of the Wide-Margin with its opaque
38-gsm paper, but ghosting never bothered me even in the Pitt.

Finally, we come to the layout issues in which the Bibles
differ:  print size and margin.  The Wide-Margin is set in fairly standard 7.9
Lexicon No. 1.  The Pitt, however, is
unabashedly a small-print Bible, with the same setting shrunk down so that it
becomes 6.75 Lexicon No. 1.  I normally
don’t care for reading from small-print Bibles, but the combination of
unusually readable typeface (Lexicon No. 1 was specially designed to be readable
at small sizes) and crisp Jongbloed printing makes the Bible work as a
reader.  I can hold it about 8-9 inches
from my nose and read it without straining.

The Wide-Margin, on the other hand, offers the quirk of. . .
wide margins.  I have to say—those look
really odd in a Bible.  In a volume that
is governed by such brutal design constraints, all the white space seems almost
prodigal, like a man emptying his bank account to throw a giant party.  However, I can’t argue with the beauty of the
effect.  I think that in terms of sheer
artistry, the setting of the Wide-Margin nearly equals that of the Crossway
Legacy, which is the most beautiful Bible I know.

However, those wide margins will also prove the undoing of
the Wide-Margin as a reader for me.  I’ve
only preached two sermons out of the Bible, but I can tell already that I *hate* reading a context that I’ve marked
up that heavily.  It’s too unsubtle, like
one of those bloggers who uses a hard return after every sentence and
italicizes every other word in order to make his (generally pedestrian) writing
seem thoughtful and weighty and profound. 
I don’t need the red underlining to tell me that faith, love, and hope
are significant concepts in 1 Thessalonians 1:3.  It’s just annoying.

**PRODUCTION QUALITY.** Both the
Pitt Minion and the Wide-Margin Reference are Jongbloed Bibles, which means
that they are as well produced as a Bible can be.  The characters are magnificently crisp, the
print is even, and the paper has that perfect cream tint.  You’re not going to find a Jongbloed Bible on
the endcap at Family Christian for $15, but at least you get what you pay
for.  Of course, the paper in the Pitt is
not particularly opaque, but that puts it in the same category as every other
thinline ever made.  It just comes with
the territory in a carry Bible.

**CONCLUSION.** Wayyy back in 2013, my discontent with
the Crossway Legacy came to a head when I happened to do my morning reading out
of a Pitt Minion and found that I liked the Pitt better than the Legacy.  I’ve since come to realize, though, that my
preference is as much a compliment to the Pitt as a criticism of the
Legacy.  Even though it’s a little Bible,
it Just Works for me as a reader. 
Likewise for the same setting in the Wide-Margin.

However, I think there’s room for an even better Bible
here.  I would like to see Cambridge
reproduce the same setting in a full-size narrow-margin Bible.  The trim size for this Bible would be about 6
¼“ x 9”, and its typography would be about 8.8/9.2 Lexicon No. 1.  I think it would be one of the best readers
on the market for people who prefer double-column Bibles, and it would also be
an all-rounder candidate in a way that the Pitt Minion isn’t.  Besides, there are all kinds of people who
have “aged out” of being able to use their Pitts who would be grateful for an
upsized replacement.



However, I’m not going to allow the perfect to become the
enemy of the good.  On their own, the
Pitt Minion and Wide-Margin Reference deserve a 9/10 as reading Bibles, neither
being particularly better than the other. 
Overall, the duo scores at the rate of 27/30.
