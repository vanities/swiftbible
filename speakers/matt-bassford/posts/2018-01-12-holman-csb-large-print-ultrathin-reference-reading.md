**OVERVIEW.** I’m kinda on the multi-volume reading
Bible kick these days, but for a single-volume Bible with chapter and verse
numbers, the CSB performs admirably.

**INTRODUCTION.** As I noted earlier, I read the New
Testament in the LPUT.  That’s probably
enough to give me a sense of how the Bible works as a reader.  Here’s what I thought:



**SIZE:** Big, light, soft, and floppy like a hound
dog’s ears.  My copy of the LPUT is
lined-to-the-edge goatskin, and large-print thinline reference Bibles do better
with edge-lining than any other format. 
This thing hangs over my hand like a goatskin towel, and I find the
experience utterly seductive.  Sometimes,
I open the Bible and hold it, even though I don’t want to read anything out of
it, because it’s so enjoyable to hold. 
It’s also light enough that I don’t feel any need to brace it against my
chest.  Basically, the LPUT feels like a
hug in Bible form.

**LAYOUT.** In the previous segment of this review, I
mentioned that the narrow center column of the LPUT meant that it could have
wider text columns.  Here is where that
becomes extremely important.  Usually,
double-column reference Bibles don’t have great characters-per-line
counts.  They don’t land in my sweet spot
of 40-60 cpl.  The LPUT does, with an
average cpl count of about 41.

This is important generally, but it’s particularly important
in the poetic sections of the Bible. 
Bibles with cpl counts around 30 (not unusual for double-column
reference Bibles) tend to mangle the lines of Biblical poetry.  The LPUT thus starts off on the right foot
here.

From there, the 2K designers made (in my opinion) one bad
choice and one extremely good choice.  On
the debit side of the ledger, they decided to indent all the poetry in the
LPUT, which forfeits precious cpl space (though they don’t further indent after
that, except in the cases of line overflow). 
I don’t care for that.

However, they did
continue from the HCSB the glorious tradition of de-widowing the text.  In double-column Crossway Bibles, for
instance, if a poetic line fits on a single line of text except for one word or
even one syllable, that one word or syllable is placed on the next line by
itself.  These one-word lines are called
“widows”, and they’re terrible for reading fluency.

The LPUT, by contrast, eliminates widows by breaking lines
only at the level of the phrase.  For
example, the first half of Job 41:23 reads, “The folds of his flesh are joined
together”  In the ESV, this would be set
as:

The folds of his flesh are joined to-

-gether.

In the LPUT, it appears as:

The folds of his flesh

are joined together.

This is a vast improvement. 
It’s on a par with the CSB designers choosing to put each line of
dialogue in its own paragraph (as is the norm in novels) instead of the ESV
decision to put several exchanges of dialogue in the same great big
paragraph.  Each translation comes with
its own design decisions, and I think the CSB is probably the best-designed
translation out there.

The gutter in the LPUT, however, isn’t great.  This isn’t the fault of the setting, though;
the designers allotted about ⅜” for the inner margins, which should
be plenty for a thinline.  However,
because the Bible has such large, stiff tabs (great for durability, rotten for
opening flat), it doesn’t open very widely at the hinge (though the
super-droopy edge-lined cover mostly conceals this).  As a result, in the prose sections toward the
middle of the Bible (like in Isaiah 36-39), a few characters at the beginning
and ending of lines start sliding into the abyss.  This isn’t a killer like it is in the
Cambridge REB Standard Text Bible, but it’s noticeable, and that’s not a good
thing.

However, on the plus side, the LPUT is a line-matched
Bible.  This isn’t absolutely essential;
at 30 gsm, the paper in the LPUT is thicker than the 28-gsm paper in, say, the
Schuyler Caxton.  However, 30 gsm isn’t
exactly construction paper either, so this does help to make the LPUT more
readable.

Similarly beneficial is the designers’ decision to make the
LPUT black-letter.  No poorly inked,
badly registered words of Christ in red here! 
I know lots of people like red-letter Bibles, but I think the overall
effect is to detract from rather than to increase comprehension of the text.

Finally, the 9.5-point Bible Serif text in the LPUT is
extremely easy to read.  I can hold the
Bible pretty much wherever I want to and still read it just fine, even with my
glasses off.

**PRODUCTION
QUALITY.** The LPUT has a China-print
text block.  Five years ago, this would
have been a trouble sign.  Now, it’s a
matter of indifference.  The print is
uniformly dark throughout the Bible, there’s a clear distinction between
boldfaced and normal text, and there aren’t any annoying splatters.  Even the finest strokes in the
cross-references are as clear as they could possibly be.  The goatskin LPUT is Holman’s first foray (in
recent years, at least) into the premium-Bible market, and the text block is no
discredit to the cover.

**CONCLUSION.** Hinges on edge-lined Bibles are
hard.  Even high-premium publisher
Schuyler has received complaints because the hinges in the various Quentels
have sometimes proven to be too stiff. 
The hinge is the main flaw in the LPUT as a reader, but it’s an
understandable one.  As a result, I think
it’s fair to give the Bible an 8.5/10 as a reader.  Overall, it gets a 27/30, which is an
extremely high (and well-earned) score.
