Last week, I wrote about 1 Corinthians 14:15 and its call
for thoughtful worship.  I then explained
why the humble children’s song “Jesus Loves Me!” is written in such a way that
even adults can sing it mindfully.  At
the end, I proposed the “Jesus Loves Me!” test. 
If a hymn doesn’t have the structure, fresh writing, and appropriate
music of a song that a four-year-old can understand, it almost certainly isn’t
a hymn that engages the understanding of an adult.

Let’s begin with the first of these attributes:  logical structure.  As we saw last week, “Jesus Loves Me!” is a
logically structured hymn.  To put things
in the terminology of high-school English, it states a thesis, supports the
thesis, and follows the thesis up with a conclusion.  Every hymn we sing should likewise be well
structured.



**The Importance of
Structure**

This might seem like a dry topic, but logical structure is
important to the mental and even the emotional impact of a hymn.  For instance, as Craig Roberts observes, “We
Saw Thee Not” is a hymn that is emotionally powerful because of its logically
powerful structure.  Across verses, it
moves chronologically through Christ’s time on earth:  His life, His death, His resurrection, and
His ascension.

Each individual verse also follows a logical pattern:  “We saw Thee not. . .” (or a variation
thereof) at the beginning of each verse and “But we believe. . .” midway
through.  This repeated idea is stirring
in itself.  Its use of John 20:29
establishes us among the blessed.  It
also permits the music to be equally stirring and dramatic.  "We Saw Thee Not" is only one example of many.  The most evocative hymns in our repertoire
all get there by using good structure.

However, not all hymns have good structure.  Rather than engaging the mind and the heart
together, they prevent the union of the two. 
Rather than being stirred by a well-reasoned, emotionally resonant
argument, the thoughtful worshiper is confused by the lack of an apparent
point.

**Grant and His
Elephant**

We readily recognize the problems with lack of focus in
other forms of writing.  Consider, for
instance, a high-school history paper with the following thesis and outline:

Thesis:  The Civil War
was the result of a series of events that increased sectional tension between
the North and the South.

1. The Fugitive Slave Act
2. The *Dred Scott* decision
3. The presidential election of 1860
4. Ulysses Grant liked elephants.



I went to a pretty good high school, so I know what would
have happened if I had submitted a paper with that outline in AP American
History.  In the margin next to the
fourth point would have been the comment, “What in the world are you talking
about?!”, scrawled in red pen, and underlined. 
Twice.

If I had gone to Mrs. Wells-Morgan after class, asking her
to change my D+ to a B+ because Grant was a general in the Civil War, I would
have gotten nowhere.  His preference for
pachyderms, however relevant it might be to a paper about Grant himself, has
nothing to do with the causes of the Civil War. 
It just doesn’t.  To insist that
it does is logically fallacious, and no history paper with such an obvious non
sequitur can be a good history paper.

**“Sanctuary”**

With Grant and his elephant firmly in mind, let’s turn our
attention to the hymn “Sanctuary”.  If we
were to outline it, its structure might look like this:

1. Lord, make me into a sanctuary for You.
2. Lord, make us into a sanctuary for You.
3. You, Lord, sent Jesus for our land, and You refine us.



There are other problems with “Sanctuary”.  Some of the writing is goofy (“for our
land”?).  I’m not sure whether the second
half of the second verse is addressed to other Christians or to God.  If the latter, is the Father part of His own
sanctuary?  The authors toss in a couple
of clichés (“tried and true”, “heart and soul”), and they don’t seem to have a
good handle on the temple symbolism of the New Testament.  According to 1 Corinthians 6, we ourselves are
supposed to maintain the purity of our bodily temple.

However, it is the structure that is the elephant in the
hymn.  The first two verses are about
becoming a sanctuary for God (though I probably wouldn’t mix individual-temple
and corporate-temple imagery in the same hymn because of the potential for
confusion), but by the third verse, we’re no longer singing about sanctuaries
at all.  It’s an obvious non sequitur.

**Defenses for
“Sanctuary”**

To the charge of flawed structure, I’ve seen defenders of
“Sanctuary” make one of two replies.  The
first of these is that the third verse actually kind of does relate to the
first two.  I don’t see it.  It is true that “refined” and “pure” are
related concepts.  It is true that they
appear together in Malachi 3.  It’s true
that Grant was a general in the Civil War, too.

However, “refine” simply doesn’t work in a hymn about the temple.  One refines things by melting them down.  You start with some kind of ore and heat it
to burn off the dross.  “Refine” has to
do with raw materials, but a temple is a finished product.  Never does the Scripture speak of refining a
temple or any other building.  To be
blunt, the Holy Spirit is a better writer than that.  The use of “refine” in v. 3 of “Sanctuary”,
rather than being a connection to the first two verses, is a sign that the
author has lost focus.

The second approach is to acknowledge that “Sanctuary” has
problems, but to insist that it doesn’t matter. 
It’s good to sing praises to God, so as long as we’re singing
“Sanctuary” to God, it must be good, right?

This raises some troubling questions of its own.  Where do we get the idea that God is equally
pleased by everything we sing to Him?  As
we saw from our study of Colossians 3:16 and 1 Corinthians 14:15, that’s
certainly not what the Bible says.  God
doesn’t want every kind of song worship. 
He wants certain kinds of song worship, and we should seek to use only
hymns that meet His standards in our singing.

If my (thankfully hypothetical) history paper above was a D+
paper, “Sanctuary” is a D+ hymn.  It
doesn’t teach false doctrine (generally, to teach false doctrine, you have to
have focus and a point), but it’s structurally deficient.  It bombs the “Jesus Loves Me!” test.  The latter is far better structured.

Most of us weren’t D+ students in high-school history.  If we were, that was likely because we
fundamentally didn’t care.

Why are we more concerned about offering a D+ paper to a
history teacher than a D+ hymn to God?
