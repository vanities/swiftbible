A few days back, my brother, friend, and collaborator Tim
Berman posted a link to the home page of a new Bible translation, the
International English Bible (henceforth the IEB).  Even though I have some serious shortcomings
as a translation evaluator (like not being able to read Greek or Hebrew), I do
have experience with a number of different English translations, and I know the
Bible (again, in English) pretty well.  I
thought I’d take a look at the IEB and report what I found.

According to [the translation's website](http://www.iebible.net/#),
the translators’ goal was to produce a Bible written at a 4th-grade
reading level that still remained faithful to the original texts.  Generally, that’s a tough balance to
strike.  The more you simplify the
language of the Bible, the more you lose nuances.  The NLT, for instance, is easier to
understand than the NASB, but the former is not a translation I would use for
detailed doctrinal study.



The website explains that the translation was the product of
“a small core of Bible scholars”.  The
only one of these scholars named is Dr. Stanley Morris, about whom I know
nothing.  Another nine scholars are cited
as advisors to the translation committee.

Honestly, I find this a little alarming.  Even when they work with the best of
intentions, a single translator or small group of translators are likely to
incorporate their own biases into a translation whether they mean to or
not.  In order to address this problem,
most major translations employ dozens of different scholars from a wide variety
of backgrounds.  The NLT had multiple
scholars behind the translation of nearly every book of the Bible.  The ESV lists a review board of more than 50
experts in Greek and Hebrew.  The fact
that the IEB doesn’t have this robust scholarly backing raises my eyebrow (the
one on the right) a little bit.

I haven’t seen a copy of the full IEB, but the translators have
made the text of six books (Genesis, Psalms, Proverbs, John, Acts, and
Revelation) freely available on their site. 
I wish they had released Romans too (Romans is the best book of the
Bible for checking for doctrinal nuance. 
Do, for instance, the translators handle Romans 3:21-26 well?), but having
six books is certainly better than having none! 
I mainly confined my investigations to two books, Psalms and Acts.

In my reading of Acts 1, one of the first things that stood
out was its use of supplied words. 
Rather jarringly, the book begins “Dear Theophilus”, noting that “Dear”
is supplied by setting it in sans-serif font. 
Right off the bat, this shows us that this isn’t what most Christians
would think of as a “literal” translation. 
It aims to take a position somewhere between the formal-equivalence
strategy (generally word-for word translation) of the NASB and ESV on the one
hand and the dynamic-equivalence approach (generally thought-for-thought
translation) of the NLT (though even the NLT doesn’t open Acts by saying “Dear
Theophilus”).  I think there’s value to
both approaches, but it’s useful to be aware of which one a particular Bible is
adopting.

The IEB also attempts to enhance clarity by breaking up the
long sentences of the Greek manuscripts into much shorter English
sentences.  Every translation that I’m
aware of, with the ponderous exception of the ASV, does this to some extent,
but the IEB really loves to chop things up. 
Compare, for instance, Acts 1:6 in the NASB, NLT, and IEB respectively:

*So when they had come
together, they were asking Him, saying, "Lord, is it at this time You are
restoring the kingdom to Israel?"*

*So when the apostles
were with Jesus, they kept asking him, "Lord, has the time come for you to
free Israel and restore our kingdom?"*

*The apostles were all
together. They asked Jesus, “Lord, is this the time for**you to rebuild the
kingdom for Israel?”*

This pretty well captures the character of all three
translations.  The English of the NASB
feels a little stilted, but it more closely reflects the syntax of the Greek
original.  The NLT is clear,
straightforward English, but it changes up verb tenses and adds significant
interpretive glosses (“with Jesus”, “free Israel”).  The IEB doesn’t gloss as much here, but it
divides the verse into two sentences and (correctly) replaces the pronoun “they”
with its antecedent, “the apostles”.

Unlike the NASB or the NLT either one, the IEB makes an
effort to get rid of “church words”.  In
Acts 1:5 in the IEB, the apostles aren’t going to be baptized with the Holy Spirit.  They’re going to be immersed in the Holy
Spirit (a Bible-wide replacement that will make people in my religious
tradition rejoice and people in a number of denominations cry translator’s bias).  Similarly, in Acts 1:21-22, the new apostle
won’t be a witness of Jesus’ resurrection. 
He will be a witness that Jesus arose from death.  (with “from death” supplied by the
translator).

Another church word that gets the axe throughout Acts is “church”,
replaced with “congregation”, though I’m not sure that this is a change for the
simpler.  Generally, the approach reminds
me of *The Sacred Writings*, Alexander
Campbell’s 1826 translation of the New Testament, which makes me wonder if the
translators have some Restoration roots. 
Along the same lines, unlike many freer translations, the IEB is more
precise in its dealings with Acts 22:16 (rather than inserting a “by” before “calling”
to avoid the textual implication that calling on the name of the Lord and
baptism are the same thing, which is what the NLT and HCSB both do).

When it comes to gender neutrality, the IEB is. . .
odd.  Its translators appear to have
adopted the approach of replacing the generic “man” and “men” of the original
text with gender-neutral equivalents (at least part of the time) while
retaining masculine pronouns as generic pronouns.  For instance, Psalm 1:1-2 in the IEB reads, “Happy
is the person who does not listen to the evil people. He does not go where
sinners go. He does not do what bad people do.”

Sadly, this decision causes many of the same problems that befall
more completely gender-neutral translations. 
The IEB does get Genesis 22:18 right (at least from the perspective that
says Paul knows what he’s talking about in Galatians 3:16), but Psalm 8 is kind
of a mess.  Compare the text of Psalm 8:4
in the NASB and the IEB, respectively:

*What is man that You take thought of him,
And the son of man that You care for him?*

*But why is mortal man
so important to You?
Why do You care about human beings?*

In addition to not being nearly as pretty, the IEB version
wrecks the logic of Hebrews 2:5-8, where the writer applies the text of Psalm
8:4-6 to the very singular and very masculine Jesus Christ.  Without “son of man” in the original psalm,
this argument no longer makes sense.

Generally, though, I think the IEB has its uses.  I wouldn’t use it to try to prove to a
Presbyterian that he has to be immersed in water for the forgiveness of his
sins (that kind of translation-dependent argument doesn’t get GNT-wielding
Jehovah’s Witnesses very far, and I don’t think it would get me very far
either), but I think it would be good in the same areas where the NIrV and NLT
excel.  As far as I’m concerned, if you’re
not reading for nuance, the simpler, freer translations are better.  I probably wouldn’t preach out of the NIrV,
but my wife uses it all the time for morning devotions with our children.  We need translations to fill that niche too.

At the moment, though, the only full-Bible version of the
IEB that’s available is a study Bible. 
That makes me unhappy.  Generally,
I don’t like study Bibles.  I think they
put too much emphasis on one interpretation of the text by putting the interpretation
next to the text.  However, there is as
yet no way to get an interpretation-free copy of the IEB.  When one appears, I’m likely to pick it up.
