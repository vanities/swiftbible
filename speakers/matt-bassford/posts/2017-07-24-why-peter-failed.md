You don’t have to be a super-duper Biblical scholar to have
heard the story of how Peter denied Jesus three times.  All the details are familiar to us:  the fire where Peter was warming himself, the
three accusers, the crowing of the rooster, and Jesus’ final significant
look.  Of all the things that Peter does
in Scripture, this may well be the most famous.

However, if we only take a surface look at the events of the
story, pretty much the only lesson we’ll come away with is how badly Peter blew
it.  Honestly, that’s not terribly
helpful, and it won’t help any of us get any closer to heaven.  This morning, then, let’s dig below the surface
for lessons that will help us to keep from abandoning the Lord.  Let’s ask why Peter failed.



**Pre-Betrayal Problems**

When we consider the four gospel accounts, it quickly
becomes clear that Peter doesn’t fail out of nowhere.  Instead, his threefold denial is the result
of spiritual problems that were flourishing in him even before Judas betrays
the Lord into the hands of the chief priests. 
Chronologically, the first of these problems is **SELF-CENTEREDNESS.** Look at
how Peter and the other disciples are described in Luke 22:24.  Sadly, this episode is only the last in a
chain of arguments that they have about who is the greatest in the
kingdom.  Ironically, they take a kingdom
that’s supposed to be about humility and self-sacrifice and try to turn it into
a tool for exalting themselves.  It’s not
surprising, then, that when Peter is called on to sacrifice himself, he fails.

Human nature being what it is, neither is it surprising that
Christians today are all too often self-centered in their discipleship
too.  This shows up everywhere.  It shows up in the brethren who stop
attending services because they don’t get anything out of it, as though the
assembly is supposed to be about them. 
It shows up in the people who complain about the work that others do
because that work isn’t done the way they prefer.  It shows up in members who get offended
because they don’t feel like they’re getting the respect or affection they
deserve.  It shows up in those who fight
and argue until every decision that is made is the one that they want.

If we’re honest with ourselves, it’s probably true that
every one of us has allowed self-centeredness to contaminate our faith at some
point or another.  We need to own this,
and we need to fear it.  If left unchecked,
our desire to put ourselves first will lead us straight to hell.

Second, Peter failed because of his **FAILURE TO LISTEN.** Consider
the interaction between Peter and Jesus in Luke 22:31-33.  The Lord here is telling Peter basically to
expect spiritual disaster.  Satan is
going to come after Peter so hard that the best Peter can hope for is that he
won’t lose his faith entirely over the mess. 
When he comes back, he’s supposed to work to strengthen others so that
they don’t fall into the same kind of trouble that he did.

Peter, though, hears this dire prophecy and shrugs it
off.  He confidently promises Jesus that
there aren’t going to be any spiritual problems on his end, even if his life is
on the line.  The *Titanic* of Peter’s spiritual life doesn’t have to be worried about
any icebergs!

In retrospect, this was obviously pretty dumb of Peter, but
it’s easy for us to be equally dismissive when others try to warn us.  Our parents, or maybe a Christian friend,
pulls us aside and tells us seriously that they’re worried about the direction
we’re going.  They don’t know what-all is
going on, but they don’t like what they do see, whether it’s a decline in
church attendance, or that we’re hanging around with the wrong crowd, or that
we’re not treating others right.  We get
this solemn warning that probably took a lot of courage to issue, and we blow
it off like Peter did.

Now, obviously, none of us are Jesus, and unlike Him, the
people who pull us aside certainly can be wrong.  However, we at the least need to invest
careful thought before we decide that they are. 
If we reflexively brush off criticism without considering it, we have no
idea whether the critique is correct or not, and if it is correct and we don’t
listen, the problem will wreck us.

Third, Peter gets bit by plain old-fashioned **PRIDE.**This is evident in his words in Mark 14:31.  At this point, there is not a doubt in
Peter’s mind that he is going to follow Jesus all the way.  He’s not at all worried that Satan, who has
been around a lot longer than Peter and is pretty sneaky about this sort of
thing, might get the better of him.  He
is emphatic in his declaration that he will die right next to Jesus if need
be.  Sadly, the next time Peter is this
emphatic about anything, it is when he is swearing and calling down curses on
himself to highlight his denial of Jesus.

The Bible does not actually say, “Pride goes before a fall,”
but it does say, “Pride goes before destruction.”  There are no words that are sweeter in the
devil’s ears than when we say, even to ourselves, “It can’t happen to me.”  I can afford to skip services.  I can afford to nurture that close
relationship with a woman who isn’t my wife. 
I can afford to start looking at those sites on the Internet.  I can afford to take a drink or two after
work.  I can do all those things.  I can flirt with every spiritual danger
imaginable, because when you get right down to it, I’m too good to get got.

Brethren, ain’t none of us too good to get got, and when we
start thinking that we are, we ensure that we will get got.  Sin is fire, brethren!  Sin is poison!  Sin is death! 
We must treat it with fear, like the deadly thing it is.  If we get cocky and careless, it will destroy
us.

Finally, Peter gets done in by his **INDISCIPLINE.** This is
obvious in Mark 14:37-40.  Jesus spends
His time in the garden of Gethsemane in prayer, but He finds Peter sleeping
instead.  He tells him point-blank that
he needs to be praying too.  Otherwise,
he’s going to fall to the temptation that is coming.  And yet, the next time Jesus comes back, He
finds Peter and the others sleeping again. 
They were too tired to do what Jesus told them they needed to do.

How often, friends, do we plead lack of energy or lack of
will as our excuse for not doing the things that we know are spiritually good
for us?  “I know I should go to Wednesday
services, but when I get home from work, I’m so tired that I don’t want to do
anything but sit in the recliner until bed.” 
“I know I should set aside time for Bible study and prayer every day,
but my schedule is so busy that I don’t ever manage to fit it in.”  Brethren, when we get to talking that way,
there we are in the garden, right next to Peter.  Jesus is telling us what we need to do to be
strong, but being strong takes too much effort, so we choose to be weak instead
because being weak is easier.

Let me tell you about easy, friends.  Easy is lots of things.  Easy is pleasant, right now.  Easy is popular.  Easy is also deadly.  The path to heaven goes up, but the road to
hell goes down, down, down, and if we don’t struggle upward, we will coast all
the way to eternal destruction.

**Post-Betrayal
Problems**

It’s obvious that even as Peter is walking into the garden
of Gethsemane, he’s bringing plenty of weak spots with him.  Even more emerge, though, after Judas betrays
Jesus.  The first of these new problems
is **LACK OF UNDERSTANDING.** Peter reveals that he doesn’t get it in
John 18:10-11.  God planned for the
Messiah to die for the sins of mankind, but the Jewish nation missed this.  They were expecting the Christ to save them
from the Romans, not from the devil.

Throughout the ministry of Jesus, Peter reveals that he
shares this misconception.  When Jesus
first tells him that He is going to be captured and killed, Peter pulls Him
aside and denounces the idea.  Here too,
when the mob from the chief priests shows up to arrest Him, Peter tries to stop
them by flailing around with a sword.  It
may be that when he insisted that he was willing to die for Jesus, he was
thinking about death in combat.  He
misses the actual point completely.

Even today, there are plenty of Christians who miss the
point of God’s work.  They look on the
church that Christ established as a family tradition, or as a social club, or
as a self-help seminar where they can learn to live their best life now.  Brethren, if those things are why we’re here,
we’re here for the wrong reason. 
Instead, the church is an assembly of those who have been saved from
their sins by the blood of Jesus, and the work of this assembly is to get as
many people to heaven as possible, both those who are already part of the
church and those who aren’t.  When we
start importing any of those erroneous views of the church, they will
inevitably interfere with its true work and expose us to the temptation to
become a stumbling block rather than a worker.

Along with the others, Peter also reveals that he is
influenced by **FEAR.** This is plainly evident in what he does,
along with all the other disciples, in Matthew 26:55-56.  Rather than identifying themselves with Jesus
by sticking around and waiting to be arrested too, they all take off.

In our lives too, fear is often one of the main reasons why
we are vulnerable to temptation.  Doing
the right thing looks like it will endanger something we value; doing the wrong
thing looks like the safe course, so we play it safe and sin.  We remain silent when we are given an
opportunity to tell others about Jesus. 
We lie when telling the truth might offend someone else or get us in
trouble.  We look to protect ourselves by
heaping up money or making friends with the world rather than trusting in God’s
promise to protect us.  Fear is a
powerful force in all of our lives.

However, even though sinning out of fear might be
understandable, at least to us, God’s word emphasizes that it is not
excusable.  When we shrink back from what
we know we should do, we are not actually shrinking back to safety.  We are shrinking back to destruction.  Jesus boldly said and did what was right,
even at the cost of His life.  If we are
His disciples, we will do the same.

Finally, Peter failed because of **ISOLATION.** Consider the
scene that Luke depicts in Luke 22:54-60. 
This, of course, is the story of Peter’s sin, but it’s important for us
to remember the setting in which the sin takes place.  The apostle John is inside the high priest’s
house, where he is safe because the high priest knows him.  Peter, though, is not allowed inside the
house, and he finds himself in the courtyard, sitting around a fire with a
bunch of the high priest’s servants. 
Many of these people are the same ones who just helped arrest
Jesus.  They’re on the other side, and
when confronted with their hostility, Peter curses, lies, and swears false
oaths to deny his Lord.

It is equally true today, friends, that Christians are
vulnerable when they’re isolated.  There’s
a reason why God didn’t institute the Lone Ranger church, where we all just try
to get to heaven on our own without anybody else.  We need each other, and the less time we
spend around one another, the more difficult our walk with God will
become.  If our interactions with other
disciples are limited to five minutes after Sunday-morning services before we
make it out the door, and we spend the whole rest of the week around worldly
people, guess which way our lives are going to trend?  It’s hard to do the right thing when we’re
surrounded by people who are leaning on us to do the wrong thing.  Let’s remember that and associate with other
Christians instead.

**Why Jesus Succeeded**

I want to wrap our study of this gloomy subject, though, by
considering how Jesus succeeded in the same circumstances in which Peter
failed.  Not coincidentally, this will
also help us to prepare for the Lord’s Supper. 
First, and perhaps most obviously, Jesus differed from Peter in His
attention to **PRAYER.** Consider the account of Luke
22:39-46.  To me, the fascinating thing
about this prayer is that Jesus knows what God’s answer is going to be before
He ever opens His mouth.  This is part of
God’s eternal purpose, after all.  He had
this moment in mind before He ever said “Let there be light.”  He has directed the history of the human race
toward this point.  There is no way that
God, hours before the consummation of the ages, is going to say “OK, fine,
let’s go to Plan B.”  Jesus knows this,
and yet He asks for the cup to pass from Him anyway.

I think there’s a powerful lesson here for us.  Jesus obviously can’t be concerned about
getting what He wants.  There’s no way it
can happen.  Instead, He’s concerned
about His relationship with God, about pouring out His heart to His Father, no
matter what’s on His mind.  We too should
remember this.  We shouldn’t pray because
God is a great big vending machine in the sky, and if we push the right
buttons, we get what we want.  Instead,
we should pray because He is our Father, and talking to Him brings us closer to
Him, no matter what the answer is.  This
closeness helped Jesus to triumph despite the great temptation of His
suffering.

Second, Jesus succeeded because of His **PURPOSE.** The Hebrews writer
describes this purpose in Hebrews 10:5-7. 
From the very beginning of His life, Jesus wrote God a blank check.  Whatever God wanted, Jesus would do, and it
didn’t matter what it was.  There was no
price that was too high for Jesus to pay in His pursuit of pleasing God, and He
proved it.  Nor did Jesus ever allow
anything, even the threat of imminent torture and death, to distract Him from
His goal.

We pledge our own undivided loyalty to the Lord quite a bit,
particularly in the hymns we sing.  “All
to Jesus I surrender,” and “I have decided to follow Jesus—no turning back,”
are two lines that represent a whole genre of similar sentiments.  I suspect every Christian here would endorse
the notion that following Jesus means writing Him a blank check.

Sadly, though, we all too often prove more distractible than
Jesus.  As in the thorn section of the
parable of the sower, all these worries and cares of life spring up, and soon
we find ourselves paying more attention to them than to the Lord.  We can’t do what we know is the right thing
to do, we think, because this bad thing will happen and this bad thing will
happen and this bad thing will happen. 
We let the devil make everything complicated, and then He wins.

The only cure for this disease is to reacquaint ourselves
with our purpose.  When we committed our
lives to Christ, we didn’t commit to living trouble-free lives or being on good
terms with the world or never having to give up anything that mattered to us.  Instead, we committed ourselves to His
glory.  If we constantly keep that
purpose in mind, we will never fall.

Finally, Jesus succeeded because of His **LOVE.** This love is defined
simply and eloquently in John 13:1.  What
a breathtaking thought!  The Creator of
the world loved His creations to the end. 
The only thing that could keep His life from being an expression of His
love for them was His death.  Nor was
this love limited to His faithful disciples. 
Even on the cross itself, Jesus prayed for forgiveness for the people
who had put Him there.  Jesus never
stopped following where love led.

If we’re honest, this confronts us with an ugly truth about
ourselves.  Ultimately, we sin because we
don’t love enough.  Down at the bottom of
every spiritual failure is our lack of love for somebody.  We don’t merely fear because we are not
perfected in love; we sin because we are not.

Any spiritual improvement in us, then, must come from a
greater will to love, not only to treat God and others as we should, but to
genuinely desire their good.  Like Jesus,
we must love to the end.  We must
persevere in loving our spouses, even when we think they aren’t treating us
right.  We must persevere in loving our
brethren, even when Christians don’t behave in a very Christlike manner.  We must persevere in loving our neighbor,
even when they meet love with hatred. 
Every time we interact with somebody, we must say to them in the silence
of our hearts, “You can do what you want, but nothing you do is going to make
me stop loving you.”  That’s loving to
the end!
