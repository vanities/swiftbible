To my mind, one of the biggest problems with the worship
wars is that it promotes an either-or mentality among the combatants rather
than a both-and mentality.  Like
traditional hymns?  Then you can’t say
anything nice about anything written after 1980!  Prefer the contemporary style?  Don’t you dare resurrect any of those moldy
oldies!

I think both of these extreme positions are equally
indefensible.  Writing songs for the
church is extremely difficult.  No one
author or era has ever succeeded in doing so perfectly, and even high-quality
imperfection is rare.  To my mind, the
ideal repertoire for the church would combine the best of every genre, with
perhaps a slight bias toward more modern works for the sake of timeliness.  However, excluding traditional hymns from
consideration is as problematic, if not more so, than excluding contemporary
praise songs.  We should consider our
accumulated corpus of hymns as something to be preserved and added to, not
discarded.



**Strong Content**

The first and greatest benefit that traditional hymns offer
is strong teaching content.  If we
believe that we should follow Colossians 3:16, we will prefer hymns with strong
content for the same reason that we prefer to listen to a preacher with a strong
Bible background instead of a Scriptural ignoramus.  Christians who want to learn will be drawn to
hymns that teach them more.

This difference in content has everything to do with the
background of the typical author from each era. 
Through the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, hymn texts were usually
written by ministers (even the likes of Robert Lowry were ministers first and
hymnists second).  Female authors of the
time were generally ministers’ wives or ministers’ daughters, and many of these
women would have become ministers themselves in our day.

Their stock in trade was the Bible, and their familiarity
with Biblical language and doctrine greatly exceeded that of most modern
Christians.  Even Fanny J. Crosby, who
was blind (and I think mostly lived in an era before Braille was common), knew
the Scriptures well enough that she could casually toss the phrase “dry,
thirsty land” from Ezekiel 19:13 into the chorus of “A Wonderful Savior”.

Today, by contrast, most contemporary song texts are written
by music ministers (a.k.a. the lead singer in the praise band), and their stock
in trade is music, not the Bible.  They
don’t write theologically deep lyrics because they don’t know deep theology.  This might not bother Christians who
primarily know Ezekiel for his bread recipe, but it does nothing to address the
modern crisis of Scriptural ignorance.

There are, of course, shining exceptions to the rule.  The Gettys know the Bible.  So does Jamie Owens-Collins.  I have an easy time teaching and admonishing my
brethren if I’m singing “In Christ Alone” and other contemporary Christian
songs of similar depth.  However, far too
much of the contemporary repertoire makes it difficult for me to carry out my
Scriptural responsibility as fully as I would like to.

**Accessible Music**

Second, traditional hymns offer us congregationally
accessible music.  There is a vast
difference between a tune written for performance and one written for
participation.  Trained musicians are far
more accomplished than the average Joe on the street, and they have no problems
with music that would baffle him.

This points to one of the underlying problems with the
contemporary repertoire.  With few
exceptions, it is written for musicians (the praise band up on stage) rather
than for average Joes (the congregation in the seats).  The range is greater, the harmonies are more
complex (often needlessly so due to unskilled part-writing), and the rhythms
are less intuitive.  As a result,
contemporary Christian songs commonly ask the congregants to sing at a level
exceeding their abilities.  However,
rather than embarrassing themselves, most of the average Joes in attendance
will silently opt out.  Those who should
be participants in worship become passive audience members instead.

By contrast, most traditional hymns are written for singers
of average ability.  This is particularly
true of the hymns from the American-gospel era, many of which were written for
use in big-tent revival meetings.  Composers
of tunes for these occasions therefore wrote at such an elementary level that
revival attendees, most of whom were unskilled singers, could pick up the tune
by the time the hymn was over.  If you
can sing at all, you can sing the likes of “There’s Power in the Blood”.

Even British hymns from the Victorian era, which often have
more complex arrangements originally written for the organ, still usually have
melodies with a range of an octave or less. 
Maybe Average Joe can’t sing the tenor or the bass, but he can sing the
melody and even worship with it.

**Regular Structure**

Traditional hymns also are more congregationally useful
because of their regular structure.  It
seems to be universally true that the more people you want working together,
the more structure you need.  There’s a
lot of difference between walking out the door going to work in the morning,
getting ready to take the family on a road trip, and planning the Normandy
landings on D-Day.

Similarly, if you want to get people worshiping together,
all other things being equal, more structure in a hymn is better.  Consistent structure throughout a hymn,
particularly regular rhythm and meter, allows the congregation to anticipate
what will happen next.  That way, instead
of worrying about the form of the hymn, worshipers can focus on the content
while continuing to sing together.

On the other hand, the inconsistent structure in many
contemporary worship songs forces the congregation to focus on form.  How should I sing this next line (with its
collection of ties, slurs, and dotted sixteenth notes inserted to accommodate broken
meter)?  How many times is the last line
going to be repeated?  Even if such hymns
had good content, it would be wasted because the congregation would be driven
to fixate on mechanics anyway.  To say
the least, this does not promote worship.

**Rhyme**

Though rhyme is a structural element, I thought it deserved
a point all its own.  Of all the possible
aesthetic elements in spoken/written/sung poetry, rhyme is one of the most
common.  Rhyme appears in poetry
literally from here to China (in fact, I’ve heard it argued that the
quintessentially English sonnet form came to the British Isles from China by
way of Italy).  When we see something so
widely adopted, we are wise to stop and ask why.

In traditional hymns, rhymes serve two important
functions.  First, they add coherence to
a verse and signpost where it’s going. 
I’ve noticed that when I’m leading singing, I’m much more likely to
start singing a line from the wrong verse in an unrhymed hymn than in a rhymed
hymn.  In the rhymed hymn, it’s much more
obvious that the wrong line doesn’t fit.

Second, rhyming adds moral weight to lyrics.  As I’ve written [here](http://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2014/06/rhyming-in-hymns.html), a scientific study from 2000 revealed that an unfamiliar rhyming proverb will
sound more insightful than one that does not rhyme.  Writers who want their hymns to be memorable
and powerful will do well to use strong rhyme.

In fact, I suspect that the contemporary abandonment of
strong rhyme has much less to do with rhyme’s usefulness than with its
difficulty.  It takes considerable skill
to rhyme fluently in English, and only a few hymnists from any time demonstrate
mastery of rhyme (Isaac Watts, James Montgomery, and Charlotte Elliott come to
mind here).  Most creators of
contemporary Christian song aren’t skilled poets any more than they are
thoughtful Bible scholars, and they don’t use strong, consistent rhyme in their
work because they can’t.  Instead, they
make up for lack of rhyme and other structural deficiencies through increased
use of repetition, which adds structure at the expense of content.

**Conclusion**

All of the above is not an argument against adopting
contemporary Christian songs in worship. 
However, it is an argument for a measured, prudent approach in doing
so.  We should evaluate modern sacred
song in the same way that we evaluate sacred song from centuries ago:  not according to its time of origin, but
according to its merits.  These merits
have nothing to do with some English professor’s list of aesthetic
criteria.  They have everything to do
with how useful a hymn is to the congregation. 
Timeliness is important, but if we adopt contemporary songs for their
timeliness despite their lack of utility, the church cannot help but be
lessened because of it.
