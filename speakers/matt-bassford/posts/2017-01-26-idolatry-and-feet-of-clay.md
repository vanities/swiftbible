Nearly anybody who has been to church more than twice has
heard that idolatry is wrong.  If you
stick around for a year or two, you will probably also be exposed to the notion
that idolatry doesn’t necessarily involve bowing down before a graven
image.  After all, Paul points out in
Colossians 3:5 that covetousness is a form of idolatry, and the worship of
Mammon is no less popular today than it was back then.

From Sinai on, the human race has been on notice that
idolatry offends God.  We shall have no
other gods before Him, even if we don’t identify the other gods as gods at
all.  When we do otherwise, we reject the
Creator in favor of our own creation, and He doesn’t much care for that.



However, I’ve been reflecting recently that He tells us to
avoid idolatry as much for our sake as for His. 
After all, what lies beneath idolatry is the universal human desire to
worship *something*.  We have been created with eternity in our
hearts, with the desire to grope for God and find Him.  We can seek to fulfill that appetite with all
sorts of things other than God, but we can never stifle it.  There is not a single human being out there
who is content with living a meaningless life.

The problem is, though, that no idol can bear up under the
weight of our devotion.  No earthly thing
can satisfy the longing for the eternal. 
This is obvious when it comes to covetousness.  Love of money will end in one of two
ways.  Either we never get money and we
spend our lives disappointed, or we get money. . . and we spend our lives
disappointed.  New cars and big houses
and fat bank accounts can’t meet the expectations we put on them.  No matter how much stuff we have, we will
remain the same old us with the same old longing for eternity, and trying to
appease that hunger with the material is like trying to fill in a bottomless
pit.

The same is true of the more subtle idols that seduce
Christians.  Our families are a gift from
God (like money), but when we put our families over everything else, we make
them into an idol (also like money). 
Instead of trying to satisfy our eternal longings with stuff, we’re
trying to satisfy them with people.

Once again like money, this longing can go in one of two
ways.  We can put our hopes in our loved
ones, yet find that they disappoint us. 
Spouses can die or abandon us. 
Children can die too, I’m here to tell you.  They can also break under the strain of our
expectations, becoming drug addicts or salutatorians.  Who can fathom the shame of the alpha parent
whose darling is accepted to U. Chicago *but
not Yale*?

Alternatively, our families can live up to all the hopes we
put on them, but then what?  With your
help, your child spends the first 18 years of life compiling the perfect
resume, getting into the perfect school, graduating with perfect grades, and
getting the perfect job.  Sure, you might
feel validated when you’re showing the graduation pics off to your friends (an
exercise in narcissism if ever there was one), but once you’re done with them,
they’re going to live their life, and you’re going to live yours.  Your relationship with them won’t be a whole
lot different than if they’d slept their way to a 2.0 GPA in high school and
gotten a job as a plumber (I know some very prosperous plumbers, mind!), and
your longing for eternity will still be there.

This is not a call to Stoicism, to gaze down at our sleeping
children and say, “They might be dead in the morning.”  Simply because something is not suited to
being an idol does not mean that it must be nothing.  It’s pleasant to have money and nice things,
and the love of family is a source of consolation and comfort in an
often-hostile world.  However, neither
money, family, nor anything else on this earth can stand in the place of God
for us.  We may well ruin them if we try
to make them do so, and we will certainly ruin ourselves.  No matter the construction of the idol, in
the end, it will prove to have feet of clay.
