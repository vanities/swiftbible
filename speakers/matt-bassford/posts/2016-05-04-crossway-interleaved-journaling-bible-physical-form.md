**OVERVIEW.** Goatskin fanciers may not agree with me,
but I thought the Interleaved Journaling Bible (IJB) was a lovely Bible.  Crossway executed a cloth-over-board cover
about as well as it can be done.  It’s
also a lot of Bible for a comparatively low price.  My only objection is to the size of the
thing.

Like most Bibles I review, this one is a review copy, sent
to me gratis by Crossway in exchange for my promise to review it.  Here’s my take on it:

**SIZE.** No two ways about it; this thing is a
brick.  It might fit into your glove box
if you drive a tank.  It might fit into
your purse if you’re Margaret Thatcher. 
Failing those two things, there is no sense in which this is a carry
Bible for anybody.  You might lug it
around because you’re a really dedicated Bible journaler and don’t want your
canvas far off when artistic inspiration strikes, but this is a very
intentional Bible.  It’s not the sort of
thing you stick in your bag in case you want to look up a verse or spend a few
minutes reading.

This isn’t a bad thing, exactly.  Blaming an interleaved Bible for being big is
like blaming lead for being heavy.  If
you want it to be anything else, you’re asking it to defy the laws of
physics.  However, it does limit the
applications for which the Bible is useful. 
As somebody who’s dedicated to the idea of specialist Bibles, I’m OK
with that.  I’d rather have a bunch of
Bibles that each excel at one thing than one Bible that is mediocre at a bunch
of things.

**COVER.** This is a hardback, a lowly, despised
hardback, but I love it.  Most hardback
Bibles that I’ve encountered have the bookboard wrapped in shiny, slick paper
like a textbook.  Covers like that take
me back to elementary school, which for me is not a place filled with happy
memories.

Not so with the IJB. 
Instead of that shiny stuff, its boards are wrapped in limestone-colored
buckram, which is a stiff cotton cloth used in bookbinding.  Nor is this just any buckram.  It’s not particularly finely woven, so the
cover has a wonderfully pronounced, coarse texture.  I enjoy the way it feels when I run my
fingers over it.  It makes me want to
hold the Bible.

I even like the design of the spine.  Thankfully, Crossway appears to have
abandoned the cluttered ESV spines of days of yore.  This one just has an ESV logo, a Crossway
logo, and “Journaling Bible, Interleaved Edition”, with short double bars
separating each element.  Beautifully
understated, says I.

I also appreciate that the spine is simply stamped with
black ink rather than gilded.  I love the
slightly fuzzy way that the ink looks now that it has soaked into the
buckram.  It suits my spare sense of
aesthetics very well.  For lack of a
better word, the design has a. . . dryness that I appreciate.

I’m not normally a ribbon guy, but I like this one.  In particular, I like the color.  It’s a golden beige that belongs to the same
palette as the ivory-toned paper and limestone buckram.  If I were to design a hardback Bible and were
a professional-caliber designer, the results would look about like this.

Of course, despite my romance with the look of the thing,
this is still a hardback Bible, with all the durability problems that come with
hardback Bibles.  The endpapers are thick
paper, which is vulnerable to shearing and tearing, and the square corners are
easy to crush.  What’s worse, the size of
the Bible only magnifies the effect of these destructive forces.

The clever, clever souls at Crossway design (my goodness,
but they’ve gotten better than they were five years ago!) spotted this problem
and packaged the Bible in a lovely, durable slipcase.  I like slipcases for Bible storage in
general, but I think the slipcase is particularly important with the IJB.  Anybody who buys one of these should carry it
around in the slipcase.  That will add to
the weight of the Bible, but it will protect it from crushing and
shearing.  With care, I think that a
slipcased IJB would have some longevity.

**TEXT BLOCK.** It’s sewn.  Due to the unusual thickness of the paper in
the Bible (I’ve read online reports that it’s 50 gsm, which would make it the
heaviest paper in any Bible I’ve ever reviewed), the signatures in the text
block are unusually thick themselves. 
When you look down on the top of the spine, they could not be more
obvious.  No glue-bound monstrosity
here!  You might crush the corners of the
IJB, but you won’t start shedding pages.

As is often the case with hardback Bibles, the corners of
the pages in the IJB aren’t rounded, nor are the edges gilded.  However, the edges have been trimmed, so that
any journaler who wants to draw or paint on the edges will be able to do so.

**VALUE.** In terms of dollars per pound, this Bible
has to be up at the top of the list!  It
retails for $39.99, which is $5 less than any of Crossway’s hardback
Single-Column Journaling Bibles.  Given
that it’s about twice the size they are, I can’t explain its lower price.

Also, I believe that the IJB is unique in its format.  I’ve seen KJV’s with half the page devoted to
blank space, but I’m not aware of any other interleaved Bible that is currently
in print.  If you want whole blank pages
in your bound Bible to write or journal in, this is your Bible.  I have no idea whether people will be
interested in this, but Crossway deserves kudos for trying the idea out.

The size is a definite drawback in my rating system, as are
the durability problems that come with hardback Bibles.  However, I think the IJB has earned an 8/10
in Physical Form.
