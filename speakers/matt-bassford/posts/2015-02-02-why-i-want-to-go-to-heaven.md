In the year or so since I enrolled as a full-fledged Bible
geek, I’ve come to enjoy having a set of Bible-geek friends online who (mostly)
do not hail from churches of Christ. 
They’re generally quite religious, though, so they link to a number of
religious posts and articles that I would never otherwise have seen.  Some of these I absently dismiss, but others
make me think.

One such post that I read recently was the provocatively
titled ["10 Reasons Christian Heaven Would Actually Be Hell"](http://www.alternet.org/10-reasons-christian-heaven-would-actually-be-hell).  Though I think the author’s view of heaven is
a little slanted (too much time taking Revelation literally, for instance),
many of the questions he raises are legitimate. 
Won’t everything be the same in heaven? 
Isn’t it going to be kind of gaudy? 
How will those in heaven handle the suffering of those in hell?  And so on. 
The author uses these questions to construct a vision of heaven as a
sterile prison which is scarcely preferable to an eternity of torment.



Even as I was reading the article, though, I felt an unease
with the author’s analysis that I couldn’t quite put my finger on.  After thinking about it, I realized what the
problem was.  The post focused
exclusively on what the Bible says about heaven without considering at all what
the Bible says about Christianity.

More than anything else, the Christianity of the Bible is
about relationships.  The heart of the
Christian faith is defined by Jesus in His exchange with a scribe in Mark
12:28-31:

*One
of the scribes came and heard them arguing, and recognizing that He had
answered them well, asked Him, "What commandment is the foremost of
all?" Jesus answered, "The foremost is, 'HEAR, O ISRAEL! THE LORD OUR GOD IS ONE LORD; AND YOU SHALL LOVE THE
LORD YOUR GOD WITH ALL YOUR HEART, AND WITH ALL YOUR SOUL, AND WITH ALL YOUR
MIND, AND WITH ALL YOUR STRENGTH.' "The second is this, 'YOU SHALL
LOVE YOUR NEIGHBOR AS YOURSELF.' There is no other commandment greater than
these."* (NASB)

To this, we must also add the words of the Lord in John
13:34, “A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another, even as I
have loved you, that you also love one another.” (NASB)  The three guiding aims of the Christian,
then, are these:  Love God with
everything you have.  Love your neighbor
as much as you love yourself.  Love your
brother more than you love yourself.

These three principles lead to a focus on others that
non-Christians sometimes struggle to comprehend, especially in our
individualistic, fragmented society. 
I’ve read that on average, Americans invite others into their homes for
a meal about once a year.  My wife and I,
by contrast, entertain regularly, and the biggest limiting factor on our
hospitality is that non-Christians are often suspicious of dinner
invitations.  No, we’re not going to lock
you in our house and give you the hard sell. 
We just want to get to know you better. 
Besides, as millions of mothers know, food is a wonderful way to express
love.

Christians shouldn’t want distance.  They shouldn’t want space.  Instead, they should want to be immersed in
the lives of others, especially other Christians.  Now, I’m certainly not going to claim that
I’m thrilled with everything that every one of my brethren ever says or does,
but it is still true that my relationships with them are the defining feature
of my earthly existence, and I find that it makes my life rich and rewarding in
ways that my introverted soul would never have realized on its own.

All of this, of course, is to say nothing of the Christian’s
relationship with God.  Most of the
instruction of the New Testament is focused on other people, but all of it is
focused on God:  through prayer, through
Bible study, and even through the painstaking obedience that our society finds
so tiresome.  The love of Christians for
God inspires them to obey, and obedience teaches them things about their
Creator that they can learn in no other way. 
There are certainly many things I have yet to learn about God, but today
I know more about Him than I ever did before, and tomorrow, I’ll know more
still.

Heaven to me, then, isn’t about pearly gates and golden
streets, however metaphorical they may be. 
Instead, it is an opportunity to finally and fully achieve my greatest
longings.  If all my life, I have
earnestly sought for God and for other Christians, then what better reward can
there be than to spend eternity with all of them?

The congregation at Joliet likes to laugh at me because I
constantly identify different passages as “one of my favorites,” but one of my
favoritest of all is 1 Thessalonians 4:17. 
It reads, “Then we who are alive and remain will be caught up together
with them in the clouds to meet the Lord in the air, and so we shall always be
with the Lord.” (NASB)  Sartre was dead
wrong.  Hell isn’t other people.  Heaven is. 
It’s something that every true disciple understands, and it’s something
that no unbeliever can ever grasp.
