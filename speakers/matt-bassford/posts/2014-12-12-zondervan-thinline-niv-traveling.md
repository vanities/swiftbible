As has been true throughout this review, the Thinline NIV
got much more of a workout than the norm. 
Instead of accompanying me as I putter around town, it went all the way
to Missouri with me.  While there, I used
it to preach a couple of sermons and as my source for Scripture throughout a
week-long hymn-writing seminar.  Here is
what I thought:

**SIZE.** The Thinline NIV is a perfectly average
thinline.  It’s a little larger than the
Zondervan Thinline NASB, a little smaller than the Crossway Thinline
(ESV).  As such, we would expect it to
pass the glove-box test, and indeed it does. 
Though obviously I can’t fit it in my hip pocket, I never had trouble
stowing it for travel.



Sadly, however, my inattention during the trip highlighted
my need for a Bible that I *can* stow
for travel.  On my way back from church
Sunday night, I didn’t go to all the trouble of carrying it to the glove box,
putting it in, and then storing the rest of my gear separately.  Instead, I tossed it in the trunk with my
suit coat (and all the other stuff that floats around in our trunk on a road
trip) and figured that it would make the 10-mile trip back to my dad’s with
little trouble.

Wrong.  Somewhere
along the way, perhaps due to my wife’s. . . enthusiastic driving, something
got rammed into the Bible.  The result
highlights one of the problems with Zondervan’s DuoTone.  The goatskin cover of my Leonard’s rebind of
the Zondervan Thinline NASB is much thicker than the DuoTone of the Thinline
NIV, and the NASB has a leatherette rather than paper endpaper.  As a result, its corners offer some resistance
to end-on force, and goatskin is more elastic than DuoTone.

However, the Bible that actually got clobbered was poly
rather than goat, and it suffered as a result. 
The lower corners of pages from Hosea to Luke are now bent, and the
lower corner of the back cover itself has a visible fold in it.  None of this affects the usefulness of the
Bible; it just makes me rue my carelessness every time I pick the thing
up.

It also highlights one of the hidden benefits of premium
covers—durability.  Poly covers look nice
and feel nice.  Anyone other than a Bible
dork would probably be hard-pressed to tell the difference between poly and
premium leather.  The latter, though, is
going to hold up better.  Many people
baby their premium Bibles; there was a guy in the hymn-writing seminar who
carried his Clarion around in the original box.

If that’s you, that’s great. 
For those like me, however, who are not as thoughtful with our
possessions, a premium Bible is able to take a beating and still retain its
beauty.  Goatskin will age much more
gracefully than poly does, even if it is badly treated.

Of course, all of this is much more a reflection on me than
it is on the Thinline NIV.  You can’t
expect a poly Bible to perform better than a poly Bible does, after all, and
it’s not the Bible’s fault that I wasn’t careful with it.  As travel Bibles go, the Thinline NIV is
excellently sized.

**CROSS-REFERENCES.** Here too, despite using the Bible for a
week in a classroom setting, I never really looked at the
cross-references.  I did announce to the
class several times that I was using the Not Inspired Version, so apparently it
is more important to me that my Bible offer me opportunities for low-grade
humor.  Regardless, light
cross-references were available at the bottom of the page.  I simply didn’t use them.

**CONCLUSION.** One of the main selling points of
thinlines is their portability.  They
give up a lot (large print, opaque paper, robust cross-references) to get
there, but get there they do.  The
Zondervan Thinline NIV receives the typical thinline grade for its utility as a
travel Bible:  4.5/5.
