The size that
I want my perfect Bible to be varies from task to task, so the only two
elements that remain constant here are its cover and its binding.

**BINDING.** I’m not a Bible
collector.  I don’t care whether the
cover of my Bible is made from the natural-grain hide of the prince of the
goats of the Nigerian highlands.  However,
that doesn’t mean I’m content to spend the rest of my life studying the
Scriptures in paperback, either. 
Instead, I want two things out of my Bible covers.



First and
foremost, I want them to hold up, which probably means that they have to have a
genuine-leather or premium-leather cover. 
Even though most Bibles perish when the glued text block gives up the
ghost, a Bible with a sewn text block may have the cover die first.  I know from experience that a cloth-and-board
hardback Bible won’t stand up to heavy, repeated use.  Mine got beat up at the corners, and it
finally split down the hinge of the endpapers. 
I have a couple of bonded-leather Bibles where the cover seems
determined to race the glued text block to destruction.  I might be persuaded to buy a cloth-and-board
Bible for a reference volume, but I don’t see any reason to purchase a
bonded-leather Bible ever again.

The next
grade up, the imitation-leather polyurethane covers, are better than the first
two alternatives, but here too, experience has proven that the cover will start
to look shabby after about five years, and I want more mileage out of my Bibles
than that.  Only genuine-leather and
premium-leather covers offer the best hope of durability.

However, it’s
also important to me that my Bibles be reasonably pleasant to hold.  I like covers that are fairly soft and
flexible.  Polyurethane covers actually
perform quite well at this, but at the next price point, genuine leather often
does not.  Some genuine-leather Bibles
might start out a little bit stiff, but with a little bit of use and breaking
in, they soften up and flex nicely. 
Other genuine-leather Bibles, however, are as hard and stiff as they can
be.  No amount of flexing can persuade
them to quit acting like plastic and start acting like leather.

Genuine-leather
Bibles like that can drive me up to the next and final price point, premium
leather.  A premium-leather Bible certainly
ought to be both durable and delightful to the touch.  After all, you’re paying for the privilege!

I’ve never
owned an edge-lined Bible, but I’ve been pretty happy with Bibles with the more
prosaic paste-off binding.  I like the
endpapers to be leatherette if at all possible.

**TEXT BLOCK.** The rule here is that a text block must be sewn, sewn,
sewn.  What this means is that rather
than being a collection of individual pages stuck in a strip of glue, the text
block comprises a series of little booklets, called signatures, that are
stitched together with thread.  A Bible
with a sewn binding is an order of magnitude more durable than a Bible with a
glued binding.  If you’ve ever purchased
a Bible and, after about a year of use, it got a big crack in 1 John and
started shedding pages, that’s a sign that it was glued.  If you don’t want to leave a trail of pieces
of the word of God wherever you go, only buy Bibles that are sewn.

Of course,
desiring a sewn Bible is one thing, but determining whether a Bible actually is
sewn is another.  If the description of a
Bible on the box or on a website says it is “Smythe-sewn”, that means the
binding is sewn.  Other publishers will
use less descriptive terms, like “Lay-Flat” or “Lifetime Warranty”, but they
too generally mean that a Bible has a sewn binding.

Likewise, if
you’re examining a physical Bible, there are a couple of ways to determine
whether it is sewn.  First, if you peek
under the headband (the strip of string at the top of the spine inside the
cover) and see the folded ends of a whole bunch of little booklets, the Bible
is sewn.

Similarly, if
you open to the middle of the Bible and run your fingers down the gutter (the
crease in the middle where the pages meet), and you feel bumps and hear a
crackling sound, that means the binding is sewn.  Those bumps are called cockling.  In higher-end Bibles, cockling is often
considered a defect.  However, just about
every mid-range Bible with a sewn binding that I’ve encountered has
cockling.  It’s a small price to pay for
the vastly increased durability, and it’s a sign that the durability will be
there.

Also, every
Bible you purchase from a high-end Bible publisher (Schuyler, Allan, Cambridge,
et al) is going to have a sewn binding. 
Even Crossway, the primary publisher of the ESV, has gone to sewn Bibles
through the vast majority of its lineup, perhaps all of it by this point.
