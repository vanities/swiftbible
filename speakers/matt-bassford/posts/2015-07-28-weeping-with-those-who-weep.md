*Rejoice with those who
rejoice, and weep with those who weep.*

-- Romans 12:15 (NASB)

I’m not a crier.  I
don’t think I wept at my daughter’s funeral. 
I know I didn’t at my mother’s.  I
preached it.  My father never shed a tear
either, even though it was the love of his life in the casket.  Instead, he watched the proceedings with
heartrending stoicism.  Maybe this
stemmed from some misguided idea of masculine nobility.  I think, though, that it just means that
people in my family, men especially, aren’t criers.



We grieve differently. 
I call my dad at least once a week and talk with him for an hour or
more—sports, politics, the Bible, with never a mention from him of any personal
subject except one.  We talk about my
mother, her good heart, her love of the Lord, her truly indomitable
personality.  We poke at the wound, knock
some of the scabs off, make it bleed a little bit, but it’s good blood, it’s a
good hurt, and it’s much better to say the words and remember and hurt than it
is to choke the words down and pretend my mother doesn’t matter.  We still do this, more than two years after my
mother’s passing, and I think we’ll keep doing it until one of us is gone too.

What about church, though? 
Too often, our assemblies give no notice of unhealed or barely-healed
wounds.  We want everything to be
joyful—joy in the beauty of the creation, joy in the blessings of this life,
joy most of all in Jesus and our salvation. 
Because we have these things, we reason, shouldn’t everything in our
lives be joyful?  When disaster strikes,
shouldn’t we observe some decent period of mourning, six weeks for a
miscarriage, three months for a sibling, six months for a parent, and so on,
and then get back to being joyful Christians? 
In consequence, our spiritual expression often has a shrill shallowness
to it, like a hymn in which the harmony consists of only one chord.

Tragically, that view of Christianity only half-resembles
what it’s like to actually be human. 
Yes, there is constant joy in my heart for all of the above reasons and
a thousand more, but there is a constant song of sorrow too.  Life on earth is disappointing, frustrating,
sometimes crushing, and those things have all left their mark on me.  I don’t find that the sorrow cancels the joy
out, or the joy the sorrow.  Instead, if
anything, the sorrow enriches the joy. 
I’ll never see my mother again *here*,  but how I look forward to seeing her *there*! 
Without the loss, the hope would be incoherent.

We sing about our hope. 
Why shouldn’t we sing about our loss too?   If we know that “Be With Me, Lord” is a hymn
that Sister Evelyn associates with her late husband’s funeral, and it always
chokes her up when it’s led, that’s not a reason to avoid “Be With Me,
Lord”.  It’s a reason to sing it.  If everybody else in the congregation knows
about Sister Evelyn, and they get choked up too, so much the better.

The gathering of the Lord’s people ought to be a safe place
to mourn, even if we are mourning an event that took place years or decades
ago.  There are enough things that we’re
judgmental about; surely we don’t need to add grief to the list!  A weeping Christian isn’t a bad
Christian.  A Christian who can’t get
over it isn’t a bad Christian. 
Certainly, we should seek consolation in Christ, but earth isn’t the
place where God has promised to wipe away all tears.  It’s OK to carry some grief with us, until we
die, even, because grief is the single best reminder that hoping in this life
is foolish.

Nor should we fear to offend God by our grief.  He is mindful of our frame, and in many ways,
He makes more allowances for us than we do for ourselves.  Take Psalm 88.  The writer is anguished, despairing, and
embittered, toward God most of all.  It
doesn’t tie up in a neat little bow at the end. 
Instead, it’s just as dark as when it began.  I would never write a hymn like that, I would
be shocked to hear a prayer like that, yet it is an inspired text that God
wants us to learn from.  Perhaps most of
all, it is authentically human.  When our
interactions together fail to express what our lives are really like, those
expressions fall short of what God wants them to be.
