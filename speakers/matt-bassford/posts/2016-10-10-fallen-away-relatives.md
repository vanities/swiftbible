A couple of weeks ago, I ran a post on my blog that invited
readers to suggest a post topic.  Among
several other requests, I received a private message that said this:

“Our congregation has gone through a tough situation with
having to withdraw from a member living in willing, unrepentant sin. This had
been made more difficult by the fact that multiple generations of the
individual's family are also members- and some have chosen to observe this
separation, and a few have not. What is the role that believing family members
play when the Church withdraws?”

This is hardly a rare situation, but it is a difficult
one.  As I thought about it, I realized
that I couldn’t possibly do the topic justice in a thousand-word blog
post.  In fact, even in a sermon, there
are relevant passages that I don’t have time to explore.  However, I think that in 45 minutes, we can
get to the heart of the matter.  Let’s
consider, then, how we should handle fallen-away relatives.



**Sin in Corinth**

Our study of the matter has to begin with 1 Corinthians 5
and its sad tale of sin in Corinth.  Much
of the time, we come to this chapter to proof-text out v. 11, but I think that
we have to study the entire context to understand Paul’s actual point.  Let’s start, then, with what he says about **THE PROBLEM**.  This appears in 1 Corinthians 5:1-2.

One of the most important things to notice about this text
is that Paul doesn’t identify only one problem with the situation.  He identifies two.  The first, of course, is the member of the
church in Corinth who has his father’s wife. 
He is engaged in an intimate relationship with his stepmother.  As Paul observes, even the immoral Gentiles
of Corinth see the problem with that one!

Second, though, Paul points to the Corinthians’ reaction as
a problem.  They’re not grossed-out about
this.  They’re not upset.  They’re not getting rid of the guy.  Instead, they’re happy.  It seems likely that the Corinthians’ skewed
view of freedom in Christ includes freedom from any rules about sexual
morality, a viewpoint shared by many so-called “Christians” around us.

This is extremely important, and it should shape the way we
read everything else Paul says in this chapter. 
Paul isn’t only looking to solve the problem of gross immorality.  HHHHe’s
looking to solve the problem of Christians condoning gross immorality, and his
solution will address both issues.

The next part of the context sets out **PAUL’S DECISION.** He explains
it in 1 Corinthians 5:3-5.  There are
some things here that we can’t imitate. 
This is, after all, a display of the authority of an apostle.  Even though Paul isn’t present in Corinth, he
is able to call on his authority to make the judgment that the church in
Corinth already should have made.  They
haven’t taken action to remove the wicked man from their midst, so Paul is
going to decree it for them.

I think that’s all that “delivering him to Satan”
means.  It means that he is on notice
that he has been evicted from the kingdom of the beloved Son and returned to
the dominion of darkness.  Likewise, “the
destruction of his flesh” refers not to some kind of physical punishment, but
to the shame that such a member must necessarily feel at being subject to
public rebuke.

In this section, I think there are two lessons for brethren
today.  The first is that no church can
tolerate evil in its midst.  If a
congregation has a member who is living an ungodly life, they must be made to
understand that their behavior is unacceptable, and if they don’t mend their ways,
they must be made to understand that they are no longer part of the
congregation.

Second, though, Paul identifies his goal not as the shaming
of the sinner, but as the salvation of the sinner.  He pronounces this judgment not because he
wants to dispose of him, but because he wants him to repent.  We too must always remember the value of a
soul, even in the midst of sin.

In the next section of this text, Paul warns the Corinthians
against **THE LEAVEN OF WICKEDNESS.** Consider 1 Corinthians 5:6-8.  In Matthew 13, Jesus tells His disciples that
the kingdom of heaven is supposed to be like leaven.  One application of this is that we’re
supposed to have a leavening effect on the world around us, subtly but
constantly influencing our friends and neighbors for good.  However, Paul points out here that wicked
people in the church can have a leavening effect too.  If evil is tolerated, it has a constant
influence for bad.

In practice, whenever a church allows one of its members to
openly practice evil, it will eventually lead to a church in which every evil
is openly practiced.  A standard that is
not followed in every particular is no longer a standard.

We’ve seen this in the gay-marriage debate of the past
several years.  Outside and even inside
the Lord’s church, there are all too many self-proclaimed Christians who ignore
the Lord’s teachings on marriage, divorce, and remarriage in Matthew 19,
probably because their congregations contain many people who would be affected
by it.  However, they still want to draw
a line when it comes to 1 Corinthians 6 and its condemnation of the practice of
homosexuality.

Brethren, that’s as hypocritical as it can be, and when
gay-rights advocates condemn evangelicals for such a contradictory position,
they’re exactly right.  How dare we
strictly apply the Scripture to someone else when we do not first apply it to
ourselves?  In the end, the position will
prove to be unsustainable, and churches that welcome the unscripturally married
will in time also find themselves welcoming practicing homosexuals.  Either you follow the Bible, or you don’t.

This brings us to Paul’s **APPLICATION.** It appears in 1
Corinthians 5:9-13.  In addition to the
instruction to put the unrepentant sinner out, he tells the Corinthians not to
associate with him and not even to eat with him.  Some have suggested that the eating here is
the Lord’s Supper, but I think that’s inconsistent with Paul’s instructions
about the Lord’s Supper in 1 Corinthians 11. 
There, each disciple is instructed to examine himself to make sure that
he’s worthy to partake.  It’s our place
to make that judgment about ourselves, not anyone else’s to make it for us.

Paul, then, is telling the Corinthians not to share ordinary
meals with the man, and based on what he has already said, we know he has three
reasons for saying this.  The first is
his concern for the sinner’s soul.  This
harsh treatment is necessary to shock him out of his sin and restore him to
faithfulness.  Second, Paul is concerned
that if their previous relationship continues, this man will continue to leaven
and corrupt the church.

Third, though, the Corinthians should avoid the sinner not
only because he isn’t good for them, but because they aren’t good for him.  These are not sin-rebukers.  These are sin-celebrators!  They’re not going to lead him to repent
because they don’t think he has anything to repent of.  This man has failed in his duty to the Lord,
but it’s equally true that the they have failed in their duty to him.  As a result, the best thing they can do for
him is to stay away and not make matters worse.

**Christ and the Sinner**

At this point, the issue seems cut-and-dried.  When dealing with any Christian who has
fallen away, whether relative or not, shun them!  However, if we reach that conclusion at this point,
we’ve overlooked an entire category of relevant passages, those having to do
with Christ and the sinner.

Let’s begin by looking at Jesus’ teachings on **CHURCH DISCIPLINE.** Here, let’s read from Matthew
18:15-17.  Even though it’s not strictly
topical, let me first observe that this is a command of the Lord that
Christians are more likely to ignore than obey. 
The meaning is plain.  If you
believe that your brother has sinned, it is your responsibility to go to your
brother, period, end of story.  It does
not say, “Go to your family.”  It does
not say, “Go to your best friend.” 
Sadly, though, it is often the case that when we believe a brother has
sinned, the one whom we think has done wrong is the last to find out about
it!  When we act in this way, we add our
sin to theirs.

When we follow the Matthew 18 process, though, when we go to
our brother privately, then to him with a few others, then bring his sin before
the congregation, and he refuses to repent all the way through, there’s only
one place it can lead.  Jesus says that
to us, the sinner needs to be like a Gentile or a tax collector.  He means that we can no longer treat them as
part of us.

However, we also see the Lord **EATING WITH SINNERS.** There
are many passages that testify to this, but let’s look at Mark 2:14-17.  In order to appreciate what’s happening here,
we first must understand what a “sinner” is in the gospels.  In His interactions with Levi, Jesus isn’t
talking to some Gentile.  Instead, as we
should probably be able to tell from the name, Levi is Jewish.  He’s somebody who’s part of God’s covenant
people, but he’s not keeping covenant. 
Instead, as a tax collector, he’s making himself rich by preying on his
fellow Jews, and he probably isn’t bothering to follow the rest of the Law
either.

However, Jesus still goes with Levi to his house and hangs
out with all of Levi’s sinner and tax-collector buddies.  The Pharisees were horrified by this.  How could a prophet of God possibly eat with
those whom God’s people were supposed to shun? 
For that matter, how do we ourselves square this with 1 Corinthians 5
and Matthew 18?

What’s fascinating is that Jesus and Paul use almost exactly
the same reason to justify opposite courses of action.  Back in 1 Corinthians 5, Paul says that the
Corinthians aren’t to eat with the sinner so that he will repent.  Here, Jesus says that He is eating with these
sinners so that they will repent.  Paul
and Jesus are advising different tactics in different circumstances, but they
are pursuing the same goal.  When we read
the Lord’s commandment to treat disobedient brethren like Gentiles and tax
collectors, we must remember that He treated tax collectors like this.

Indeed, throughout His ministry, Jesus’ motivation is
clearly **LOVE FOR THE SINNER.** His own words well illustrate what this
means in Luke 15:1-7.  We often
universalize this parable, and I think we’re on safe ground when we do, but in
its original context, Jesus is talking about the sinners of the Jewish
nation:  tax collectors, prostitutes, and
everyone else who wasn’t faithful to the Law. 
Jesus’ behavior toward these people is strikingly different from any
other first-century Jewish religious leader we know about.  He doesn’t shun them.  He doesn’t despise them.  Instead, He goes out and actively looks for
them so that they can be saved.

Even though Jesus eating with sinners outwardly looks like
the Corinthians eating with the man who had his father’s wife, the motivations
could not be more different.  The
Corinthians might have thought they loved that man, but their actions make it
clear that they didn’t.  What they loved
instead was their own distorted view of grace. 
They loved the freedom that they thought they had to run roughshod over
every social convention and taboo.  It is
never loving to encourage the sinner in their sin, and that, of course, is
exactly what the Corinthians were doing. 
In Christ, we are free, but we are free to behave like Him, not to
behave like them.

Before the Lord’s Supper, let’s think about all of these
things.  Let’s remember the love of Jesus
for us in our sin, that He loved us enough to seek us out, to come to this
sin-sick world and lay down His life for us so that we might be healed.  Second, let’s ask if the love of Christ truly
abides in us.  Is it evident in the way
we treat one another?  Does it reveal
itself particularly in the way we seek to restore our brethren who have
wandered from the faith?  Let’s ponder as
we partake.

**What We Must Remember**

In the final portion of our lesson, I want to look at what
we must remember about all of this. 
First, we must consider the example of **BALANCE IN THESSALONICA.** Here,
consider 2 Thessalonians 3:14-15.  In
this text, Paul offers two commandments. 
The first is not to associate with the disobedient.  The second, though, is to admonish as a
brother.  At first glance, these commands
appear contradictory, but I think we ought to read them as complementary
instead.  “Don’t associate with” has to
be modified by “admonish”, and “admonish” has to be modified by “don’t
associate with”.  When a Christian leaves
the Lord, things can’t be the same between us. 
This is, after all, someone who has trampled underfoot the Son of God,
counted the blood of the covenant as an unclean thing, and insulted the Spirit
of grace.  We should find this deeply
upsetting.

At the same time, though, our recognition of the seriousness
of the offense should not diminish our love for the offender.  In all of our interactions, we should have
one goal in mind:  to do everything we
can, use every lever, push every button, to convince them to return to
faithfulness.  Jesus didn’t go to Levi’s
house to hang out and have a good time, and that shouldn’t be our approach to
modern-day Levis either.  If we eat with
them, it must be because we have the heart of Christ rather than the heart of
Corinth.  Our entire aim must be to call
the sinner to repentance.

This is further modified by **FAMILY RESPONSIBILITIES.** For
a list of several such responsibilities, look at Colossians 3:18-21.  I’ve said before that baptism doesn’t wash
away marriages, but it’s also true that falling away doesn’t end marriages
either.  Even if your spouse is now disobedient,
they are still your spouse, and that relationship comes with obligations and
duties that you can’t avoid.

The same is true for parents and children.  In the unlikely event that my father fell
away, he would still be my dad, and God would still require me to honor him in
every sense of the word.  Likewise, even
though the relationship between parents and children alters as both grow older,
I don’t believe that fathers ever leave behind the responsibility to offer
spiritual guidance to their children. 
Otherwise, what a man’s children do after they leave the home would have
no bearing on his fitness to serve as an elder. 
When a man’s children become unfaithful, that’s a sign that he needs to
invest more time in that relationship, not less.

Once more, though, we must be careful to avoid a Corinthian
attitude toward the sin of others.  If
our relationship with a relative is just the same when they’re unfaithful as it
would be if they were faithful, it shows that we don’t appreciate the
seriousness of the situation.  We also
must be honest with ourselves about our motivations.  If we aren’t continually talking with them
about the state of their souls, why not? 
Is it really because we’re looking for the right moment to say
something, or is it because we’re afraid of conflict and don’t want to rock the
boat?  We can have great influence on
those closest to us, but we must make sure they aren’t influencing us to
silence.

Finally, let’s look at what the Hebrews writer reveals about
the pursuit of **PEACE AND HOLINESS.** Read with me from Hebrews 12:12-16.  In context, the weak knees appear not to be
our own members, whether literal or metaphorical, but others.  In our interactions with anybody, we should
be looking at two main goals.  The first
is peace, not merely peace between them and us, if possible, but peace between
them and God.  The second is
sanctification, both our holiness and theirs. 
Once again, we must not overvalue either of these things at the expense
of the other.  Keeping watch over our
holiness is important, but it cannot become so important that we never have
anything to do with any kind of sinner. 
Similarly, peace is important, but we cannot be so concerned about
absence of conflict in our earthly relationships that we gloss over truth.

The Biblical principles here are clear.  The application of those principles is going
to vary from situation to situation.  I
know a brother who threw his teenager out of the house because they were
rebellious and wouldn’t listen.  It
completely changed the youngster’s life, and they returned to the Lord and
continue to serve faithfully to this day. 
I’m familiar with other situations where family members maintained a
relationship with the one who had fallen away, but used that relationship for
constant reminders to repent and return. 
After decades, their lost loved one was persuaded to come back.  In all things, our guiding star must be love,
not the worldly counterfeit that winks at sin, but the love that the Lord
revealed for the lost, a love that looks to heaven and does whatever must be
done to get the loved one there.
