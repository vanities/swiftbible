Prayer is when we talk to God; the Bible is God talking to
us.  If I said that once when I was
teaching our four-year-old class this year, I said it several dozen times.  I certainly can’t remember the first time I heard
it.

However, I think this is a proverb that it’s easy for us to
say without internalizing.  Maybe part of
the problem is that on one level, the Bible isn’t written to us.  None of us are members of the first-century
church in Rome.  The burning religious
question of our day is not whether Gentiles have to be circumcised in order to
become part of God’s people.  In fact,
many of the most famous misreadings of Romans have come from people who didn’t
attach sufficient weight to the fact that it was the burning religious question
of Paul’s day.



The Scriptures were written to them:  people who lived thousands of years ago in
modern-day Israel and Turkey and Greece and Italy.  Forgetting the differences between those times
and ours, and even the differences between the time of Moses and the time of
Jesus, is a sure ticket to interpretive disaster.  No; Jeremiah 29:11 is not a promise to
modern-day Americans that soon they’ll be able to afford the house with that
three-car garage.

The Scriptures are written to them, and yet the Scriptures
are written to us too.  Though the New
Testament especially is rooted in a particular time and setting, its message is
for every time and setting.  The question
of circumcision might not be important to us, but Paul’s underlying teaching
about justification by faith certainly is! 
The cultural details change, but the gospel doesn’t.

Because of its inspired genius, the Bible is and always will
be an astonishingly relevant book.  If we
listen (whether literally or by reading), it will invariably tell us things
that we need to hear.  To the honest
heart, the word of God is never out of season.

I found myself reflecting on this at the end of my Bible
reading this morning.  I’ve had a busy
schedule these past few days (massive amounts of car time, for one thing) and
allowed myself to fall behind on my daily reading, so I decided to catch up in
one big chunk.  Among other things, this
took me through Romans 7-9.  I’ve been
using an HCSB for my readings recently (dirty secret:  it’s because I really like the cover), and
I’ve not been terribly thrilled with the way the translation handles the
doctrinal nuances of, say, Romans 5, but I love its rendition of Romans
8:35-39:

Who can separate us from the love of Christ?

Can affliction or anguish or persecution

or famine or nakedness or danger or sword?

As it is written:

Because of You we are being put to death all day long;

we are counted as sheep to be slaughtered.

No, in all these things we are more than victorious

through Him who loved us.

For I am persuaded that not even death or life,

angels or rulers, things present or things to come, hostile
powers,

height or depth, or any other created thing

will have the power to separate us

from the love of God that is in Christ Jesus our Lord!

If ever there was a message for today, this is it!  These days, there seems to be a dark cloud
hanging over the United States.  Many
Christians have the sense that we’re watching a golden age come to its end, and
neither presidential candidate is going to solve the problem.  In addition to these national woes, many of
us also face our own private problems and struggles.

Doesn’t matter.  We
are still more than victorious through Him who loved us, and when I read that
this morning, I literally got goosebumps. 
Even if we’ve made a mess of things, even if we don’t see how,
everything is going to work out for the glory of God.  There will be twists and turns along the way
(nobody wants to be counted as sheep to be slaughtered), but in the end, all
the good guys will be part of God’s overwhelming triumph.  To quote Gandalf the Grey, now that is an
encouraging thought!

Yes; we have to sort through various cultural peculiarities
from 2000 years ago as we seek to understand the Bible.  However, once we’ve got things worked out
(well, mostly worked out), the Scriptures will in turn help us to sort through
the peculiarities of our own culture and our own personal circumstances.  There’s a sense in which my story is unique
and your story is unique.  No two people
are exactly alike.

However, because of the basic humanity we share, the most
important things about each of us are common to all of us.  It is to that core of shared identity that
the Scripture speaks.  It will be
relevant and meaningful and real to every day of our lives, so long as we are
willing to give it the attention it needs to do its work.
