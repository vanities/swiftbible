If you’re thinking about leaving the Lord’s church, or
indeed have already left it, this one’s for you.  People who aren’t are certainly welcome to
read it (yay clicks!), but I’m primarily concerned with those who are in the
midst of walking out of the church building to join some other church or to
join nothing at all.  This is for you.



I certainly haven’t been preaching for as long as many men
have, but I’ve been at it for more than 10 years now.  In that time, I’ve seen more people than I
would like vanish from the pews, never to be seen again.  It seems to me that these people come in two
main categories.  The first is made up of
those who don’t like what the Bible says. 
The Bible says, “Don’t fornicate;” they want to fornicate.  The Bible says, “Don’t be a drunk;” they want
to be drunks.  And so on.

Bluntly put, these folks don’t want to be Christians because
they don’t want to follow Christ.  They
would rather be worldly, immoral, and ungodly instead.  They prefer the lust of the flesh, the lust
of the eye, or the pride of life to doing the will of God.

If that’s how you feel, well, Jesus isn’t for you, and His
church isn’t for you.  Now, I think
that’s a disastrous way to feel, and I hope you will change, but religion based
on anything other than love for God and desire to keep His commandments will
never be more than a sham.  If you don’t
have that love and desire, there’s not much point in going through the motions
to fool somebody.

Thus for Door Number One. 
However, in my experience, there’s another reason why Christians leave
the church.  They love God, and they care
about serving Him, but they’ve been burned. 
They were struggling spiritually, and a brother in Christ offered a boot
to the rear instead of a helping hand. 
They trusted a sister and shared a confidence, only to find their dirty
laundry spread through the entire congregation. 
They asked a question in Bible class and got a smackdown instead of an
answer.  They found themselves in the
midst of an entire church of narrow-minded, self-righteous legalists who were
less concerned with “It is written” than with “We’ve always done”.

There are often two sides to these various stories,
especially the last one.  I’m not
terribly concerned here with sorting out the objective rights and wrongs.  Some church messes would defy the wisdom of
Solomon, much less me!  Instead, I’m
concerned with the subjective feeling of grave offense, with the Christians who
walk away from the church filled with the bitter certainty that they have been
betrayed by their brethren.

For you, I have five words. 
Don’t hold Christians against Christ.

The Lord’s design both for His disciples and His church is
flawless.  Generally, our execution of
both leaves much to be desired. 
Christians can be (and often are) loud, obstinate, short-sighted,
insecure, and even downright evil.  In
groups, they have an astonishing knack for blundering their way into folly and
disaster.  I myself try to be the wise
teacher and the helping hand, but I’ve probably been the smackdown artist and
the boot to the rear.  I say “probably”
because even if I can’t think of anything, I know that I’m capable of engaging
in immense stupidity without even realizing it.

That’s not Christ’s fault. 
It’s my fault, and it’s not fair to blame the Master for the failures of
the messenger.  He is, after all,
everything that I am not:  perfectly
humble, perfectly wise, perfectly righteous, and perfectly loving.  He has never failed anybody.  Where we in His sandals might regard mankind
with contempt, He regards us with compassion. 
Rather than rejecting the imperfect, He calls them to Himself.

His calling, though, is not merely to follow Him on a
lone-ranger basis.  Instead, in our
imperfection, we are supposed to follow Him with other imperfect people.  That’s what a church is:  a collection of various forms of
imperfection.

In the church, we’re supposed to learn from the strengths of
others, and through the years, I've benefited immeasurably from the good
example of countless brethren.  However, we
are also supposed to learn from our encounters with others’ weaknesses.  They give us the opportunity to show mercy in
imitation of Christ.

Do all those obstinate, short-sighted, and occasionally evil
Christians deserve mercy?  Of course
not!  No one ever deserves mercy.  That is its essence.  They don’t deserve mercy, but we’re supposed
to extend mercy to them anyway, just as Jesus Himself showed mercy to us,
though we ourselves did not deserve it.

When we get fed up and leave instead, we are refusing to
learn one of the most important lessons that Jesus has to teach.  He wants us to stick around.  He wants us to sort through the mess.  Most of all, He wants us to learn to bear with
and forgive, as the Lord has forgiven us.

When we leave, then, insisting that our love for Christ is
undiminished, but we simply can’t handle Those Christians Over There anymore, we
are attempting to separate the inseparable. 
Inevitably, the way we treat one another shows our regard for Him.  We love, not because Christians are such a
lovable bunch, but because He first loved us, and we cannot reject His calling
and example without rejecting Him.
