The other day, I encountered [this article](http://www.msn.com/en-us/news/us/the-evangelical-scion-who-stopped-believing/ar-BBxJWVX) on the MSN news page.  It tells the story of Bart Campolo, the son of a famous evangelical preacher who began
his career as an evangelical preacher himself but since has become a “preacher”
of secular humanism.  It’s tough to judge
tone in writing, but I get the sense that the author of the article regards
this as a good thing and hopes that Campolo will be a trailblazer for others
who will abandon conservative Christianity *en
masse*.  What he offers is a kind of
church for atheism, one that encourages community and kindness toward others while avoiding
all that yucky theism-miracles-and-eternal-punishment stuff.

Certainly, I’m in favor of anybody being kind, and I don’t
think Campolo is any less saved now than he was 30 seconds after he recited the
sinner’s prayer, but I don’t see his proclamation of the good news of atheism as
the start of anything big.  As the
article observes, atheist communities are notably hard to get off the ground,
and despite all of Campolo’s charisma, I believe he’ll encounter the same issues.



The first of these problems is the incoherence of the system
of ethics that he preaches.  Be nice to
people, he says.  Be kind.  Be joyful. 
Lead a good life without God.

OK.  Great.  Why?

It’s certainly not because doing so generates some kind of
external good.  For instance, in my time
as a preacher, I’ve spent countless hours sitting alone with and talking with
shut-ins—a nice thing to do, right? 
Because shut-ins are necessarily in poor health, most of those people
since have died.  Nobody remembers the
time I spent with them but me, and life goes on unheeding.  I didn’t change the world.  In earthly terms, at least, I didn’t make any
kind of difference.  Not a whole lot of
motivation to spend my time with shut-ins there.

In response, I think Campolo would say that even if my
actions didn’t make any difference to anybody else, they made a difference to
me.  I came away from all that time with
the knowledge that I had done the right thing. 
I got an altruistic warm fuzzy.

That’s true as far as it goes.  I do get altruistic warm fuzzies from
visiting shut-ins.  However, if we only
do the right thing because it makes us feel good, that’s nothing more than
hedonism with an ethical gloss.  The
underlying moral of the story isn’t “Do the right thing.”  It’s “Do what makes you feel good.”

Therefore, if doing the wrong thing makes me feel good,
that’s what I should do.  Being faithful
to my wife is the right thing to do, but adultery sure looks like a lot of fun,
so I’m going to try that instead. 
Treating all people equally is the right thing to do, but I really enjoy
the sense of superiority that comes from racism, so I’m going to be a
racist.  There’s no real system of ethics
here.  It’s Judges
21:25 redux.

I don’t think Campolo’s flock is necessarily going to be
smart enough to work all that out logically for themselves, but they will
certainly sense the problem and behave accordingly.

Second, Campolo’s attempts to found an inclusive community
of the faithless will run into exactly the same problem as others’ attempts to
found an inclusive community of the faithful. 
During the last part of his preaching career, some of his friends
observed that he was really a Unitarian Universalist in evangelical
clothing.  However, surveys reveal that the
UU’s and other similarly liberal denominations are shrinking at a fantastic
rate.

They all share the same problem:  communities define themselves as much by who
does not belong as by who does, and the members of a community that is
indistinguishable from the wider world will soon vanish into that world.  There’s no particularly compelling reason to
remain an Episcopalian, for instance. 
You don’t have to affirm your belief in anything.  They’re not going to hold you to a standard
of conduct.  They’re not going to tell
you that you’re going to hell if you leave. 
That leaves. . . staying because you like the liturgy, I guess?

Inevitably, groups that don’t offer a strong self-definition
are going to have all the cohesion of a strip of soggy toilet paper.  This leaves Campolo with two choices for his
humanist flock.  Either they stick to
come-as-you-are inclusiveness and fall apart, or they opt for a form of
exclusion that isn’t logically coherent. 
Sure, you might get people together by shouting, “Down with racists and
homophobes!”, but why?  If the answer is,
“Because I don’t like racists and homophobes,” how does that make me any
different from them?  Certainly, it’s not
possible for a secular humanist to prove that racism and homophobia are
objectively wrong.

Finally, Campolo must contend with the logical weakness of
secular humanism generally.  If it is
possible for the human race to improve itself and its world, recorded history
hasn’t shown much sign of it.  Admittedly,
there have been all sorts of technological advances over the past several millennia,
but those advances have not been matched by equivalent moral growth.  The recent atrocities in Aleppo would be
entirely familiar to an eyewitness to the siege of Jerusalem in 70 AD.  We haven’t had a world war in 60 years, but
sooner or later, there will be another one, and when it comes, it will come
with unprecedented savagery and fury.

The history of mankind is and always will be marked by people doing
awful things to others.  Only someone (and judging from the picture of Campolo's rally, a white, upper-middle-class someone) who lives in the Pollyanna-ish setting
of the early 21st-century United States could imagine
otherwise.  Our peaceful lives are very much
the exception, not the rule, and our descendants probably will not fare as well
as we do.

To paraphrase Tolkien, there is no hope in men.  Humanists like to make much of the problem
that the existence of evil poses for believers, but it poses even more of a
problem for them.  We live in a horribly
broken, sin-marred world, and it is beyond our power to mend.  That gives us two alternatives:  a stark and bitter nihilism, or trust in a
Redeemer who promises to lift us up by a single, transcendent act
of grace.  Campolo is trying to find a
third way, but like all those who have looked for it before him, in the end, he’s
going to be disappointed.
