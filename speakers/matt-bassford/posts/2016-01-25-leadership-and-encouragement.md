One of the most common New-Testament analogues for the
church is the human body.  There are
several ways in which is this comparison is useful, but one of them is
certainly the differences that exist among the members of the body.  My body is not a big undifferentiated blob,
nor is it a collection of any single body part. 
Instead, I am made up of different parts with different and clearly
defined roles.  I don’t try to brush my teeth
with my feet.  I use my hands instead.

In the same way, individual Christians in the body of the
Lord have different, defined roles. 
Every one of us has something we’re supposed do, or else the church
suffers.  Paul provides us with a list of
such things in Romans 12, and two items on the list are leaders and those who
encourage.  If these things are part of
our gifts, we need to learn to perfect our skills.  Even if they aren’t, all of us can help at
least somewhat.  Let’s consider God’s
take on leadership and encouragement.

 **Christ, Our Leader**

An excellent place for us to begin this study is by looking
at the greatest leader of them all, Christ. 
The book of Hebrews identifies at least three different places that He
leads us, and a study of them will help us to prepare our minds for the Lord’s
Supper.  The first of them is **OUTSIDE THE CAMP**.  Here, let’s read from Hebrews 13:11-14.  The gospel narratives tell us that Jesus was
crucified on a hill outside the city of Jerusalem.  The Hebrews writer reveals, though, that this
seemingly insignificant fact has both typical and practical significance.

First, Jesus’ death outside the city links Him with the
sacrifices for sin that were burned outside the camp of the Israelites.  Second, it provides us with an example.  Jesus suffered outside the gate, so we have
to go to Him there, bearing the same shame that He bore.

This is not an easy cross for Christians to take up.  Our society likes to pretend that it’s very
open-minded and tolerant, but with every year, it grows increasingly intolerant
of those who don’t toe the line of secular humanism.  If we don’t act like the godless people
around us act, if we don’t talk like they do, and especially if we proclaim
Jesus as Lord when they don’t, we’re going to stand out for certain.  We probably aren’t going to get people
confronting us about it, but they are going to think differently about us
because of it, and they may well talk about us behind our backs.

Brethren, that’s simply the way it is if we want to follow
Jesus.  He didn’t get nailed to a cross because
everybody in His society liked Him so much. 
Having people in the world dislike us is part of the definition of
discipleship.  Either we want to be
disciples, or we don’t.

On a brighter note, Jesus leads us **WITHIN THE VEIL**.  Take a look
at Hebrews 6:19-20.  As is usually the
case in Hebrews, this is part of a comparison to something in the Old
Testament.  It’s a reference to the
Israelite high priest going through the veil of the tabernacle into the Most
Holy Place one time a year to make atonement for the sins that the people had
committed ignorantly.  The Hebrews writer
wants us to understand that Jesus has done that for us, only not in the
tabernacle or the temple, but in the true Most Holy Place, in the presence of
God in heaven.

However, the text presents Jesus not only as our intercessor
but also as our forerunner.  He went into
the presence of God so that we can go there too, which is something that
Christians do every time we pray.  A lot
of people in the world will pray to God without even thinking to ask whether
they have the right to pray, and their thoughtlessness gets them about what you
would expect.  Sin separates us from God
and causes Him not to hear our prayers. 
Only the people who have been sprinkled by the blood of Jesus can expect
to come before Him and be heard.

The right to pray, particularly the right to seek
forgiveness of sins, is one of the most precious privileges that mankind can
have.  It’s tragic when Christians who
have this privilege rarely or never take advantage of it.  Let’s be people who continually go where
Jesus has gone for us.

Finally, Jesus leads us **TO
THE JOY SET BEFORE US**.  The Hebrews
writer explains in Hebrews 12:1-3.  This
passage brings together two of the themes we’ve seen already this morning:  the great hostility shown by sinners to Jesus
and the great reward He received.

We’re supposed to follow Him in both things.  As in so many places in Scripture, the theme
here is “Keep going.”  Keep going past
the sins that Satan uses to try to lure us from the path of Christ.  Keep going past all the reminders of the ways
we messed up in the past.  Keep going
through all the abuse that the world and even some Christians will dish out for
those who are determined to follow the Lord.

If we endure like Jesus endured, we will get what He
got.  We don’t have to earn our way to
heaven on our own.  We don’t have to be
whiz-bang amazing Christians who get written up in the history books.  All we have to do is keep going and not
quit.  This doesn’t have to be forever.  It only has to be until we die or the Lord
returns.  I’ve heard it said that people
can stand anything if they know it’s not going to last.  Our troubles aren’t going to last
either.  If we keep going, we win.

In preparation for the Lord’s Supper, let’s ask ourselves a
question.  We often sing the hymn, “Who
Will Follow Jesus?”  It’s kind of a
bouncy, upbeat number, but as we’ve seen, this is a sober question.  Following Jesus means daring the scorn of the
world.  Following Jesus means being
devoted to prayer.  Following Jesus means
outlasting every sin and hardship for the sake of a reward in heaven.  Who will follow Jesus?  Am I following Jesus?  Let’s consider this as we partake.

**An Unlikely Hero**

Now that we’ve considered what we should do to follow our
Leader, let’s study what we can do to lead and encourage others.  Our example today will be an unlikely hero
from the book of Judges, the judge and prophetess Deborah.  She’s an unlikely hero because she is, to be
precise, a heroine.  We see **THE PROBLEM** that she confronts in
Judges 4:1-3.  Like many of our problems,
this one has both an external and a spiritual dimension.  The external dimension is Jabin king of
Canaan and his head goon Sisera, who have invaded with 900 iron chariots and
made the lives of the Israelites miserable. 
This external problem, however, wouldn’t be a problem at all if it were
not for the spiritual problem that preceded it. 
Jabin and friends are able to run wild in Israel because the Israelites
have been pursuing strange gods and have forfeited God’s protection.

This should remind us, friends, that in our lives too, many
if not most of our external problems have spiritual roots.  If we try to address the external without
considering the spiritual, we’re going to fail. 
For instance, let’s say that we’re unhappy with our spouses because we
don’t like the way they’ve been acting recently.  Fill in the blank here for every marriage,
I’m sure.  If we try to supply an
external solution by parking said spouse on the couch and expressing our
unhappiness, usually that solution turns out not to be a solution at all!  Instead, we need to consider the spiritual
roots of our external problem.  How have
I been falling short of the Lord’s expectations in the way I treat my
spouse?  How have I been creating
resentment that has led them to respond? 
Once we get the spiritual side of things straightened out, that usually
helps us with the external too.

Next, we meet **DEBORAH**herself, in Judges 4:4-7.  To me, her
conduct here exemplifies two things: 
recognition of her role and willingness to help within it.  There were some important things that Deborah
could do.  She could judge Israel and
even proclaim the word of the Lord. 
However, because she was a woman, she was clearly unsuited to be a war
leader.  In that day, commanders were
expected to fight in the ranks with their men, and having a general who wasn’t
trained to fight or even physically capable of fighting was a serious handicap.  Deborah knew her limitations, so she went to
Barak and invited him to lead where she couldn’t.

Similarly, friends, if we want to lead and encourage, we
need to have a handle on what we can and can’t do.  Everybody can be a Barnabas.  There are no age or gender requirements on
quietly encouraging our brethren in their work. 
However, not everybody can be an Apollos.  Statistically speaking, more than half of all
Christians aren’t male, and even of those who are, a substantial number would rather
have their toenails pulled out with pliers than preach a sermon.

To put things another way, we need to abide by the Lord’s
rules for our roles in the kingdom.  No
matter how much we need elders, I don’t get to be an elder yet.  I’m not qualified.  We also need to serve according to our
gifts.  Not everybody is good at everything,
even if the Scripture allows them to do it. 
At the same time, though, we must not mistake fear for ungiftedness.

However, Barak raises **AN
OBJECTION**.  The story unfolds in
Judges 4:8-10.  It’s obvious that Barak
is cherishing some doubts about the whole enterprise.  He wants to see if Deborah is as confident
about God’s will as she claims to be. 
He’ll risk his life in battle only if she does the same.  In response, Deborah warns him that it’s
going to cost him some of the glory he would otherwise have had, but she’s
willing to share the danger, even though she’s only a woman.

This might be the most important lesson in the whole
chapter.  It’s hard to be a leader if you
aren’t out in front.  It’s hard to
encourage somebody to work if they can look over and see that you’re not
working.  I’ve known brethren who missed
the boat on this one.  They were great at
coming up with work for me to do, but they weren’t about to share in the work
with me.  Even when I did what they
wanted me to do, I generally did it with a smirk.

In other words, friends, we can’t only be leaders.  We have to be servant-leaders.  We can’t be shouting encouragement from the
stands.  We have to be on the field.  Leadership doesn’t consist of saying, “Be
imitators of Christ, while I sit over here and eat popcorn.”  Instead, we will only be able to make an
effective appeal if we can say, “Be imitators of me, as I am of Christ.”

**God’s Victory**

The second part of the story of Deborah tells us about the
victory that God wins.  This begins when **THE CANAANITES GATHER**.  Judges 4:11-14 describes these events.  There appears to be some cognitive dissonance
in this section.  On the one hand, we see
the same 900 chariots that have been oppressing the Israelites for 20 years
mustering to do the same thing.  On the
other, we see Deborah promising victory against this undefeated force.  What gives?

Of course, it’s Deborah’s words that supply us with the
answer.  She’s so confident because she
knows that this time God is going to be fighting for the Israelites, and that’s
going to make all the difference.  They
were doomed without God, but with Him, they’re going to be unstoppable.

Our experience will be no different.  The biggest problems that we have in life
aren’t the ones that are outside of us.  They
are the ones inside of us.  They are all
the places where we know what God says but reply, “I don’t wanna.”  Those are the things that introduce problems
into our marriages and other relationships. 
Those are the things that hold our congregation back from being what God
wants it to be.  If we want change for
the better, it has to start with the heart. 
If we give those hearts over to God, the external problems that appear
so daunting will fold like a cheap suit. 
That’s always true.  A godly
leader will remind the Christians around him of its truth.

Inevitably, then, when the two armies come together, **THE ISRAELITES TRIUMPH**.  The completeness of this victory is recounted
in Judges 4:15-22.  When I read this
story, I notice two things:  how God
delivers the expected, but also how God delivers the unexpected.  Let’s start with the expected first.  If we spend any time in the Old Testament at
all, we quickly realize that when God fights for the Israelites, the result is
invariably bad guys lying dead in heaps. 
So it is here.  Their vaunted iron
chariots do the Canaanites no good. 
Instead, they are killed to a man.

We see the unexpected, however, emerge when Sisera dies at
the hand of Jael, the wife of Heber the Kenite. 
This is weird on any number of levels. 
First of all, there’s peace between Heber and Sisera’s boss.  The iron chariots haven’t been messing with
Heber and his household, so they don’t mess with them.  Second, Jael actively offers hospitality to
Sisera.  She offers him a refuge inside
her tent, she gives him some milk to drink, she promises to lie to anybody
coming by, and she practically tucks him in for his nap.  Then, once he’s asleep, she hammers a tent
peg all the way through his skull and into the ground!

This is not how things went down in the Middle East 3500
years ago.  Back then, hospitality was a
sacred responsibility.  Once you offered
somebody hospitality, you were responsible for treating them like a member of
your own family.  It was simply not done
to kill people you invited into your own home. 
On the basis of this custom, Sisera had every reason to trust that Jael
would look out for him and take care of him, but what he got was a nail through
the temple.  It’s baffling that Jael does
this.  The Bible never explains her
motive for such a treacherous act.  All
we know is that God made it happen because He had said through Deborah that He
would.

Today, we should act in such a way that we will benefit both
from God’s expected blessings and His unexpected blessings.  There are some ways that God works in our
lives that we almost take for granted because they’re so familiar.  I know that if I pray for wisdom, God will
help me to become wiser.  I know that if
the need arises, God will take care of me through His people.  I greatly appreciate all of those things, but
I’m not exactly surprised when they happen.

However, we also need to seek God in the areas where we
aren’t expecting good things to happen. 
Perhaps the foremost example here is evangelism.  Through the years, I’ve seen some of the most
unlikely people obey the gospel.  I’ve
seen people who had little to nothing to do with the church their whole lives
give those lives to the Lord after a single study.  It’s completely unpredictable, and when we
refuse to talk about Jesus with somebody because We Just Know they’ll never
listen, we may well be rejecting an opportunity to sow seed in a fertile
field.

Finally, we see the Israelites **FOLLOW THROUGH** on their victory. 
This is simply described in Judges 4:23-24.  Sure, Sisera and his army are all dead, but
Jabin is still king in Hazor.  Deborah
and Barak, along with the rest of the Israelites, make sure that he doesn’t
stay there.  Now they’ve got the
advantage, and they press it until his kingdom is destroyed.

Here, we encounter a problem that isn’t only a Christian
problem, but is also a human-being problem generally.  Last Monday, I saw a Facebook meme that
declared the day Official Give-Up-On-Your-Resolutions Day.  It’s 18 days in.  We’ve already blown it.  Why keep up the pretense?

God’s answer to the persistence problems of mankind is
leadership.  It’s striking that in Romans
12, the attribute that Paul most associates with leaders is diligence.  Leadership means saying, “Hey, everybody; we
need to go this way!” and then continuing to lead everybody that way.  It means making sure that those who follow
don’t get distracted and wander off by January 18th.

This is hard.  I know
that I myself have plenty of trouble sticking with the things that I have
determined to do, much less coming up with the emotional energy I need to help
others stick to the Lord’s plans.  This
is where study and prayer come in as a daily part of the leader’s spiritual
diet.  With God’s help, we can help
others be diligent in serving Him too.
