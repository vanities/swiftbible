Many of us know the children’s song that goes, “Read your
Bible, pray every day, and you’ll grow, grow, grow!”  Most of the rest would agree with its
sentiments.  Preachers from all across
the Christian spectrum will assert that believers ought to spend more time
reading their Bibles--on a daily basis, if possible.

However, we rarely give as much thought to the tools we use
for the task.  It is as though to us
“Bible” exists in some idealized space, and we don’t consider the effect that
the actual, physical Bible we use will have on our reading comprehension or
even on the likelihood that we will read at all.



In extreme cases, this effect is obvious.  Take, for instance, the older Christian with
failing eyesight who is handed a compact Bible and told to read from it.  Try as he will, the words on that page will
never become more than a meaningless pattern of dots and squiggles.  His heart is defeated by his Bible.

Even if our own difficulties are not so dramatic, format has
at least some effect on all of us.  The
list of physical attributes that affect our ease of reading is long:  text font, text size, paper opacity, presence
or absence of line matching, and many more. 
The scope of this post, though, is limited to only one such factor:  line length.

Line length is important because of the way that we
read.  My four-year-old daughter, who is
in the process of learning to read, reads at a letter-by-letter level:  “Are-eh-tee. . . Urrrrr-aaaaahhhh-tuh. . .
Rat!”  As she progresses, she will learn
to take in entire words at a glance.

Most of us, though, who are accustomed to reading, read more
than one word per glance, often many more. 
This is something that any of us can test.  Pick up a Bible with a long line length, say,
any of Crossway’s single-column ESV offerings. 
Open it to a random page.  Glance
down at a random line.  How many of the
words on that line make immediate sense to you, and how many are outside of
your focus?  Count the number of
characters, including spaces, in the chunk of text that makes sense.  To coin a phrase, that number is your scan
length.

We will have the easiest time reading a Bible in which the
average line length is as close as possible to our scan length.  Glancing at a line takes a certain amount of
effort and concentration.  In a Bible
where the line length corresponds to our scan length, that glance is as
meaningful as possible, so reading is as easy as possible.

For example, my own scan length is a little less 60
characters.  Let’s say I’m reading a
Bible with a line length of 50 characters. 
I can entirely comprehend a line with a single glance, and that glance
comes away with 5/6 of the meaning that it can possibly hold.  That’s good.

On the other hand, when I come to a Bible with a line length
of 70 characters, I start having problems. 
I glance down at a 70-character line and come away with 60 characters’
worth of meaning.  So far, so good.

However, I still have the remaining 10 characters on the
line to contend with.  I’m going to pick
one of two options.  Either I’m going to
guess at those last 10 characters from context and move on to the next full
line, or I’m going to spend my next full glance on those characters.

Both options are bad. 
In the first case, I might guess wrong and lose comprehension.  In the second case, my glance only holds 10
characters of the 60 characters that it could. 
That’s like hauling freight in a truck that’s 5/6 empty.  It’s inefficient, it slows me down, and it
takes me out of the text.

Of course, if my scan length were 35 characters, a
70-character Bible would be great.  It
would take me two glances to read each line of prose, and every glance would be
fully freighted with meaning.  A
double-column Bible with a line length of 35 characters would be equally
efficient, or perhaps even more so (my eye would not have to return back to the
left at the end of every line).

Similar efficiency concerns arise with poetry in
double-column Bibles.  Most double-column
settings have line lengths of 40 or fewer characters.  However, most poetic lines in the Psalms, for
instance, are longer than 40 characters. 
This means that the setting frequently breaks poetic lines and often
leaves “widows” of one word, or even one syllable, in the next line down.  When I read such a setting, my glance at the
first part of the line comes away with 40 characters’ worth of meaning, which
is fine.  However, my glance at the widow
below it may only yield 5 characters’ worth, which is extremely inefficient.

These numbers will vary from reader to reader, and from
this, we must conclude that there is no such thing as a one-size-fits-all,
perfect reading Bible.  In my case, many
of the single-column settings that others love give me fits.  Each of us must determine the most effective
setting for him- or herself, either through trial and error or by determining
personal scan length and evaluating settings accordingly.  I’ve spent a lot of quality time counting
characters per line in the Bible excerpts on christianbook.com!  This may seem like a lot of work, but when we
consider the importance of fully comprehending the Scripture, the time we
invest in it is time well spent.
