As has been true for a couple hundred years now, Jesus is
extremely popular among Americans.  This
might seem strange to us; after all, we live in an era where more and more
people pay less and less attention to the Bible.  I would suggest, though, that these
apparently contradictory data are causally related.  I think Jesus is beloved by so many precisely
because they know so little about what He actually taught, and if they were to
learn the truth, they wouldn’t like Him so much.

In fact, that’s pretty much the way that things went when He
was still on the earth.  Sure, He had a
lot to say about mercy and love and all the cuddly stuff, but He also stepped
on a lot of toes and pierced a lot of hearts. 
Today, we’re going to look at a series of confrontations from Mark that
illustrates the problems people had with Him. 
Let’s consider Jesus, the stumbling stone.



**Authority**

The first of these confrontations is over the subject of
authority.  It begins with an exchange
about **THE BAPTISM OF JOHN.** Look at Mark 11:27-33.  Jesus goes to the temple with His disciples,
and immediately He is challenged by the chief priests, the scribes, and the
elders.  These are some heavy hitters!  It may well be that most of the Sanhedrin is
on hand at this point.  They demand to
know by what authority Jesus is doing “these things”.  What things?

This is a question that we have to answer with context.  Earlier in Mark 11, we see that Jesus had
just been to the temple the day before, and while He was there, He had chased
out all the moneylenders and animal sellers, flipping tables over and generally
trashing the place.  Now that He has
returned to the scene of the crime, so to speak, the Jewish leadership accosts
Him to demand an explanation for what they thought of as His outrageous
behavior in cleansing the temple.

However, there’s another layer of meaning to this
demand.  By this point in time, Jesus has
already raised Lazarus from the dead, which means that the Sanhedrin has
already met and has already decided that Jesus has to die.  This is their first attempt on His life,
because no matter how He answers the question, He will provide them with reason
to condemn Him.  If He says, “I did this
on my own authority,” then they arrest Him for profaning the sacred grounds of
the temple.  If He says, “I did this on
my Father’s authority,” then they arrest Him for claiming to be the Son of God.  Provided all of this goes smoothly and
quietly, they’ve got Him either way.

As clever as they are, though, Jesus is even cleverer.  He knows there’s a tiger behind Door Number 1
and Door Number 2 both, so he refuses to open either one.  Instead, He asks them an authority-related
question.  He wants them to tell Him
whether the baptism of John was from heaven or from men.  Now, the chief priests find themselves on the
horns of a dilemma.  They never went to
be baptized by John, so if they say it was from heaven, they condemn
themselves.  If they say it was from men,
they offend the people, who believed John to be a prophet.  They too can tell that there’s a tiger behind
each door, so they refuse to walk through either one.  That suits Jesus just fine, because it gives
Him an excuse not to answer them.  The
first attempt thus ends in failure.

However, now that Jesus has parried their first thrust, He
counterattacks with **THE PARABLE OF THE
VINE-GROWERS.** Let’s read here from
Mark 12:1-12.  The way that Jesus begins
this parable is not original to Him. 
Instead, it’s taken from the fifth chapter of the book of Isaiah, as all
of His listeners would have known.  Also,
I think this is another one of Jesus’ speeches with a visual aid.  As Jesus is talking about the wall and the
tower of the vineyard, it’s easy to imagine Him gesturing to the walls and buildings
of the temple.  Immediately we see Jesus
beginning this parable in a way that is both Scripturally and spatially
relevant.

However, after that opening, the parable takes a different
turn.  In Isaiah, the problem is that the
vineyard should have produced good grapes but only produced worthless ones.
Figuratively, this stood for the nation of Judah, which should have served God
but instead devoted itself to sin.  For
that, God was going to uproot Judah like an unproductive vineyard.

In Jesus’ parable, though, the problem isn’t the
vineyard.  The vineyard is producing just
fine.  Instead, the problem is the
vine-growers, who are keeping the vineyard’s produce for themselves instead of
giving it to the rightful owner.  When he
sends messengers to warn them, the vine-growers beat the messengers up.  Finally, he sends his son to demand what is
rightfully his, but the vine-growers kill the son in the misguided notion that
they will get to keep the vineyard. 
Jesus warns that this will lead the vineyard owner to dispossess and
destroy the vine-growers.

Of course, the Jewish leaders get the point
immediately.  In Jesus’ parable, they are
the vine-growers.  With all the animal
sellers and moneychangers in the temple, they are the ones who are taking the
sincere devotion of the people and subverting it for their own gain.  They are the ones who are rejecting the
prophets God sends to warn them.  Indeed,
they are the ones who are going to kill Jesus Himself, in the notion that
somehow this will allow them to keep their racket going.  Jesus points out, though, that this can only
end in double failure.  Because of their
sin, God will remove them from the leadership of His people and give that
headship to Jesus instead.

What’s particularly brilliant about Jesus’ parable is that
even though He’s implying all sorts of things the chief priests don’t like,
He’s not actually saying any of it.  At
this point, they have no excuse to arrest Him. 
They’re just about mad enough to grab Him anyway and let all the excuses
come after the fact, but they’ve got another problem.  Jesus has drawn a crowd, and if they arrest
an innocent prophet, they will most likely start a riot.  They’ve been publicly humiliated, and there’s
not a thing they can do about it.  They
stomp off, and round one goes to Jesus.

**Death and Taxes**

The second confrontation between Jesus and the Jewish
leadership concerns the subjects of death and taxes.  Technically, I guess this should be taxes and
the resurrection, but in life, there are some bad jokes that you just can’t
avoid.  Let’s start with the subject of **PAYING TAXES** to Caesar.  Consider the events of Mark 12:13-17.  Right off the bat, there’s something funny
going on here.  The text tells us that
Jesus was accosted by a mixed group of Pharisees and Herodians.  The problem is, though, that the Pharisees
and the Herodians never associated with one another of their own volition.  The Pharisees wanted to get rid of the
Romans, but the Herodians were Roman collaborators.  It’s definitely a case of oil and water
mixing!

What has brought them together, of course, is that even
though they don’t agree on anything else, they agree on the need to take Jesus
down.  As a result, they come to Him with
a trick question.  It seems
straightforward enough:  should you pay
taxes to Caesar?

However, this is actually another attempt to put Jesus on
the horns of a dilemma.  If He says no,
you shouldn’t pay taxes to Caesar, which is what the Pharisees believe, the
Herodians will have Him arrested for plotting against the Roman
government.  If He says yes, you should
pay taxes to Caesar, which is what the Herodians believe, the Pharisees will
accuse Him of being a Roman collaborator, and He’ll lose His popular support.  This is complete hypocrisy—after all, they
themselves don’t agree on the answer—but they think it will be good enough to
trap Jesus.

Once again, when Jesus is presented with a choice between
two doors with tigers behind them, He finds a third way.  This time, He finds it by answering a
question in a way that they had not anticipated.  He asks for a denarius, they bring it to Him,
and He asks whose likeness and inscription are on it.  This is a Roman province, they use Roman
money, so it’s got a Roman emperor on it, probably Augustus or Tiberius.  Jesus then points out that if it’s got
Caesar’s stamp on it, to Caesar it should go. 
However, He also points out that God should have what belongs to
Him.  His face doesn’t appear on any
coins, but every human being is made in His image and likeness.  Our taxes, then, go to Caesar, but our lives
go to God.

The text tells us that Jesus’ opponents marveled greatly at
Him, and it’s not hard to see why.  They
thought they had Jesus in a trap, but His answer is so perfect that nobody can
find fault with it.  The Herodians got what
they claimed to want—agreement that you should pay taxes to Caesar—but the
Pharisees got what they claimed to want too—agreement that you should give your
life to God.

Of these two rhetorical traps Jesus has set, the one for the
Pharisees is subtler.  It looks like they
could take up the cry, “Jesus said to collaborate with the Romans”, but Jesus
hasn’t left them the room they need to argue back.  After all, they have to agree that we should
serve God because we’re created in His image, and once they do that, they have
to accept the logic that Caesar gets what’s in his image too.  They’ve been checkmated, and they know it.

As impressive as all of this is, let’s be sure that we don’t
get so distracted by the rhetorical fireworks that we miss the point.  This isn’t simply about the Pharisees giving
to God what is God’s.  It’s about us
doing the same.  When we’re doing our
taxes, eventually we arrive at a finite amount we have to pay.  It’s usually a larger amount than we would
prefer, but when we write the check, the debt is discharged in full.  That’s not how serving God is.  We can’t discharge our obligation by showing
up here on Sundays and Wednesdays.  God
has to be first of all, but He also has to be first in all.  Whether we’re on the job or with our families
or hanging out with friends, all those things have to belong to God too.

Now that Jesus has homered off the Pharisees and Herodians,
the Jewish leaders go to the bullpen and bring out the Sadducees.  They come with their best stuff, which is a
question about **THE RESURRECTION.** We see how this encounter goes in Mark
12:18-27.  The Sadducees argued that
there was no resurrection, that once you were dead, you stayed dead.  One gets the feeling that the story about the
woman who married seven brothers is the Sadducee equivalent of asking, “But
what about the thief on the cross?”. 
It’s the argument they trot out every time the subject comes up.  If there is a resurrection, then the one
woman will have seven husbands who are all brothers, which would have been
unacceptable to Jewish thinking on any number of levels.

Jesus, however, demolishes their killer argument by informing
them that marriage is an earthly rite only. 
Because there is no marriage in heaven, the story of the woman married
to seven brothers presents no problem. 
He then proceeds to prove that the Sadducees should have known that
there was a resurrection simply from reading the Pentateuch.  When God spoke to Moses at the burning bush,
He said, “I am the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.”  At that time, all three of those men had
died, but God is the God of the living, not the dead.  If God identifies Himself as One who is still
their God, they must still be alive.  The
Sadducees are dead wrong.

Of course, as impressive as this logical proof is, it pales
in comparison to the proof of the resurrection that Jesus would offer in less
than a week.  All this is taking place on
Tuesday.  On Friday, Jesus would be
crucified, and on Sunday, He would rise from the dead.  After that event, more than 500 people
wouldn’t have to turn to Exodus anymore to prove the resurrection, because they
themselves would have seen and heard and even touched the risen Lord.  Today, we are gathered to celebrate that same
death, burial, and resurrection, not because we have seen, but because we have
believed.  Jesus is the Son of God.  It’s shown in His words, as He argued better
than any man ever could, but it’s shown even more clearly in His power.  Let’s contemplate these things as we share
together in the bread and in the cup.

**Love and Lordship**

In the third section of our story, things get quite a bit
less combative as Jesus and some others discuss the topics of love and
Lordship.  This begins with a question
about **THE GREATEST COMMANDMENT.** Look at Mark 12:28-34.  First of all, let’s take note of the
difference between the questioner here and the questioners in the previous two
sections.  The Jewish leaders, the
Pharisees, the Herodians, and the Sadducees were all trying to trap Jesus, with
the goal of getting Him arrested and executed. 
That’s not the case here.  This
unnamed scribe doesn’t appear to be part of any plot.  He simply happened to be on the temple
grounds while the previous discussions were taking place, was impressed with
Jesus’ answers, and decided to ask Him a question not meant to result in His
death.

This is not the first time Jesus is asked such a question;
we see a lawyer ask Him the same thing in Luke 10, right before the parable of
the Good Samaritan.  I don’t get the
sense that either the lawyer there or the scribe here don’t know the answer and
want Jesus to tell them.  Instead, it’s
almost like the question is an entrance exam. 
An expert in the Law would ask somebody this to determine whether he was
an expert in the Law too.

Unlike the other questions, Jesus answers this one straight
up, the scribe praises Jesus’ answer, and Jesus in turn praises the scribe’s
answer.  At this point, nobody else asks
Him any more questions, not because Jesus has slapped the scribe down, but
probably because they recognize that Jesus has passed the entrance exam with
flying colors.

Today, we can all pass the entrance exam.  Probably just about anybody who has been a
Christian for any length of time can identify the two greatest commandments as
“Love God,” and “Love your neighbor.” 
Our concern is not with the entrance exam, but with the final.  On the Day of Judgment, we’re not going to be
asked whether we know the two greatest commandments, but whether we lived
them.  It doesn’t matter whether we can
check off all the check boxes on our Christian checklist.  The five acts of worship carried out
faithfully every Sunday for 50 years mean nothing in the context of a loveless
life.  God wants obedience, yes, but it
must come from the heart, and unless we live with the love of Christ in us, He
will never count us as His.

Now that Jesus has finished answering everybody’s questions,
He has one of His own, about **THE SON OF
DAVID.** Consider Mark 12:35-37.  Here, we see Jesus posing a conundrum from
the 110th Psalm.  Today, when
we read that psalm, it shouts “Messianic!” to us, and apparently the usual
reading of it 2000 years ago wasn’t much different.  The Jews recognized that the psalm was
written by David, and that he is speaking prophetically of the coming of the
Anointed One.

As Jesus points out, though, there’s a problem with the
simple reading.  During the psalm, David
addresses the coming Messiah as “my Lord”, and that simply isn’t how they did
things 2000 years ago.  As is true in
many societies even today, the Jews of the time of Christ had a tremendous
respect for age.  It was unequivocally
the role of the child to revere the father, not vice versa.  And yet, here we have David describing his
remote descendant as “Lord”.

Much like He did earlier in the parable of the vine-growers,
here Jesus isn’t saying much, but He’s implying a whole lot.  Apparently, many of the Jews on hand were
able to follow Him to His conclusion.  If
David never would have said “Lord” to a merely human descendant of His, then
the Christ is going to have to be someone who is more than merely human.  Here, as elsewhere in the gospels, Jesus’
claim to be the Son of God is bolstered not merely by His own words, not merely
by the miracles that He worked, but by the testimony of prophecies hundreds or
even thousands of years old.  Today,
those prophecies can still reassure us that our faith in Him is not misplaced.
