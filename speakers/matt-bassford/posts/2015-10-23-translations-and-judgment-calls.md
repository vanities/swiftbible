Because I review Bibles containing a number of different
translations, all of those translations make their way into my preaching as
well.  Over the past couple of years,
I’ve preached from nine different translations, and if I ever get the
opportunity to review an NRSV, that number will rise to 10.

Generally, the members of my congregation are fairly
tolerant of my foibles, so they bear up under the translation parade fairly
well.  However, I do start getting some
good-natured grumbling when I deploy an NIV11 or, especially, an NLT.  In my religious tradition, it is commonly
said of such translations that they are “paraphrases” or “commentaries”.  The implication is that they are inferior to
more conservative translations that Just Say What The Bible Says.



There certainly is a point to be made here.  As compared to word-for-word
(“formal-equivalent” is the term of art here) translations like the NASB and
ESV, the NIV and NLT are much more on the thought-for-thought (“dynamic-equivalent”)
end of the spectrum.  They rely on Greek
texts just like the word-for-word translations do, but the goal of their
translators was to tell the reader what the text means rather than what the
text says.

Much of the time, I like the result.  When I’m preaching from a formal-equivalent
translation, I nearly always read the verse, explain what it means, and then
make an application.  When I’m preaching
from a dynamic-equivalent translation, by contrast, I often find myself
skipping the second step because the text already says what I think the meaning
is.  There’s no need to rephrase into 21st-century
American English because the translators have already done that.

Of course, there are times in both the NIV and NLT where I
don’t agree with the interpretation that the translators have placed on the
text.  In fact, I have rewritten sermons
because I thought the NLT rendering of a passage I wanted to use was so far
off-base that I didn’t even want to read it out loud.  Here, I think, is where the conservative
critique of the thought-for-thought translations finds its source.  Those translations are so full of judgment
calls (the argument goes) that we should opt for word-for-word translations
that only give us the straight truth.

What this argument overlooks, though, is that formal-equivalent
translations don’t eliminate translators’ judgment calls.  The translators of the NASB made just as many
judgments as the translators of the NIV11. 
However, those judgments arose at the level of the word rather than the
level of the thought.

I say this not to pick on the NASB.  The same thing is true of the ESV, the NKJV,
the old KJV, or any other formal-equivalent translation you care to name.  The problem here lies in the nature of human
language.  Translating *Koine* Greek to English is not akin to
converting inches to centimeters or pounds to kilograms.  There is no bright-line one-to-one conversion
between languages.  Instead, because
human speech is rich, subtle, and inexact, translation is much more an art than
a science.

This is evident in something as simple as word count.  I’ve read that the total word count in the
standard Hebrew and Greek critical editions (the *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia*, Nestle and Aland’s *Novum Testamentum Graece*, etc.) is
somewhere around 550,000 words.  Our
English Bibles contain many more words than that.  The ESV has 757,000.  The NASB has about 783,000.  A modern KJV has 790,000.

All of these “word-for-word” translations contain half again
as many words as the Hebrew and Greek originals because Hebrew and Greek don’t
express thoughts in the same way that modern English does.  Translators have to put in all kinds of
articles and prepositions and conjunctions that the original authors didn’t
consider necessary, but that are necessary in order for the translated text to
make sense to an English reader.  If
translators didn’t do that, our Bibles would read like the English text in an
interlinear, which generally makes the old ASV seem a stylistic jewel by
comparison!

Even in cases where there is a word-to-word correlation,
translation still requires a great deal in terms of judgment calls.  Just as Hebrew and Greek syntax doesn’t line
up exactly with English syntax, Hebrew and Greek words don’t line up exactly
with English words either.  There is no
mechanistic way to “always pick the right word”.  Instead, the translator has to rely on his
training, experience, and understanding of the text to make his choice.

For instance:

* In all the formal-equivalent Bibles I possess,
  variants of the Greek *baptizo* are transliterated
  as variants of the English “to baptize”, even though *baptizo* means “to immerse”. 
  Judgment call.
* In Philippians 1:1 and 1 Timothy 3:8-13, the
  Greek *diakonos* and its variants are
  transliterated as variants of the English “deacon”.  In Romans 16:1 in the various
  formal-equivalent translations, the same word is translated “servant”, almost
  certainly because it is applied to Phoebe, who was a woman.  Judgment call.
* In the Greek in Revelation 21:16-17, heaven is
  described as being 12,000 stadia long, wide, and tall, with walls that are 144
  cubits thick.  The NASB, however,
  converts these numbers to 1500 miles and 72 yards respectively, even though (I
  think) they are likely chosen for numerological and symbolic rather than
  literal significance.  Again, judgment
  call.



I’m no Greek scholar; these are simply examples I happen to
know because I’ve studied them.  I am
sure there are many others.  Certainly
the judgment calls of the NIV and NLT at the thought level can have a dramatic
impact on the way we read the text.  However,
the judgment calls of the KJV, NKJV, NASB, and ESV at the word level can have
an equally dramatic impact.

My point here is not that we should chuck all of the
established translations and go out and make our own.  In fact, I think that’s a terrible idea.  I don’t think any translation of the Bible
gets it right all the time, but if men and women who have dedicated their lives
to the study of ancient languages can err, how much more will a bunch of
amateurs be led astray by their biases and preconceptions?  A little knowledge is a dangerous thing.  When it comes to the original languages of
the Bible, a little knowledge is a *very* dangerous
thing!

Instead, my point is that no matter what translation we use,
we should approach it with a mixture of deference and criticism.  Like a lawyer or a doctor is entitled to
respect in his area of expertise, so too is a translator, but even experts err
sometimes.  Be ready for that, but don’t
be too quick to assume it, either. 
Sometimes, the text really *doesn’t*line up with our doctrinal biases!

We should also take care not to confuse a different approach
for a mistake.  At times, I think that
the antipathy toward the NIV and NLT arises because it sounds different from
what we’re used to, and what we’re used to must be accurate.  After all, we’ve been using it all these
years, so it must be right, right?

However, this results in us according the familiar a greater
degree of deference than it deserves. 
For instance, I hail from a religious tradition that teaches and
practices believer’s immersion for forgiveness of sins.  If you ask us “What must I do to be saved?”,
we’re likely to reply with Acts 2:38.

Imagine, then, that from the beginning of English
translation, translators had consistently translated *baptizo* with “to immerse”. 
“To immerse” is in the KJV, it’s in the ASV, etc.

Suppose that into this immerse-y world is introduced the
NASB, exactly as it is in the real world today. 
Instead of “to immerse”, it uses “to baptize”.  From churches of Christ, there would be
seismic howls of outrage!  There would be
denunciatory sermons by the dozen, the translators would be condemned as false
teachers, and I’d be about as likely to show up at church with a Koran as I
would with an NASB!

And yet, this is a
judgment call that we accept in all our Bibles today simply because we’re used
to it.  I suspect that members of other
religious groups can think of similar judgment calls they accept out of
familiarity.  Unfamiliar, then, doesn’t
necessarily mean indefensible.  It simply
means different.  When it comes to
translations, the more difference we’re willing to consider, the better the
grasp all of us will have on the truth.
