In many ways, reviewing the Premium Gift Bible as a preaching Bible is like entering a Toyota Corolla in the Indy
500.  You’re asking it to do something it
wasn’t really designed to do.  After all,
the Premium Gift Bible shares a text block with the normal Crossway Thinline, the
Classic Thinline, the Vintage Thinline, and even the Heirloom Thinline (though
I believe the Heirloom Thinline is printed in the Netherlands, not China).  Even the poorest preacher is going to be able
to scrape together the pocket change necessary to buy a Bible with a ribbon
marker!  Nonetheless, here are my
thoughts about how the Premium Gift Bible serves in this capacity.

**PREACHING.** One thing that
the Premium Gift Bible has going for it is its size.  At 5 ⅜” x 8⅜”, it’s not far at all from the
size of my half-sheet sermon outlines, which protrude a little from the side
but not at all from the top and bottom.  Of
course, nearly all the other Bibles in the Crossway Thinline family (which, in
addition to those listed above, embraces the Kid’s Thinline Bibles, as well as
the various permutations of the Pew and Worship Bible) will have the same
dimensions.

To be quite honest, I don’t
particularly like the looks of the leading in the Thinline text block.  Its width varies from column to column, but
throughout most of the Bible, the leading is so extreme that the text nearly
looks double-spaced.  To my eye, that’s
not terribly attractive.

However, I must concede that it
may well be useful.  At first glance, the
verse numbering for the Premium Gift Bible looks like the same system as in the
rest of Crossway’s Bibles—the dreaded quotation-mark verse numbers that gave me
fits in the Personal Size Reference and the Legacy.  In reality, though, it isn’t.  In my PSR and Legacy, both of which have
newer settings than the Premium Gift Bible, the tops of the verse numbers are even
with the tops of the capital A’s.  In the Premium Gift Bible, perhaps a quarter of the verse number extends above the top of
the capital A’s.

This is a subtle change, but it
appears to be a significant one.  For one
thing, I think it forces the leading in the Premium Gift Bible to be as extreme as
it is.  Otherwise, the verse numbers
would start bumping into the descenders in the line above them.  However, that little projection also makes
the verse numbers stand out more.

As a result, I didn’t have any
problems tracking down verse citations. 
One of the sermons I preached with the Premium Gift Bible required me to
locate and read several passages in a row, and I was able to do so without
difficulty.  True, the Premium Gift Bible is
double-column rather than single-column, but in paragraphed Bibles,
single-column is a vice rather than a virtue. 
It makes you scan a longer line looking for numbers.  You might not think so to look at it, but the
Crossway Thinline setting actually works well in the pulpit.

One other feature of the Premium Gift Bible deserves mention.  It doesn’t
have a ribbon marker.  In my sermons, I
commonly return to the same context for repeated readings, so it’s important to
me to be able to mark my place.  The Premium Gift Bible doesn’t offer that innate capacity unless you drape it over the
edge of the pulpit, which isn’t the most professional look for a preacher!

I ended up using a scrap of paper
as my bookmark, which, even though it wasn’t terribly classy-looking either,
was at least less conspicuous.  Again,
this isn’t really the Premium Gift Bible’s fault. 
If you’re going to need a ribbon in your Bible, either pay the extra
$7.50 for a Bible with a ribbon or insert one yourself.

**TEACHING.** I didn’t teach an
adult Bible class during the Premium Gift Bible’s tour of duty at the church
building, but I did teach several sessions of our 10-12-year-olds’ class.  Really, this isn’t a terribly different
experience.  When I’m teaching a Bible
class, I’m not going to stay sitting down unless you put my feet in
quick-setting concrete, so I did plenty of wandering around and waving my Bible
in the air for the middle-schoolers too.

The Premium Gift Bible is a nice size
for that.  Its 8-point type is large
enough for me to read easily, but it’s small enough that I didn’t notice its
weight.  In many ways, the thinline form
factor is a useful one for a Bible.

The Premium Gift also shares a
subtly clever feature with many other Crossway text Bibles.  It isn’t entirely a text Bible.  In Acts 2:38, it won’t give you three other
references to places where the word “baptized” appears like a Foundation SCR
will, but it will tell you that the quotation in Acts 2:25-28 is from Psalm
16:8-11.

These quotations are always few enough in
number that the references may be tucked discreetly at the bottom of the
page.  They’re so discreet, in fact, that
I didn’t realize they were there at first. 
However, Old-Testament references are one of the two main things I use
cross-references for (the other being parallel accounts in the gospels).  As a result, the Premium Gift Bible offers me most
of the utility of a reference Bible without suffering from the distraction and
ugliness of a column of cross-references. 
This makes it a strong candidate for use as a teaching Bible.

**STUDY.** Here too, the Premium Gift Bible performed surprisingly well. 
The size is handy, and the type is large enough for me to read without
difficulty.  I was expecting the Premium Gift Bible to leave the usual Crossway trail of missed verse citations in its wake,
but that didn’t happen.  As far as I can
tell, I got all of my citations right in the two weeks I used it in my office.

Once again, I think the key is
the slight difference in the positioning of the verse numbers.  That little projection above the upper line
of the capital letters after them (it looks like the top of the verse numbers
is even with the tops of the ascenders of lower-case h’s and l’s) is enough to
make them stand out to my eye.

This is a subtle difference.  I doubt I would have seen it if I hadn’t gone
looking for the reason why I was suddenly getting verses right in a Crossway
Bible.  However, it makes a big
difference in use.  Although a
paragraphed Bible, the Premium Gift Bible can compete with verse-by-verse Bibles in
precision of use.  I regret that Crossway
went away from this format in latter Bible settings.

**CONCLUSION.** In evaluating
the Premium Gift Bible, I might have put a Corolla on the racetrack, but this
commutermobile chugged its way to a respectable finish.  I don’t want to like it (ugly
double-spacing!), but I do.  Of the several
Crossway Bibles I’ve used, this is the one I would be most likely to keep in my
office.  If it had only had a ribbon
marker, I would have given it a 9/10, so evaluate the higher-end versions of
this text block accordingly.  The Premium Gift Bible itself, however, merits an 8/10.
