You know the drill by now:  two weeks with the Bible in question sitting
on my desk downstairs, waiting for me to do my daily Bible reading every
morning, plus being around for me to read a random chunk of Scripture whenever
I feel like reading a random chunk of Scripture.  Here’s what I thought of the REB:

**SIZE.** With its just-a-Bible
dimensions, the REB is right where I prefer my reading Bibles to be.  It’s maybe a little heavier in the hand than
I expect, but I don’t really notice that once I have it open and am immersed in
the text.  I certainly don’t have to
brace it on something; even at its larger-than-thinline size, I can still rest
it in one hand or hold it up like a paperback. 
It occupies the high end of the sweet spot for reading-Bible size, but
it’s still in it.

**LAYOUT.** Here is where the
Standard Text really justifies itself as a reading Bible.  As a rule, I’ve found that I prefer to read
from double-column, paragraphed text Bibles, and the REB meets these
qualifications.  Because it is a text
Bible, it doesn’t have a column of references competing for horizontal space,
which means that each of the text columns can contain slightly more than 40
characters per line.

This is useful everywhere, but
it’s particularly useful in the Psalms and other poetic sections of the Old
Testament.  Like most modern Bibles, the
REB attempts to break Bible poetry out by line, and this is where the majority
of double-column Bibles underperform. 
Their shorter line length, often in the mid-thirties in terms of
characters per line, means that they constantly break poetic lines in mid-line,
which is a distraction for the reader.

However, the REB manages poetic
lines with greater grace than a typical double-column setting.  This is true for two reasons.  The first is its text-only nature.  The second is the decision of the REB
designers not to indent poetic lines over from the left margin of the
column.  Only the ends of already-broken
lines are indented.  This means that
every line gets to enjoy a full 40 characters’ worth of space, and it results
in many fewer broken lines when compared to the Crossway Thinline, a
double-column text Bible that does indent.

Like any double-column setting,
the Standard Text is still going to lose a number of battles.  There are still plenty of places where it breaks
a line and leaves a word or two dangling forlornly beneath.  However, it wins more of those battles than
it loses.  I would estimate that in it,
about 55 percent of the poetic lines in Psalms do not have to be broken, which
is a higher percentage than nearly any other double-column setting can
boast.  I find that it handles the poetic
portions of the Bible, which make up a quarter of the whole, in an acceptable
fashion.

Furthermore, I’m entirely willing
to tolerate acceptable performance in poetry for the way that the REB handles
in prose.  Single-column Bibles shine in
poetry, but for my visual/mental quirks, double-column Bibles excel in prose.  The REB is an excellent prose reader even
among double-column Bibles.

Its greater line length helps
here too, but I am particularly fond of its paucity of section headings.  For some reason, some translations of the
Bible include a section heading about every other verse.  I find that to be a dreadful distraction and
a nuisance when I am reading.  In many
cases, abundant section headings break up Biblical contexts and interfere with
comprehension.  I’ve grumbled about the
way that chapter and verse numbers hinder my understanding of the text for
years.  Section headings present as great
an obstacle for much less utility.

I’ve never seen a physical copy
of the REB’s predecessor, the New English Bible.  However, I understand that the translation
was marked by an almost complete absence of section headings.  The REB has retreated somewhat from such an
extreme stance, but it still offers many fewer headings than any other Bible
I’ve ever used.

For instance, in the Sermon on
the Mount, an NASB will have 10 section headings.  An ESV will have 22 (and indeed, I first grew
to loathe section headings while studying the Sermon on the Mount in an
ESV).  The Standard Text has. . . one (It
reads, before Matthew 5:1, “The Sermon on the Mount.”  Even I can’t argue much with that one.).  In consequence, I can understand the Sermon
on the Mount in one big blurt, which, in my judgment, is the way that Matthew
wanted me to understand it.

There are only a couple things
that I don’t care for about the REB’s layout. 
The first is the tight gutter. 
It’s a slight annoyance when first I begin to read, but then I start
unconsciously flattening the Bible out and forget about it.  Second, the Bible isn’t line-matched.  This is hardly surprising; this setting dates
from 1996, when few, if any Bibles were line-matched, and it isn’t really
noticeable, but it is still less than ideal.

**READABILITY.** I also find the
Standard Text to be an easily readable Bible. 
This is promoted first of all by the designers’ decision to use 8/9
Swift for the text.  That’s a strong
choice.  It has the pronounced x-height
common to nearly all modern Bible typefaces, but it also has a more solidly
horizontal feel than the Lexicon-family typeface used in many Crossway Bibles
or the ITC Weidemann of the Zondervan Thinline NASB.  It’s easy for me to read, and it lends a
certain dignity to the text.

Also, because the Standard Text
is a just-a-Bible Bible rather than a thinline, its paper is thicker and more
opaque.  The paper has a coated feel to
it as well, which probably further increases its opacity.  The text block is thicker as a result, but
this is a tradeoff well worth making in a reader.  There is still some ghosting, but I don’t
notice it when I’m actually reading.

**CONCLUSION.** I’ve already
discussed my translation-based reasons for wanting to use an REB as a
reader.  However, I’ve found that in my
readers, I care as much about the text block as I do about the
translation.  I want the text block to be
as well-designed and –executed as possible, so that it offers as little
distraction from the text as possible. 
The Standard Text is firmly in the “I love to read from this Bible” category.  Only the tight gutter keeps it from
perfection.  I award it, then, a 9/10 as
a reader, and a 25.5/30 overall.
