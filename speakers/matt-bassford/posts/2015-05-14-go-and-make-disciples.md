*And Jesus came up and
spoke to them, saying, "All authority has been given to Me in heaven and
on earth. Go therefore and make disciples of all the nations, baptizing them in
the name of the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, teaching them to
observe all that I commanded you; and lo, I am with you always, even to the end
of the age."*

- Matthew 28:19-20

I know how to fix every problem your local congregation
has.  No; I’m not kidding.  Yes; I know how to fix that one too.  This is not because I’m so very, very
smart.  It’s because the Lord Jesus told
me, and indeed all of us, how to fix them in the three verses above.



We often refer to Matthew 28:18-20 as the Great Commission,
and we use it as a proof text for the importance of evangelism.  In reality, though the text assumes that
evangelism has occurred, it is not explicitly about evangelism.  Instead, in Jesus’ own words, it’s about
making disciples.

According to the Lord, making a disciple begins with
baptizing them in the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit.  If you didn’t teach them the gospel first,
you’re doing something wrong.  That’s
where the implied evangelism comes in. 
However, we complete the discipleship process by teaching the baptized
to observe all the commandments of the Lord.

A disciple, then, is someone who:

1.     
Has been baptized into Christ,

2.     
Has been taught all the commandments of the
Lord, and

3.     
Observes them.

If we fully follow this process with every member of a local
church, all of those members will be like Christ, and the church will be
problem-free.  Every single problem that
a local church has, then, must be a discipleship failure.  Either a Christian hasn’t been taught all the
commandments of the Lord, or he isn’t observing them.

If this is the problem, the words of Jesus make the solution
blindingly obvious:  Bible, Bible, and
more Bible.  Teach them, and teach them
to observe.  Show them what Jesus calls
them to do and why they must do it. 
God’s word is rich with both whats and whys, and a proper study of it
will yield both.  This is not always
easy, but it is simple.

By contrast, human wisdom often wants to supply an
institutional solution to individual problems of discipleship.  Indeed, it may be that this is the driving
force behind every deviation from the Scriptural pattern.  Christians are singing unenthusiastically,
ignoring the instruction of Ephesians 5:19-20, so we have to bring in the
instrument to pretty things up. 
Christians aren’t being hospitable as Hebrews 13:2 commands, so we need
a fellowship hall where visitors and fringe members will feel welcome.  Christians aren’t following the evangelistic
pattern of Acts 8:4, so we need a missionary society to spread the gospel.  Christians aren’t caring for the world’s poor
as per Galatians 6:10, so churches need to take on that role themselves.  There are countless other examples just like
these.

The problem is that musical instruments and fellowship halls
and missionary societies and general benevolence aren’t solutions to the
discipleship problem.  Ephesians 5:19-20,
Hebrews 13:2, Acts 8:4, and Galatians 6:10—those are the solutions!  All the human innovations only make the
problem worse.  They communicate to the
failing disciple that he isn’t really failing because all those things are the
church’s job anyway.  As a result, the
spiritual life of the Christian becomes a shell of what God wants it to be.

Of course, all those institutional solutions have two big
advantages in human eyes.  First, they’re
big, they’re flashy, and they’re concrete. 
Nobody can look at a fellowship hall and doubt that This Church Cares
about being hospitable.

Second, they allow the church leadership to “solve” a
problem without having to trust the rank-and-file members.  “Why should we trust those people to do the
right thing?  Look at the way they’re
failing now!”  It’s obviously time for
the leadership, with its superior wisdom and love, to step into the breach and
make things right without having to rely on the inferior spirituality of the
pew-sitters.  Of course, this is a
self-fulfilling prophecy.  Those whom we
do not trust soon become untrustworthy.

By contrast, God’s way requires patience and a willingness
to accept delayed gratification.  As we
all know from bitter experience, spiritual growth is hard!  Sometimes it takes years for a disciple to
embrace what the Lord asks of him. 
However, we must remember that today’s John Mark can become, well,
tomorrow’s John Mark.

Second, we have to trust the brethren.  We have to believe that deep down inside,
they love the Lord and want to serve Him. 
This is quite a leap of faith for many preachers and elders, but it is
one that we must take.  After all, if it
is not true, if we have somehow ended up with a group of “Christians” who are
indifferent to Christ and to His word, the church is doomed anyway, and there is
nothing we can do to save it.  Once we
make that leap, though, our members may well astonish us with their willingness
to serve when we teach them the right thing to do and trust them to do it.

One example here will suffice.  During a business meeting last year, the men
of the Joliet church decided that our financial situation wasn’t as healthy as
it ought to be.  All kinds of
institutional solutions to this problem suggest themselves to human
wisdom:  bake sales, raffles, Bingo night,
and so on.  Instead, the men asked me to
preach a sermon on giving.  I took for my
text the entirety of 2 Corinthians 8-9, Paul’s powerful appeal to the church in
Corinth for money for the needy saints in Jerusalem.  I showed the brethren what God wanted them to
do and told them I trusted them to do it. 
Since that time, now six months ago, our contributions have consistently
been over budget.

I say this not to point out what an awesome preacher I am
(I’m not; far from it) but to point out what an awesome weapon the sword of the
Spirit is.  It has the power to change
hearts and the power to convict hearts that are already good.  Too often, though, we leave the sword in the
scabbard and try to solve our problems with anything but the actual solution.  We don’t need to change to meet the needs of
a changing time.  Instead, we need to go
back:  back to the word of God, and to
God’s command to use it to make disciples.
