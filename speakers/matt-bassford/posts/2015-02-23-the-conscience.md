Sometimes, my mind moves down torturous paths to arrive at a sermon topic for the week.  At other times, the path is quite straightforward.  This is one of those times.  A couple of weeks ago, Josh Collier was talking to me in the coatroom and said, “Hey, would you mind preaching a sermon on the conscience?”  I replied, “I’d be happy to do that.”  So. . .  I’m preaching on the conscience.

Despite the low-effort origin, the conscience is a worthwhile topic for a sermon.  After all, in the world around us, the conscience reigns supreme.  People decide what’s right and wrong based on what they feel is right and wrong.  Now, the conscience is certainly crucial.  People without consciences are sociopaths.  However, as important as the conscience is, ours can still fail us unless we take care of it.  This morning, then, let’s see what the Scripture has to say about the conscience.

**The Conscience Generally**

Let’s begin by considering the witness of the word about the conscience generally.  First of all, the Bible defines WHAT IT DOES.  We see the conscience in operation in 1 Samuel 24:5-6.  This little vignette takes place while David is running for his life from Saul.  Saul is jealous of David and determined to kill him, but as long as he continues to live, Saul remains the anointed king of Israel.  David and his men were hiding in a cave, but Saul unwittingly entered the cave to relieve himself.  David could have killed his enemy where he squatted, but instead, he cut off a piece of Saul’s robe, which he would later use to prove his regard for Saul.  However, even that later troubled David, because he knew that to use a knife on the Lord’s anointed, even just to cut a strip off his robe, was wrong.

That’s the way that our consciences operate too.  When we do something we know we shouldn’t, they nag us about it later and make us feel guilty.  This is certainly not a pleasant process, but it’s necessary.  Our consciences are God’s warning system.  They tell us when we’ve sinned and need to repent.

The Scriptures also show us how the conscience operates generally WHEN IT WORKS WELL.  We see this in Romans 2:14-15.  Paul here is describing an extreme version of something that we’ve all experienced.  Some people are just good people.  They may never have set foot inside a church building, but they basically live the way that the Bible teaches.  They’re people of integrity.  They don’t lie, they work hard when they’re on the job, they are faithful to their spouses, they don’t get drunk, they don’t use bad language, and so on.  Sometimes, they behave with such selflessness that they put many actual Christians to shame.

As Paul says here, these are people who have trustworthy consciences.  Even though they may not have been taught God’s law, they do a much better job of discerning right from wrong than most others.  When they do right, they reliably feel good about it.  When they do wrong, they reliably feel bad about it.

This is how our consciences should operate too.  I’ve often heard the conscience described as our moral compass.  We can use it to tell where we’re going and where we’ve gone spiritually.  A good conscience is like a compass that reliably indicates where north is.  You can use it as a standard and a guide.  Our conscience should confirm us when we do right, even when that right goes against society’s standards.  For instance, it’s right to tell our neighbor about the gospel, even though most people think that’s rude.

Likewise, our conscience should reliably indicate when we have done evil, even if it’s an evil that most people in our society would be fine with.  I don’t think most outsiders would bat an eye if I revealed online that I were a fan of the “A Game of Thrones” HBO series.  However, if I actually were to watch the thing, my conscience would make me miserable, because I know the show is filled with smut.

That’s the way the conscience ought to function, but it is often not that way that consciences do function.  The Scripture reveals that it is possible TO SEAR THE CONSCIENCE.  Paul puts this quite graphically in 1 Timothy 4:1-2.  I think we’ve all seen branding irons in operation in Westerns and TV commercials.  You heat a piece of metal formed in the shape of a ranch’s insignia red-hot, then press it against the flank of a cow.  The hot iron will burn away the animal’s hair, follicles, and nerves in that area, so that the ranch’s symbol will always remain visible.  That’s a brand.

Imagine, then, for a moment, taking one of those red-hot branding irons, and, instead of pressing it into the flank of some poor animal, pressing it against our own skin instead.  Imagine the sound.  Imagine the smell.  Imagine the pain.  Forever after, that spot would be a dead lump of scar tissue.

Paul wants us to understand that just as it is possible for us to sear our physical flesh, we can also sear the conscience.  We can so abuse it that it is no longer capable of feeling anything.  Just like somebody could jab at that brand on our arm with a knife until the blood ran, and we wouldn’t feel it, so too we can maltreat our conscience until it no longer objects to the most awful sin.

In this case, of course, it is not a hot iron that is the offending instrument.  It is sin itself.  The more we sin, the more we lose the capacity to recognize the wrongness of sin.  The repetition has a searing effect.  Sure, that first time we sinned, we felt terrible about it, but after a little while, we went back to the sin, and the second time, we didn’t feel nearly as guilty.  In the end, our conscience has been seared.

The Scripture also reveals THE RESULT of this searing process.  Look what Paul says in Acts 23:1.  On its face, Paul’s claim doesn’t seem that extraordinary, until we realize that he is also speaking of the time when he was still Saul of Tarsus.  Saul of Tarsus raided the homes of innocent men and women, dragged them off to prison, tortured them, and then voted for their judicial murder—all with a clear conscience.

The point is plain.  Unless we take care of our consciences, we can’t count on them to warn us away from even the most extreme sins.  Of course, we can be seared to sins less extreme than persecution of the church.  In fact, I think this is often the case with Christians who quietly practice things that most other Christians vocally disapprove of.  Yes, I know that much of Scriptural interpretation is a matter of individual judgment, but if our individual judgment keeps on coming out differently from the judgment of the most mature Christians we know, that’s a clue that something might be amiss.  Just because we’ve talked ourselves around to believing that porn use is OK under certain circumstances doesn’t mean that it actually is.  Searing the conscience isn’t just a problem for other people.  It can easily be a problem for us.

**Cleansing the Conscience**

Now, we’re going to turn our attention to cleansing the conscience.  Not surprisingly, this will help us to prepare our minds for the Lord’s Supper.  First, though, we’re going to consider not Jesus, but the precursor to His sacrifice, the FAILED CLEANSING of the Law of Moses.  Look at what the Hebrews writer has to say in Hebrews 9:9-10.  As we look at this text, though, we have to be careful not to sell the Old Law short.  It was handed down by God, and it was instituted for a reason.  It taught God’s chosen people all kinds of important things about sin, without which, the sacrifice of Christ would have made no sense.

However, there is something the Law could not do.  It could not make the worshiper perfect in conscience.  Even as the Israelites were going through the elaborate rituals of animal sacrifice, even as they were doing everything God told them to do, they had a sense of “Is this all there is?”  We see the prophet Micah grappling with this issue in Micah 6.  He proposes thousands of rams, ten thousand rivers of oil, and even his firstborn child as potential sacrifices for sin.  How could any of these things purchase forgiveness?

The problem is that sin demands blood.  Sin demands death.  How could the blood of a goat that isn’t me pay the debt of my sin?  How could the death of a bull I bought in the marketplace cleanse me from guilt?  I’m different than those things are, so my cleansing must require a better sacrifice.

We find the answer in THE CLEANSING OF CHRIST.  Look just a couple verses down, in Hebrews 9:13-14.  Here’s the point:  Goats and bulls and burned-up heifers were enough to fulfill the earthly, fleshly, ceremonial requirement of the Law.  All of those things were carried out according to God’s rules.

However, the sacrifice of Christ is superior.  Like the animal sacrifices of the Law of Moses, it was carried out according to God’s will.  This was the sacrifice that God specified to inaugurate the new covenant.  The offering itself, though, wasn’t of a particularly fit or pretty animal.  It wasn’t even Micah’s firstborn son.  Instead, it was the offering of the only begotten Son of God, who put on flesh and dwelt among us.  Unlike us, instead of spending His life in transgression and rebellion, He lived perfectly, keeping every requirement of the Old Law flawlessly.  Then, He gave His life for us on the cross.

Who can argue with a sacrifice like that?  Sure, Micah or Saul of Tarsus could stand by the altar in the temple and look at the smoking carcass of some poor dumb animal and say, “Is that really enough to rescue me?”  However, who of us can contemplate in our mind’s eye the awesome spectacle of the crucified Christ, of the very Creator laying down His life for the creation, and say, “That sacrifice isn’t good enough to atone for my sins?”  Friends, if the sacrifice of Christ isn’t good enough, nothing ever has been or will be good enough, because there is no greater sacrifice that conceivably could be offered.  This is God who died.

Despite this, there are plenty of Christians who get caught up in fretting over some sin they’ve repented of and been forgiven for.  I’ve talked about this before, but this morning, my approach is going to be simple.  When you find yourself thinking in this way, think about Jesus.  Think about how willingly He went to the cross, how He remained silent when He could have summoned twelve legions of angels.  Think about the shame of His suffering and the triumph of His resurrection.  Think about Him now, seated at the right hand of the throne of God.  Think about how He says to you, “Your debt is paid, because I paid it.”  Who are we to cling to the guilt of our sins when Christ has offered Himself to cleanse it?

The Scripture also describes the mechanics of our conscience BEING CLEANSED.  Let’s look here at 1 Peter 3:21.  There’s translation difference here worth looking at.  The ESV, which is what’s on the screen, reads, “for a good conscience,” as does the NASB.  However, other translations here will make it sound like the appeal is from a good conscience rather than for a good conscience.  However, that doesn’t fit with the rest of the verse.  It tells us that baptism saves, so why would we have a good conscience before we are actually forgiven?  Also, the interlinear I looked at confirmed the NASB and ESV reading.

The implications of this are extremely significant.  If baptism is our appeal to God for a good conscience, it is in our willingness to be baptized that we acknowledge our guilt.  We’re saying, “I know, Father, that I’ve sinned against You, that I deserve the worst punishment You could mete out, but I want more than anything else to be freed from this guilt, to be allowed to live in order to serve You.”  It is during the act of baptism that we receive God’s answer, that Jesus saves us from our sins.  Those sins are gone forever, and they need not burden our minds any more.  God has made our conscience good.

Because our consciences are now clear, we have BOLDNESS TO APPROACH God in heaven.  This is the Hebrews writer again, this time in Hebrews 10:22.  This points out something about the presence of God that we don’t often consider:  that it should be very intimidating to the sinner.  Imagine for a moment that you’re a drug dealer with a dozen kilos of cocaine in the trunk.  Up ahead, you see a traffic checkpoint, and finally, it’s your turn.  Are you all breezy and carefree and “Hey, officer!  How you doin’?”  Or, instead, are you terrified that he’s going to find out what you’re up to and bust you?  Of course, with God, there is no possibility of Him overlooking even our most secret sins.  He is perfectly wise, perfectly holy, and perfectly just, so the very last place a sinner should want to be is in the presence of God!

For the Christian, however, things are very different.  We can approach God with boldness because the blood of Christ has erased the stain of our sin from our consciences.  We would absolutely have been doomed before, but now we can come to Him without fear, both in prayer and through eternity.

Before the Lord’s Supper, let’s consider all these things.  There is nothing we could do on our own to deal with the guilt we bear, but what we could never accomplish, Jesus did.  He died and rose again, all for our sakes.  As we partake of the Lord’s Supper, let’s remember Him and the power of His victorious blood.

**Caring for the Conscience**

As we’ve seen this morning, the conscience is spiritually vital, but it’s also something that we can sear and corrupt.  This tells us, then, that we need to care for our consciences so that they don’t become spiritually useless.  The first step in taking care of the conscience is to LISTEN TO INSTRUCTION.  Paul makes this point in 1 Timothy 1:5.  It’s like this.  Let’s say, ladies, that you were baking a cake for your mother-in-law, and the recipe calls for 1 ½ cups of sugar.  How are you going to do that?  Are you just going to dump a pile of sugar in the bowl and say, “That looks about right,” or are you going to measure?  Let’s say, men, that you were cutting a baseboard for a section of wall 39 ¼ inches long.  How are you going to do that?  Are you going to eyeball it and say, “Yup; that looks like 39 ¼ inches to me,” or are you going to measure?

That’s exactly what the Bible is.  It is the standard of measurement for our conscience.  It’s how we calibrate our sense of right and wrong to make sure that we reach the right result.  If we are ignorant of God’s word, we’re just guessing about what’s right and what’s wrong.  We’re eyeballing it, and we’re no more likely to get our eyeball measurement right than I am to correctly estimate how long 39 ¼ inches is.  Without an understanding of the Scripture, we can easily make mistakes in both directions.  We can be wracked with guilt about things that are actually perfectly acceptable, or, conversely, we can be light-hearted about violating the law of God.  A conscience like that is spiritually useless.  Instead, we need to listen to instruction from the word.  It will make our conscience good, just like a ruler with correct markings is a good ruler.  Of course, the more we hear, the more exacting our consciences will get, so that they can make even the finest distinctions.

There are times, though, when we ought to listen even to an improperly trained conscience.  A flawed conscience cannot make a sin righteous, but it can make a perfectly legitimate act into sin for us.  Take, for instance, a sister in Christ who was raised attending a charismatic church.  All her life, she was taught that it was a sin for women to wear pants and have uncovered heads.  Once she became an adult, she realized the truth about modern-day spiritual gifts and obeyed the gospel, but when she considers putting on a pair of jeans and going out in public, her conscience still bothers her.  Even though the Bible certainly doesn’t require her to wear skirts, her conscience does, and she has to confine herself to that kind of attire until she gets her conscience better trained.  If we believe something is sin, to us, it is sin.

Second, we must care for our consciences by KEEPING THEM BLAMELESS.  Consider Paul’s perspective on this in Acts 24:16.  No less than he, it should be our goal also to keep our consciences blameless.  This is similar to the good old-fashioned mirror test.  We should always live in such a way that we can look ourselves in the eye in the mirror without being ashamed.  I can think of times in my life when I haven’t been able to pass the mirror test, when I didn’t want to look at the reflection of my own face because I knew I’d done wrong.  Of course, there are scoundrels who can consider their own scoundrelly faces without a qualm, but if we have properly trained our consciences with the word, we won’t be able to do that.

However, the mirror test shouldn’t just be a 7-in-the-morning thing.  It should be a whole-life thing.  If all we do is periodically consider ourselves and say, “Oh no!  I’ve blown it!” then we’re doing it wrong.    Instead, we must always live with the mirror test in mind.  We should train ourselves to say, “If I do this now, I’m not going to want to look myself in the eye tomorrow morning.”  The conscience is not merely to make us feel guilty after we have sinned.  Instead, it’s supposed to warn us of sin before we commit it.

Also, we must HONOR OTHERS’ CONSCIENCES.  Let’s look at the scenario that Paul lays out in 1 Corinthians 8:9-11.  In this case, he’s dealing with an issue of conscience that doesn’t face us today:  the eating of meat sacrificed to idols.  Some Christians in the first century understood that idols were nothing, that false gods weren’t any kind of gods at all.  Others, though, who may well have come from pagan backgrounds, felt that they were participating in idolatrous worship simply by eating meat that had been offered to an idol but then sold in the marketplace to recoup the temple’s costs.  They were so concerned about this that they believed it was a sin to eat any kind of meat at all, because you couldn’t be sure.

At this point of 1 Corinthians 8, Paul has already shown that idols don’t matter.  It was not a sin to eat meat.  However, it was still sin to lead a brother to violate his own conscience.  In other words, if a brother whose conscience was perfectly OK with eating meat did so in front of another Christian who thought it was wrong, he might influence that Christian to eat meat himself and so sin against his own conscience.  In that case, both the brother and the weak Christian he influenced would be sinning.

This passage, however, does not teach the tyranny of the conscience.  My own actions, taken in good conscience, cannot violate the conscience of another Christian.  Let’s say, for instance, that there’s a Christian at Joliet who believes it’s wrong to celebrate Christmas at all.  I don’t think I make any secret of the fact that my family and I celebrate Christmas as a family holiday.  If I mention that in passing from the pulpit, that Christian doesn’t have the right to come to me and say, “Your celebration of Christmas violates my conscience.”  That’s not possible.  He might not like it, he might think it’s wrong, but my practice cannot offend someone else’s conscience.  However, I do have to be careful to make sure that I’m not leading my brother to do something himself that he thinks is wrong.  If next December, he buys a fake tree and some stockings with his conscience protesting all the while, then we’re both in trouble!

Finally, we must be willing to SUFFER for conscience’ sake.  Peter, who certainly had quite a bit to say about suffering, introduces this idea in 1 Peter 2:19.  As we all know, the right road isn’t necessarily the easy road.  Let’s say that you made a mistake at work, and it caused a major disaster.  However, the mistake isn’t traceable back to you.  If you remain silent, in fact, the blame will probably land at somebody else’s feet.  The easy thing to do is to say nothing, and if somebody else gets in trouble, oh well.  The right thing to do is to acknowledge what you’ve done, even if it gets you fired.  Nobody likes to get dressed down.  Nobody likes to suffer.  However, if we’re true disciples, there’s something that’s even more important to us than avoiding pain.  That’s being able to look ourselves in the eye.  It’s, one day, being able to look God in the eye too.
