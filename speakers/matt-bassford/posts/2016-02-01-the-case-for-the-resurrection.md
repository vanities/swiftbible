From time to time, one of my Facebook friends will post a
link to a free Kindle book on some spiritual topic.  I usually grab those because there isn’t a
reason not to.  They’re free, and if I
get anything worthwhile out of the book, I’ve come out ahead.

Sometimes, though, the book is worth substantially more.  I’ve found that to be true for my most recent
Kindle freebie, *The Case for the
Resurrection*, by Gary R. Habermas and Michael Licona.  In fact, if I had known what I know now, I
would gladly have paid full price for it. 
I find the beliefs of the authors about salvation and some other topics
a little sketchy, but I think it’s the single best book on apologetics that
I’ve ever read.  For the next couple of
sermons, I’m going to steal shamelessly from it because I think it’s that good.  Both for our own sakes and for the sake of
those we want to teach, let’s consider the case it makes for the resurrection.

 The thing that I find particularly powerful about this book
is that it does not start with the conviction that the Bible is inspired.  There are all kinds of arguments for the
resurrection that do, but they do nothing to persuade someone who does not
already believe in inspiration.  By
contrast, the authors of *The Case for the
Resurrection* do not in their arguments give the Bible any more deference
than they would give an ordinary historical document, and yet, even at that
level of skepticism, they are still able to show that the resurrection of Jesus
is reasonably certain.

This is not, of course, the same thing as proving the
resurrection beyond a shadow of a doubt. 
However, it’s unfair to demand that the evidence for the resurrection
rise to that standard.  After all, we
don’t require that level of proof for other historical events.  How can any of us know for certain, for
instance, that George Washington crossed the Delaware?  None of us saw him do it.  Maybe it was all just a big PR campaign to
get him elected president!  However, all
the historical evidence for the crossing of the Delaware makes us reasonably
certain that the event actually happened, and we can say exactly the same thing
about the resurrection of Jesus.

The authors are able to meet the “reasonably certain”
standard of proof using what they call **“THE
MINIMAL-FACTS APPROACH”.** Here’s what
they mean by this.  Rather than relying
on data that are disputed in the community of religious scholars, they are only
going to use data that nearly all scholars accept as being factually correct.  Believing scholars who accept inspiration as
we do will agree to these things, but so will skeptical, agnostic, and outright
atheist scholars.  Of course, there are
always going to be a few cranks who deny these things, but the evidence is
against them.  If the case for the
resurrection can be built on facts widely accepted to be true, we have little
reason to doubt that the resurrection occurred.

In particular, the authors rely on five widely accepted
facts to build their case.  The first
fact is that **JESUS WAS CRUCIFIED.** It’s a ridiculous exercise to even bother
turning to the Bible to establish this point. 
There are probably hundreds of passages in the New Testament that
discuss the crucifixion in some way. 
Likewise, there are extrabiblical sources that attest that Jesus was
crucified.  For instance, Josephus notes
of Jesus in *The Antiquities of the Jews,* Book
18, Chapter 3, “. . . Pilate, at the suggestion of the principal men among us,
. . . condemned Him to the cross.”  For
this and many other reasons, Dominic Crossan, former co-chairman of the
ultra-liberal Jesus Seminar, writes, “That He was crucified is as sure as
anything historical can ever be.”

The second minimal fact is that **THE DISCIPLES BELIEVED THE RISEN LORD HAD APPEARED TO THEM.** In order to prove this point, we don’t
even have to turn to the four gospels. 
In fact, we have reason not to; the dates for the writing of the gospels
are disputed, and we can prove the point using parts of the Bible that are
indisputably early.

Our first key evidence for this point comes from the apostle
Paul.  Again, his authorship of some of
the epistles attributed to him is disputed. 
We’re going to use only the epistles that everybody agrees he
wrote.  The book of Galatians is one such
epistle.  In Galatians, consider
Galatians 2:9.  This is evidence that
Paul knew three of the most important apostles: 
Peter, John, and James the brother of the Lord.

1 Corinthians is another letter that everybody acknowledges
was written by Paul.  It’s one of the
most reliably datable books of the Bible, too, almost certainly written around
AD 53, about 20 years after the crucifixion of Jesus.  It records Paul’s views on the preaching of
the apostles in 1 Corinthians 15:9-12. 
This passage makes four points.  Paul
considered himself an apostle, and he believed that he worked even harder at
preaching than the other apostles. 
However, both he and they preached the same message:  that Jesus had been raised from the dead.  Paul’s testimony here is very good and very
early evidence that the first disciples believed they had seen the risen Lord.

A little earlier in 1 Corinthians, we come to another proof
of very early Christian teaching:  the
oral tradition preserved in 1 Corinthians 15:3-5.  Before the New Testament was written, early
Christians still needed some way to transmit important information about the
faith.  They used hymns, poetry, and
carefully composed creeds, or statements of belief.  Nearly every scholar will agree that this
text is one such creedal statement.  In
fact, many scholars will contend that Paul heard this from Peter and James on
his first visit to Jerusalem, which happened at most five years after the
crucifixion.  Who better to teach about
Jesus appearing to Cephas than Cephas?

Third, the convictions of the apostles are expressed in a
wide variety of other writings:  the
sermon summaries recorded in Acts, the gospels themselves, and the testimony of
men in the next generation after the first-century church, writers like Clement
and Polycarp.  All of these sources
assert that the disciples proclaimed that they had seen the risen Lord from the
beginning.  Even if a skeptic wants to
dispute that Luke wrote Acts, for instance, somebody early was still saying it.

In addition, both Biblical and extrabiblical sources make it
clear that the disciples believed what they proclaimed.  They showed their faith in the strongest way
possible:  by being willing to suffer and
die for it.  Consider Peter’s defiance of
the Sanhedrin in Acts 5:27-29.  Those are
not the words of a man who knows he is proclaiming a fraud!  Consider the fate of James the brother of
John, who does die for his beliefs early in Acts 12.  Consider Paul’s catalogue of suffering in 2
Corinthians 11.  After the time of the
New Testament, all sorts of writers, from Clement and Polycarp again, through
Ignatius, Tertullian, Origen, and Eusebius, asserted that Peter, Paul, and
other early followers of Jesus willingly died for their faith.  There is no evidence whatsoever that any of
the men who claimed to have seen the Lord ever recanted, even under torture.

It is true that men will sometimes give their lives for bad
causes.  Here, we need only think of the
9-11 hijackers.  However, there’s an
important difference between those men and the early disciples.  The 9-11 hijackers died for their faith in
something they had not seen.  Peter,
Paul, and the rest died for their faith in something they claimed to have
seen.  The fact of their belief that they
had seen Jesus after His death is sufficiently well attested to convince nearly
everybody.

Our third minimal fact is **THE CONVERSION OF SAUL**.  Once
again, there is wide agreement that at one point in his life, Saul of Tarsus
was a persecutor of the church.  He
himself acknowledges this in Galatians 1:13-14. 
In fact, he reveals that his persecution of the church was widely known
in Galatians 1:22-23.  Saul wasn’t just
an enemy of early Christians.  He was a
famous enemy of early Christians!

However, then Saul underwent a startling change.  He transformed from a persecutor of
Christianity to one of its loudest proponents. 
He himself gives us the reason for this change in 1 Corinthians
15:8.  He became a disciple of Christ
because he believed he had seen the risen Lord. 
Luke tells the same story in the various conversion accounts in
Acts.  Similarly, the usual collection of
writers from a generation later also reports the specifics of Saul’s
conversion.  Nobody worth listening to
questions that Saul made a dramatic U-turn with his life because of his new
belief.

This is important for two reasons.  First, Saul was an avowed enemy of Christ and
everyone who followed Him.  I’ve heard it
said that a miracle never occurs except in the presence of those who were
predisposed to accept it.  That is not
true of Saul.  He certainly did not
expect to have the risen Lord appear to him, and he probably didn’t even want
to!

Second, it is true that people make dramatic shifts in their
religious convictions all the time.  I’ve
known atheists who became Christians, and, sadly, Christians who became
atheists.  However, these dramatic
changes are almost always the result of secondary evidence.  The atheist becomes a Christian because his
friend studies the Bible with him. 
That’s not the case with Saul. 
Nobody taught him about Jesus. 
Instead, he believed that he actually saw Jesus and converted on that
basis.

Our fourth minimal fact is **THE CONVERSION OF JAMES, THE LORD’S BROTHER**.  We don’t know as much about James as we do
about Paul, but we do know enough to build a case around him.  The Scripture presents James as the leader of
the first-century conservatives, the Jews who continued to follow the Law of
Moses along with believing in Christ. 
For instance, in Galatians 2, Paul refers to Judaizing false teachers as
“men from James”.  He’s also the one who
settles the dispute of the first Jerusalem council in Acts 15 by expressing his
conviction that Gentile Christians didn’t have to be circumcised.  When somebody with James’ Jewish credentials
says this, it cuts the legs out from under the party of the circumcision.  Once again, there’s plenty of extrabiblical
evidence too that James was an extremely devout Jew.

Interestingly, this devotion to the Law of Moses leads James
not to accept Jesus as Messiah during His ministry.  Consider the account of John 7:1-5.  Even though John is subject to scholarly
skepticism, this is the sort of thing that nearly all scholars accept as true
because of what’s called the principle of embarrassment.  Early Christian writers are not likely to
report something that makes Jesus look bad unless it is true.  Why would a believer make up a story that
puts the Lord in a bad light?  However,
this story, which presents Jesus’ own brothers as rejecting Him, does exactly
that.  Similarly, James, along with the
other brothers of Jesus, is part of the gang of family members that tries to
drag Jesus off to the loony bin in Mark 3. 
While Jesus is on earth, James is not a believer.

However, something happens, and we see what the something is
in 1 Corinthians 15:7.  This is why we
know James the Lord’s brother as an apostle—he believed the risen Christ had
appeared to him too.  From this point on,
James quickly rises in importance in the early church, to the point that Paul
cites him in Galatians 1 as one of its most prominent members, only five years
after the crucifixion.  Finally, after
the time of the composition of the New Testament, there are plenty of
extrabiblical writers, both Christian and non-, who will attest that James was
martyred for his faith in Christ too. 
His spiritual U-turn isn’t as dramatic as the one that Paul went
through, but it’s clear that his life was transformed by what he sincerely
believed was an appearance of Jesus.

Our final minimal fact is **THE EMPTY TOMB.** The authors
acknowledge that this fact is a little bit different from the others.  Instead of being accepted by nearly all
Biblical scholars of whatever religious persuasion, this one is affirmed by
only around 75 percent of them.  This
isn’t a nearly complete majority, but it’s substantial enough that the argument
is still worth advancing.

The authors of *The
Case for the Resurrection* advance three main arguments in support of this
fact.  The first is that the church began
in Jerusalem, which is the very place where the non-empty tomb would have been
located.  From the beginning,
Christianity had all sorts of enemies, the very same men who had crucified
Jesus in the first place.  We see
evidence of their perplexity in Acts 4:13-17. 
Annas, Caiaphas, and the rest are dead-set against Christianity, and
they are very intelligent men.  If Jesus’
body is still in the tomb, don’t you think that at least one of them would have
said, “Hey!  Why don’t we go get His
corpse and parade around with it to prove His followers are idiots?”  Of course, there’s not a shred of evidence
that such a thing ever happened.

Instead, we see the story that the Jewish leaders do advance
in Matthew 28:12-15.  Even in the time of
Justin Martyr, a Christian apologist who lived 100 years later, the enemies of
the gospel are still making this same argument. 
There was no resurrection because the disciples stole the body.

However, this argument concedes something very
important.  It concedes that the tomb was
in fact empty.  There was a mysterious
disappearance of the body that should have been there, and the foes of
Christianity had to come up with some way to explain its absence.

The third argument in favor of the emptiness of the tomb is
that the disappearance of Jesus’ body was first reported by women.  Look at Mark 16:1-6, probably the earliest
gospel account of all.  This too is a
story that is probably true because it is embarrassing.  2000 years ago, women weren’t exactly
considered equal with men.  Neither the
Jews nor the Romans considered their testimony to have the same weight as
testimony from a man.  We see an echo of
this in the disciples’ own unwillingness to believe the report of the women in
Luke 24.

If you’re going to make up a story about the empty tomb,
you’re going to make the initial witnesses men, ideally men with a great deal
of clout.  Joseph of Arimathea and
Nicodemus were conveniently available as early disciples who were members of
the Sanhedrin.  Those are reliable
witnesses!  Why not have one of them
discover the absence of the body?  When
the gospel writers report that women found an empty tomb, it’s a strong
indication that they’re telling the truth.

In summary, let’s consider our five facts.  We’ve seen that there is a near consensus
that Jesus was crucified, that His disciples believed the risen Lord appeared
to them, and that Saul and James converted because they too believed they had
seen the Lord after His death.  We’ve
also seen that most, though not all, scholars believe that Jesus’ tomb was
found empty that Sunday morning.

There are a number of different explanations that skeptics
advance to try to explain these five facts. 
Indeed, we’re going to consider all of those explanations in next week’s
sermon.  However, there is only one
theory that fits all the facts.  It is
that Jesus truly did rise from the dead.

On the basis of the evidence, then, we can be reasonably
certain that the resurrection of Jesus actually took place.  If that is true, then a whole bunch of other
things follow.  It confirms Jesus’ claim
to be the Son of God.  It lends credence
to the Scriptural accounts of Jesus predicting His death and resurrection
before He was crucified, which shows that He was a prophet.  If Jesus is the subject of one confirmed
miracle, that makes the stories of Him and His followers working other miracles
far more likely.  Similarly, if Jesus is
the Son of God, His conviction that the Scriptures were and are the word of God
suddenly becomes very significant.  In
short, once you begin with the resurrection, you can logically establish every
other element of our faith.

Last but hardly least, the resurrection of the Lord
establishes the meaning of His death. 
When He and His disciples tell us that He died for our sins, and that if
we believe in Him, we can have life in His name, those things too are made
certain by His resurrection.  Before the
Lord’s Supper, then, let’s reflect on what His sacrifice has purchased for us,
and on the confidence we can have that our faith is not vain.
