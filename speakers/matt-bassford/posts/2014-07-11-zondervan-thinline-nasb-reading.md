Sad to say, this Bible has really
sapped my motivation to continue reviewing new Bibles.  Why? 
Because I love reading out of it so much that I am reluctant to spend
two weeks away from it test-driving other readers.  This is my favorite reading Bible, hands
down, and I’ve been using it for that purpose almost exclusively for the past
six weeks.  Here’s why:

**SIZE.** The Thinline NASB is a
delight in the hand.  I don’t know why,
but it is about half an inch shorter than the ESV Thinline from Crossway, and
that decreased size and weight make it even more comfortable to hold than
thinlines usually are.  When I’m reading
this Bible, I don’t pay any more attention to it than I do to a Kindle or a
paperback book.  The form factor is so
right that I can dive straight into the content.

**LAYOUT.** As I’ve said before,
I think Zondervan did an amazing job with the setting of this edition, and this
is never more obvious than when I am using it as a reader.  To begin with, it’s paragraphed rather than
verse-by-verse, which is the preferred layout for a reader.

In addition, this is a text Bible
with no cross-references in a center column, the lower right-hand corner, or
anywhere else.  This has a couple of
important consequences.  First, it makes
the layout clean and pretty.  Say what
you will about the utility of cross-references, they simply aren’t pleasing to
the eye.  They’re a hieroglyphic jumble
that clutters up the page.  Without them,
the Thinline NASB’s layout offers little distraction from the text.

Second, without a center column,
which is the usual position of cross-references in a double-column Bible, the
text columns of the NASB line run from margin to margin.  In consequence, they are some of the widest
columns I’ve ever seen in a double-column Bible.  Each line of prose has about 43 characters in
it, counting spaces, which is more than even Cambridge’s Pitt Minion can
offer.

This has a tremendous impact on
the Thinline NASB’s utility as a reader. 
Most single-column settings bug me. 
I can’t read all the way to the end of a line without actively shifting
my eyes over and breaking my immersion in the text.  When I’m tired, even the Foundation SCR,
which has 57ish characters per line, will start hiding words from me.  Not so with the Thinline NASB.  Five minutes before bed, I can still easily
read the entirety of each line.

Conversely, I find that the
Thinline NASB reads more smoothly than double-column editions with 30-35
characters per line, which includes most double-column reference settings.  Even though it’s not nearly as distracting as
the sideways eye-flick, I find that shifting my eyes down to the next line is a
tiny distraction too.  With more words
per line, the Thinline NASB causes that distraction less frequently.

It has also been my experience
that the Thinline NASB reads well even in the poetry sections of
Scripture.  Over the past six weeks, I’ve
read through much of the book of Psalms, and despite the problems inherent in double-column
settings of Bible poetry, I haven’t had any trouble getting lost in the
text.

This too is a characters-per-line
issue.  Because the Thinline NASB has
more characters per line than any other double-column setting of my
acquaintance, it has to break poetic lines less frequently.  An examination of Page 488 (which contains
parts of Psalms 94-96) reveals that a solid majority (39 lines to 30) are not
broken due to lack of space.  Only 16 of
the broken lines are of the dreaded one-word-break variety.  This is not as good as the performance of the
Foundation SCR, for instance, but it isn’t exactly a serious problem
either.  From my perspective, the
Thinline NASB is about as good a reader as I’m likely to find.

**READABILITY.** I’m still
fairly young, and my vision troubles are with distance, not with close-in
reading, so I’m able to manage with Bibles with fairly small type.  I think that the typeface used for the text
of the Thinline NASB is quite readable, but the fact remains that it’s only 7.8-point.  For me, that’s great.  For others, it may not be.

It is also true that the Thinline
NASB is printed on extremely thin (I’m guessing 20 gsm-ish) and not very opaque
paper, and despite the beauties of the setting, it isn’t line-matched.  Generally, this doesn’t give me any trouble, but
there is one exception:  when I come to
the words of Christ in red.  I don’t have
a doctrinal objection to this, but its execution leaves something to be
desired.

The words-in-red sections of
Scripture are the only places where I really notice this edition’s thin paper
and lack of line-matching.  For some
reason, black-behind-red ghosting is worse for my eyes than
black-behind-black.  Also, although this
isn’t a words-of-Christ-in-pink Bible, the red print is much darker on some
pages than others, and it occasionally suffers from broken letters.  I think Zondervan would have been better
served to simplify its execution by leaving the words of Christ in black.

**CONCLUSION.**Despite the
annoyances offered by the words-in-red sections, I can’t think of any other
Bible I would rather use as a daily reader. 
I give the Zondervan Thinline NASB a 5/5 in this regard.
