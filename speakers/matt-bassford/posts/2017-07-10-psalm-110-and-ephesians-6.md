In our study of the prophecies concerning Jesus, it’s easy
to focus on the ones that identify Him as the suffering Servant.  Psalm 22 and Isaiah 53 are two of the most
common readings before the Lord’s Supper, and when we recognize that Jesus’
suffering and death were predicted hundreds of years before His birth, it
strengthens our faith.

However, there’s an equally important strain of prophecy
that predicts that the Messiah will be a conquering king, and sometimes, those
prophecies don’t get as much play.  This
is in keeping with our tendency to focus on the death of Jesus rather than on
His resurrection, even though first-century Christians tended to regard His
death, burial, and resurrection as one event. 
So that we remember the glory of our Lord along with His suffering,
before the Lord’s Supper this morning, let’s look at one of those neglected
prophecies.  Let’s consider Psalm 110.



Within this short psalm, which is only seven verses long,
the first logical division concerns **CHRIST
THE LORD.** Let’s read, then, Psalm
110:1-3.  Parts of this context are cited
all over the place in the New Testament, by everybody from Paul to Jesus
Himself.  When we consider the text, it’s
easy to see why.  There’s a lot in here
that doesn’t make sense outside a Messianic context.

For instance, let’s begin with the opening line.  Today, in our egalitarian society, we might
be tempted to read right through this, but it would have posed major issues for
people 2000 years ago.  In Mark 12, Jesus
points out the problem.  His
contemporaries, just as we do today, recognized this as an obviously Messianic
prophecy.  It’s about the Christ, who was
to be a descendant of David.  In the
Israelite and Jewish world, ancestors were more important than
descendants.

However, Psalm 110 was written by David, and in the opening
line, the ancestor calls the descendant “my Lord”.  This is major cognitive dissonance.  It would be like if we went to visit the
White House and the President came out and started shining our shoes.  That’s just not how things work!  In Mark 12, Jesus doesn’t reveal the answer
to the riddle, but today we know what it is. 
David calls the Messiah “Lord” because the Messiah, though David’s descendant,
existed before he did, and is not merely human, but both human and divine.

In addition, God invites the addressee of the psalm to sit
at His right hand until He has made His enemies into His footstool.  According to the references in my Bible, this
is cited no fewer than eight times in the New Testament, which makes it one of
the most significant prophecies in all of Scripture.  Once again, this is a bewildering text in a
first-century context.  I honestly don’t
know what sense you could make of it if you were trying to read it as being
about ordinary human things.

We, of course, benefit from the inspired explanations from
Acts 2 on.  Jesus literally did sit down
at the right hand of the throne of God after He ascended to heaven.  He has been reigning there for 2000 years
now, and He will continue reigning until God defeats death, His last
enemy.  A bewildering promise makes
perfect sense when we apply it to the right man.

The second half of the psalm is about **CHRIST THE HIGH PRIEST.** David
raises this subject in Psalm 110:4-7. 
Once again, we start off with an utterly baffling reference, even once
we decode it.  Melchizedek is a minor
character in the book of Genesis who shows up at one point in the life of
Abraham to bless him in exchange for ten percent of the spoils from the battle
Abraham just fought.  Even though he’s
not an Israelite, he’s described as a priest of God Most High.

In this psalm, David plucks Melchizedek from obscurity and
declares that God has sworn to the Messiah that He will make Him a priest
forever according to the order of Melchizedek. 
To a first-century Jew, this would make no sense.  There was no priestly order of
Melchizedek.  Instead, the only priests
were the sons of Aaron, of the tribe of Levi. 
What’s more, how can somebody be a priest forever?  The Levitical high priests didn’t continue forever.  They died like everybody else.

The meaning of all this would probably have remained obscure
if the Hebrews writer hadn’t devoted an entire chapter plus portions of other
chapters to explaining it.  The way that
Abraham honors Melchizedek shows how superior he is, and if Melchizedek is
greater than Abraham, he’s greater than Abraham’s descendant Levi too.  When Christ comes as a priest according to
the order of Melchizedek, then, He comes as a superior kind of priest.  He is fit to be priest not because of His
priestly descent, but because of the oath God swore to Him.  Unlike all the Levitical priests who died,
Christ continues as priest forever because He never dies.

In addition, because Melchizedek was king of Salem in
addition to being a priest, a priest according to the order of Melchizedek is
going to be a king too.  This was not at
all part of the Jewish tradition.  In 2
Chronicles 26, Uzziah king of Judah is cursed with leprosy because he tries to
burn incense in the temple like a priest. 
Kings and priests were supposed to be separate, but both of those
functions are united in Christ.  As a
result, Christ doesn’t merely intercede between the people and God.  He goes out and beats up on His enemies too,
and indeed, when earth comes to its end, everyone, whether living or dead, will
acknowledge His overwhelming triumph.

Momentarily, we’re going to partake of the Lord’s
Supper.  Before we do, though, let’s
reflect on what we’ve learned about the Lord. 
Let’s be amazed by the way that prophecies a thousand years old couldn’t
possibly be applied to anyone else, yet fit Him perfectly.  Let’s remember Him as our great High Priest,
who paid the price for our sins by offering His own blood in the presence of
God in heaven.  Finally, let’s honor Him
as the great King who reigns even now, who is present with us and will lead us
to eternal victory.  Let’s consider all
this as we partake.

For the rest of our time this morning, I want to finish off
our study of Ephesians.  If you’ll
recall, Ephesians 1-3 is the doctrinal part of the book.  It explains the marvelous salvation that God
has given us and how much we will benefit eternally from His grace.  The rest of the book is the so-what.  It outlines how we ought to show our
gratitude for the grace that has been given to us.  I preached on Ephesians 4 a few weeks ago and
on Ephesians 5 about a year ago, so let’s wrap things up this morning with a
study of Ephesians 6.

**Relationships**

The first main section of this chapter has to do with
relationships, and the first relationship that it considers is between **PARENTS AND CHILDREN.** Look here at Ephesians 6:1-4.  In order to understand where Paul is coming
from here, we must understand the context. 
This is part of a discussion that begins all the way back in Ephesians
5:21.  There, Paul tells us that in all
of the relationships of our lives, we are supposed to submit to others because
of reverence for Christ.

Let’s start with the children first.  According to the Holy Spirit, their
responsibility is to honor and obey their parents.  For this reason, I would imagine that most of
the people here with Christian parents have had this verse quoted at them at
least once.  This morning, though, I
don’t want to focus on the what.  I want
to focus on the why.  Paul quotes Exodus
20 here and notes that it ends with a promise: 
“That it may go well with you and that you may live long on the land.”

There’s an important point here, kids.  You need to listen to your parents because
listening to your parents is the road to a long, happy life.  This might not seem like the fun thing to do,
but I guarantee you it’s the wise thing to do. 
I know a sister who thought she knew better than her parents when it
came to boys.  She started fooling around
with the running back on the football team, and he got her pregnant at age
14.  Think that changes your life
trajectory a little bit?  For myself,
I’ve never been sorry when I listened to my parents.  I’ve only been sorry when I didn’t.

Similarly, fathers, and indeed parents generally, have a
responsibility to submit to their children. 
This means first of all not provoking them by being pointlessly petty
tyrants.  No child will react well when
held to impossible or inconsistent standards.

Second, parents submit by bringing up their children in the
discipline and instruction of the Lord. 
There’s nothing in the Bible that says we owe our children a good
secular education, but we do owe them the best spiritual education we can give
them.  When we don’t make assembling with
the saints our top priority, we’re letting our children down.  When we don’t reinforce that spiritual
teaching with more spiritual teaching at home, we’re letting our children
down.  When we don’t live godly lives
24/7, we’re letting our children down. 
They deserve our best.  Let’s give
to them.

Likewise, God’s rules govern the relationship between **SERVANTS AND MASTERS.** Consider Ephesians 6:5-9.  Of course, the situation here is a little
different than the situation in modern workplaces.  The servants in Ephesians are literally
slaves and the masters are literally slaveowners.  However, there are still some principles that
carry over in both directions.

For servants, Paul’s basic point is one that will strike
fear into the hearts of would-be malingerers everywhere.  Christ expects His people to serve their
earthly masters the way they would serve Him. 
They need to work hard all the time, not merely when the boss is
standing over them.

When I was at Menard’s the other day, I saw an example of
what men-pleasing employees do.  Menard’s
is different from most other stores in that their cart corrals have a roof and
partial sides.  When I was on the way
back to my car, I noticed a couple of the cart collectors hanging out inside
one.  They weren’t getting carts; they
were standing around texting and laughing it up.  I guess they knew from experience that if
they did that, the managers inside the store couldn’t see them.

We, though, must remember that whether or not the manager
can put eyes on us, God can and does. 
Maybe we can find a way to slack and not get fired, but we will never be
able to find a way to slack and please Him. 
Employees must remember that Christ is their true Master.

Indeed, Paul says that this applies not only to those who
are servants in earthly terms, but to those who are masters.  Some employers and managers show kindness to
their underlings and inspire them to work well; others are jerks who rely on
coercion and fear.  In either case, God
knows which is which, and the supervisor who is a Christian must remember that
he is going to get back from His heavenly Master what he dishes out to his
earthly servants.

**Spiritual Warfare**

The final section of Ephesians 6 primarily concerns itself
with spiritual warfare, and this begins with the apostle describing **OUR STRUGGLE.** Let’s keep going in Ephesians
6:10-13.  This is a text with a simple
message.  Every single one of us is in
the middle of a spiritual war zone, and unless we recognize that fact, our
spiritual enemies are going to kill us.

I generally have a rule that I don’t use Civil War
illustrations from the pulpit, but in this case, there’s one that’s too perfect
to pass up.  It’s from the battle of
Stones River, December 1862.  The battle
opens with a massive Confederate attack on the Union right that catches the
Union army completely by surprise.  They
are literally sitting around their campfires cooking breakfast when the Rebel
line of battle comes through the cedars. 
How well do you think the opening part of that battle went for the
Union?  Sadly, I fear that all too many
Christians on our spiritual battlefield are engaged in the equivalent of
sitting around cooking breakfast.  Their
service to God is lackadaisical and halfhearted.  They’re worried about this and that and the
other thing, but they aren’t worried about safeguarding their souls or their
family’s souls.

Brethren, we must never think that because we aren’t giving
much thought to the devil, he isn’t giving much thought to us.  Indeed, the opposite is true.  Satan never misses an opportunity.  His line of battle is coming through the
trees up on us.  Either we are vigilant
and prepared to fight for our lives, or he is going to roll over us.

Preparedness does not consist of reading spiritual self-help
books or following the course of human wisdom. 
Instead, it consists of taking up the whole armor of God and doing all
that we need to do in order to stand firm. 
Notice, friends, that Paul doesn’t say, “Take up whatever pieces of the
armor of God are most convenient for you.” 
He doesn’t say, “And having done part of what you should have done, to
stand firm.”  That’s not how it
works.  Either we are all the way
prepared, or we are unprepared and will be easy meat for the spiritual forces
of evil in the heavenly places.

Paul tells us how we must prepare as he lays out the pieces
of **THE ARMOR OF GOD.** This famous text appears in Ephesians
6:14-18a.  As many of us probably
remember from childhood, Paul compares various spiritual attributes to the
equipment of a Roman soldier.  He tells
us that we must arm ourselves for battle with truth, righteousness, readiness,
faith, salvation, and the word of God. 
Contextually, I think that in addition to these, he means for us to
understand prayer as part of our spiritual equipment.

That’s all well and good, but how exactly are we supposed to
do this?  How do we put on all these
pieces of spiritual armor?  I think it
all has to begin with the time we spend with the word.  We get truth from the word of truth.  The Bible also describes itself as the word
of righteousness.  How else can we hope
to learn to be righteous except from it? 
Similarly, we will never develop the readiness that comes from the
gospel of peace apart from the gospel of peace. 
Faith comes by hearing, and hearing by the word of Christ.  The gospel is the good news of our
salvation.  Only then, once we have used
the word to defend ourselves, can we use it to go on the attack and bring the
gospel to others, and in that work, there is no greater asset than prayer.

This draws a pretty distinct line between the spiritual
haves and have-nots, doesn’t it?  On the
one hand, you have the haves, the disciples who immerse themselves in the word
both inside and outside the assembly. 
They’re ready to resist the onslaught of the devil and fight back.  On the other, you have the have-nots, the
disciples who don’t immerse themselves in the word.  They won’t be ready.  Brethren, we don’t need to spend time in the
word because it’s entertaining.  We need
to spend time in the word because our souls depend on it.

Finally, let’s consider Paul’s **CLOSING COMMENTS.** They’re
found in Ephesians 6:18b-23.  As Paul’s
ordering implies, prayer is more than armor. 
It’s a weapon.  We can use it to
protect not just ourselves, but others. 
As a result, he urges the Ephesians to pray for all the saints and for
him particularly.  This, I think, is one
of the passages that really shows Paul’s humanity.  We think of him as this super-courageous
apostle, the man who proclaimed the truth to people who wanted to kill him.  And yet, here we see him asking the Ephesians
to pray that he will be bold, not once, but twice.  Even somebody like Paul needed the help that
came through the prayers of others.

There are two lessons here for us.  The first is that we ought to pray for
others.  We should pray for those who are
weak, for Christians who seem to be wavering in their faith or even those who
have offended us in some way.  We should
pray too for the strong, for those who seem to have got it all figured out
spiritually.  As Paul’s example reveals,
no matter how it may look from the outside, nobody actually has it all figured
out!  The most mature Christian on the
planet will still be grateful for the prayers of others because he’s mature
enough to see how much he needs the help.

Second, we must learn that we need others to pray for
us.  I think all of us know the
temptation to put on a front, to act like we’re the perfect Christians who are
doing such a wonderful job of following Jesus that we’re on a glide path right
through the pearly gates.  In reality, of
course, we aren’t, and every one of us knows that.  If we want to see those pearly gates at all,
we’re going to need prayer along the way!

Paul concludes
Ephesians, as he often does his epistles, with some personal remarks and a
blessing.  The Ephesian church is
important enough to him that he’s sending Tychicus to update them on his
status.  In addition, he cares about them
enough to bless them with peace, faith, love, and grace.  2000 years later, may those same blessings
attend all of us!
