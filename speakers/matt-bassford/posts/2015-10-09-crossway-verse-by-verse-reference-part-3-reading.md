**OVERVIEW.**  We’re kind of putting a plow horse on the
racetrack here.  This is a specialized
preaching Bible, so obviously it’s not going to be quite as good as a reader.  However, it still performed creditably, and
it will serve well for those who prefer to read from verse-by-verse Bibles.



As per usual, I spent two weeks doing my daily Bible reading
out of the Verse-by-Verse Reference. 
Here’s what I noticed about it:

**SIZE.** I’m fond of saying that I like to read
out of Bibles that remind me of paperback fantasy novels (yes, I’m one of Those
People).  However, the VbV is anything
but paperback-like in its dimensions.  It
reminds me much more of those beefy Robert Jordan hardbacks, in length and
width if not in girth.  However, the size
wasn’t really a problem for me.  The
Bible doesn’t weigh very much for a full-size Bible, so I never got tired of
holding it while I was reading.

**LAYOUT.** I’ve never reviewed a Bible with quite
the same layout characteristics as the VbV. 
It’s a double-column Bible, which is nothing special.  However, it pairs standard-size 9-point print
with dimensions that usually accompany a large-print Bible.  As a result of this, it has really long lines
for a double column Bible.  I make it out
to have about 49 cpl.  That’s almost as
many as the Foundation Side-Column Reference, and the SCR is a single-column
Bible!

Normally, this kind of line length would make its presence
felt most in the poetic sections of the Old Testament:  Psalms, Proverbs, and the like.  There, it ought to cut down on the one-word
“widows” that make reading Bible poetry in double-column Bibles more of a
chore.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEh8eQdnSltYrnMZHOQXufT1x1A1lZOcLM_6LUmjX6yMQDMblBujBr-JbIskhP56SHaoBeXUmIzeN5NWpET9kxVnIr9dL3K5YFOui8HNEtYBVpQhC19zE8WevMSAtq8_s8T94o2BS7wNNg/s1600/R+prose.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEh8eQdnSltYrnMZHOQXufT1x1A1lZOcLM_6LUmjX6yMQDMblBujBr-JbIskhP56SHaoBeXUmIzeN5NWpET9kxVnIr9dL3K5YFOui8HNEtYBVpQhC19zE8WevMSAtq8_s8T94o2BS7wNNg/s1600/R+prose.jpg)



However, this simply wasn’t a consideration in my experience
of the VbV.  Unlike every other
non-King-James Bible that I’ve seen, the VbV sets the entirety of the Bible in
prose.  Because the text is not broken up
into poetic lines, there are no widows to worry about.  A cursory scan of the Bible doesn’t reveal
any widows at the ends of verses either. 
They’re much easier to avoid in a verse-by-verse prose Bible, and the
designers did.

If I had to guess, Crossway set the Bible in prose for a
couple of different reasons.  First, this
is designed to be a pulpit Bible, and in the pulpit, a poetic setting is
pointless.  You’re not reading for
comprehension up there, and all the widows might make you miss the underlying
sentence structure.  For a preaching
Bible, it makes sense to set the whole thing in prose.

Second, verse-by-verse is a space-hungry format.  All those half-lines of white space add up
over the course of 1200 pages!  Poetic
setting is also a space-hungry format; all those one- and two-word lines are
pretty inefficient too.  By collapsing
the poetry into prose, the designers of the VbV probably made it 50-100 pages
shorter than it would otherwise have been.

However—and here’s the funny thing—I didn’t feel like
turning the OT poetry into prose made it harder to understand.  During my review period, my daily reading
plan took me through part of the Psalms, so I had plenty of opportunity to
consider this.  Never did I come away
from my reading feeling like I lost comprehension because of the prose.  Whatever I lost from the lines not being
broken out poetically, I more than gained back from the absence of widows.

This suggests, then, that at least every double-column
verse-by-verse Bible, and maybe every double-column Bible, period, should set
the whole Bible in prose.  Versifying the
poetry works great in single-column Bibles, but if you can’t pull it off
smoothly in double-column Bibles, why try?

However, another preaching-friendly attribute of the VbV
proved to be a stumbling block for me when I was reading.  As with a few other Crossway designs (most
notably the Crossway Thinline), the VbV has some serious leading.  As per the box, the Bible is set in 9-point
Lexicon No. 1, but just eyeballing it, I would guess that it has something like
11-point leading.  That’s a lot of
leading!

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEife639kVU7iMNSE9uZHWC3VkRgPd0CSf1HK_G7gOf0YjnvtU2dZ-aR-i3i_nGc5t-7vnDWe1lzaaLYvQi5udWxbgFcOxU64YEo8OciIBuxGuWMtbfZvSQCKc0054HefTxQ8P8nW36-oQ/s1600/R+open.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEife639kVU7iMNSE9uZHWC3VkRgPd0CSf1HK_G7gOf0YjnvtU2dZ-aR-i3i_nGc5t-7vnDWe1lzaaLYvQi5udWxbgFcOxU64YEo8OciIBuxGuWMtbfZvSQCKc0054HefTxQ8P8nW36-oQ/s1600/R+open.jpg)

As a result, there’s a lot of white space between each
line.  I suspect that Crossway included
it to make each line distinct.  That way,
when you’re performing a public reading, there’s not as much chance of your
eyes jumping lines on you.  In a Bible
optimized for preachers, this is a sensible design choice.

It didn’t bother me in my poetry readings in the Old
Testament, but I wasn’t a fan when I was reading from the prose of the New
Testament.  I think I read poetry much
more slowly than I read prose, spending more time dwelling on each line.  However, when I’m reading prose, I’m reading
for the gist and move quickly from line to line.

Though there’s no way to be sure, I felt like the very
generous leading acted like a miniature speed bump at the end of each line,
making my eyes work harder to find the next line and keep up my train of
thought.  By contrast, when I’m reading
from the Foundation Side-Column Reference, which is set in 11-point Times New
Roman with 10.5-point leading (which means that they took out some of the white
space built into the typeface), my eyes can move from line to line without
hindrance.

Leading aside, the VbV gets lots of other things right as
reading Bible.  For instance, the gutters
are fine.  Half an inch isn’t enough to
write in, but it’s more than enough to keep the text by the inner margin from
sliding into the abyss.

Similarly, the VbV deprives me of the opportunity to segue
into my usual rant about red-letter Bibles. 
It’s black-letter, which is a design choice that I heartily
endorse.  The beauty of black-letter
Bibles lies in their simplicity.  You
don’t have to worry about words of Christ in orange or pink.  You don’t run the risk of registration
errors.  As a result, all the things that
can (and often do) go wrong in a red-letter Bible couldn’t go wrong in the VbV.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEhWjpptEnNUeHEmrS473bACvlkM0sfYePGB3aPp9J1BHfnL1x2zpsDGg6p3rdkRGOOEVgEI-POORAEAMoOImxC4UoR1FTcnSTXT6wMY9onO_D9MFaIErb8PM91l708haoR3QYwBGtov8w/s1600/R+blackletter.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEhWjpptEnNUeHEmrS473bACvlkM0sfYePGB3aPp9J1BHfnL1x2zpsDGg6p3rdkRGOOEVgEI-POORAEAMoOImxC4UoR1FTcnSTXT6wMY9onO_D9MFaIErb8PM91l708haoR3QYwBGtov8w/s1600/R+blackletter.jpg)

Strangely, unlike most other recent Crossway designs, the
VbV isn’t line-matched.  This isn’t a
huge deal; the paper is still good enough to keep ghosting from being a
problem.  However, it still makes the
Bible slightly harder to read.  Finally,
at 9 points, the font in the VbV is more than adequate for a reading Bible.  Lexicon No. 1 is very readable at small sizes
anyway.

**PRODUCTION
QUALITY.** This could be better, but
it could also be a lot worse.  I’ve been
a Jongbloed fanboy ever since I figured out what a difference it made for a
Bible to be Jongbloed-printed.  However,
the VbV is produced in China rather than the Netherlands.  There’s nothing unusual about that; nearly
every mass-market Bible is printed there. 
Irony, that.

It’s true that since all of the major Bible publishers
offshored their production, the Chinese have learned a lot about their
craft.  As a representative of the bad
old days, my 2008 Foundation Side-Column reference has pretty terrible print
quality.  The fine lines in the
references especially are thick and almost blotchy.  Nothing like that is evident in the VbV.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgBongDKVfuslmpGSsCbB7SWhlAo-6AntWJFAdMCi6Ue5-cqOqEu_HWZyFey8HeDadYEFuhccDC39GFU8ZZQdHPMRfxpycStS4KbMtmB8Z-jgeR3bt6oFRQ2w_tkLExX3DLDzUUyHLY-Q/s1600/R+not+linematched+%25282%2529.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgBongDKVfuslmpGSsCbB7SWhlAo-6AntWJFAdMCi6Ue5-cqOqEu_HWZyFey8HeDadYEFuhccDC39GFU8ZZQdHPMRfxpycStS4KbMtmB8Z-jgeR3bt6oFRQ2w_tkLExX3DLDzUUyHLY-Q/s1600/R+not+linematched+%25282%2529.jpg)



However, there are still some problems.  For instance, there’s speckling on the top of
p. 896, where it looks like some excess ink got splashed on the page.  I’d like to see China-print Bibles evolve to
the point where they’re the equal of Jongbloed products, but that day hasn’t
arrived yet.

By contrast, the paper in the VbV is pretty stinkin’ good
for a mass-market Bible.  My utterly
uncalibrated eyeball would put it somewhere in the 30-35 gsm range.  This doesn’t banish the ghosts completely,
but it does confine them somewhat.

**CONCLUSION.** Not surprisingly, the
built-for-the-pulpit VbV leaves something to be desired as a reader.  It gets a lot of things right, but the
leading in the prose sections bothered me. 
I think it’s worthy of a 7.5/10 as a reader, which nets it a 24/30
overall, certainly a respectable score.
