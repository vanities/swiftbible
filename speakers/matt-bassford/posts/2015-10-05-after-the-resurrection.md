If someone asked one of us what the single most important event recorded in the Bible was, I suspect that most of us would answer with the crucifixion of Jesus.  After all, His death on the cross for the sins of mankind is all that stands between us and eternal condemnation.

However, as important as the death of Jesus is, His resurrection was equally important.  Without the resurrection, there would be nothing to tell us that Jesus was any more than another criminal executed by the Romans.  However, with the resurrection, we see Jesus, in the words of Paul in Romans 1, “proclaimed the Son of God with power”.  In other words, it is the resurrection that shows that Jesus was the Messiah He claimed to be, instead of merely another fraud.  The resurrection, then, is at the heart of our faith.  Let’s look this morning at the very end of the gospel of John, at what he says takes place after the resurrection.


**The First Day of the Week**

After Jesus’ appearance to Mary Magdalene in the garden, which we’re not going to look at because I preached on it about a year ago, John discusses various disciples’ encounters with Jesus on the first day of the week.  This narrative begins with **JESUS’ APPEARANCE** to the disciples, probably in the very same upper room where they ate the Last Supper.  John’s account of this appearance begins in John 20:19-20.  This occurs at a moment of tremendous emotional strain for the disciples.  After all, their Lord had only been crucified last Friday, and many of them were witnesses to the crucifixion.  They were meeting with the doors shut because they were afraid that the same Jewish conspiracy that had taken Jesus was going to get them too.

However, some of the news wasn’t bad, just bewildering.  That morning, some of the women had gone to the tomb, found it empty, and met an angel who insisted that Jesus had risen.  Mary Magdalene was noisily claiming that she had met Jesus herself.  Peter and John had seen the empty tomb too, though not any angels.  In addition, a couple of out-of-town disciples had come barging into their meeting, claiming that they had also seen the risen Lord.  At this point, though, most of Jesus’ followers are still skeptical.  If we had also seen Jesus die on the cross and perhaps carried His dead body ourselves, wouldn’t we be too?

The debate is then ended, though, by the appearance of Jesus Himself.  Even though the doors are shut, poof!  He materializes right in the middle of the disciples.  However, Jesus is no ghost.  They can clearly see the wounds in His hands and side.  Even though this text isn’t clear on the matter, John claims in 1 John 1 that he touched as well as saw Jesus.  Jesus has been bodily raised from the dead, and they rejoice.

Next, we see Jesus impart **THE HOLY SPIRIT** to the disciples.  Consider John 20:21-23.  In many ways, this is a text that is parallel to the Holy Spirit coming upon the apostles in Acts 2.  As always, let’s pause to appreciate the symbolism here.  The Greek word for “spirit” is pneuma, which also means “breath” or “wind”.  In Acts 2, the Holy Spirit makes its appearance as a mighty wind.  Here, it is imparted by Jesus breathing on the disciples, the literal breath of God.  Of course, one of the things that distinguishes the living from the dead is that corpses don’t breathe.  Here, Jesus clearly does.

Jesus then goes on to tell the disciples what it is that they’ve gotten.  He reveals that if they forgive the sins of any, they will have been forgiven, and also that the opposite is true.  This does not mean that the disciples now have the power to forgive sins.  Instead, it means that they will be able to correctly identify those whom God has already forgiven, or not.  This sounds a lot like the spiritual gift of knowledge.

Here, and in a couple of other places in Scripture, we see people receive spiritual gifts on more than one occasion.  The twelve disciples, after all, received from Jesus the power to heal the sick and cast out demons way back in Matthew 10.  Here, they gain the gift of knowledge.  In Acts 2, they’re going to receive the gift of speaking in tongues.  Of course, we can’t look for any of these miraculous gifts today, but it’s still important for us to recognize that the process was more complicated than we often think.

However, there is one important figure who is absent on this first day of the week, the apostle **THOMAS.**  We see the story for which he is most famous in John 20:24-28.  What strikes me about this is how emphatic Thomas’ refusal to believe is.  As far as he is concerned, he is not going to believe the stories the others are telling until he touches the wounds of Jesus himself.  This is not the response of a man who is keeping an open mind on the subject of the resurrection.  This is a man who is convinced that Jesus is still dead and so imposes a standard of proof that he thinks is impossible to meet.

In many ways, Thomas’ response is a reasonable one.  It’s impossible for dead men to just up and start wandering around on their own again.  Thomas is confounded, though, by the fact that the impossible has already occurred, and next Sunday, when the disciples are gathered again, Jesus appears to him.

Here too, we see an encounter that is worth dissecting.  Some skeptics claim that Jesus wasn’t actually raised from the dead, that instead, the disciples saw an impostor.  There are a number of problems with that theory, but in this text, we see one of the foremost.  If this is an impostor, it is a really, really dedicated impostor!  The wounds that Thomas is touching are as real as they can be, to the point where he can put his fingers into the nail holes in Jesus’ hands and put his whole hand into the spear wound in the Lord’s side.  In other words, for this to be an impostor, he would have had to have been willing to be stabbed in the side by a Roman spear, which would probably have killed the guy anyway!  In reality, there is no good explanation for Thomas’ experience other than that Jesus Himself was truly raised from the dead.

This is important not only for what it says about what happened to Jesus, but for what it says about what will happen to us.  Over and over again, the Scripture compares the resurrection that we will receive to the resurrection that Jesus received.  His was certainly a bodily resurrection, which means that we can anticipate the resurrection of our bodies too.  As 1 Corinthians 15 reveals, those bodies will be transformed and glorified, but they will still bear an essential relationship to our bodies here, like an oak to an acorn.

John concludes this narrative, though, with a description of **HIS PURPOSE** in writing.  This appears in John 20:30-31.  These words are often applied to the entirety of the gospel of John, and I think it’s not a mistake to do so, but John is speaking in particular about the content of John 20 only.  Back in John 19, there’s a similar statement at the end of the crucifixion narrative.  In effect, John is telling us there, “Jesus really, really died, and here’s what I saw that proves it.”

Here, he’s saying the opposite.  He’s saying, “Peter and I saw the empty tomb, Mary met Jesus in the garden, He appeared to all of us in the upper room, and Thomas literally touched the wounds of Jesus the very next week.  There are plenty of other things that happened after Jesus rose from the dead that I could tell you about, but these four things are enough for you to believe the truth.”  They are.  The people who knew Jesus best, who watched Him die, who took Him down from the cross, who buried Him, testify also that He rose from the grave.  As the example of Thomas shows, they didn’t expect it to happen, but it did.

Before the Lord’s Supper, let’s reflect on these things.  The resurrection of Jesus isn’t some kind of comforting fairy tale.  It’s historical fact, better attested than any other event from ancient history.  It happened, and the implications are earth-shattering.  Because Jesus lived again, we who believe in Him will live again too.  Let’s consider the power and wonder of these things as we partake.

**Jesus in Galilee**

Next, we come to the beginning of one of my favorite stories in Scripture (as always, such claims from me should be taken with a grain of salt), the story of the disciples encountering Jesus in Galilee.  It begins with Peter and half a dozen other disciples **GOING FISHING**.  Let’s read from John 21:1-3.  All of this, of course, is taking place in the forty-day span between Jesus’ resurrection and His ascension.  Given that the time frame is so short, and that Jesus ultimately ascends from the Mount of Olives, we might wonder what the disciples were doing back in Galilee.  The reason here is quite simple.  Even though John doesn’t record it, both the angels and the resurrected Jesus told the disciples to go to Galilee.

This is not the same as it would be for us to hoof it to Texas and back in 40 days.  Even the far side of the sea of Galilee is only about 90 miles from Jerusalem, so the disciples, who are probably pretty fit after several years of hiking around after Jesus, wouldn’t have much trouble getting there and back in the allotted time span.  Regardless, there they are in Galilee, waiting for Jesus to show up, when Peter decides there’s no point in sitting around.  He decides to go fishing, and another half-dozen disciples decide to come with him.

These are professional fishermen, and we know that James and John, at least, were successful enough to have a second boat staffed by servants, but tonight is not their night.  They catch nothing.  At this point, those of us who remember the story of the calling of Peter, Andrew, James, and John, which also begins with the foursome catching nothing, might start to suspect something funny is going on.  It doesn’t seem, though, that any of the disciples have caught on.  Perhaps catching nothing was a fairly common occurrence for them.

In any case, they toil futilely all night, until they hear **AN ECHO** from the past.  John tells the story in John 21:4-8.  Now, I think this is inexpressibly cool.  Here’s the way the story in Luke 5 goes:  Peter, Andrew, James, and John have fished all night, caught nothing, and returned to shore to wash their nets.  Jesus happens by and asks Peter if he can borrow his boat for a floating pulpit.  Peter agrees, which isn’t too strange, because Peter and Jesus have had dealings before.  After He’s done speaking, Jesus tells Peter to let down his nets one more time.  This is something of a ridiculous request.  It would be kind of like me telling Jeff Andrews how he should design the production lines at Tootsie Roll.  Who does Jesus think He is, that He can tell the professional fisherman how to fish?  Regardless, Peter does what he asks, and the resulting catch is so great that it swamps not just Peter’s boat, but James and John’s boat too.  Peter recognizes that a miracle has happened and falls down at Jesus’ feet.  Jesus tells him, “Follow Me.”

Here we are, then, about three years after that initial miracle in Galilee.  The disciples are once again fishing all night.  Once again, they have no success.  Once again, Jesus happens by, except this time they are prevented from recognizing Him as Jesus.  Once again, Jesus instructs them to do something that the pros know is futile—casting their nets on the other side of the boat.  Once again, they listen.  Once again, they catch a massive haul of fish, so many of them that they aren’t even able to bring the net into the boat.  After this, John says to Peter, “It’s the Lord.”  He was really going out on a limb there, wasn’t he?  Peter acknowledges the presence of Jesus in typically dramatic fashion, by putting his clothes on and hurling himself into the sea.  The others follow him in more subdued fashion, dragging the net full of fish.

Of course, it’s not any kind of accident that all of these events from the initial call of Peter, Andrew, James, and John are repeating themselves.  Jesus has set this whole thing up, and He’s stage-managing it to make a point.  The first event signified the calling of the disciples.  This one, then, is a re-calling, a re-dedication to Jesus’ work.  They abandoned Him in the garden, but they’re going to get another chance.

This is such a heartening passage to me, friends, because it reminds me that God is a God of second chances, not just for the disciples, not just for me, but for all of us.  We can commit to serving Him and then fall flat on our faces.  We can mess up being a Christian as thoroughly as it can be messed up.  You know what?  God’s grace is sufficient even for that.  If we’re willing to get back up and try again, He’s willing to give us the opportunity.  Our circumstances may be changed, but His mercy is still the same.

Now that Jesus has announced Himself so unmistakably, it sets the stage for **BREAKFAST**.  This is described in John 21:9-14.  Once the disciples struggle to the shore, they find another familiar sight:  a fire with bread and fish on it.  These are fishermen.  They’ve been eating fish not only on Fridays, but for their whole lives.  They doubtless ate plenty of fish during their time with Jesus; after all, back in the day, fish was cheap protein.  Now, they’re about to share one last meal with their Teacher.

Before that, though, they do something that might seem weird to us.  They count the fish, of which there turn out to be 153.  Probably, once they’re done talking to Jesus, the disciples take these 153 fish to Capernaum and sell them.  This is an intriguing detail because it’s an odd number.  Any student of the Bible quickly realizes that often in Scripture, numbers aren’t just numbers.  When we read about the 144,000 in Revelation 14, we shouldn’t take that as being literally 144,000.  It’s twelve twelve thousands, and the number twelve is significant because of the number of the tribes of Israel, the number of the apostles, and so on.  If some first-century Jew were making up this story, there probably would have been 144 fish.  However, John doesn’t describe 144 fish, or 72 fish, or anything like that.  Instead, it’s 153.  This is not a significant number, except that it accurately captures the number of fish that were in that net.  John relates it to establish that this whole story is not a made-up event.  Instead, it’s something that really happened.

**Jesus and Peter**

The final section of John 21, and indeed of the entire gospel, concerns a conversation primarily between Jesus and Peter.  This conversation begins with Jesus asking Peter about his **LOVE** for Him.  John describes what takes place in John 21:15-17.  This is an extremely well-known part of Scripture.  Jesus asks Peter three times if he loves Him, Peter expresses his love three times, and each time Jesus ends the exchange by telling Peter to tend His lambs, or shepherd His sheep, or some such variation.

In times past, much has been made of the fact that Jesus uses the Greek word agapao when He asks Peter about love, but when Peter answers, he does so using the Greek word phileo.  However, this variation in wording is probably not significant.  For one thing, Jesus and Peter weren’t even speaking Greek.  They were speaking Aramaic, so those specific words never passed the lips of either one on this occasion.  Second, it was a common stylistic practice 2000 years ago, just as it is today, to use synonyms for stylistic reasons instead of repeating the same word over and over again.  That’s probably all John is doing here—mixing it up a little bit.

Once we ignore the red herring, the true meaning of the text emerges.  What Jesus is emphasizing to Peter, and what Peter may be a bit slow in recognizing, is that if Peter really loves Jesus, he is going to express that love by taking care of Jesus’ flock.  Though obviously our situation is a little different than Peter’s, the same principle applies.  We love God?  Great!  We love Jesus?  Outstanding!  Let’s prove it by getting out there and taking care of God’s people.  Let’s look after the shut-ins.  Let’s be out in front of everybody in our willingness to teach Bible classes.  Let’s reach out to the people at church we don’t know.  Let’s open our homes to other Christians whenever we can.  Let’s call and e-mail and Facebook the members we haven’t seen at services for a while.  In short, let’s do everything we can to help one another get to heaven.

Next, Jesus tells Peter to **FOLLOW HIM**.  Look at John 21:18-19.  In many ways, this is the climax of the whole chapter.  Remember, at the end of the Luke 5 story, Jesus tells Peter and the others to follow Him.  I daresay that when Peter did, he didn’t have the foggiest idea what he was getting himself into.  He saw all kinds of amazing things, heard three years’ worth of spellbinding sermons, and came to the conclusion that Jesus was the Messiah, the anointed Son of David who had come to deliver the Jewish people from their Roman overlords.  Of course, things didn’t work out according to Peter’s plan.  Instead, he saw his Messiah betrayed, taken, and ultimately executed.  He even denied Jesus to save his own skin.

Now, though, from the God of second chances, Peter gets a second chance to follow.  Jesus is very clear about what this means.  If the recent example of the crucifixion isn’t lesson enough, Jesus tells Peter that he too is going to be taken by his enemies and killed.  If Peter wants to follow Jesus, that’s going to mean submitting himself to execution for the glory of God.

I’ve often heard Christians wonder if they had a good handle on what they were getting into when they were baptized.  I myself remember quite clearly what I was thinking when I obeyed the gospel, and even though I knew that I loved Jesus and wanted to live for Him from now on, there was a great deal that I didn’t understand.  Over the decades since, I’ve come to learn more about what following Jesus means, and if He wills, in the decades to come, I’ll learn still more again.  That’s the way it is for all of us, I think.  No new Christian knows what he’s getting into.  It’s simply not possible.  Jesus’ call to follow Him will constantly change in meaning as we grow in Christ.  The question is whether we answer it.

After this dramatic moment, Peter turns the conversation to **JOHN**.  This exchange is documented in John 21:20-25.  At a guess, Peter is pretty upset at this point.  John tells us that he was already upset in v. 17, and it’s likely that his mental state was not improved by Jesus telling him that he was going to be killed for the Lord’s sake.  Here, as elsewhere through the gospel of John, John is coy about identifying himself, but it’s clear that John is the third person involved in the conversation.  Peter wants to know about John.  Peter is going to have to die for Jesus; well and good.  But what about John?  Does he have to die too?

Jesus’ response is effectively that this shouldn’t matter to Peter in the least.  What difference does it make to Peter whether John is going to remain alive until the second coming (which John hastens to note is not actually going to happen)?  No matter what happens to John, Peter’s job is still to follow Jesus.

This is something that we should keep in mind when we’re tempted to compare our situations to the situations of other Christians.  Usually, we don’t get upset about the brethren who are doing worse than we are.  We get upset about the ones who seem to be doing better.  We can barely make ends meet, but they’re swimming in money.  Our children behave like wild animals, but their children are perfect little angels.  Our lives are filled with spiritual struggles, but they seem to ace this Christianity thing without even trying hard.  “What about them, Lord?” we’re tempted to ask, meaning, “When are they going to suffer like I do?”

Jesus’ answer is the same to us as it was to Peter:  “What is that to you?  You follow Me!”  Who cares how anybody else is doing?  Who cares if Brother So-and-So has a life that looks like it came from a marriage-and-family workbook?  For one thing, the lives of other Christians often look better from the outside than the inside.  We’ve all got struggles that not everybody knows about.  But even more importantly, somebody else’s situation doesn’t change our responsibility.  We’re here to follow Jesus, no matter what.

After this, John concludes with two remarks about the whole gospel.  Everything he wrote in it is true, but he didn’t write everything in it.  There are all kinds of stories that John left out because they didn’t suit the purpose of his writing.   However, from the stories he did write, we have all kinds of things to learn.
