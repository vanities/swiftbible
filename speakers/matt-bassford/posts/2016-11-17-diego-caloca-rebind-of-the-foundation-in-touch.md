The best paper I’ve ever seen in a Bible is the paper in Foundation’s Wide-Margin In Touch.  It’s better than the 38-gsm Tervakowski Thinopaque in my Cambridge NASB Wide-Margin Reference.  It’s better than the legendary French-milled paper in the 2002 Foundation Side-Column Reference.  The only paper that might be better than it is the paper in the first-edition Schuyler NASB Quentel, but I’ve never seen one of those, and that’s a Bible that doesn’t suit my purposes anyway.

I got interested in Bibles (and particularly, in wide-margin Bibles) just a little too late to get a leather-bound In Touch while stores were still stocking them.  I’m disinclined to pay $200 for a Bible on eBay, too.  In consequence, I bought a hardback In Touch instead.

As Bibles go, the hardback In Touch (now also out of print) is a strange creature.  It’s a hardback, but the text block inside is, except for corner rounding and edge gilding, exactly the same as the text block in a calfskin In Touch.  They were all printed together in 1999, but for some reason that escapes me, didn’t sell well.  Lack of references, maybe?  I’m hard on hardbacks, so I bought the Bible intending from the beginning to have it rebound, which I did earlier this year.

I started with cowhide from Springfield Leather.  Some of my hymn work over the summer brought me to the Springfield area, so I was able to stop by the store and caress the leather for myself, as opposed to ordering online, both unseen and unfelt.  I’m not quite sure what I eventually bought, but I believe it was some black bomber-jacket-finish cowhide.  It has a wonderful smell and texture (IMO, cowhide on a Bible is greatly underrated), and it wasn’t terribly expensive.

For my rebinder, I opted for Diego Caloca.  I’d been hearing great things about Diego’s work for years, and I knew that he did own-leather work.  I’m a simple soul, and I don’t have a whole lot of money, so I opted for a fairly inexpensive treatment for the In Touch:  a simple paste-down lining instead of edge-lining.  I’m a Bible user, not a Bible collector, and in my experience, the bookboard in paste-down Bibles becomes more flexible the more you use it, so that opening the Bible generally doesn’t stress the hinge much anyway.  I also asked Diego to round the corners and took advantage of an intriguing option I saw on his site:  a custom-made slipcase.

As I would expect, Diego’s customer service was great.  He figured out exactly what I wanted, put the Bible in his rebinding queue, and after six weeks, had it on its way back to me.  The only hitch came when I had to remind him that I hadn’t paid him in full yet!  Part of me suspects that if Diego were to become independently wealthy, he’d happily rebind people’s Bibles for free.  Dude loves his work.

The Bible that I got back was everything that I wanted it to be.  With the lack of edge gilding, I was hoping for an ESV Journaling Bible kind of vibe:  spare and understated.  Also, I knew the bomber-jacket leather didn’t have a very sturdy finish, so that it would quickly pick up a distressed, worn, almost dusty look.  That’s just what I got.  I’ve never been cool enough even to be reverse-cool, but the In Touch is now a hipster-ish Bible.

The slipcase also works well.  It’s considerably sturdier than the slipcases I’ve made by destroying Bible boxes, and it looks better too.  However, I soon found that the In Touch is a large enough Bible that when stored upright, its text block began to sag in the middle.  I think the damage from that would probably have been limited, but I decided to add the In Touch to my stacks anyway.  Even when it’s on its side, though, the slipcase still protects the Bible from pressure from the Bibles above it.  I consider the $30 I paid for it money well spent.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgHY-iia0yXNnE6P0UsCQNOwT8Sd48DZomiuqKmi4zqsnOz5TVHFWTTDRBGSB9dLAp6bhYVS0EggcQl0KGwfqeDJenrw-tx-Ofn533f8B20cLStJgOKM7_9TS1U160kZbAwq_wKldw4jg/s1600/NASB_Caloca_0045.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgHY-iia0yXNnE6P0UsCQNOwT8Sd48DZomiuqKmi4zqsnOz5TVHFWTTDRBGSB9dLAp6bhYVS0EggcQl0KGwfqeDJenrw-tx-Ofn533f8B20cLStJgOKM7_9TS1U160kZbAwq_wKldw4jg/s1600/NASB_Caloca_0045.jpg)


I don’t have any intention of using the In Touch quite yet.  My Wide-Margin still has many virgin pages.  Once I’ve got it filled up, though, my plan is to adopt the In Touch as my primary preaching Bible.  It’s entirely possible that by that point in time, my eyesight will have worsened enough that I can’t handle the 7.9-point Lexicon in the Wide-Margin anymore.  If so, the laser-crisp 10-point Times New Roman in the In Touch will be more than useful.

Regardless, I’m entirely pleased with my rebinding project even now.  The In Touch text block is a gem.  It was too nice a Bible to leave in hardback.
