When the first reports of the San Bernardino shooters
emerged, they left me confused.  I like
my world to make sense.  Shooters are
supposed to be alienated loners.  They’re
not supposed to be married couples with small children who had to have known
that once they started shooting, they would never see their child again.  People like that are too rooted to engage in
nihilistic acts of (self-)destruction.

Over the weekend, though, reports began to emerge that one
of the victims, Nicholas Thalasinos, was a born-again Messianic Jew who was one
of the co-workers of the male shooter, Syed Rizwan Farook.  They had had heated arguments about Islam
(ironically, about whether Islam was a religion of peace or not).  Apparently, Thalasinos also said all sorts of
angry things about Islam on Facebook and Twitter.  It’s not hard to reach the conclusion that
Farook acted in part because of Thalasinos’s provocation.  I’m sure there were other reasons as well, but
his co-worker was in the mix.



Despite the conclusion that many online seem to have drawn,
this proves nothing about whether Islam is a religion of peace or not.  As with any major world religion, Islam is
not some sort of unitary system of faith. 
There are a number of ways that self-identified Muslims behave, and many
of them are vastly different from one another, just as is true of self-identified
Christians (Rather than engaging in debate about what constitutes a “true
Christian” or a “true Muslim”, for purposes of this post, I’m accepting all
religious self-identifications as valid).

Sure, you can make an argument from the Qur’an about what
Mohammed’s earliest followers were like. 
You can make inferences from the news about what al-Qaida and ISIS are
like.  However, neither one of those
things gives us any logical basis for drawing conclusions about what “Islam” is
like.  Saying that all Muslims are like
seventh-century Arab conquerors or 21st-century terrorists is akin
to saying that all Christians follow the Pope. 
In fact, the latter argument is much better.  There are many more Christians who follow the
Pope than there are Muslims who engage in sectarian violence.

Instead, we are much better served to draw conclusions about
the power of fear, suspicion, and hatred. 
If, as seems probable, the shooting was the result (at least partially)
of a holiday-party argument between Thalasinos and Farook, one of those men
became a criminal, but both were sinners. 
The Lord certainly condemns murder, but He condemns anger and hatred on
equal terms.  Consider His words in
Matthew 5:21-22:

*“You have heard that the ancients were told, 'YOU SHALL NOT
COMMIT MURDER' and 'Whoever commits murder shall be liable to the court.' But I
say to you that everyone who is angry with his brother shall be guilty before
the court; and whoever says to his brother, 'You good-for-nothing,' shall be
guilty before the supreme court; and whoever says, 'You fool,' shall be guilty
enough to go into the fiery hell.”* (NASB)

Jesus warns us against fear, anger, and hatred in part
because they are all corrosive emotions. 
They eat away at our souls, and when we express them to others, we
damage them too.  As Paul warns us in
Galatians 6:7, we reap what we sow.  If
we sow anger, fear, and hatred, we will reap a harvest of anger, fear, and
hatred directed at us.

In consequence, I believe that the most troubling effect of
the San Bernardino shooting is not the 14 people who died in its course, tragic
though their deaths are.  Instead, it is
the outpouring of anti-Muslim prejudice online, almost all of it coming from
Christians.  Let’s assume I’m wrong.  Let’s assume that 99.9 percent of Muslims,
rather than being people like us who merely want to live quiet, prosperous
lives, are out to get us.  Let’s assume
they’re our enemies.

If that’s the case, in Luke 6:27-29, Jesus tells us
explicitly how we are to view and treat Muslims.   We are
supposed to love them, do good to them, bless them, and pray for them.  And that, I’m afraid, is something I haven’t
seen on Facebook at all recently.  If, as
I’m arguing, 99.9 percent of Muslims aren’t actually our enemies, how much more
should we be doing these things?

I suspect that some people reading this are thinking that I
need to get my head out of the clouds and focus instead on the real world and
real dangers.  It is true that loving our
enemies has never been part of the wisdom of the world.  However, following the wisdom of the world
has never been part of discipleship either.

I agree that we need to pay attention to the real dangers
that are before us.  However, the real
danger here is not that of being gunned down by a black-clad Muslim with an
assault rifle.  All of us are far more
likely to die in a car accident than in a mass shooting.  Even if that were not true, though, even if
all of us were at risk daily from Islamic death squads, they still wouldn’t be
the real danger.

The real danger is, and always will be, the danger of our
fear of Muslim extremists turning us away from following Christ.  In Matthew 10:28, Jesus warns, “Do not fear
those who kill the body but are unable to kill the soul; but rather fear Him
who is able to destroy both soul and body in hell.”  When our souls fall prey to prejudice, anger,
and hatred, we die in the only sense that matters.  The true death toll of the San Bernardino
shooting may well be not in the dozens, but in the millions.

If we want to believe that Muslims are our enemies,
fine.  Let’s treat them the way that
Christ said to treat enemies.  If we do,
I think we will find that as anger, fear, and hatred bring forth fruit
according to their kind, so too do grace, kindness, and love.
