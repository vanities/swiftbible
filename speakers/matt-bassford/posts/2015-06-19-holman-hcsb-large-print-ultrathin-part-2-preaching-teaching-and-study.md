**OVERVIEW.** The Large-Print UltraThin works well as a
preaching Bible.  The only thing about it
that gave me trouble was its comparatively large size in our comparatively
small pulpit.  Also, it’s not really a
good candidate for writing, highlighting, or underlining.  As a teaching Bible and a preacher’s-study
Bible, the LPUT was nearly flawless. 
However, I did find that the HCSB translation itself was much less
precise than I was expecting.



\*             \*             \*

As always, I tested the LPUT’s usefulness as a preaching
Bible by taking it everywhere with me for two weeks, during which I used it for
three sermons, four auditorium Bible classes, and two less formal studies.  I also used it for my primary office and
study Bible while doing the bulk of the writing of next quarter’s
auditorium-class workbook.  Here’s what I
thought of it:

**PREACHING**.  At a trim size of 6 ¼“ x 9 ¼“, the LPUT is
slightly larger than I prefer a Bible to be. 
Sometimes this didn’t bother me; sometimes it did.  On the “Didn’t” side of the ledger, I would
certainly put the way the LPUT feels in the hand.  Most large-print thinlines have a good hand
feel, and this one didn’t disappoint.  I
never noticed its weight when I was holding it, and even though it isn’t
edge-lined,  the lusciously soft cowhide
cover has quite a bit of drape to it anyway. 
On several occasions when I had the Bible open in my hand, I found myself
caressing the side of the spine with my index finger.  The more I used it, the softer and limper it
got.  It was yummy.

On the “Did” side of the ledger, though, is the Bible’s
size.  The surface of the pulpit in the
Joliet church building is fairly small, and I found myself struggling to find
room for both the Bible and my sermon notebook. 
Ultimately, I ended up resting the LPUT on the computer monitor that is
set flush into the surface of the pulpit. 
Even that wasn’t perfect, though, because it obscured part of my
slides.  The moral of the story is, I
think, that if you want to use the wide-winged LPUT and a sermon notebook at
the same time, you’re probably going to need a fairly good-sized pulpit to be
comfortable.

As it happened, all three of the sermons I preached were
topical, and I didn’t put Scriptures in my PowerPoint presentation for any of
them.  In consequence, I used the LPUT up
a couple of dozen different passages on the fly.  It performed well.  Other reviews of the same Bible have noted
the reviewer’s annoyance with putting book and chapter information in a footer
(at the bottom of each page) rather than in a header (at the top).  Honestly, it wasn’t a problem.  I very quickly adapted to thumbing through at
the bottom corner instead of at the top.

Also, I didn’t have any verse-citation issues due to
formatting.  The verse numbers in the
LPUT are large and easy to read, so I never had trouble locating the passage I
wanted.

Like many large-print thinlines, the LPUT has larger margins
than normal.  The outer margin is ⅝”, and
the inner margin is ½“.  This might lead
one to believe that it would be a better-than-average note-taking Bible.  However, even though the room is there, the
paper in the LPUT doesn’t lend itself to note-taking.  I tested it by taking my red Pigma Micron 005
and making a small red mark on the opposite side of the page containing the
table of contents.  Sadly, the paper is
so translucent that the mark is clearly visible.  Bible-writers need to beware of this one.

**TEACHING.** Generally, I’d rather have a small Bible
at the lectern than in the pulpit.  It
frees me to wander about the auditorium or classroom where I’m teaching.  Though the LPUT isn’t a small Bible, it is
still a light Bible (the trade-off for that super-translucent paper).  I enjoyed wandering around the front of the
auditorium as I used it.  Once again, the
delectable cover made me want to hold it.

Like any center-column-reference Bible will, the LPUT has
the full range of HCSB cross-references. 
I’ve heard complaints about this cross-referencing system before, but I
wasn’t really called upon to use it, so I can’t speak to its comparative
utility.  For those who want a
cross-referenced Bible, though, this one has ‘em in spades.

My last comment about the LPUT concerns the
translation.  This is the first time I’ve
ever used an HCSB, so I was interested to see how I would like it.  The introduction to the HCSB claims that in
comparison to the generally formal-equivalent translations (NASB, ESV, NKJV)
and the generally dynamic-equivalent translations (NIV, NLT, REB), its
translators employed a philosophy called optimal equivalence, which supposedly
adopts the virtues of both and the vices of neither.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEiIl8y4PiiUhKvgfzm6L-0PjY59GeA27YmSsFNVEYGTR8nJQRCxkQGHEpEidakdnWhcp52Taie0hVV3o0rRRL6zrraXsmBJkic0qRoF3IaCOC6epTbcoCSJdY5eW4sbiBfvATLPddFM2Q/s640/PTS+optimal.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEiIl8y4PiiUhKvgfzm6L-0PjY59GeA27YmSsFNVEYGTR8nJQRCxkQGHEpEidakdnWhcp52Taie0hVV3o0rRRL6zrraXsmBJkic0qRoF3IaCOC6epTbcoCSJdY5eW4sbiBfvATLPddFM2Q/s1600/PTS+optimal.jpg)



In practice, though, I found that the HCSB read and felt
much more like a dynamic-equivalent translation.  I was particularly struck by this when our
Bible class turned its attention to Acts 22. 
Acts 22:16 is a Scripture of particular importance to my religious
tradition, so I was alarmed to find that Acts 22:16 in the HCSB reads, “And
now, why delay?  Get up and be baptized,
and wash away your sins **by** calling
on His name.” [emphasis added]

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgEXxz9Q223o16ONIrWoTZODL_dzztiPyc7DG67qKm64Fs4zh1zCvCPZbaRaOYNtCfMyJxGB6oj2TKm0EMsBzTDT_rPjqGG3BbAjfJI3b_mgFpKjIwSShuRvT_GY2hkaeCW2-GKj_V1Iw/s640/PTS+Acts+22.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgEXxz9Q223o16ONIrWoTZODL_dzztiPyc7DG67qKm64Fs4zh1zCvCPZbaRaOYNtCfMyJxGB6oj2TKm0EMsBzTDT_rPjqGG3BbAjfJI3b_mgFpKjIwSShuRvT_GY2hkaeCW2-GKj_V1Iw/s1600/PTS+Acts+22.jpg)



I’m not aware of any Greek text that contains “by” in Acts
22:16.  It is a non-notated interpretive
addition by the translators that completely changes the meaning of the verse.  Of all the major modern translations, only
the unabashedly dynamic-equivalent NLT has adopted a similar interpretive
addition.  Even the NIV11 and REB omit
it.  Though I’m sure I will continue to
use the HCSB from time to time, just as I use the NLT, it’s not a version I would
choose for close study.

**STUDY.** In terms of layout and design, I was
perfectly happy with the LPUT at my desk. 
Again, the squeezably soft cover invited squeezing, the print was quite readable,
and I didn’t make any citation mistakes in my work—at least, not mistakes that
I’ve found yet.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEjC5CqO_75PUFoNIe0PkjM7mGlBh8Fouq-rlVqPoovohAwv9gLP0lNhlW6QrxZDAy9VDnIAKFxOWzcUHY10KXQ6clKKFH1kgWcaG2bQk2EizVVBfYIUccPP-SZSXyDDKbTTkl2HQLSfGA/s640/PTS+maps.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEjC5CqO_75PUFoNIe0PkjM7mGlBh8Fouq-rlVqPoovohAwv9gLP0lNhlW6QrxZDAy9VDnIAKFxOWzcUHY10KXQ6clKKFH1kgWcaG2bQk2EizVVBfYIUccPP-SZSXyDDKbTTkl2HQLSfGA/s1600/PTS+maps.jpg)



However, I once again found myself noticing that the HCSB
felt like a dynamic-equivalent translation. 
I make allowances for that when I’m reviewing an NIV or an NLT, but I
wasn’t expecting to have to make similar allowances for the HCSB.  Much of my work during this review period was
on a workbook covering, among other things, the epistle of 1 Peter in a fairly
careful, verse-by-verse fashion.  I’m
used to using formal-equivalence translations, and the HCSB never read enough
like those for me to feel comfortable reading it closely.  Eventually, I hunted down an NASB pew Bible
and read it and the LPUT side by side to get a feel for the nuances of the
text.

**CONCLUSION.** I’m a Bible reviewer, not a translation
reviewer.  I know a whole lot more about
layout and design than I do about the original languages of Scripture.  Even though I had a great deal to say about
my impressions of the HCSB as a translation, I don’t think it’s fair to include
those things in my evaluation of the LPUT as a physical book.  As a physical book, the LPUT is pretty
useful.  I loved holding it, it did
everything that I needed a Bible to do, and it didn’t lead me into any citation
errors.  Only its length and width in the
pulpit annoyed me in the least.  Also,
its less-than-opaque paper makes it a less-than-ideal choice for
note-taking.  As such, I think it
deserves an 8/10.
