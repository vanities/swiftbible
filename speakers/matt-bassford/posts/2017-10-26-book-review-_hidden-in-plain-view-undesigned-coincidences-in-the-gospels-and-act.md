My wife and I aren’t much for watching TV.  Instead, after we’ve put the kids to bed, we
generally spend our evenings sitting around in our living room, reading and
chit-chatting.  I’m what you might call
an interactive reader.  When a passage in
a book catches my attention, I’ll say, “Hey, Wifelet, listen to this!” and read
her the passage.  Generally, the more
passages I quote, the better I like the book.

When I was reading Lydia McGrew’s *Hidden in Plain View*, I stopped to read so many passages that eventually
my wife gave me a look and said, “Should I just read the book myself?”

I told her she should. 
I think you should too.



*Hidden in Plain View*is
an apologetic work, and it resides in my favorite category of
apologetics—internal evidences for the authenticity of Scripture.  I appreciate the work of all those who argue
for creationism, intelligent design, and so forth, but that’s not really my
thing.  I’m not a scientist, so I’m not
necessarily going to be able to identify the weak spots in the arguments of
either evolutionists or creationists.  I
have to take the arguments of the latter on faith, which I never like doing.

However, when it comes to the Bible, I do know a fair bit
about it, and I can tell when an author is twisting or misrepresenting the
textual evidence to make a point.  What’s
more, arguments from the Bible are also much easier for seekers for the truth
to understand.  For example, I’ve used
Gary Habermas and Michael Licona’s *The
Case for the Resurrection* in kitchen-table studies with great effect.  As a result, I find books about internal
evidences to be particularly valuable.

*The Case for the
Resurrection* is my favorite apologetic book, but I think *Hidden in Plain View* is nearly as strong.  Its focus is on what McGrew calls “undesigned
coincidences”.  An undesigned coincidence
occurs when a Biblical author, almost in passing, mentions a detail that
corroborates the account of another Biblical author.

This approach (not original to McGrew, though she’s the
first to adopt it in more than 150 years) is particularly interesting to me
because it co-opts one of the favorite arguments of antagonists of the
Scripture:  these authors can’t be
telling the truth because their stories are different.

Actually, McGrew replies, the differences in accounts are a
sign of authenticity, not its opposite. 
No two people are the same, so they won’t tell the same story in the
same way either.  By contrast, if
multiple witnesses tell the same story exactly the same way with no variations,
either they are colluding or getting their information from a common
source.  Varied accounts, not identical
ones, indicate the truth.

Independent witnesses tell independent stories, but their
stories, if truthful, will hang together. 
For instance, let’s say John is on trial for running a red light.  Witness A says that John told him earlier in
the day that John was supposed to meet his wife for dinner at 6.  Witness B reports that he saw John run the
red light at 6:04.  Witness A’s
testimony, though not about the event in question at all, makes it more likely
that Witness B is telling the truth.

McGrew spends *Hidden
in Plain View*pointing out that the accounts of the gospels frequently
coincide with each other in this way, as does the book of Acts with various
Pauline epistles.  These aren’t proofs
aimed at the core fact of Christianity in the way that *The Case for the Resurrection* is, but together, they make a case
that is nearly as important, that the history of the New Testament is based on
the testimony of truthful eyewitnesses. 
In fact, these coincidental hallmarks of authenticity appear as
frequently in the stories of the miracles of Jesus as they do in the accounts
of the teaching of Jesus.

Let me cite a couple of different examples in connection
with the feeding of the 5000.  McGrew
points out that in John 6:5-7, Jesus asks Philip where they can buy bread to
feed the crowd.  This is a detail that
connects with nothing else in John’s version of the miracle.  However, Luke 9:10 reveals that the feeding
of the 5000 took place near Bethsaida. 
From there, McGrew goes to John 1:44, an unrelated story that mentions
in passing that Philip was from Bethsaida.

Why does Jesus ask Philip where He can buy bread?  Because Philip is the local boy, and if
anybody knows where to get that much food around Bethsaida, Philip would.  Neither Luke nor John intends to make this
point, but when their two accounts are read together, the point emerges because
both accounts are truthful.

Similarly, one of the curiosities concerning the feeding of
the 5000 is that, as Matthew 14:21 reports, there were 5000 men plus an unknown
number of women and children.  Why would
that be?  Again, a comparison of
different accounts provides an answer. 
Matthew 14:19 records that Jesus asks the crowds to sit down on the
grass.  John 6:10 notes, though, that it
was actually only the men who sat.  Mark
6:40 offers an additional detail—those who sat were organized by hundreds and
fifties.

From this combination of gospels, a coherent explanation
emerges.  Only the men were counted at
the miracle because only the men were sitting down and organized into groups
that made counting feasible.  There’s no
way that the gospel writers would have colluded to produce these
coincidences—they’re much too subtle to be the result of fraud.  Instead, they offer circumstantial detail
that confirms the authenticity of one of the most spectacular miracles of
Jesus’ career.

McGrew details dozens of coincidences like these.  She has a lot to say, and, equally
importantly, she says it well.  Many
academic works on the Bible contain worthy thoughts, but the worthiness is
concealed beneath mounds of jargon and leaden prose.  McGrew, by contrast, writes clearly and
deftly.  Anyone with a passing
familiarity with Scripture will be able to follow her arguments, and a more
accessible book is a more useful one.

I’ve read plenty of books about the Bible that left me
shrugging.  Either they didn’t have
anything to say, or they said it badly (and bad writing gives me hives).  McGrew’s workfalls into neither trap.  People
who care about the Bible will benefit from this book, and they will be able to
understand it.  It’s good to believe.  It’s even better to know why we believe, and *Hidden in Plain View* does a fine job of
handing us reasons to go with our convictions.
