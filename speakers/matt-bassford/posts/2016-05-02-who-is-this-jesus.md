One of the weird things about Bible study is that when
you’re listening to someone else, they can often take you to places in
Scripture you would never have gone on your own.  For instance, the Sunday I was on vacation
recently, I heard a sermon that started off with the story of Jesus stilling
the storm.  The preacher pointed out that
when the sea calmed, the disciples became afraid and asked, “Who is this?”  The event raised questions about the identity
of Jesus.

That got me to thinking. 
The preacher had been reading from Luke’s account, and sure enough, in
just about every chapter through the first part of Luke, there is a similar
question.  Luke’s whole narrative is
structured around people who want to know who Jesus is.  We may think we know the answer today, but
the question is still extremely important. 
This morning, then, let’s flip through the first half of Luke and ask
along with His contemporaries, “Who is this Jesus?”



**Early Definitions**

We’re going to begin this morning by looking at some of the
earliest definitions of Jesus in Luke. 
The first of these revelations is that Jesus is **NOT JOSEPH’S SON.** This story
unfolds in Luke 4:14-30.  Rather than a
case of “local boy makes good”, this is a case of “local boy gets chased out of
town”.  Jesus shows up in the Nazareth
synagogue on the Sabbath, reads a passage about the Messiah from the book of
Isaiah, and announces that He is its fulfillment.

However, the Nazarenes don’t even attempt to test Jesus’ claim.  They dismiss it.  They rhetorically ask, “Isn’t this Joseph’s son?”  If He really is the Messiah, how come He
isn’t healing their sick like He healed the sick of Capernaum?  Jesus then implies that God hasn’t sent Him
to the home folks at all, but rather to those who will listen, so the home
folks get mad and try to kill Him.

Even today, we can get spiritually sidetracked because we
think we know who Jesus is, but are as badly wrong as the Nazarenes were.  We think we have a handle on Jesus because we
were raised in the church and sat through all kinds of kids’ Bible classes and
sermons about Jesus.

Brethren, that is not the way to come to know the Lord!  I do the best I can up here, and the teachers
downstairs do the best they can, but the best preachers and teachers in the
world are no substitute for seeking Him ourselves.  Unless we are in His word ourselves, unless
we are studying, meditating, and praying ourselves, our understanding of Jesus
will be every bit as limited as the Nazarenes’ understanding.  Our very familiarity will blind us to our
ignorance.

On the positive side of the ledger, the next question
defines Jesus as **A HEALER.** Consider here the narrative of Luke
5:17-26.  I think this is one of the
places where surface familiarity with Jesus can blind us to the truth.  The story of Jesus healing the paralytic who
is lowered through the roof is a Bible-class staple.  When we did the miracles of Jesus in VBS a few
years back, one of the teaching groups rigged up a pulley from the ceiling so
that they could literally lower a little stretcher in the middle of the
story.

However, as wonderful as it was that Jesus was able to heal
the man’s physical problems, that is not at all the point.  Notice that the first thing that Jesus says
to him is, “Your sins are forgiven.”  We
see his inability to walk as a serious problem indeed, but Jesus knew what his
worst problem was and solved that one first.

That’s what gives rise to the “Who does He think He is?”
questions.  The Pharisees can’t accept His
claim to forgive sins, any more than we would accept some other man’s claim to
do the same.  Jesus then heals the
paralytic, not because healing paralytics is a nice thing to do, but because
His power to heal in the physical realm shows His power to heal in the
spiritual realm.  When the paralytic gets
up and walks, that’s primarily important because it shows that his sins are
forgiven.

Jesus’ power to heal is still every bit as great, but we
need to make sure that we keep our eyes on the healing that is most
important.  Through Him, we often pray
for physical health for ourselves and others, but I think the real danger for
us lies in over-emphasizing what He does for us mentally.  It is true that Jesus will help us through
the problems of life.  It is true that
Jesus comforts us when we are alone or sad. 
However, those things are not the point of our relationship with Jesus,
any more than the physical healing of the paralytic is the point of that story.

Instead, most of all, we need Jesus to heal our spiritual
illnesses.  There are other comforters
out there, but He is the only answer to the problem of our sin.  When we are alone with our guilt, when we are
staring eternal death in the face because we have done evil in the sight of
God, that is when our need for the healing of Jesus is simple and
absolute.  Only He can help us.

**Shedding More Light**

Further questions in Luke shed more light on both the work
and the character of Jesus.  The next question
comes from one of His friends, John the Baptist, who asks whether He is **THE EXPECTED ONE.** Look at Luke 7:18-35.  This is a fascinating story for two
reasons.  The first is that the question
is a setup.  John the Baptist knows who
Jesus is.  He has already declared Him
the Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world.  John is not asking for his own benefit.  He’s asking for the benefit of the disciples
who are bringing the question to Jesus.

Second, even though the question is a setup, Jesus doesn’t
answer it simply, at least in part because the question doesn’t have a simple
answer.  Jesus knows that John is
referring to the Messiah, so He heals a bunch of people and cites prophecies
that predict that the Messiah will do exactly that.  According to the terms of the Scriptures,
Jesus is the Expected One.

However, that’s not where the inquiry ends.  Jesus might be the Messiah the Jews should
have been expecting, but He isn’t the Messiah they actually were
expecting.  The Jewish religious elite
didn’t accept John because he didn’t live up to their expectations, but the
despised, sinful tax collectors did because they didn’t have those expectations
to blind them to the truth.  Similarly,
Jesus was going to be rejected by those same religious leaders, even though He
was very different from John, because of the ways that He managed to violate
their expectations too.

Today, friends, we must be careful that we aren’t blinded to
the truth by our expectations for Jesus. 
So many people in the world think that Jesus stands for unconditional
acceptance, yet in reality, Jesus has more to say about hell than any other
figure in the Bible.  When it comes to
fire-and-brimstone preachers, He’s at the top of the list.  We ourselves might look on Jesus as the
foundation for a comfortable life, yet His command for us to take up our
crosses and follow Him is a command to be uncomfortable for His sake.  We need to let our expectations for Jesus be
shaped by what the Bible says, rather than by our own preconceptions.

Next, Jesus departs from expectations in showing Himself to
be **A FRIEND OF SINNERS.** Consider Luke 7:36-50.  It’s funny that among Christians, “friend of
sinners” is one of the most beloved titles of the Lord, but when it shows up in
7:34, it’s not meant as a compliment.  In
this story, Jesus shows what it means. 
He’s eating in the house of Simon the Pharisee, and a sinful woman shows
up and starts weeping at His feet, anointing them with perfume, and wiping them
with her hair.  Simon thinks this is
contemptible.  Jesus ought to realize
that this wretched woman is defiling Him simply by touching Him!

In reply, Jesus turns the tables on his arrogant host by
pointing out that she is showing greater love for Him than Simon did.  The sinner is giving the prophet first-class
treatment, not the Pharisee.  On the
basis of her obvious repentance, He tells her that her sins are forgiven, which
gives rise to the same incredulous questions that the story of the paralytic
did.

This time around, though, I think the answer to the question
is different.  The story of the paralytic
shows Jesus’ power.  That’s not true
here.  He doesn’t prove His power to
forgive sins by working a miracle. 
Instead, this story illustrates His compassion.  Jesus is one who forgives sins because of His
love for the sinner.  Simon the Pharisee
probably wouldn’t have considered the sinful woman worthy of forgiveness, but
Jesus helped her because He wanted to help her.

There are two lessons here. 
The first is that even when we are in our sins, Jesus is still our
friend.  Even if we don’t consider
ourselves worthy of love, Jesus does. 
Second, it should remind us that as His disciples, we should be friends
to sinners too.  This doesn’t mean that
we run with them in their sin, but it does mean that we love them enough to
help them find their way to Him.

**Finishing the Picture**

The final three questions in the first half of Luke finish
our picture of Jesus.  The first of these
reveals Him as **THE LORD OF WIND AND
WATER.** This is the story that
launched the whole sermon, and it appears in Luke 8:22-25.  Back in the days before taxis, the easiest
way for Jesus and the disciples to get across the Sea of Galilee was by
boat.  The Sea, really a freshwater lake,
is about seven miles across the way they were going, so sailing saved quite a
hike.  However, in mid-sail, a terrible
storm kicks up, so much so that the disciples are convinced that their two
choices are to awaken Jesus or drown. 
They pick Door Number One, Jesus barks at the storm, and it obediently
slinks off, leaving the lake calm.  This
terrifies the disciples even more than the tempest had, and they start
speculating about who Jesus must be, to still a storm with a word.

Geek that I am, I can’t help but speculate about how much
power this took.  We think of air as
being, well, light as air, but the reality is that there is a tremendous amount
of mass and energy in a thunderstorm.  When
we rub our feet across a carpet, we might generate a little spark of static
electricity.  The motion of a
thunderstorm generates lightning bolts that stretch for miles.  Storms are spectacularly powerful, but Jesus
dispelled that huge force as easily as we might turn off a TV.

We need to remember this side of Jesus when we’re going
through trial in our lives.  Not all
storms are literal, and sometimes, it’s the metaphorical ones that are most
terrifying.  Here we are, nearly drowning
in our troubles, and for all we can tell, Jesus isn’t doing anything.  He might as well be asleep in the bottom of
the boat!  Friends, regardless of how
things looked, Jesus wasn’t about to let that boat go under, and He won’t let
us go under either.  We can always rely
on Him.

The next question highlights an emerging awareness that
Jesus is **A THREAT TO RULERS.** Let’s see how this goes in Luke
9:1-9.  At first glance, this context
appears to be made up of two parts that don’t go together.  In Part 1, Jesus sends out the twelve to
proclaim the gospel.  In Part 2, Herod
the tetrarch, whom history knows as Herod Antipas, suddenly gets curious about
Jesus.  Why this now?  Why don’t we see Herod interested in Jesus
when He raised the dead or healed the blind? 
In this part of the story, Jesus isn’t even doing anything!

The key, I think, is that the twelve are going around
proclaiming the kingdom of God, and in that phrase, the key word is
“kingdom”.  Herod Antipas isn’t a king,
even though he would very much like to be one. 
Instead, he’s about a quarter-king. 
Anybody who’s preaching the kingdom of God probably has somebody in mind
to be king, and it’s probably not going to be Herod, who is famous for killing
one of God’s prophets.  That’s enough to
get a guy to wondering, especially if you’re a member of the house of Herod, in
which extreme paranoia was a survival trait!

The reaction of despots to Christianity today is no different.  I used to wonder why totalitarian dictators
are always so hard on those who claim to be Christians.  After all, Christians are supposed to be
hard-working, peaceable, taxpaying citizens, right?  Sounds like the kind of citizen any nation
would want!  The problem is, though, that
Christians have pledged their allegiance to someone higher than any state, and
no totalitarian ruler can tolerate an authority greater than he is.  In consequence, the Christians have to
go.  Even here, brethren, we must remember
that no matter what our birth certificate says, we are first and foremost
citizens of heaven.  We need to live like
it.

The last question in our study this morning, and indeed the
last question about Jesus in Luke until He is put on trial, shows Him to be **THE CHRIST.**Let’s read Luke 9:18-21 here. 
First, notice the significant change here.  All of the other questions we’ve studied have
been asked about Jesus by somebody else. 
This is a question Jesus Himself asks His disciples.  The implication is plain:  by now, they’ve seen enough that they should
be able to put the pieces together. 
Peter does, and he proclaims Jesus as the Christ, which is the ultimate
answer to every question that we’ve seen.

However, even here, Jesus’ response is strange.  He tells them not to tell anybody.  In our publicity-saturated day, this doesn’t
make any sense, but it makes a whole lot of sense if you’ve got Herod Antipas
watching you like a hawk.  Part of being
the Christ was being a king, and if Jesus had publicly proclaimed Himself as
the Christ, Herod would have done everything he could to kill Him.

Jesus, though, knows that His safety is only temporary.  In Jerusalem, He’s going to be killed by His
enemies for saying exactly the same thing about Himself that Peter said about
Him.  His humiliation, suffering, and
death were all part of God’s plan. 
However, that wouldn’t be the end. 
In the most fearful miracle of all, after three days, Jesus would rise
from the dead.

Before the Lord’s Supper, let’s reflect on these things.  Let’s consider this extraordinary portrait of
a man who was rejected by those who knew Him best, but could heal all illness.  He both met and defied expectations,
rejecting the so-called righteous and befriending sinners.  He wielded great power and threatened every
earthly power.  He is the Christ, the
Holy One of God, who lived and died and rose and reigns for us.  Let’s remember Him as we partake.
