If the Legacy is designed to serve any purpose at all, it is
designed to serve as a reading Bible. 
Its microscopic verse numbers offer little help during preaching and
studying, but they also offer little hindrance to reading.  The font is a readable 9/10-point Lexicon No.
1.  The paper is nearly the equal of the
paper in the renowned 2002 Foundation Side-Column Reference NASB.  Each line of text is matched with the line on
the opposite side.  At 6” x 9”, the
Legacy is on the large side, but certainly not large enough to pose problems as
a reader.

Even the layout of the Legacy is meant to be
reader-friendly.  In an obvious homage to
Robert Bringhurst’s The Elements of Typographic Style, section headings are
removed from the body of the text and set instead in the spacious outer
margins.  The Legacy is the loveliest
text block I’ve ever owned, hands down.

And yet it doesn’t work for me as a reader.

I feel almost guilty acknowledging this.  The Legacy should work; indeed, I bought it
(or, rather, had my wife buy it for me) because I expected it to work.  I spent hours drooling over the layout in the
“Look Inside!” pop-up window in Amazon’s Books section.  I was sure I was going to love reading out of
the Bible.  Instead, I found reading it
difficult, effortful, a weary slog instead of an exhilarating ride.  It had me wondering what was wrong with me
spiritually that I was having such trouble with reading the Bible.

The clincher came one morning when, for whatever reason, I
essayed my Bible reading in a Pitt Minion instead of the Legacy.  Pitts are wonderful, but they are compact
Bibles, not reading Bibles.  However, my
Pitt, double-column, 6.75-point type, center-column references and all, proved
to be an easier read for me than the Legacy. 
I was astonished, but I didn’t argue with experience.  To the shelf went the Legacy; out came the
Pitt.

More recently, when I field-tested the Legacy as a reader
for two weeks just to make sure that I was right about not being comfortable
with it (I really want to like it as a reader!), I had the same experience.  There was only one exception.  It was on a snowy day when I was stuck in the
house, so I sat down with my back to the enormous bow window in our living room
and read about ten chapters out of. . . Romans, I think.

In the amazing, perfectly diffuse light coming through the
window, I had no trouble reading the Legacy. 
I slipped effortlessly into the text in the way that I had longed to for
months.  However, when I trotted it out
the next morning for my daily reading at my desk in our downstairs, which has
good, but not great lighting, it went back to being a trial.

This is hard to explain, but I believe that the explanation
for the Legacy’s underperformance may lie in the same quarter as its
inspiration—with Bringhurst.  Bringhurst
notes that the ideal length for a line is between 40 and 66 characters.  He further prescribes that a book with fewer
than 500 words per page should be laid out in single-column form, but a book
with between 500 and 1000 words should have two columns.

The Legacy, by contrast, has about 73 characters per
line.  Page 1278, which looks like a
typical page of prose, has 609 words of text. 
Both of those figures lie outside the Bringhurstian optimum.  If the same section of text were laid out at
60 characters per line, the resulting page would contain almost exactly 500
words.  We cannot know unless we were to
ask him ourselves, but I suspect that Bringhurst would say of the Legacy that
it attempts to carry too much meaning in each line.

That matches my own subjective experience of reading the
Legacy.  The lines are too long for me to
get through easily.  I take in the first
55 characters of a line with a single glance, but then I must spend a second
glance on the remaining 18.  Taking in so
little information throws me off my reading rhythm and takes me out of the
text.

In practice, then, the Legacy’s “line length” is the same as
a line in a double-column reference Bible, except that line length in the
reference Bible is at least consistent. 
Only if the light is truly excellent, as it was on that snowy day, is my
peripheral vision able to pick up the words at the end of a Legacy line so that
I can comprehend an entire line with a single glance.

However, I still believe that everyone who is interested in
reading out of the ESV should buy and own a Legacy, even if they have the same
problem with its prose sections that I do. 
As difficult as the prose layout may be, the layout of the Legacy’s
poetry is magnificent.  All of that
glorious white space in the outer and bottom margins makes the poetic portions
of the Bible (perhaps 25 percent of the whole) come to life.  It looks like a book of poetry.  I don’t think I understood the book of
Lamentations until I read it in the Legacy. 
The Legacy isn’t a terribly expensive Bible, at least not in a TruTone
binding ($30 at wtsbooks.com), and having a poetic setting of the Bible that is
that good is well worth the price.

All in all, then, my reaction to the Legacy as a reader is a
strange mixture of opposites.  I love the
layout, I love the print size, I love the paper, I love the poetry presentation, but
I struggle to use it as a prose reader. 
This is by no means a universal experience.  I ended up giving my Legacy to my
photographer, and he thinks it’s the most wonderful reading Bible in the Whole
Wide World.  No problems with the line
length for him!  However, here as always,
my evaluations are based solely on my own experience.  As a reader for me, I give the Legacy a
3.5/5.
