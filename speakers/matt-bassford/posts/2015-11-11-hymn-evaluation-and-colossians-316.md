Last week, I advanced the notion that what is most important
about a hymn is not whether we like it, but whether it helps us to achieve
God’s goals for our worship.  I asserted
that there are several passages in Scripture that define those goals, but I did
not explain.

Today, then, I want to look at perhaps the most important of
those texts, Colossians 3:16.  In my
religious tradition, this verse is commonly used to establish that song worship
should consist only of singing without musical accompaniment.  Certainly, many people disagree with that argument.

Whether we agree with it or not, though, we must acknowledge that it is not the original point of the
verse.  Paul did not write Colossians
3:16 to settle a controversy between a-cappella and instrument-using
Christians.  In the first century, no
such controversy existed.

Instead, just as the first part of Colossians 3 describes the standard for Christian conduct (godly, righteous, merciful, etc.), Colossians 3:16
describes the standard for Christian singing. 
According to Paul, it should have the following characteristics:

 **Singing should
express a rich indwelling of the word.** The
verse begins with the instruction to, “Let the word of Christ richly dwell
within you.”  In my life, I’ve been
blessed to know a number of Christians who have had this rich indwelling of the
word.  They’ve spent so much time with
the Bible that it practically oozes out of their pores.  You can hear Scriptural overtones in nearly
every sentence they utter.

Paul wants us to understand that our song worship ought to
be an expression of that kind of distilled Biblical wisdom.  This is only possible if the hymns we sing
contain the same Scriptural depth themselves. 
Otherwise, we’re trying to force Niagara Falls through a garden
hose.  The channel is inadequate to
convey the power behind it.

Our repertoire contains many Scripture-rich hymns, from
“When I Survey the Wondrous Cross” to “The Battle Belongs to the Lord”.  In all of these cases, the words of the text
make it clear that the author has devoted him- or herself to study of the word
and has used his writing to express that study. 
For instance, in “The Battle Belongs to the Lord”, I recognize
references to Ephesians 6, Joshua 1, 1 Samuel 17, Isaiah 54, Luke 22, Isaiah
59, 1 Peter 3, and Luke 21 (I admit; I had to look up Isaiah 54:17 and 59:19,
but I knew they were in there somewhere!). 
I can’t sing the hymn without singing to others from the word of
God.  Every hymn we sing should have the
same spiritual depth.

By contrast, many Christians argue that a hymn is suitable
for worship so long as it “doesn’t teach false doctrine”.  What a lackluster endorsement that is!  Consider Joel Osteen.  The big criticism of Joel Osteen isn’t that
he says things that are clearly wrong. 
It is that he says nothing of substance at all.  He dishes out platitudes when he should be
preaching the word of God.

Likewise, there are all too many hymns that dish out
platitudes when they should be breaking the bread of life.  Perhaps the most egregious offender here is “Precious
Memories”.  Search though I may, I can
find no evidence in it (in the version we sing, at least) that the author ever
even opened a Bible.  Far from revealing
a rich indwelling of the word, it reveals no indwelling of the word.  What place does a song like that have in the
worship of God?

**Singing should be the
congregation instructing the congregation**. 
Colossians 3:16 continues on to say, “. . . teaching and admonishing one
another with psalms and hymns and spiritual songs.”  In other words, in God’s ideal worship
service, everybody is teaching everybody else.

This implies two things about the hymns we should use.  First, they should be within the abilities of
the congregation to sing.  Second, they
should be within the abilities of the congregation to understand.

Much could be said about what makes a hymn singable, even by
a group of untrained singers.  However,
all the technicalities boil down to simplicity and intuitiveness.  A hymn should be transparent so that the
singers can focus on content.  Difficult
(to an untrained singer) or poorly arranged music, awkwardly written lyrics,
and broken musical and lyrical patterns all create distractions.

For instance, just about any song leader who reads music
will tell you that the surest way to wreck “Follow Me” is to sing it the way
that it’s written.  The hymn as written
sets up a pattern and then breaks it, twice (“Jesus” in v. 2 and “mortal man”
in v. 3).  If the song leader follows the
printed music into the broken pattern, the singing will collapse in confusion,
and it will take everybody a measure or two to sort themselves back out.  “Follow Me” has strong content, so it’s worth
singing anyway (content always trumps technique), but better technique would
have made it a better hymn.

A good hymn is easy to sing, but it is also easy to
understand.  Here is an area where many
older hymns fall short.  Because they
were written in the stilted style of bygone centuries, they contain many
difficult, unfamiliar words and use an unnatural, convoluted sentence
structure.

The poster child here is “The Spacious Firmament”.  Personally, I love the hymn and think it’s
beautiful.  I have no trouble
understanding Elizabethan English, so the words make perfect sense to me.  However, I think it’s a bit much to ask the
brethren to wait 31 syllables before they get to the first verb.  All the great content doesn’t matter when
it’s obscured by archaic style.

The meaning of a hymn can also be obscured by its musical
arrangement.  Consider, for instance,
“The Greatest Commands”.  On the printed
page, I love the hymn.  Four verses; four
Scriptures.  What could be better for
expressing a rich indwelling of the word than the word itself?

When I’m listening to v. 1, I still love it.  When I start singing in v. 2, yup, still
loving it. In v. 3, however, a problem develops.  It’s a problem with my brain.  Like many men, I have a one-track mind.  I do one thing, at a time, ever.  If Lauren asks me to stop by the store on the
way home from work, the only way I can do that is by thinking about stopping at
the store the entire time I’m driving.  If
I don’t, reflexes will take over, and I will arrive in my garage empty-handed.  As a multitasker, I am a disaster.

When I sing “The Greatest Commands”, I can process one
stream of input, no problem.  Two, I can
handle if I concentrate.  At three, tiny
wisps of steam start leaking out of my ears. 
At four, I give up.  For all the
teaching and admonishing everybody else is doing of me, they might as well be
singing in Sanskrit.

I recognize that many people have a different experience
with the hymn.  Most of them are
women.  Sure; if you’re able to
simultaneously cook dinner, talk to your mom on the phone, tidy up the living
room, and keep the baby from falling down the stairs, you can probably grasp
the meaning of all four parts of “The Greatest Commands” at the same time.  You think in parallel.

I think in series, and probably most of the folks on the
lower two parts are thinking in series too. 
I would never introduce a hymn that only half the congregation could
sing.  Why sing a hymn that only half the
congregation can understand?

**Conclusion.** It is true that Colossians 3:16 also
includes “singing with thankfulness in your hearts to God,” but I’m going to
postpone my discussion of vertical singing until Hebrews 13:15.  This post is long enough already.  Hopefully, its point is clear.  Some hymns are deficient in content; others
are deficient in technique.  It’s
possible to work through some technical problems, but there’s no rescuing a
hymn with content problems.  “I like it”
is no reason to sing a song that keeps us from achieving God’s goals for our
worship.

14:15 and singing with understanding!
