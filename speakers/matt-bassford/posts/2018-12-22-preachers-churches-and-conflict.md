Like many other preachers, I read Terry Francis’ series
about church-preacher relationships with great interest.  Though Terry is, of course, writing from his
own perspective and his own experiences, he said a number of things that
resonated with me.  After reading them
all, though, I was struck by an additional thought.

Fundamentally, I think this is about preachers and churches
struggling to resolve conflict with one another.  There are exceptions to the rule, but
generally it seems to be true.  You work
with a church until a serious conflict arises, then the serious conflict blows
you up, and you move on.  Nobody seems to
keep working with the church through the serious conflict.



I think preachers and churches (particularly church leaders)
are both to blame.  The trouble begins with fear and lack of trust.  People
aren’t willing to have important conversations because they’re afraid.

Let’s consider the church side first.  Generically, it’s hard to come to a preacher (or
any man) and tell him, “I’ve got a problem with what you’re doing.”    Most Christians aren’t comfortable doing
that.

At times, I think it’s
particularly hard because of the personality of the preacher.  Some preachers are cuddle bears; others
(myself included) are not cuddle bears. 
The prospect of trying to correct men like us can be intimidating!  Like Terry, I believe Matthew 18 is for
everybody, but I can see why brethren, especially conflict-averse brethren,
have trouble putting it into practice.

As a result, preachers are often talked about rather than
talked to.  The problems continue to
fester, the poison continues to spread, until finally the eldership or the
men’s meeting gathers its courage and gang-tackles the preacher.  At that point, there is no possible way to
reach a peaceful resolution.  The
preacher is on his way out; the only question is whether he will leave a
smoking crater behind him as he goes.

Many of the same things apply to preachers, with the added
pressure of financial security.  Every
preacher who considers bringing a criticism to the eldership or the men’s
meeting also considers whether speaking up will have him updating his
resume.  It feels much safer to choke
down complaints, even at the cost of years or decades of bitterness.  In consequence, preachers do a lot of talking
about instead of talking to as well, generally to other preachers.  Matthew 18 is a knife that cuts both ways
here.

Frankly, I’m as guilty of this as anybody else.  At my performance evaluation a month or two
ago, the elders asked me if I had any concerns about their leadership.  I had come prepared to talk about my own
failings at great length, but their question caught me flat-footed.  To use Terry’s language, I had bought into
the employer-employee paradigm much more than the elders had.  I wasn’t able to respond usefully because it
had not occurred to me that I could trust my “employers” with criticism.

My solutions to
spiritual problems are boring.  They are
either “more Bible” or “stronger relationships”.  I think both apply here, especially the
second.  We’re afraid of one another
because we don’t trust one another, and we don’t trust one another because we
don’t have deep relationships with one another. 
I’ve always been able to resolve conflicts with brethren I knew well and
talked to all the time.  Problems arise
when that other interaction isn’t happening.

Here, then, is my solution to preacher-church conflict.  Whoever you are, if you’ve got a problem with
another Christian, talk to them.  It
doesn’t even have to be about the problem at first.  Have them over to your house for dinner.  Invite them to watch a ball game or go
fishing with you.  Get to know them.  Reveal yourself to them.  Earn the right to be open.  After all that, if you’ve still got your
problem, you will find it much easier to speak truth in love.
