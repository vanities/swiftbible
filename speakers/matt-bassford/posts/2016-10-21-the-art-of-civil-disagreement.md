Elections often generate a lot of emotional discussion, but
this most recent cycle seems to be particularly rancorous.  I attribute this to two factors:  an increased polarization of American
society, and a decreased understanding of how to handle disagreement.  Our society’s approach to differing opinions
is weirdly bifurcated these days.  In
person, it’s considered offensive to bring up a difficult subject, to the point
where many colleges seek to protect their students by limiting expression.  Online, however, anything goes.  People whom I know to be decent, respectable
folk in real life become vindictive and spiteful once they get in front of a
keyboard.  It reminds me of one of those
families that bottles up disagreement until it explodes in a nasty, apocalyptic
argument.

This bifurcated existence is no way for Christians to
live.  In fact, both halves of it are
wrong.  If we don’t learn how to handle
sensitive issues in person, we will never succeed in teaching anyone else the
gospel.  Similarly, ungodly conduct is no
less ungodly because it happens online. 
We need to learn the art of civil disagreement.  I am by no means adept at this, but here are
some guidelines that everybody can put to good use:



**Examine Ourselves.**  It’s tough to follow Jesus without learning
to become a navel-gazer.  Self-honesty is
an essential habit for the Christian. 
This means being honest about both our beliefs and our motivations for
holding them.  None of us can claim to have
found the simple, obvious truth on every subject, such that anyone who
disagrees with us is either stupid or evil. 
Very often, we see what we see because our background and inclinations
predispose us to see it, and others without the same influences will see it
differently.

We must learn to step outside ourselves to examine our own
beliefs, to be forthright about the times when our own reasoning is weak or
unclear.  If we refuse to engage in this
self-examination and instead ignore the flaws in our own approach, when
somebody else without our blinders brings those flaws up, our reaction is
likely to be angry and extreme.

**Assume Good Will**.  In my experience, most people don’t live
their lives in bad faith.  They don’t
knowingly advance bad arguments for the sake of rhetorical advantage.  They don’t go around seeking to harm
others.  Even when they are not
thoughtful and wise in the positions they advance, they are at least doing so
in the sincere belief that their way is best.

As a result, whenever we encounter anyone who disagrees with
us, it is useful for us to assume that they are acting out of good will.  Even if we don’t agree with their convictions,
we can at least offer them personal respect for that, and both respect and its
lack will communicate themselves quite clearly.

**Seek to Understand**.  Just as it is true that not everything we
believe is simply and obviously right, so too it is true that not anybody who
disagrees with us is simply and obviously wrong.  It’s as important to understand their
arguments as it is to understand our own. 
We should read books and articles whose authors disagree with us, and in
person, we should engage people who do.  If
we aren’t willing even to make the attempt, our refusal reveals a lack of
intellectual honesty.

The point of listening is not to find an opportunity to get
our next jab in.  It is to thoughtfully
consider what the other is saying.  Even
if we aren’t persuaded by it, that understanding will teach us how to approach
and persuade them.  Conversely, nobody is
willing to listen to someone whom they sense isn’t listening to them.

**Be Humble.** Every one of us has been wrong before;
every one of us will be wrong again.  In
fact, the disagreement we are having right now may be that next time we are
wrong.  As a result, we must listen not
only in order to persuade, but in order to be persuaded if necessary.

Pride will make this difficult.  Sometimes, we paint ourselves into a
rhetorical corner where we’re afraid to admit that we’re wrong, even if deep
down we know it.  It might make us look
bad!  In my experience, though, it’s far
better for relationships if we are willing to acknowledge error and move on
than if we cling to an unsustainable, though familiar, position.  I have far more respect for those who are
willing to say, “I’m wrong” than for those who are unwilling even to admit the possibility.

**Persuade.** The difference between contention and
persuasion is subtle, yet profound.  When
we argue, our audience is ourselves and those who already agree with us.  When we seek to persuade, our audience is
those who disagree.  In the first case,
our goal is to score points on someone whose views we don’t respect.  In the second, our goal is to bring a person
whom we do respect to a better understanding. 
Argument angers and divides.  Persuasion
helps and heals.  The former only pleases
the devil, but the latter is of great use in both earthly and heavenly things.

**Ignore
Provocation.** As we’ve already observed,
most people today aren’t good at handling disagreement.  If our views differ from theirs, they are
likely to attribute bad motives to us, insist that we are clearly and obviously
wrong, and treat us disrespectfully. 
When we’re treated in this way, it’s easy for us to become angry.  However, what’s easy and satisfying is almost
always counterproductive.  We gain
nothing from descending to the level of those who are doing it wrong.  However, when we take the high road even when
the other is being disagreeable (and on some level, they usually know they
are), it may well return the conversation to a more civil course.

**Know When to
Quit.** Sometimes, for whatever
reason, people simply won’t listen.  We
need to recognize when we have exhausted the possibilities of the conversation
and look to bring it to a close.  Maybe
we can return to the conversation later when tempers have cooled, but now is
not the time to keep going.  When
persuasion is no longer possible, continuing to re-argue the same ground won’t
get us anything we want, and it is likely to damage the relationship.  The satisfaction of winning a debate (at
least in our own estimation) can’t compare to the harm of losing a friend.
