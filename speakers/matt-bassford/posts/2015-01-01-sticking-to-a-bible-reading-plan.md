Yesterday, I generated a fair amount of chatter on Facebook by posting a status about completing a read-through of the entire Bible in
2014.  Even though Thursday isn’t my
typical post-about-Bibles day, I thought that some might be interested in my
experiences as they formulate their own New Year’s resolutions.

Despite having been a preacher for 10 years, a Christian for
25, and having purposed to read through the Bible on a number of different
occasions, 2014 was the first time I actually pulled it off.  Here’s what I think made the difference for
me, and what I hope will make a difference for others:



1.      
**FIRST
THING IN THE MORNING.** All of the
time-management gurus say that if you really, really want to make something
part of your routine, you should schedule it for right after you roll out of
bed.  That way, you won’t get caught up
in doing something else and forget to do it.

This was certainly my experience with a daily Bible reading.  As soon as my alarm went off (or I woke up
before it), every morning, I threw on some clothes, went downstairs to my desk,
opened my Bible, and read.  Sure, this
meant that I had to get up about 15 minutes earlier every morning (your reading
speed may vary), but I’m confident that putting my reading first made the
difference between success and failure.

2.      
**THE RIGHT
PLAN.**  The variety of Bible-reading
plans out there is nearly limitless. 
Presumably, every one of them appeals to somebody.  Look through a list, and pick the one that
most appeals to you.  Do you want to do
readings seven days a week, or only five? 
Do you want to stick in the same book throughout the reading, or would
you rather jump around?  Are you OK with
spending three-quarters of the year in the Old Testament, or do you want to do
a New-Testament reading every day too?  Figure
out the answers to these questions, and you’ll be able to find a plan that will
suit.

3.      
**ELECTRONIC
REMINDERS.**  Even the most
conscientious of us sometimes forget to do things that we intend and desire to
do.  That’s why people make lists and
keep appointment calendars.  The same
principle applies to Bible readings. 
Nowadays, there are all kinds of websites that will send you—out of the
kindness of their hearts, for free—a reminder to do your scheduled reading.   I
asked for reminder e-mails from esv.org, but they will also send reminders  via RSS, iCal, or mobile device. 

These reminders are especially important in the first few weeks, before reading
becomes habitual.  That way, on days when
you get caught up in doing something else and forget, your e-mail or smartphone
will remind you that you really need to be doing your reading.  I wouldn’t have finished without this
automated help.

4.      
**THE RIGHT
APPROACH.**  For many of us, our
primary interaction with the Bible has been via painstaking verse-by-verse
study in Bible classes.  Close study
certainly has value, but that verse-by-verse mindset is the wrong way to
approach a daily reading.  The Bible is
so densely written that it is possible to analyze every word of every verse,
but that kind of close analysis is time-consuming and exhausting.  If we try to devote that level of attention
to the whole of a four-chapter reading, it will wear us out, and we won’t be
able to make a habit of it.

Instead, we need to learn to read the Bible like we do any other book.  When I sit down with a science-fiction novel,
I don’t painstakingly parse every sentence to extract every drop of
meaning.  Instead, I read at a fairly
relaxed level of attention, and if I miss some things, that’s perfectly all
right.  I can always go back and re-read
later if I have to. 

This should be our approach to the Bible too. 
It’s not a sin to read the Scripture at a relaxed, moderate level of
attention.  Indeed, it’s spiritually
beneficial.  There is certainly meaning
in the Bible at the level of the verse, but there’s also meaning at the level
of the chapter and the book, and when we emphasize each verse, we often
overlook those larger themes.  When
you’re doing a daily Bible reading, then, don’t try to figure everything
out.  Get the gist, and move on.

5.      
**THE RIGHT
TRANSLATION.**  The verse-by-verse
preoccupation of many Christians also influences our attitude toward different
translations.  We often prefer and
advocate the so-called formal-equivalence translations, in which (at least to a
large extent) every Greek word is translated into an English word.  The translations produced in this way include
the familiar roster of KJV, NKJV, NASB, and ESV, which embraces the vast
majority of what I see people in the pews using.

These translations are ideal for close textual study because their
word-for-word approach does an excellent job of capturing the nuances of the
original Greek.  However, this
faithfulness to nuance comes at a price. 
Languages don’t differ from one another only because they use different
words.  Instead, they have different
idioms, different syntax, and generally different ways of saying things.  A translation at the word-for-word level will
have English words, but it may well maintain Hebrew and Greek modes of
expression.  These things make
formal-equivalence translations harder to understand than the same thought
expressed by a native English speaker would be. 
This can lead even Christian who don’t recognize the problem to become
discouraged and give up.

Formal equivalence, though, is not the only intellectually honest way to
translate the Bible.  Other translation
committees have adopted a dynamic-equivalence approach, which seeks to express
the thoughts of the original languages in the way that an English speaker would
express them.  Translations like this
include the NIV and NLT, which, unlike its predecessor, The Living Bible, is an
actual translation.

These translations may not be as well
adapted for close study (I myself really like the NIV wording about 95 percent
of the time but think they got it wrong the remaining 5 percent), but if we’re
not doing our reading at the close-study level (and we shouldn’t be), then
nuance-for-nuance faithfulness isn’t that important.  If all we’re looking for is the main idea of
the text, the dynamic-equivalence Bibles will help us to see that main idea
more easily than a formal-equivalence Bible will.

This is not to say that nobody should use a
formal-equivalence Bible for a daily reading. 
Even though I did at least some reading out of eight different
translations in 2014, I used the NASB more than I used anything else.  I don’t particularly struggle with the NASB
wording (the stuff they made me read in law school makes the NASB look like *Go, Dog. 
Go!*), and I wanted to read out of the translation I use for most of
my preaching too.  However, others may
find that they’re more likely to do their reading if they use a translation
optimized for big-picture reading.

6.      
**THE RIGHT
BIBLE.** Finally, the physical form of
the text matters a great deal too.  The
experience of reading exactly the same translation will vary significantly in
different formats.  Reading the NASB on a
smartphone is not the same thing as reading it in a paper Bible.  I’m not going to opine on whether paper is
preferable to plastic, but I will say this: 
if you’re going to read the Bible on an e-reader, make that choice
because you prefer e-readers, not because the e-reader is cheaper and more
convenient.  Studies have shown that most
people comprehend a text better when they’re reading it from an actual book
rather than from a screen.  You may well
be an exception, but don’t assume that you are simply because you already have
a Bible app on your iPhone.

Within the realm of paper Bibles, format makes a difference too.  Some Bibles are formatted for study.  They have “self-pronouncing” text (in which
all the names have pronunciation marks instead of simply being spelled out),
columns of references, and verse-by-verse layout.  All of those things can be useful to the
Bible student, but for the Bible reader, they may well prove a hindrance.  Instead, the best reading Bibles often have
the cleanest layout, with few references, no pronunciation marks, and
paragraphed text.  That helps the reading
to flow, especially if you’re one of those people who simply must look up every
cross-reference you encounter! 

Other formatting questions, such the number of text columns per page (generally
either one or two) vary in their answers according to individual taste.  I’m a double-column Bible guy, but many
aren’t.  If you don’t know which you
prefer, wander into a Christian bookstore and spend a few minutes reading from
both varieties.  Figure out which one you
like best.

Even the construction of a Bible can make the difference between following a
reading plan and abandoning it.  Most
Christians use utilitarian Bibles, the Fords and Toyotas of the Bible
world.  These are quite functional
(except for the glue-bound Bibles that fall apart in six months, but that’s a
whole ‘nother rant), but they are not terribly lovable.

There are, however, Bibles that emphasize fine workmanship as much as
content.  These are the fine Bibles of
the Bible world, the Mercedeses and Lexuses. 
Their printing and paper are often better, and the covers are made from
premium materials such as calfskin and goatskin.   Naturally, they are also more
expensive.  The highest-end Bibles retail
for more than $200.

Many Christians cannot fathom spending that much on a single Bible, and in
truth, nobody needs a Bible with a goatskin cover.  However, it’s also true that nobody needs to
eat at a steakhouse when there’s a McDonald’s down the street, yet we still
patronize steakhouses and indeed look for excuses to patronize them.  Sometimes we spend more money because we
enjoy the experience that we’re purchasing.

This, in a nutshell, is the justification for buying a fine Bible.  That fine Bible may well be not only useful
but enjoyable to use.  My current Bible
is a premium Bible, a Cambridge Pitt Minion NASB with a calf-split cover, and,
quite honestly, I enjoy holding it so much that I look for reasons to pick it
up.  I find that reading from a Bible
like that makes the experience of getting up before the sun does to do a
reading something I anticipate rather than dread. 

Yes, I know it’s silly, but human beings are silly sometimes.  Why not use the silliness as motivation to do
something spiritually useful?  If a $250
Bible will help you to read through the Bible in a year, where you wouldn’t
otherwise, that’s money well spent!

That’s what I’ve got. 
In addition to the Lord’s help, I attribute my success in following a
Bible-reading plan to these factors. 
Hopefully, you’ll be able to use them to do the same!
