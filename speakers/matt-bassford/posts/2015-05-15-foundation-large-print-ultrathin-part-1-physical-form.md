**OVERVIEW.** Despite the “UltraThin” in its name, the
LPUT is actually a full-sized Bible, not really a carry Bible at all.  My particular review copy is of the premium,
calfskin variety.  The edge-lined binding
is lovely, and the text block is sewn like almost all Foundation Bibles.  With a retail price of $150, the LPUT offers
respectable value when compared to other low-premium Bibles.

\*             \*             \*

As I implied above, the LPUT is a Bible that I solicited and
received from Foundation.  I hadn’t
requested an edge-lined calfskin Bible; indeed, I had merely been hoping to
avoid a bonded-leather review copy! 
Nonetheless, calfskin is what I got. 
Just like other Foundation calfskin Bibles, the LPUT arrived not only in
the standard Foundation box, but in a black velvet back.  I can’t see myself carrying the Bible around
in the bag (it would look kind of like a woman’s clutch purse),but it sure does
look pretty when you open the box!

**VITAL STATISTICS**

**Translation:**  New American Standard Bible, Updated (1995)

**Cover:**  Calfskin

**Binding:**  Sewn

**Trim Size:**  6 ½” x 9 ¼”

**Text Block Width:**  1”

**Weight:**  2 pounds, 2 ounces

**Column:**  Double

**Layout:**  Verse-by-verse

**Font Size:**  Standard (ITC Weidemann 10)

**Words of Christ:** Black

**Cross-References:**  Yes

**Ribbon Marker:**  2

**SIZE.** Travel Bible, this ain’t.  On americanbiblesales.com, Foundation markets
the LPUT under the “Compact/Thin” category, and I suppose that compared to the
Foundation SCR, for instance, this is a thin Bible, but there is nothing
compact about it.

I dutifully went out to the garage to try to fit the LPUT in
the glove box of my family’s minivan.  I
won’t say that the glove-box door *almost* closed,
but it came closer than I was expecting—again, probably due to the Bible’s
unusually slim profile compared to its height and width.  It’s kind of like a greatly upscaled Pitt
Minion—thinner than you would expect for the size.

As a result of this, the LPUT doesn’t weigh very much.  At slightly over two pounds, it’s probably
one of the lighter full-size Bibles available. 
In the hand, it feels much more akin to just-a-Bible Bibles like the
Holman NKJV Large-Print Personal-Size Reference than it does to monsters like a
Cambridge Wide-Margin.

**COVER.** The LPUT is the first edge-lined Bible
I’ve ever owned, however briefly, and I have to say that Foundation’s choice to
offer a premium version of this text block was an inspired one.  Small Bibles are always going to be stiff,
and thick Bibles are going always going to be hindered by the weight of the
text block, but just as soon as I finished breaking it in, the LPUT laid flat,
bonelessly even, in Genesis 1.  I only
lay Bibles flat when I’m writing in them, but that’s still a seductive sensory
experience.  It’s like playing with a
black leather Slinky.

The outer cover is calfskin, though impressed with a
pebble-ish grain rather than the smooth grain I associate with calfskin Bible
covers.  The inner liner appears to be
made from the same material.  There are
no problems with cover fit or finish.

Interestingly, and I don’t know whether this is always the
case with edge-lined Bibles, the entire liner is one piece rather than the two
I had been expecting.  At the top and the
bottom of the spine, the leather tabs that attach the liner to the book block
only begin about a quarter-inch in, and so the top and bottom quarter-inch of
liner run without interruption around the spine.  A Bible sadist could take the top and bottom
half-inch of cover and bend it backwards all the way around without tearing
anything.  Again, I don’t know if this is
the norm, but it’s pretty cool regardless.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEiW3a8LI_rmHs6C2tu9K9iWLNxbouOlvVZjKwFU2MeYx65lT8IgVuZwCkCT17p7aMJM3r_TzTyB0heIcT9yB0dOP5L97w-DWXewnrdR2MItLwgnZKZaG-vf82t8iXmC3CoCM9e-21GphQ/s640/X.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEiW3a8LI_rmHs6C2tu9K9iWLNxbouOlvVZjKwFU2MeYx65lT8IgVuZwCkCT17p7aMJM3r_TzTyB0heIcT9yB0dOP5L97w-DWXewnrdR2MItLwgnZKZaG-vf82t8iXmC3CoCM9e-21GphQ/s1600/X.jpg)

The cover itself has all the flexibility one would expect of
an edge-lined Bible.  There is no
bookboard in between the layers of leather, so the aforementioned Bible sadist
could roll either the front or back cover up like thick cloth.  That too is awfully fun to play with.  It’s probably a sign that I don’t need to
adopt an edge-lined Bible as my primary. 
I’d be fidgeting with the cover the whole time I was preaching.

The cover also has some nice aesthetic touches.  There’s an engraved line running around the
perimeter of the cover half an inch from the edge, and there are also five
spine hubs.  They’re flatter and squarer
than most spine hubs of my acquaintance, but they still look sharp.  Other than the hubs, the spine contains little
in the way of ornamentation.  As compared
to Bibles from Crossway, for instance, the LPUT only identifies itself as an
NASB twice.  The perimeter line is the
only marking on the cover proper besides the inevitable ISBN stamp.

I’ve never used an edge-lined Bible before, much less
preached from one for years, so I can’t speak to the durability of the
species.  Nonetheless, I suspect that the
limpness of the cover is a strength, not a weakness.  Standard paste-down bindings use the
endpapers to attach the cover to the book block, and their weak point is the
hinge, where the thick paper or leatherette will start to split down the crease
after repeated openings and closings. 
Leather, though, flexes much more easily than does leatherette, so
opening and closing doesn’t put much stress on it.  I suppose those leather hinges would fail eventually,
but they would most likely outlast the book block.

My one question about durability with the LPUT concerns the
endpapers.  On each side of the book
block, a piece of leatherette and a piece of thick paper sandwich the leather
tab cut from the liner, thus connecting it to the book block. The front
endpaper looks fine, but the leatherette and thick paper of the back endpaper
appear to be separating just a bit at the corners opposite the hinge.  The separation is slight, an eighth of an
inch or less, but it’s still noticeable.

This is an aesthetic
rather than a structural issue, and Bad Things would have to happen to the
Bible before it became a structural issue. 
Also, even the aesthetic problem is easily correctable.  With a dot of Elmer’s glue, I could fix the
separation so completely that nobody could tell there had been a problem.  However, I mention this for the sake of those
who insist that their premium Bibles should arrive Absolutely Perfect.

Generally, the LPUT is a lovable Bible.  I would enjoy preaching from and reading from
a Bible that feels like this.  From a
sensory perspective, the only aspect of it that I can criticize is the
smell.  Disappointingly, the leather
smells less leather-y and more like a new pair of sneakers.  It’s not unpleasant, precisely, but it isn’t
exactly luxurious either.

**TEXT BLOCK.** Sewn. 
Foundation advertises it as such, and even though I have trouble making
out the signatures in the text block by looking at the ends of the spine, the
Bible is still obviously sewn.  No glued
text block can open flat like that, and besides, I’ve seen binding thread in
the gutter.  I still think the book block
is the most likely part of the LPUT to fail, but it won’t be the binding that
goes, and it won’t happen before my four-year-old enters college.

The gilding on the LPUT is the standard spray-on stuff,
though it did seem thicker than normal. 
I had to go through the Bible page by page and separate the pages to
keep the gilding from sticking them together. 
There certainly isn’t any red-under-gold art gilt or anything like that,
though.

**VALUE.** This is an interesting question.  I would not buy a bonded-leather Bible from
Foundation or anybody else, but Foundation does produce a genuine-leather
version of the LPUT for $50 retail.  Foundation
genuine leather is good stuff.  It starts
out stiff, but it breaks in nicely, and it takes an incredible amount of
abuse.  My dad has used his
genuine-leather 2002 SCR so much that the impressed grain has relaxed and you
can see the pores of the original pigskin, but that nasty cover is still
holding on tight to that equally nasty text block.  As far as I know, the genuine-leather LPUT’s
use text blocks from the same (Chinese) source as the calfskin LPUT’s (at
least, the calfskin text block looks mass-market to me), so there’s no
advantage there.  If I were paying my own
money for an LPUT, I’d stop at genuine leather.

However, I’m also a fine-Bible philistine.  I buy my Bibles at the intersection of cheap
and durable, and I’m not paying extra money for an edge-lined anything by
anybody (extra money for a Jongbloed text block is a whole ‘nother story).

Others see things differently, and that’s perfectly
legitimate.  For people who *really* like to spend money on Bibles,
the LPUT’s setting also appears in Allan’s NASB Readers Edition, which has not
only edge-lined goatskin and art gilt, but also a text block printed by
Jongbloed.  One of those will cost you
twice as much as a calfskin LPUT, though.

The calfskin, then, is a nice low-premium option.  It represents a different take on low-premium
than you get from Cambridge, which makes its low-premium Bibles by putting
premium text blocks in paste-down bindings. 
Instead, Foundation’s philosophy with low premiums looks more like the
top-grain-leather Bibles offered by Crossway. 
In both cases, what the publisher offers is a mass-market text block in
a nicer cover.  If you’re looking for a
really nice Bible for a gift, or if you simply want a Bible that’s enjoyable to
hold from the moment you take it out of the box, a Bible at this level might
make sense for you, and the calfskin LPUT offers considerable value for the
price.

**CONCLUSION.** Even though it’s certainly not a carry
Bible, the LPUT has a lot to offer at the full-size level.  The edge-lined cover is luxurious, the text
block is solid, and the overall Bible is fairly priced.  I give it a 7.5/10 for Physical Form.
