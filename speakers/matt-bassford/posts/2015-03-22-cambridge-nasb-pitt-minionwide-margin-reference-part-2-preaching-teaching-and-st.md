**OVERVIEW.** More than any other parts of my review,
my “Preaching, Teaching, and Study” analysis will look for different
characteristics in different sections. 
It’s not surprising, then, that I found myself liking the Wide-Margin
and the Pitt Minion for different applications.



\*             \*             \*

As I always do, I put both the Pitt Minion and the
Wide-Margin through their paces at the church building for two weeks
apiece.  I preached, I think, three sermons
out of each, taught Bible classes, conducted several informal studies, and
prepared sermons and other study materials. 
Here’s how I thought they did.

**PREACHING.** Unlike many, I’m not at all averse to
using small Bibles in the pulpit.  A Pitt
Minion is slightly smaller even than the thinlines that I tend to use most
often, but it wasn’t a problem.  At home
and in my study, I make a habit of reading with my glasses off (an attempt to
keep my eyes from getting even worse), but I preach with glasses on, which
means that I can easily read the Pitt, even from arm’s length.  It’s also true that small, handy Bibles make
me happy.  However, the small size of the
Pitt wasn’t really an asset in the pulpit either.  When I ventured out from behind the podium, I
never brought the Bible with me, so its handiness wasn’t particularly
relevant.

In fact, there was one sermon where using the Pitt bothered
me a little bit.  I had chosen to preach
a topical sermon without putting the various single Scriptures up on PowerPoint
slides, which meant that I did a lot of page-turning and passage-hunting while
preaching it.  Finding the right verse
was harder than normal because the paper in the Pitt is so thin that it doesn’t
separate well.  At one point in
particular, I think I overshot my page about half a dozen times in
succession!  It wasn’t a big problem, but
it was annoying, and it’s something to think about with Bibles with light
paper.

Also, obviously, I didn’t try to write sermon notes in the
Pitt.  The margins in that Bible are so
small that even the attempt would have been pointless.

My experience preaching with the Wide-Margin was quite a
contrast.  As I’ve already described, I
did write my sermon notes in the Bible, which meant that I used it in a very
different way.  I’ve never liked the
notion of taking my notes away from the pulpit tucked into my Bible.  The extra piece of paper has always seemed
very fumbly to me.

However, when the notes are part of the Bible itself, I felt
free to roam.  Even though the Wide-Margin
is undeniably a less handy Bible than the Pitt, I spent much more time
preaching with it in hand.  I could
wander wide of the pulpit, keep talking, and if I ever lost track of my train
of thought, glance down to reorient myself.

Of course, even the margins of the Wide-Margin don’t have as
much room as a sheet of copier paper, so my in-Bible notes had to be less
verbose.  That created some challenges of
its own during my third Wide-Margin sermon. 
I’m hypoglycemic, I hadn’t eaten as large a breakfast as I should have,
so during the home stretch of the sermon (my preaching on Sunday morning
generally totals about 45 minutes in three different pulpit appearances), my
brain had burned through the available glucose, and it started slowing do-o-o-wn.  Be warned that if you aren’t used to it,
preaching from nothing more than a few notes can be demanding!

I didn’t have any setting-related problems with either the
Pitt or the Wide-Margin.  I don’t
remember any missed citations.  I think
it helps the cause of the Cambridge setting that the superscript verse numbers
do not, in fact, resemble quotations marks.

Even taking my blood-sugar difficulties into account, the
Wide-Margin is the clear winner in the pulpit. 
In fact, I intend to continue using it for expository sermons until I
run out of passages to expound!

**TEACHING.** I used the Pitt primarily and the
Wide-Margin entirely to teach our adult auditorium class, a class that is
customarily taught by a standing teacher. 
I do set up a lectern in the center aisle of our auditorium, but I
rarely use it.  Instead, I move back and
forth across the front of the room in order to engage anyone who makes a
comment.  I may well spend 15-20 minutes
at a time away from the lectern, though not in the same spot.  I’m not as concerned with conveying specific
points when I teach.  Rather, my goal is
to lead a thorough discussion of whatever (hopefully relevant) subject the
class wants to discuss.

In a setting like that, the Pitt shines.  The Bible is so light that I take it with me
without a second thought, not because it has notes in it, but in case a comment
brings a passage to my mind, and I want to look it up.  By contrast, I left the Wide-Margin on the
lectern the whole time, choosing to carry my class workbook with me instead.

The Pitt and the Wide Margin share a setting, so both have
the same massive NASB cross-reference suite. 
As it happened, I believe I did end up using cross-references with both
Bibles.  I suppose that means that either
I’m losing my memory, or the folks in the pews are getting craftier!

In the less-formal, sitting-around-a-table studies I led,
both the Pitt and the Wide-Margin performed well.  I don’t remember one proving to be more
advantageous than the other.

Overall, though, I think that given the choice between my
two weapons of righteousness, the Pitt is a superior tool for teaching.

**STUDY.** Both the Pitt Minion and the Wide-Margin
did well with their work in my office at the church building.  Once again, the double-column, paragraphed
setting proved its worth.  I think I went
a whole month without missing a citation due to the Bible I was using.  My own inattention to detail was another
matter, but there isn’t a Bible in the world that can fix that!

In fact, the only difference that I noticed between the two
had to do with size.  When I’m writing a
sermon, I prefer to sit at my desk, typing on the keyboard with my Bible in my
lap.  I could easily use the Pitt for
that because it’s such a short Bible, though it is wide for its height.  The Wide-Margin, however, made me push back
from my desk farther than I wanted to go. 
I never did find a way to position it that was completely comfortable to
me.

The surprise winner for preparation, then, is not the
Wide-Margin, but the Pitt Minion!

**CONCLUSION.** The combination of the Wide-Margin
Reference and the Pitt Minion really shines on Sunday and in work-related
preparation throughout the week.  For the
way that I want to preach now, at least, for the way that I want to preach
expository sermons now, the Wide-Margin is ideal.  I’m a preacher, and that means to me that I
ought to own and use the very best tool for my trade.

Similarly, the Pitt Minion is an ideal tool for Bible
classes.  Its combination of small size
and weight on the one hand and robust cross-reference system on the other is
hard to beat.  So long as my eyes can
continue to make out the small print, I’m fine with using it to teach.

Finally, the Pitt Minion is very good in the church-building
office.  I wasn’t wowed by it in that
setting, so it’s possible that some other Bible would perform better, but it
gave me no reason to complain of its performance.

In short, the Pitt/Wide-Margin duo is a potent one.  Both Bibles have noticeable strengths, and
they cover well for each other’s weaknesses. 
Together, I believe they deserve a 10/10 for preaching, teaching, and
study.
