A couple of weeks ago, I wrote a post about the legalities
behind altering copyrighted hymns.  As a
result, I received a couple of questions on the subject.  In both cases, the questions were part of
longer Facebook comments, but I’m going to try to boil them down to their
essence.  The first asked whether
alteration of a copyrighted hymn was allowable under the fair-use doctrine of
copyright law.  The second asked whether
some changes were so small that they wouldn’t even be considered alteration.



Let’s start with the fair-use doctrine.  In U.S. law, this is codified at 17 U.S.C. § 107.  The statute reads in part:

*. . . the fair use of a copyrighted work,
including such use by reproduction in copies or phonorecords or by any other
means specified by that section, for purposes such as criticism, comment, news
reporting, teaching (including multiple copies for classroom use), scholarship,
or research, is not an infringement of copyright. In determining whether the use
made of a work in any particular case is a fair use the factors to be
considered shall include—*

*(1) the purpose and
character of the use, including whether such use is of a commercial nature or
is for nonprofit educational purposes;*

*(2) the nature of the
copyrighted work;*

*(3) the amount and
substantiality of the portion used in relation to the copyrighted work as a
whole; and*

*(4) the effect of the
use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.*

For us, the most relevant part of the statute is not the
four-part fair-use test, where most litigation has taken place.  Instead, it is the section that outlines the
purposes for the fair-use exception. 
According to the statute, the fair-use exception is designed to protect
“reproduction. . . for purposes such as criticism, comment, news reporting,
teaching, scholarship, or research.”

Some uses of copyrighted hymn material do fall under these
categories.  For instance, quoting a hymn
in full so that we can argue about its value on Facebook has a comment/teaching
use.  The work product there isn’t really
the hymn.  It’s the commentary about the
hymn, for which the hymn itself is a necessary adjunct.

Likewise, hymn parodies are protected as criticism by the
fair-use exception.  This is particularly
important to me; I would imagine that I parody a hymn at least once every day
of my life, to the point where I sometimes struggle to remember the words of the original.  I’m free to do so, and even
to publish my parodies, sheet music and all. 
In legalese, such a use is considered to be transformative rather than
derivative.

However, altering a line in a copyrighted hymn because you
think it’s better that way (unless you mean the alteration as a parody) doesn’t
look anything like any of the purposes cited by the statute.  You’re still using the hymn as a hymn.  You’ve just tweaked it a little bit.  That’s not transformative.  It’s not conveying a different message.  It’s only derivative, and it isn’t covered by
the fair-use exception.

At the other end of the scale, another reader asked whether
some changes might not be so small as to not qualify as an alteration at
all.  In his comment he cited a licensing
arrangement that he felt gave him the right to rearrange a contemporary hymn,
provided that he continued to pay royalties to the author.

I’ve never compiled a hymnal or supplement, so I don’t know
a great deal about the various licensing arrangements out there, but I have
looked at licensing my hymns under CCLI. 
I don’t care about making any money off of them (and CCLI would pay a
pittance anyway), but CCLI licensing would certainly make distribution of my
work easier.

However, after reading what scanty information CCLI had
onsite, I concluded that I didn’t want to go through with licensing any of my
hymns, precisely because the license appeared to give licensees so much scope for
alteration and arrangement.  That may
actually be where the multiple versions of various contemporary praise songs
come from.  Somebody with a CCLI license
alters the arrangement, but somebody else assumes that the altered arrangement
is a clean copy and reproduces it.

Of course, all of this applies only to CCLI and similar
licensing agreements.  As always, a
copyright holder can sell or give away any rights that he wants to surrender,
and a license is certainly a way to do that. 
Something similar is going on when hymnal editors remove a verse from a
copyrighted hymn.  They’re definitely
paying royalties, and they’re probably alerting the author to the change as
well.

As for what constitutes “alteration” in the absence of a
license, I haven’t found that much information on the subject.  Generally, lawsuits arise over money, not
artistic integrity.  The owner of
“Sanctuary” isn’t going to sue over a changed word as long as he still gets his
money.

The most interesting treatment of the matter that I’ve run
into online is [here](http://mollykleinman.com/2008/10/20/cc-howto-no-derivatives/). This defines what a derivative work is in the
setting of a Creative Commons No-Derivatives license.  However, I think a similar analysis would
apply even outside of a Creative Commons setting.  All copyrights work in the same way, and
there is an implied “No Derivatives” attached to every normal copyright.

For our purposes, the key point in the post is that mechanical
alteration of a work (for instance, scanning sheet music and making a PDF out
of it) does not constitute production of a derivative work, but creative
alteration does.  Because changing words
is an activity requiring judgment, it strikes me a creative alteration.  You can do that if you have direct or implied
permission, but otherwise, you can’t.

In the case of fair use and alteration alike, the story
seems to stay the same.  Don’t take
somebody else’s hymn and rewrite it. 
It’s unethical, and it’s illegal too.
