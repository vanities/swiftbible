When it comes to hymns, I am a technician, no doubt about
that.  In my writing, I strive to imitate
the example of great writers of centuries past while still speaking in modern,
accessible language.  I’m quite at home working
with the hymnist’s trinity of rhythm, rhyme, and meter.  I think it’s an effective way to write;
indeed, I even encourage others to adopt it.

However, technique isn’t really what makes a hymn work.



Technique might get your hymn published if the editors of
the hymnal have a clue about what good technique looks like.  Technique, however, isn’t what gets your hymn
sung.  Instead, what gets your hymn sung
is. . . a good title.

I’m totally serious. 
Though I speak with the tongues of men and of angels, but have not a good
title, my hymn is a dead duck.  This is
true for two reasons.  First of all, a
good title is the only advertising your hymn will ever have.  When the songleader, in the time-honored
practice of brotherhood songleaders everywhere, is frantically flipping through
the hymnal five minutes before services start, putting together his song list,
he’s not looking at neato little Scriptural quotations halfway through the
second verse.  He’s looking at titles.  He will lead familiar hymns that have
appealing titles.

Second, and even more importantly, a good title is an
abstract of the hymn.  In three or four
words (that should also feature prominently in the hymn text), it expresses a
concept that Christians want to sing about. 
The more vivid and powerful that concept is, the more appealing the hymn
will be.

This isn’t the same thing as having quality content
throughout the body of the hymn.  There
are hundreds of hymns that are filled with junk writing that are sung because
they have a good core concept.  “He Bore
It All” has miserable technique, a nearly blasphemous tune. . . but a strong
central thought.  It’s important to
Christians that Jesus bore all their sins, so they want to sing about it, even
if they fail to perceive the irony of a toe-tapper about the blessed Savior
suffering pain and agony.

Even more impressively, a hymn with a strong core phrase
can, if the phrase is compelling enough, overcome mediocre music.  I used to think this was impossible.  I used to think hymns without good tunes were
dead ducks.  However, this is plainly
untrue.  We sing “Amazing Grace” even
though its tune, NEW BRITAIN, makes everybody think of off-key bagpipes.  Why? 
Because the grace of God IS amazing, and every Christian worthy of the
name is eager for a chance to sing about that!

FAITHFULNESS, the tune to “Great Is Thy Faithfulness” is a
peculiar combination of difficult and boring. 
If you doubt me, try the experiment of singing the three verses, and
then the chorus.  The quality of the
melody will assert itself about midway through the second verse.  Nobody goes around humming FAITHFULNESS while
he’s doing yard work.   Even I don’t.

However, we love to sing the hymn in worship, and among
those who debate such things, “Great Is Thy Faithfulness” is widely accounted a
great hymn.  Once again, the key to the
hymn’s success is a tremendous core thought. 
Christians who know their Bibles will recognize Lamentations 3:23 (along
with ten thousand Scriptures beside); Christians who don’t know their Bibles will
still recognize that the greatness of God’s faithfulness is really, really
important.  Chisholm uses the hymn to say
all kinds of profound, profoundly Biblical things.  I suspect, however, that he could have
spent his time talking about the home o’er the foam where we’ll nevermore roam,
and "Great Is Thy Faithfulness" would still be sung.  He already
had the congregation sold from the first four words of the hymn.

Now, none of this is to say that good tunes, good Scriptural
content, and good technique are unimportant. 
After all, we want hymns with tunes so good that Christians will hum
them while washing the car (try getting “I Am Thine, O Lord” out of your head
once it’s found its way in).  It’s a
whole lot better than humming Katy Perry. 
God tells us that He wants us to sing hymns that teach and admonish, so
we’d better be stuffing our hymns with phrases and concepts from the
Bible.

And yes, good technique, that bugbear of the novice hymnist,
is valuable too.  Rhyme heightens the
impact of good content.  Consistent
rhythm and meter help the congregation sing together (and if extremely
inconsistent, may even succeed in destroying a hymn with a good core
thought).  Smooth, polished writing
pleases the substantial minority of Christians who care about the English
language.  “Ancient Words” has a great
title, I admit, but I shall never forgive it for its failure to supply a direct
object to “impart”.  Felicity of style
also deprives future hymnal editors (or website administrators, or whatever) of
a reason to exclude your hymn.

All of these things will make a hymn with a strong central
concept better.  Their absence will
weaken a hymn with a great core thought and wreck a hymn with a thought that is
merely good.  However, none of them can
breathe life into a hymn that isn’t animated by a stirring phrase.  The presence or absence of that phrase will
determine whether a hymn is sung.
