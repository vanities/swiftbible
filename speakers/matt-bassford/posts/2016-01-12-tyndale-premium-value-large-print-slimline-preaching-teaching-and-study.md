**OVERVIEW.**  In format, this Bible doesn’t allow me to
write in it like I want to, but it performs well other than that.  The NLT definitely has some quirks as a translation,
though, both positive and negative.



As I normally do, I put the Premium Value Large Print
Slimline (“PVLPS”) through a two-week trial period.  It so happened that this particular trial
period was a busy one.  In addition to my
usual office and study routine, I preached four sermons, taught three
young-adult Bible classes, and led a couple of men’s studies.  Here’s what I thought:

**PREACHING.** I thought the PVLPS performed adequately
in the pulpit.  It does, however, lack
the first trait I require of preaching Bibles these days.  In no sense is it a Bible in which I can
write my sermon notes.  First of all, it
isn’t a wide-margin Bible.  Its inner and
outer margins are all quarter-inch, which is only enough room to write a few
words if you turn it sideways.  Likewise,
the paper in the PVLPS isn’t very opaque. 
It’s about as translucent as you would expect from a typical thinline,
maybe even a little bit worse.  I didn’t
even try writing in it, but I’m sure the result would have been terrible
bleed-through had I tried.

As a result, I copied my outline into my trusty sermon
notebook.  Like many Bibles, the PVLPS
has too wide a wingspan to comfortably share our narrow pulpit with a
notebook.  When I wasn’t reading out of
the PVLPS, I usually perched it on top of the computer monitor that is set into
the pulpit’s face.  This kept it out of
the way of my notebook, but it kept me from reading from my PowerPoint
presentation, which I do now and again for emphasis.

I didn’t miss any citations when I was using the PVLPS, but
I had a hard time locating the correct verse sometimes.  This was particularly noticeable with one of
the sermons I preached (I actually preached it twice, once in each of two locations).  It was a topical sermon during which I cited
11 different passages, and there were a handful of occasions when I spent more
time verse-hunting than I wanted to.



In fairness to the PVLPS, this may be a matter of suffering
by comparison.  In my usual preaching
Bible (which is a wide-margin Bible that allows me to copy my sermon notes into
the margins), I mark the beginning of readings with brackets.  The other Bible I’ve used in the pulpit
recently is the Crossway Verse-by-Verse Reference, and it’s always going to be
easier locating verses in a verse-by-verse Bible than in a paragraphed Bible
(which the PVLPS is).  However, it is
certainly true that both of those Bibles work better for preaching than the
PVLPS.

One final point about the PVLPS deserves mention.  The PVLTS is an NLT, which is an unabashedly
dynamic-equivalent translation.  In other
words, the goal of its translators was to accurately convey the meaning of the
original authors rather than accurately translating each of the words the
original authors used.  As a result, the
NLT will read very differently than formal-equivalent translations like the
NASB and ESV.

This had both its good and its bad side.  For instance, the textual sermon I preached
was on the entirety of the book of Micah, and I find Micah much easier to
understand in the NLT than in the NASB. 
In fact, I find Micah in the latter to be downright opaque in places.

On the debit side for the PVLPS, I hail from a very
conservative religious tradition in which the vast majority of adherents use
formal-equivalent Bibles.  In
consequence, after I was done preaching, I had a couple of different people
tell me that they had difficulty following my reading because it varied so much
from what they had in their Bibles.  I
don’t have anything against dynamic-equivalent translations; in fact, I think
they’re better for some things than formal-equivalent translations.  However, it’s probably not wise to use a
translation that’s too different from the translations used by most of your
congregation.

**TEACHING.** The PVLPS got a pretty good workout as a
teaching Bible too.  I never taught any
Bible classes standing up during the trial period, but at a pound and a half,
the PVLPS wouldn’t have been terribly difficult to hold for 45 minutes.

When it comes to cross-references, the PVLPS is a tweener
Bible.  It contains citations for
Old-Testament prophecies quoted in the New Testament, in addition to a host of
fine translators’ notes.  It does not,
however, contain citations to parallel passages in the Synoptic Gospels.  I care more about prophetic citation than I do
about Synoptic citations, but the latter are nice to have too.  I don’t remember any instance, however, when
I actually used any of the cross-references that were present.

However, my teaching time did highlight another one of the
quirks of the NLT as a translation.  In
an effort to make the Bible more accessible to those who aren’t already
familiar with it, the NLT translators opted to rephrase many of the theological
terms in other translations into common speech. 
This is certainly a defensible approach, but at times, it sacrifices too
much in the way of textual precision.

For instance, during one of the men’s classes I taught, I
somehow ended up giving an impromptu explanation of what justification by faith
is.  During the explanation, I turned to
Romans 3:26, which in the NASB describes God as revealing His righteousness “so
that He would be just and the justifier of the one who has faith in
Jesus.”  The NLT rewords this as, “[God]
himself is fair and just, and he declares sinners to be right in his sight when
they believe in Jesus.”

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEjqsFi0lyG5JCbatcYhYqOevaoJbIyJ6GStlWBVChRm9BX8pHjA8DcNyBvg7iTq99yVlzRV-yv66rrYvcSmT_SBM0I3hvmMWAsfCeM2eYQQHcYJUTgwcZJHuVzyVdjVZvKL5Moz7Vkh4Q/s1600/PTS+Romans+3.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEjqsFi0lyG5JCbatcYhYqOevaoJbIyJ6GStlWBVChRm9BX8pHjA8DcNyBvg7iTq99yVlzRV-yv66rrYvcSmT_SBM0I3hvmMWAsfCeM2eYQQHcYJUTgwcZJHuVzyVdjVZvKL5Moz7Vkh4Q/s1600/PTS+Romans+3.jpg)



The NLT is easier to understand on its face, but it obscures
Paul’s point.  He is actually saying that
God’s justice on the one hand and His desire to justify the sinner on the other
hand are opposed to each other.  They can
only be reconciled through the death of Jesus. 
I suspect that there are many other such places where the NLT sacrifices
nuance for the sake of clarity.

**STUDY.** Surprisingly, though, it was in my office
that I was happiest with the PVLPS.  The
size was fine; I had no trouble typing with it in my lap.  I never felt the need to use any more
cross-references than the ones the Bible contained.  I did, though, spend a lot of time with the
translators’ notes as I was studying Micah. 
They pointed me to a much richer understanding of the Hebrew wordplays
in Micah 1 than I had ever come to while using the NASB, which has much more
cryptic notes there.  Once again, I think
the NLT translators’ notes are outstanding.

The format of the PVLPS also didn’t present me with any
problems.  The 9-point print was easy to
read, and I didn’t miss any citations as a result of the double-column
paragraphed format.

I did, however, find myself doing a lot of “triangulating”
while working with the PVLPS.  I commonly
found myself returning to the NASB to see what a given verse “really”
said.  By contrast, when I’m using an
NASB, I won’t do the same thing with an NLT.

In truth, I think this is a point in favor of using
translations that are outside the norm. 
If I’m used to the NASB, using only an NASB won’t get me out of the
mental rut I’ve already worn during previous study of a text.  On the other hand, the NLT gets me out of the
rut and makes me think.



**CONCLUSION.** As a Bible, the PVLPS performed
well.  The only objections I have to it
are that I can’t write notes in it, that it’s too big to coexist easily with my
sermon notebook, and that its paragraphed format makes me work harder in the
pulpit.  The translation, however, was noticeably
different from my NASB norm, both for good and for bad.  Generally, though, I’ll give the PVLPS a
7.5/10 for preaching, teaching, and study.
