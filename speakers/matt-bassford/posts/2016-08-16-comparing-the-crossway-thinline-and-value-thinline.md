My opinions have shifted a lot since I started reviewing
Bibles back in late 2013, but one thing has remained constant practically since
the beginning.  I’m a big fan of the
Crossway Value Thinline.  It’s a Bible
that I approve of not just aesthetically, but philosophically.  The Value Thinline is that wondrous, amazing
thing:  a sewn Bible that you can
generally find online for less than $10.

I care about this because I’ve had a lifelong hate-hate
relationship with glued Bibles, even though I didn’t know what a glued Bible
was until a few years ago.  I grew up
using a glued NASB77.  Predictably, it
had a chunk of pages starting in about 1 John that fell out.  In 2005, I started preaching out of a glued
NKJV.  In less than a year, it started
shedding pages.  I shifted to another
glued NASB.  In less than a year, same
story.

|  |
| --- |
|  |

I dislike the feeling of carrying around a bonded-leather
folder of loose Bible pages that may or may not be in the correct order, but
more than that, I dislike the message that a poorly constructed Bible sends
about Bibles generally.  A glued Bible is
like the pretty embroidered hand towel in your mother’s guest bathroom.  Both of them are there for looks.  You’re not supposed to use either one of
them, because if you do, they’ll get trashed.

“No!” I cry with all my heart.  “No!” 
The Bible is for everybody, and that means that everybody needs a Bible
that can stand up to hard use.  That
eight-year-old sitting next to his parents with his bad haircut and his buck
teeth, he needs a Bible that he can read as much as he wants without destroying
it.  Everybody does, and the Value
Thinline is inexpensive enough that it can be a Bible for everybody.  With that sewn binding, the only way pages
are going to fall out of your Value Thinline is if you rip them out.

As a result, the Value Thinline is my giveaway Bible of
choice.  I’ve bought more of them than
any other Bible.  They’re cheap, they’re
tough, and the ESV is a solid all-round translation.  I’m particularly fond of giving them to
people who show up for Bible studies without a Bible.  People like that are usually unfamiliar with
the books of the Bible, so if they’re using a Value Thinline and I am too, I
can direct them to passages via page number instead of book, chapter, and
verse.

Recently, I found myself down to my last Value Thinline, the
one I’d been using to study with others. 
Rather than buying another Value Thinline, I decided to go for the
upgrade and buy a genuine-leather non-Value Thinline.  I justified my purchase by convincing myself
that it actually didn’t cost very much. 
If I made the new GL Thinline my go-to ESV, I could give away not only
my Value Thinline, but also the Crossway Large-Print Compact residing on my
Bible shelf.  See?  It’s practically free!

Regardless, until the next time I cross paths with somebody
who needs a Bible, I’ve got both a Thinline and a Value Thinline on my
hands.  If ever you were wondering what
the difference between the two is, this is your big chance to find out!

**STUFF THE THINLINE
OFFERS:**

**A Leather Cover.**  I’ve said lots of bad things about Crossway’s
genuine-leather covers in the past, but it’s evident from my recent purchases
and review copies that they’ve changed their ways.  This is about the most lovable
genuine-leather cover I’ve seen.  It’s
soft, it’s flexible, and it’s got a nice impressed grain that conceals the
leather’s porcine origin.

The TruTone cover of the Value Thinline isn’t bad, exactly (it’s preferable to
most bonded leather), but it does have one great weakness:  heat and sunlight.  If I leave my Value Thinline in my car in the
summer, the sun will melt the cover. 
This is, in fact, a fairly likely outcome; I’m not exactly careful about
bringing my Bible in from the car after a study.  A leather cover, by contrast, though it will
dry out over time, will stand up to my neglect much better.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgpdMjY0xpRLEFKa9p75C4DMVSW3LKdGcvmYeA2xbpRPeHeLCujgGJQP7ZdfkjDGR3olUMSZpXi-fydDVaxJ-mgie1mEZv_HARJgMTfEgTcW6KLMHC9806gtg7KhpIeiI3NdujRvrCtHA/s640/DSC_0270-Edit.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgpdMjY0xpRLEFKa9p75C4DMVSW3LKdGcvmYeA2xbpRPeHeLCujgGJQP7ZdfkjDGR3olUMSZpXi-fydDVaxJ-mgie1mEZv_HARJgMTfEgTcW6KLMHC9806gtg7KhpIeiI3NdujRvrCtHA/s1600/DSC_0270-Edit.jpg)

**A Marker Ribbon.**  I’m not one of those guys who wants half a
dozen Berisford ribbons sprouting out of the top of his Bible like tassels on a
cornstalk, but there are times when I find that a single marker ribbon comes in
handy.  The Thinline has one.  The Value Thinline doesn’t.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgN8Cxc6s5fR2HoMNsX1u1xQtZszohYsypJAt5l_jVlx-4KX2dhVSA5TNL3oL-uk4Q54WysRlEWtZCFGGL5yY-9jyVGvL_57OhG8l2PKoWg1_qYFzKt2RpQxN4cZGy8QcYe7ZEqThBUSw/s640/DSC_0262-Edit.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgN8Cxc6s5fR2HoMNsX1u1xQtZszohYsypJAt5l_jVlx-4KX2dhVSA5TNL3oL-uk4Q54WysRlEWtZCFGGL5yY-9jyVGvL_57OhG8l2PKoWg1_qYFzKt2RpQxN4cZGy8QcYe7ZEqThBUSw/s1600/DSC_0262-Edit.jpg)

**Presentation and Family Record Pages.**  If you want that set of thick, glossy white
pages in the beginning of your Bible, you’d best turn to the Thinline.  Personally, I have absolutely no use for
these things, but I assume publishers put them in for some reason.

**Maps.** These are typically more for folks in the
pews than for preachers and teachers.  During
services, I’m too busy to idly examine maps, and when I’m in my office, I have
a whole Internet full of Bible maps to choose from.  The maps in the Thinline are typical, though
the perspective drawing of first-century Jerusalem is pretty cool.  However, the Value Thinline offers zero maps.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgS-ePX4Sa1F3HtbIIfx0oofO5H7DDAcLWToYsnY3jgxAhgT1V4S8NcvggMsD25vVm3s7z3lguSPVKoV9eHLad5ZoLzdfyknshS1wgJ_pj4ADqTal3vM9aB4pkUBTNbRx1uKAH9ShCZbQ/s640/DSC_0267-Edit.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgS-ePX4Sa1F3HtbIIfx0oofO5H7DDAcLWToYsnY3jgxAhgT1V4S8NcvggMsD25vVm3s7z3lguSPVKoV9eHLad5ZoLzdfyknshS1wgJ_pj4ADqTal3vM9aB4pkUBTNbRx1uKAH9ShCZbQ/s1600/DSC_0267-Edit.jpg)

**Crisper Print.** When you look at the Thinline and the
Value Thinline side-by-side, it’s obvious that they didn’t come from the same
printer.  The print in the Thinline is
much sharper, recalling almost the difference between Jongbloed-printed and
China-printed Bibles a few years back. 
It is, in fact, a good illustration of how far China-print Bibles have
come these days.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEiVf7DkNKKuHpI_Ai35jrS_pvUyjjYSKWp_yYKCbTgaKdbuAvjJhe99HdmOZGGD9zZ2tLM_e5Nak_NJl33fSUpdo-6lT5BrII0XX5hx_6e56j31coHpCi7SU-ruofT9wNu7ckiW1vTZzA/s640/DSC_0253b.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEiVf7DkNKKuHpI_Ai35jrS_pvUyjjYSKWp_yYKCbTgaKdbuAvjJhe99HdmOZGGD9zZ2tLM_e5Nak_NJl33fSUpdo-6lT5BrII0XX5hx_6e56j31coHpCi7SU-ruofT9wNu7ckiW1vTZzA/s1600/DSC_0253b.jpg)

**Red Lettering.** For those who are determined that the
words of Christ must be in red in their Bibles, you need to opt for the
Thinline rather than the Value Thinline. 
As red lettering goes, this is about what I’ve come to expect from the
most modern Bibles.  The color of the ink
isn’t too bad—no more words of Christ in pink or orange—but there are registration
problems.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEigVTQQC9kn38hdzEmgLEOojxYs1Ldb6i2Vcg3mazqud6-mVD5mnUP87wNzhgwMgPqbQdZqSZbYTrvTV-44cgjEm-iJ8Uv1V5p1MpHzEM8gJYIPwt4tRfiBa_FdrLQHOeKr7xxsgrK8ag/s640/DSC_0256-Edit.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEigVTQQC9kn38hdzEmgLEOojxYs1Ldb6i2Vcg3mazqud6-mVD5mnUP87wNzhgwMgPqbQdZqSZbYTrvTV-44cgjEm-iJ8Uv1V5p1MpHzEM8gJYIPwt4tRfiBa_FdrLQHOeKr7xxsgrK8ag/s1600/DSC_0256-Edit.jpg)

In other words, the printer had trouble getting the lines of red ink and the
lines of black ink to line up.  In John
20-21 in my copy of the Thinline, I’d guess that the discrepancy is about two
points’ worth.  If I were reading the
passage, I would notice the problem, but that may be because I’m a big grump
about red lettering.

**A Box.**I suspect that most Bible purchasers throw away the box about five minutes after they open it.  However, once you've accumulated dozens of Bibles (as I have), you really start to appreciate the box.  The Value Thinline doesn't come in one; the Thinline does.  In the fullness of time, I'm going to razor out one side of it and convert it into a slipcase.

**STUFF THE VALUE THINLINE OFFERS:**

**More Opaque
Paper.** Even though the Thinline has
signatures of record pages and maps that the Value Thinline doesn’t have, it is
actually the Value Thinline rather than the Thinline that is the fatter
Bible.  Fewer pages plus greater girth
usually means thicker, more opaque paper, and that’s the case here.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEg8BgjOxAhlL5o4M3TYcWwXZUr2yrJGvURIGCFu5J2O723N_xGTMGZJzdqbtOJ7kaqxHK3pelp6FqYAKd66kjcZu453KUf94tsSP_PKDCsZ2qnPpg60qdVgDjbIyKLdFhGEZSsdn9kAuA/s640/DSC_0272.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEg8BgjOxAhlL5o4M3TYcWwXZUr2yrJGvURIGCFu5J2O723N_xGTMGZJzdqbtOJ7kaqxHK3pelp6FqYAKd66kjcZu453KUf94tsSP_PKDCsZ2qnPpg60qdVgDjbIyKLdFhGEZSsdn9kAuA/s1600/DSC_0272.jpg)

The Thinline and Value Thinline share a setting, and though it’s an admirable
setting in many ways, it isn’t line-matched. 
As a result, when I compare corresponding pages in the two, the ghosting
in the Thinline is much more pronounced. 
I don’t know why they printed the more expensive Bible on such thin
paper, but they did.

**Less Cockling.** Cockling is a series of little bumps in
the gutter of a Bible.  When you run your
finger up and down the gutter in a Bible with significant cockling, it will
make a bunch of crackling noises.  Some
Bibles like this will even crackle when they open.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEiptkf1aPa64-gWwsvZhHZ6bXglVNYQOi7GmJiPwd_ZcAjZzuPFaxcb_65YJtZJkEBthI5XxhTEBEt0HWcMf_ueBsUNiUsgxKyXkkDJAqMhIqIB9AOPcj34kt3VF6pWssoBxs2a2P8jVQ/s640/DSC_0254.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEiptkf1aPa64-gWwsvZhHZ6bXglVNYQOi7GmJiPwd_ZcAjZzuPFaxcb_65YJtZJkEBthI5XxhTEBEt0HWcMf_ueBsUNiUsgxKyXkkDJAqMhIqIB9AOPcj34kt3VF6pWssoBxs2a2P8jVQ/s1600/DSC_0254.jpg)

Both my Thinline and my Value Thinline are printed against the grain, which
normally makes a Bible more susceptible to cockling, but for some reason,
cockling in the Value Thinline is almost nonexistent.  The Bible opens so cleanly that I actually
started wondering if Crossway had betrayed me and started making its Value
Thinlines glue-bound.  Not so; I found
thread in the middle of my Value Thinline. 
For whatever reason, it’s a nearly cockling-free Bible, whereas the
cockling problems in the Thinline are pretty bad.

**Black Lettering.** The Value Thinline is a black-letter
Bible because it’s cheaper to produce black-letter Bibles, but in my opinion,
it also makes for a superior text block. 
No registration problems here.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEikw4tF29XAvpJ4cTJDH6f8TahPALBOTo1pNRhanmw1akBcSavYVE8Jxu1xkp98-CsDL_McYTnNe7Pr6k5xOSivD2Jp3h6rd7lFf82TdBj1kpdMJ7i3oMBrFHhVKLLZkiNvC8W2FkoT0w/s640/DSC_0231-Edit.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEikw4tF29XAvpJ4cTJDH6f8TahPALBOTo1pNRhanmw1akBcSavYVE8Jxu1xkp98-CsDL_McYTnNe7Pr6k5xOSivD2Jp3h6rd7lFf82TdBj1kpdMJ7i3oMBrFHhVKLLZkiNvC8W2FkoT0w/s1600/DSC_0231-Edit.jpg)

**Bolder Print.** I mentioned above that the Thinline has
much crisper, finer printing than the Value Thinline does.  However, because the print in the Value
Thinline isn’t quite so crisp, the text looks noticeably bolder.  This isn’t a large-print text block by any
stretch of the imagination, but people who alllmost need reading glasses may
find the Value Thinline easier to read.

**Lighter Weight.** Because of those record and map pages,
the Value Thinline is a lighter volume than the Thinline.  It’s only by an ounce, true (1 pound, 3
ounces to 1 pound, 4 ounces), but every little bit helps, right?

**Lower Cost.** The Value Thinline retails for $15.  A genuine-leather Thinline retails for
$50.  Even when you find both deeply
discounted online, the latter is still going to cost about three times as much
as the former.

In short, I’m still happy with my purchase of the Thinline,
and I hope I’ll be able to use it for years to come.  However, the comparison between it and its
more economical sibling is not nearly as one-sided as I would have
expected.  For what I wanted, the
Thinline was still the cheapest way to go. 
However, if I were going to rebind a Thinline, I’d start with a Value
Thinline text block.
