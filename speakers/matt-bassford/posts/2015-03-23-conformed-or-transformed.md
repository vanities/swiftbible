All of us know that it’s much easier to destroy than to build.  I put I don’t know how many hours into house maintenance every year, but I know that if my family and I walked away tomorrow, in just a few years, the house would become unlivable.  Likewise, many of us have invested painful months in some program of exercise, only to see the results slip away after only a few weeks of slacking.

Sadly, the same thing is true of our spiritual fitness.  It takes considerable effort to grow in Christ, but once we turn our energies elsewhere, we begin to drift away almost immediately.  In fact, if we haven’t been paying attention, it may be that we are in the midst of falling away right now without even realizing it.  We can’t let that happen.  We have to pay attention to the signs of encroaching worldliness in our lives.  Let’s ask this morning, then, whether we are being conformed to the world or transformed into the image of Christ.

**Signs of Conformity**

First of all, let’s consider the things that the Scripture identifies as signs of spiritual conformity and ask whether they appear in our lives.  The most obvious of these signs is IMMORALITY.  Peter depicts this quite graphically in 1 Peter 4:3-5.  What Peter describes here is not an occasional lapse into sin.  Instead, it is a way of life that is driven by the lusts of the flesh.  This is about people who endure the work week so they can go out and get drunk on Friday night.  This is about people who love the wild and sexually unrestrained lifestyle that is fueled by drunkenness.  These are people who are constantly lured onward by the devil with promises of greater and greater pleasures, but ultimately only find misery, despair, and death.

I know this should be blindingly obvious, but I’ll say it anyway:  anybody who lives this way isn’t living like Christ.  He’s living like the world.  As Peter points out, once someone leaves the world and turns to the Lord, the absence of this kind of behavior should be a point of conflict between him and his former friends.  When this isn’t a point of conflict because you’re still doing the same thing, you haven’t actually left the world.  I would like to believe that this is a sermon point that misses everybody in the room, that nobody at Joliet needs to hear this.  Surely we all know better than to be drunken and immoral, right?  However, if I were to learn otherwise, it wouldn’t be my first unpleasant surprise as a preacher.  Even if this doesn’t apply right now, but especially if it does, let’s live like people who are holy instead of wicked.

A sign of conformity that probably hits much closer to home for all of us is MATERIALISTIC WORRY.  Jesus spends much of the Sermon on the Mount on this topic, but we see the core of His discussion of it in Matthew 6:31-32.  Here, He contrasts the repose and trust that the Christian is supposed to have with the frenzied search for material possessions that characterizes the ungodly.  Sadly, even for many who wear the name of Christ, our relationship with money and things is much more characterized by frenzy than by repose.  This is a problem because it shows that we’ve bought in to the world’s perspective.

Because we are blessed with living in the United States, none of us have to worry about food in our bellies and clothes on our backs.  Instead, the worry in our lives is generally the product of materialism.  We think a nice house will make us happy, so we buy more house than we can afford.  We think a new car will make us happy, so we trade in our perfectly serviceable beater and start the car payments back up.  We think we need the latest iPhone with an unlimited data plan, so we add another contract there too.

As a result of these things, we paint ourselves into a financial corner.  We have too much money going out every month for too much stuff, we don’t have anything left over to save for future needs or give to the Lord’s work, and every time another bill arrives in the mail, our blood pressure spikes.  The problem isn’t the income.  The problem isn’t even really the outgo.  The problem is the worldly, materialistic heart that looks to things to satisfy when the only source of true contentment is God.  This is how the people of the world live.  They don’t know any better.  We, though, are the Lord’s people, and we should know better.

A third sign of conformity to the world is FRIENDSHIP WITH THE WORLD.  Look at James 4:4.  I think we can sum up the spiritual problem here as going along to get along.  It is true that God expects His people to be friendly and gracious to everyone.  We should treat everyone we encounter with courtesy, respect, and love.  However, our goal can’t be to ingratiate ourselves with the world.  If it is, that will inevitably distort the obedience that we owe to God.  As Jesus Himself says in Luke 6, when everybody likes us and speaks well of us, that’s a sign that we’re walking in the footsteps of the false prophets, who told people what they wanted to hear, not what they needed to hear.

If we want to follow in the footsteps of Christ instead, we will do the opposite.  No one has ever loved the human race more than Jesus Christ, but when He spoke the truth out of that great love, most of His listeners didn’t appreciate it.  Even before His betrayal and crucifixion, there were several points in His ministry when His audience tried to kill Him because they couldn’t handle the truth.  If we are truly disciples of Jesus, we will be willing to speak truth in love too, even knowing that the enmity of some will be the result.  If we aren’t willing to do that, it shows that we would rather be friends with the world instead.

Finally, conformity to the world is revealed by SELFISH AMBITION.  This too appears in James’ epistle, in James 3:14-16.  This is the wisdom we see on display everywhere around us:  the wisdom of the world.  Its favorite word is “I”.  The wisdom of the world is all about what I think, what I feel, and what I want.  It has no interest in the perspectives of others, nor any sensitivity to their feelings.  It doesn’t even stop to consider that the thoughts and feelings and wishes of others might be significant.  It will happily trample all of them on the way to achieving the desires of the glorious, magnificent self.

James points out that this worldly wisdom always causes disorder and sin.  How could it be otherwise?  No matter how highly we may think of ourselves, those around us aren’t likely to believe that we’re God’s gift to the 21st century.  When we keep pushing to get our way all the time, they’re going to start pushing back.  Strangely enough, the worldly wise fail to recognize their role in this conflict.  When they keep on getting into arguments at work, it’s the fault of their co-workers.  When their family lives don’t go well, it’s because they’re being let down by their parents, spouses and children.  When trouble starts brewing at church, it’s because all those other Christians don’t understand the truth.  In other words, people like this will pin the blame everywhere but where it belongs.  We must take care that we don’t conform to this dismal type.

**Transformed by Jesus**

Instead of being conformed to the world, we are supposed to be transformed by the character and behavior of Jesus.  As we consider this, it will help us to prepare our minds for the Lord’s Supper.  First of all, we are supposed to be transformed BY HIS LOVE.  Look at how Paul puts this in 2 Corinthians 5:14-15.  Once we confront the truth of what Jesus has done for us, this is the point to which we are driven.  His sacrifice is stark and absolute.  What did He surrender for us?  Everything.  He left the eternal delights of heaven to come down to the mud and the stink of this wretched planet, to live among us, to walk among us, and ultimately, to be nailed to a cross and killed for the crime of daring to love.  Even though we remember this every week, it should still shock us that God would offer Himself so completely for us.

The love of Jesus is truly incomprehensible, and it demands not only our attention, but our lives.  Once we acknowledge the depth of our debt to that love, it has to change everything we do, everything we say, and everything we think.  Its moral authority over our lives is so great that Paul describes its effect in equally stark terms.  Because Jesus died, we die too.  His example erases our self-will.  It compels us to offer ourselves as completely to Him as He offered Himself for us.  Once we do that, we are no longer concerned with being conformed to the world.  Instead, we live as those who have been mesmerized by the cross.

Second, we must be transformed BY HIS GRACE.  Consider here Titus 2:14.  As the text makes clear, this is actually a twofold transformation.  First, Jesus transforms us by redeeming us from lawlessness.  This reminds me a little bit of what I think of as diet fatalism.  Diet fatalism is when you’re unhappy with your weight, so you determine to start eating right and exercising.  You go to the gym every day for a week, you live on rice cakes and rabbit food, but when you step on the scale Monday morning, you find that you have actually gained 1.2 pounds.  At that point, many of us throw up our hands in despair.  If we’re going to gain weight no matter what we do, we might as well sit on the couch and eat garbage and have fun doing it!

There is a sense, though, in which this kind of fatalism in the spiritual realm is justified.  On our own, even if we resolve to start living for Jesus, the sins we have already committed will still doom us.  There is nothing we can do that will please God, so there’s not really any point in trying.  The redemption of Jesus, however, brings hope to this hopeless existence.  Through Him, we get the fresh start we need.

Once we get this fresh start, though, once we have been purified as a people, we must be zealous for good works.  To us, Christianity must be more than a fire-insurance policy.  The point is not to do the minimum necessary to squeeze through the pearly gates.  Instead, it’s to live for God and glorify Him the way we always wanted to.  We must not ask the question, “How little can I get away with doing?”  Instead, it should be, “How much can I give for all that Christ has given me?”  Our experience of His grace is supposed to transform our mindset and our entire lives into expressions of gratitude and love.

Third, we must be transformed BY HIS EXAMPLE.  Here, let’s consider 1 Peter 2:21.  What makes the life of Jesus so distinctive is not His ability to experience joy.  Instead, it is His ability to endure suffering.  Even though He had done nothing wrong, He bore up under shame and willingly humbled Himself to the point of death on the cross, for His brethren and for God.

The wisdom of the world, by contrast, has no interest in suffering.  If somebody else has to suffer so that we can get what we want, well, that’s just too bad.  Can’t make an omelet without breaking eggs, after all. By contrast, the wisdom of the world literally cannot imagine inviting humiliation or pain for the sake of someone else.  To those who are driven by selfish ambition, such an act is incomprehensible.

However, when we resolve to walk in the footsteps of Jesus, we are resolving most of all to imitate Him in His suffering.  We must be willing to bite down on our anger, to be gracious instead of insisting on our own way, to put somebody else’s dreams above our own, and to compromise on what we want because what we want most of all is the good of others.  The great disciples of the first century understood this.  Peter, Paul, and the rest filled up the sufferings of Jesus because they too cared more about God and others than they did about themselves.  We may think we’ve restored the first-century church because we don’t have a piano and we baptize for the forgiveness of sins.  However, true restoration only comes when we embrace humility and suffering and self-sacrifice because we have embraced the example of Jesus.

Before the Lord’s Supper, let’s remember first of all everything that our Lord has done for us.  When He cried, “It is finished!” on the cross, it was finished.  He had surrendered everything He had because we needed it all.  That, friends, that magnificent act of courage and love, is supposed to be the guiding star of our lives.  How are we doing in that?  Do our lives reflect the love and grace and suffering of Christ?

**Signs of Transformation**

Really, no matter which way our spiritual lives are headed, our actions will reveal the truth about us.  Just as there are signs of conformity to the world, so too there are signs of transformation into the image of Christ.  The first of these is HOLY CONDUCT.  Consider Peter’s take on the subject in 1 Peter 1:14-17.  Notice that the call to holiness is absolute.  Peter doesn’t say that because God is holy, we have to be holy on Sunday mornings.  He doesn’t say that because God is holy, we have to be mostly holy, except when it comes to our favorite sins.  Instead, he tells us that we must seek to be holy in all our behavior.  There can be no room for the sins of the flesh in our lives.  Everything that we do and say and think must belong to God.

Notice also that Peter describes holiness as a family thing.  We are to be holy because we are obedient children who address God as “Father”.  I’m sure that all of us can identify traits that we have that come from our families.  In my case, my strong will, opinionated nature, and dark sense of humor mark me out as belonging to the Bassford family.  Those things are obvious in my dad, they’re obvious in my siblings, and so it’s hardly surprising that they’re obvious in me too.  They’re part of my DNA.

In the same way, friends, holiness is supposed to be part of our spiritual DNA.  Our Father is holy, Christ our Brother is holy, so holiness in us shows that we belong to the same spiritual family.  If we don’t show God’s holiness in our lives, we also show that the family we claim isn’t the family we belong to.

Second, we make our transformation obvious by laying up TREASURE IN HEAVEN.  Our Lord tells us so, in Matthew 6:19-21.  As is often the case in Scripture, Jesus here is speaking elliptically.  The “not” here stands for emphasis, not negation.  It is not sinful to have a big house, a big car, a big bank account, or even a big bunch of stuff.  It’s not even sinful to work hard to earn those things.

However, friends, if we’re working hard to lay up treasure on earth, we’d better be working even harder to lay up treasure in heaven.  Our contribution to the Lord’s work had better be the top category in the family budget, not the “Other” category.  If we’re willing to stay after close at work to get the job done, we’d better be putting in even more extra time in serving the Lord.  If a conflict arises in our lives between work and the Lord, putting Him first shouldn’t even be a decision.

In short, our lives can’t be work-centric, money-centric, or stuff-centric.  They have to be God-centric.  This can’t be a matter of good intentions.  It has to be a matter of actions.  If we say to ourselves, “I put God first,” but the way we spend our money, time, and energy tells a different story, we don’t actually put God first.  Christians have to be different, and if we aren’t different, we aren’t laying up treasure in heaven.

Third, the transformed disciple HUMBLY SEEKS GOD.  Look at James’ take on this in James 4:6-10.  Generally, those who claim to be Christians but prefer friendship with the world regard religion as a hobby.  Sure, they’ll show up on Sunday, mumble their way through a few hymns, and do their best not to fall asleep during the sermon, but they’re generally content for their relationship with God to remain at an acquaintance level.  They like Him okay, but they don’t really think they need Him.

By contrast, if we want to be friends with God, we have to hunger for Him.  We have to crave closeness with Him, and the thought of life without Him should fill us with horror.  We have to be deeply conscious of our sin and deeply aware that we don’t have any right to approach God apart from His mercy and grace.  We have to cling to Him the way that a drowning man clings to a life preserver.

Most of all, we have to come to Him on His terms, not on ours.  Lots of people think of being a Christian as a lifelong negotiation with God:  “Okay, God, I’ll give you Commandments A, B, and C, but this week, Commandment D is right out.  Next week, we might have to talk about Commandment C too.”

Friends, when we get to thinking that way, we completely miss the nature of our relationship with God.  We are not His equal.  We do not have bargaining power.  We are cockroaches, but because He is rich in mercy, He is offering us the opportunity not to be squashed.  Life is what we get out of it, and even though we have to sign away everything else to get it, that’s still a good deal!  It is folly and madness to test the limits of God’s mercy by arguing with Him about what we are and are not going to surrender.  Instead, the only sane response is to come humbly to Him, repentantly, on His terms.  It’s our only chance at life.

Finally, our spiritual transformation reveals itself in WISDOM FROM ABOVE.  James describes this wisdom in James 3:17-18.  Instead of living like the selfish strivers of the world, we should reflect the humility and selflessness of Christ.  We should be people who look not to make waves, but to make peace.

When Derek and I were studying James 3 a few weeks back, I said to him, “Derek, this is the Jesse Fugett passage,” and I can think of few people who understood the wisdom from above as well as Jesse did.  Everybody, and I mean everybody, who knew Jesse loved him.  He was mild-mannered, amiable, and gracious.  I never saw him in a bad mood, even when, toward the end of his life, he was in tremendous pain.  I never saw him insist on getting his own way.  He told me once about a situation in which he believed that some others had done him and his family a tremendous wrong.  His comment on the situation, 25 years later, was, “I pray for those men every night.”  Not all of us can be elders or preachers or song leaders, but every one of us can work on developing the wise, loving, peacemaking heart of Jesse Fugett.
