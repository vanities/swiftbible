There are many differences among preachers in the
brotherhood.  We come in different shapes
and sizes, we have different styles, and we often have different perspectives
on the text.  However, there is one thing
that I guarantee is true of every one of us. 
We all recycle sermons.

Interestingly, it’s evident from the Scriptural record that
Jesus Himself was also a sermon recycler. 
Take the most famous of all His sermons, the Sermon on the Mount
itself.  In Luke, the material that is
collected into one big sermon in Matthew is scattered throughout the entire
book.  One of the biggest of these pieces
appears in Luke 6, on an occasion when Jesus addresses the multitudes not on a
mountain, but in a level place.  Let’s
consider, then, the Sermon on the Plain.



**Blessings and Woes**

The first main context of the Sermon on the Plain centers
around Jesus pronouncing blessings and woes. 
First, though, we see **THE SETTING**of the sermon described.  Let’s read
here from Luke 6:17-19.  The first thing
that’s worth noticing about this account is the star power of Jesus.  This sermon likely takes place somewhere in
Galilee, but Jesus is drawing people from Tyre and Sidon to the north and Judea
and Jerusalem to the south, in addition to all the locals.  That’s about a 60-mile radius.  Remember; none of these people are getting
there by hopping in their minivans, either. 
They’re walking for several days in order to hear Jesus.

Of course, they’re not just coming for the sermon either.  They’re coming to be healed, and Luke’s
account makes clear just how spectacular that healing was.  If you were sick or demon-possessed and you
came to seek help from Jesus, you were healed. 
It didn’t matter what the problem was, and it doesn’t seem to have been
particularly effortful for Jesus either. 
From the text, I get the impression that touching Jesus was like
touching a live wire.  If you did, you
got a jolt of divine power that cured whatever ailed you.

This evidence is critically important for our faith.  Jesus didn’t travel around with a group of
confederates that He fake-healed. 
Instead, He worked spectacular miracles of healing for anybody and
everybody in the whole region who came to Him. 
These miracles were so public and so indisputable that even Jesus’ worst
enemies didn’t try to claim that He was a fraud.  They claimed that He cast out demons by the
power of Satan, but they never denied that He worked miracles at all.  Jesus’ supernatural power was so great and so
obvious that it was quite literally undeniable.

Once Jesus starts speaking, though, He begins with an
unusual list of **BLESSINGS.** Consider Luke 6:20-23.  Even today, 2000 years later, what Jesus says
is so counter-intuitive that it’s shocking. 
He’s talking to the poor, the hungry, the weeping, the hated, the
ostracized, the insulted, the scorned, and He says to them, “Y’all are
blessed!”  Um. . . what?  Even those of us who claim to be disciples don’t
talk like that.  Whenever somebody says,
“The Lord has blessed me,” 99.9 percent of the time, they’re talking about
something that is good in earthly terms. 
The Lord has blessed them with prosperity, or a new job, or a new
baby.  It’s never, “I’m so blessed
because I’m hungry!”  Or “I’m so blessed
because somebody I love died, and I’m all broken up about it!”  However, it is exactly those people whom we
don’t think of as blessed who Jesus says are blessed.

What Jesus wants us to understand, I think, is that
sometimes, what is a disaster in earthly terms is a blessing in spiritual
terms.  We don’t enjoy facing hard times
and tragedy, but those things do have a way of making us take a long, hard look
at ourselves, of refocusing us on what’s important, of forcing us to
acknowledge that we can’t make it on our own. 
In other words, it is those hard times that can drive us into the arms
of Jesus.  Of course it doesn’t always
work that way.  Some people try to kill
the pain through all kinds of debauchery, but those aren’t the people Jesus is
talking to.  He’s talking to His disciples.  He’s saying “Blessed are you,” because these
are people who took their problems to Him. 
If our problems move us to do the same, we’re blessed too.

Next, Jesus produces a similarly shocking list of **WOES.**Here, let’s look at Luke 6:24-26. 
This is an ominous passage for us because Jesus is listing out a bunch
of things that we like.  We like having
money.  We like being well-fed.  We like laughing.  We like having others speak well of us.  But if we are these things, Jesus says, “Woe
to us.”

Here too, we must look at the spiritual effects of these
physical conditions.  The problem with
these things is that because we enjoy them, the devil is going to try to use
them to separate us from God.  If we want
to hold on to them, we are going to have to let go of God.

Let’s illustrate this with one of the most obvious
examples:  having others speak well of
us.  This, of course, is about people in
the world, not other Christians.  If we
want to remain on good terms with those people in the world, though, we’re
going to have to compromise our faith. 
We’re going to have to laugh politely at the dirty joke instead of
leaving.  We’re going to have to back
down when the time comes to take a stand for righteousness.  We’re going to have to remain silent about
the gospel that everybody needs to hear. 
In short, we’re going to have to blend in by talking and behaving like
the world, because the world only loves its own.  If we do that, we’re no better than the false
prophets.  After all, hiding the truth to
make people happy is exactly what they did.

Jesus wants us to recognize, then, that all of those
blessings are actually potential weak spots, that the day may well come when we
have to give them up in order to follow Him. 
If we don’t, that’s not going to make us any better off in the long
run.  Hell is not a place of happiness
and contentment either.  We can’t miss
the pain.  The only thing we can miss is
the reward.

**A Higher Calling**

As if this were not enough, Jesus continues on to point His
disciples toward a higher calling.  This
begins with **LOVING ENEMIES.** Read with me from Luke 6:27-31.  Once again, this is a shocking, world-warping
thing for Jesus to say.  People who are
badly off in worldly terms are blessed, people who are well off in worldly
terms have woe coming toward them, and now the people we are supposed to treat
with love and affection are those who do the opposite to us!  For most of us, the longer we consider this
text, the more uncomfortable we get.

In the larger context, Jesus supplies us with three reasons
why we should do good to those who hate us. 
The first appears in v. 31.  This,
of course, is a restatement of the familiar Golden Rule, but here, it takes a
slightly different meaning.  We want
people to love us, do good for us, bless us, pray for us, and go the extra mile
for us.  Jesus wants us to understand
that the best way to reach that goal is to love them, do good for them, bless
them, and pray for them, even if they aren’t doing that for us.  Hostility and hatred will never get through
to anybody, but love and mercy might.

This means, friends, that if we have relationships in our
lives that we want to improve, the responsibility there lies with us.  This has particular application in our
marriages.  When marriages go bad, it’s
generally because they turn into a domestic version of the Hatfields and
McCoys.  Husband offends wife, so wife
decides that she’s going to snap at him. 
He resents being snapped at, so he does something that he knows she is
going to resent.  It’s a vicious cycle.

The only way for us to break the cycle is to love and do
good for our spouses, particularly when we think they don’t deserve it.  That’s when we appeal to their best side, not
their worst.  That’s when we give them
the opportunity to reciprocate with good rather than evil.  Admittedly, some spouses are stinkers.  They will never treat us well regardless of
how we treat them.  However, we should
never assume the worst.  We should give
them an opening to be gracious.

After this, Jesus introduces the next reason why we should
do good to those who hate us.  It is
because we are **SONS OF THE MOST
HIGH.** Consider Luke 6:32-36.  As Jesus points out, if we do good only to
those who do good to us, we are behaving just like the world.  We did that even before we became
disciples.  Even soulless corporate
entities like banks will lend money to those whom they believe will repay
them.  It’s no biggie if we do the same.

However, if we do good to those who have already done evil
to us, that’s something that the world does not do.  The world remembers wrongs and avenges them,
rather than blessing and praying for the wrongdoer.  The world doesn’t lend to others without expectation
of repayment, either.  When we do, we
show that there is something different about us.

In fact, Jesus says, when we love our enemies, we show that
we have taken our instruction from God. 
After all, God does all these things, and He has done them since the
creation of mankind.  He blesses the
atheist.  He blesses the idolater.  He blesses the most debauched sinner out
there.  God blesses people, even when He
knows that they are going to spend their whole lives steadfastly rejecting
them.  He doesn’t get anything out of
that.  It’s not about them.  It’s about Him.  He does it only because of His own goodness
and mercy.  Similarly, when we love our
enemies, we’re not doing that because of them. 
We’re doing it because of us. 
We’re doing it because we’re God’s children, we’ve learned from Him, and
mercy and good works are what we do too.

Jesus’ third reason why we should love our enemies is **GOD’S MERCY.** Here, let’s look at Luke 6:37-39.  Once again, we see a familiar saying, this
time “Judge not, lest you be judged,” presented in a new light.  In context, the judgment that Jesus is
talking about this time is the judgment of hating our enemies, cursing those
who curse us, lending only to those who will repay us, and so on—in other
words, all the judgments that people in the world make.  Those are the judgments that will lead us to
be judged.  By contrast, when we pardon
rather than condemn those who sin against us, that is when we ourselves will be
pardoned.

However, Jesus says, what we show others is like what we
will receive from God in kind, but not in magnitude.  If we are merciful, God will show us mercy,
all right, but He will be far more merciful to us than we ever were merciful to
somebody else.  In the words of Jesus, it
will be pressed down, shaken together, and running over.  God will heap His mercy on us the way that
some folks heap a plate when they go to Golden Corral.

Of course, the bad news is that the amplification works both
ways.  If we judge and condemn rather
than showing mercy, God is going to pour out His judgment and condemnation on
us, pressed down, shaken together, and running over.  That’s the last thing in the world we want!

Before the Lord’s Supper, then, let’s pause to reflect on
His teaching.  If we have any doubt about
what mercy looks like, we need look no further than the cross.  Jesus’ sacrifice of Himself is the ultimate
expression of everything we’ve studied. 
We were sinners, rebels, haters of God, but Jesus died for us
anyway.  Jesus even asked God to forgive
the very people who were behind His crucifixion!  Let’s remember that astonishing act of mercy
and ask whether mercy is evident in us.

**Hypocrisy**

In the final section of the Sermon on the Plain, Jesus
addresses the subject of hypocrisy.  This
begins with a parable about **EYE
PROBLEMS.** Here, let’s read together
from Luke 6:39-42.  In order to
appreciate what Jesus is saying here, we must first recognize who His target
audience is.  In Matthew 15, Jesus uses
the illustration of the blind leading the blind to condemn the Pharisees, but
He’s not talking to the Pharisees here. 
He’s talking to His disciples.  In
other words, this text isn’t about those bad false teachers somewhere out there.  It’s about us, and the problems that we can
cause when we correct others without acknowledging our own spiritual flaws.

The point here, I think, is that every time we set out to
judge somebody else, we should begin by judging ourselves.  All too often, Christians don’t do this.  I can think of brethren I’ve known, as I’m
sure you can too, who thought they were spiritual experts.  It was their self-appointed job to go around
setting everybody else in the congregation straight.  However, they were completely blind to their
own spiritual shortcomings, and if those were ever pointed out to them, they
supplied glib excuses that they would never have stood for from somebody
else.  In other words, your spiritual
problems are very straightforward and simple, and because you haven’t solved
them you, you must be a bad person, but my spiritual problems, now, those are a
special case!

Friends, all of us need to recognize that our first job as
Christians isn’t getting everybody else in line.  It’s getting ourselves in line.  Obviously, part of being a disciple is
helping others on to heaven, but before we can go do that, we have to be
critical enough with ourselves that we know that what we’re offering actually
is help.  Nobody needs an eye doctor who
can’t see straight himself!

Jesus continues this painful analysis by pointing out that
our words and actions are only the **FRUIT**
borne by our hearts.  Read with me from
Luke 6:43-45.  Here, the Lord wants us to
understand that hypocritical, judgmental, self-righteous behavior isn’t just
behavior.  Instead, it comes from a
spiritual flaw somewhere inside us, and as long as our hearts are that way,
that’s the kind of fruit that we’re going to bear.  High-horse Christians have high-horse hearts.

I think this is a problem that nearly every Christian
struggles with to some extent.  Haven’t
we all been tempted to criticize our brother, either to his face or to a third
party, even though we’re aware on some level that we’ve got plenty of problems
too?  We need to do some soul-searching
and figure out why it is that we want to do that.  Ultimately, I think it gets back to
pride.  Because of our pride, we have a
high opinion of our own wisdom, a high opinion of our ability to fix the lives
of others.  Similarly, our pride keeps us
from admitting our own failings, even to ourselves.  Of course, this pride is never
justified.  If we really were that
spiritually wise, why do we still have all these problems we haven’t
straightened out?  For that matter, if we
have reason for boasting in ourselves, we wouldn’t have need of Jesus.  If we replace pride with humility, a whole
lot of spiritual problems suddenly solve themselves, and we become people who
actually can help others.

Jesus’ final words in the sermon concern the subject of **OBEDIENCE.**Consider Luke 6:46-49.  This
reading begins with possibly the most pointed question in the entire
Bible.  Why do we call Jesus “Lord”, yet
not do what He says?  Because none of us
obeys Him perfectly, on some level, this applies to every one of us.  Why is that? 
Why the disconnect between words and actions?

If we’re indifferent to this problem and don’t ever work on
resolving it, Jesus points out that we’re headed for an even larger
problem.  If we hear Jesus and claim to
honor Him, but we don’t actually obey Him, we’re like a man who builds a house
without a foundation.  We’re vulnerable
to being swept away.  Worse still, we
probably don’t even realize it.  Unless
we’re total frauds who show up at church to fool somebody else, all of those
Lord, Lords are sincere.  We’re placing
our faith in Jesus, we’re placing our hope in Jesus, but we’re not surrendering
our lives to Jesus, and if we don’t do that last, then our faith and our hope
are vain.  I don’t imagine that any of
the lost will particularly enjoy the Day of Judgment, but the most miserable of
all will be those who paid lip service to the Lord, yet never lived up to the
high claims they made.
