This Bible came into my life for a very simple reason.  I requested a review copy of a Bible, any
Bible, from Foundation, and they sent this one to me.  As always with the Bibles I review, it has
spent two weeks with me in the pulpit and the church-building office, two weeks
on my desk at home as a morning reader, and two weeks going everywhere
preaching the gospel with me.

**VITAL STATISTICS**

Translation:  New
American Standard Updated (1995)

Cover:  Polyurethane
(“Leathertex”)

Binding:  Sewn

Trim Size:  5 ½” x 8
½”

Text Block Width:  1
⅛”

Weight:  1 pound, 11
ounces

Column:  Double

Layout:  Paragraphed

Print Size:  9

Words of Christ:  Red

Cross-References:  Yes

Ribbon Marker:  1



**COVER.** As far as I can tell, Foundation only
makes the Topical Reference Bible in three trims:  hardcover, bonded leather, and
polyurethane.  They sent me an exemplar
of the poly which, honestly, is what I would have preferred.  Hardbacks don’t have gilding or rounded
corners, and like most Bible geeks, I’ve long since developed an aversion to
bonded leather.  I would have preferred a
Bible in Foundation’s excellent genuine-leather trim, but they don’t make one.

Poly it is, then, and as poly Bibles go, the TRB is a
respectable member of its tribe. 
Foundation resisted the temptation to go trendy, garish, and hideous
with the cover.  Instead of being
two-tone electric blue and chartreuse or something, it’s a nice, classic
burgundy, which is the only color in which the poly TRB is available.  If we’re talking classic, I tend to prefer
black to burgundy, but I’d rather wield a burgundy poly Bible than a black
bonded-leather edition.

There’s a lot to like about the execution of the cover.  Because it isn’t a Frankenbible, the cover is
one piece.  There aren’t any seams on it
that feel like they might become failure points.  The poly has a thick, durable feel to
it.  The spine of the Bible is subtly
executed, with all the information thereon dry-stamped.  There’s a dry-stamped double border around
the perimeter of the front cover.  The
whole has a glossy rather than a matte finish, but it still isn’t one of those
Bibles you could use as a signaling mirror.

In addition to the color, the only thing that I’m not wild
about is the dry-stamped design in the middle of the front cover.  Foundation advertises it as a cross, and I
can see that, but there isn’t a straight line in the whole thing.  To my eye, it has as much in common with
hearts and flowers as it does with crosses. 
However, when I showed it to my wife, she commented that it was one of
the more masculine cover emblems that she has seen on a Bible.  I dunno. 
Look at the picture, and decide for yourself.

As one would expect with a poly Bible, the cover is joined
to the text block via paste-down binding. 
However, in a classy move on the part of Foundation, the endpapers are
the leatherette that you would see in a higher-end Bible rather than merely
thick paper.  Overall, this is a sturdily
constructed cover, about the best that you could expect at the poly price
point.

**TEXT BLOCK.** Like most (or perhaps even all) of what
Foundation produces, the TRB has a sewn text block.  Unless you just want a Bible to carry to
church for show, a sewn binding is a must. 
Only Bibles that are Smyth-sewn offer any real hope of durability.

However, even for a sewn text block, the text block of the
TRB is a quality product.  Nearly every
mass-market sewn block out there has some measure of gutter
crackling/cockling.  This problem is both
visible and audible.  Every time you open
a Bible with cockling, it makes a myriad of popping noises like a distant
arthritic cracking his knuckles.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEhQPFOQ70_0ukb71G93YCI1rEnSm6yaeJBErQd2LQ8NmelrJnqLCHn390K3xeXuhItsgfsuIG3hUSXenzaFx5h488wGREs77ryP8pCl4jRe-lDcqahXVYUNvNOV9TRmkCj452aR4cvAgg/s1600/PF3.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEhQPFOQ70_0ukb71G93YCI1rEnSm6yaeJBErQd2LQ8NmelrJnqLCHn390K3xeXuhItsgfsuIG3hUSXenzaFx5h488wGREs77ryP8pCl4jRe-lDcqahXVYUNvNOV9TRmkCj452aR4cvAgg/s1600/PF3.jpg)

Honestly, I’ve gotten used to this.  I expect the Bibles I use to be noisy when I
open them.  The TRB isn’t.  Even though it is assuredly a sewn binding (I
have looked in the middle of signatures and seen thread), it has absolutely no
cockling and opens silently.  The only
other Bible I have that will do that is the Cambridge Standard Text REB, which
retails for more than twice as much as the TRB.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgYi-LwLzJe_xbfPJ9rIGg_2m29sJXuHPEUF98IzarBamTQlzbGRCmxDXfl9qewevWEdg2vUy6QFcqK2E1HPCuQ_FCr5YPf0U2IL88WSfxH6sEk5r_LIfTr7uwtv6e-a1-_orYMzG0VGA/s1600/PF4.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgYi-LwLzJe_xbfPJ9rIGg_2m29sJXuHPEUF98IzarBamTQlzbGRCmxDXfl9qewevWEdg2vUy6QFcqK2E1HPCuQ_FCr5YPf0U2IL88WSfxH6sEk5r_LIfTr7uwtv6e-a1-_orYMzG0VGA/s1600/PF4.jpg)

This is certainly a pleasant surprise.  Foundation has had its troubles with cockling
through the years, just like every other mass-market Bible publisher, but they
got it right here.

I do, however, have one bone to pick with the text
block.  Like most Bibles I encounter, the
TRB has the words of Christ in red.  To
its credit, the TRB avoids the usual shortfalls here.  Throughout the Bible, the ink used is a nice
dark red, neither orange nor pink in hue. 
I didn’t have any trouble reading it.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEj1hghe3JkbSmhFmm7pVCZWxVgZLj5kOrj0XgW0SDsR_ncc42GE2Bf5FWqWG0BLOq2kSt2qPum8zbDpWDnJWSslczbZkymHMepRboe_ClgUUDXbV-oFbwlSHYMXimnCtfhiAgxi4krI5A/s1600/PF5.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEj1hghe3JkbSmhFmm7pVCZWxVgZLj5kOrj0XgW0SDsR_ncc42GE2Bf5FWqWG0BLOq2kSt2qPum8zbDpWDnJWSslczbZkymHMepRboe_ClgUUDXbV-oFbwlSHYMXimnCtfhiAgxi4krI5A/s1600/PF5.jpg)

However, the TRB does have some red-text alignment
issues.  As I understand it, red-letter
Bibles are run through the presses twice, once for black text and one for red,
and if the presses are out of sync, the black text and the red text will be
misaligned.  Regardless of the reason,
there are definitely alignment problems in the TRB.

For instance, when I was preaching through Luke 11, I
noticed that the red text was printed higher on the page than the corresponding
black text.  This was particularly
noticeable with the verse numbers. 
Throughout the TRB, the verse numbers are black and set in line with the
text.  However, due to the misalignment
of the red text in Luke 11, there, they become subscript numbers!  It didn’t make the Bible less useful to me,
but I noticed it, and it annoyed me.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEit158i45OdAWPeeX474NqlBJuy5fE2Ok7u1u-tVQFwOK2olArDFLWGVTnxeC6gUvFmW8E7Sr2uaE0s1GFCAat1wp536fmuTT6KvrQeUv3SA4yhntbDIYHOD11XTv0litdvaPRp9PQIcA/s1600/PF6.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEit158i45OdAWPeeX474NqlBJuy5fE2Ok7u1u-tVQFwOK2olArDFLWGVTnxeC6gUvFmW8E7Sr2uaE0s1GFCAat1wp536fmuTT6KvrQeUv3SA4yhntbDIYHOD11XTv0litdvaPRp9PQIcA/s1600/PF6.jpg)

This illustrates the practical problems with red-letter
Bibles.  From my perspective, the red
lettering doesn’t add meaning to the text (I find myself imagining Koiné-Greek
manuscripts with the words of Christ faithfully inscribed in red), it
inevitably adds to the cost of a Bible, and it is very difficult to execute
flawlessly.  If all Bible publishers
everywhere shifted to black-letter Bibles only, I wouldn’t shed a tear.

**CONCLUSION.** The Standard Text REB really is an
excellent comparison for the physical form of this Bible.  The two are the same size, both have a
well-constructed poly cover, and both have a well-made sewn text block that is
free from cockling.  There is, however,
one main difference.  The REB is
black-letter; the TRB is red-letter, and the (unsurprisingly) flawless
execution of the black letters and the flawed execution of the red letters
separate the excellent from the good.  I
award the TRB  4/5 in physical form.
