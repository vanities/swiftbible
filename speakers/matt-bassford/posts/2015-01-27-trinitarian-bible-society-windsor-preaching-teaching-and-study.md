**OVERVIEW.** The Windsor is a delightful Bible for use
in the office and the pulpit.  Its
double-column, verse-by-verse layout make locating Scripture references very
easy.  Even though it’s a smaller Bible,
I didn’t have any trouble reading it. 
Some, however, might be bothered by the lack of cross-references.

\*             \*             \*

The Windsor’s tour of duty in the pulpit and the
church-building office differed a little bit from the norm.  I only preached one sermon out of it because
we had our bi-monthly singing on the second Sunday of the review period.  However, in that one sermon, I read through
the entirety of 2 Peter, so the Bible certainly got a workout anyway.  Here are my impressions:

**PREACHING.** Despite its comparatively large print,
the Windsor is a fairly small Bible. 
It’s a little shorter and squatter than the typical thinline.  On the one hand, this means that it doesn’t
pass my fabled Bible-as-notes-holder test. 
There is definitely a fringe all the way around the Bible when I insert
a half-sheet of paper into it.

On the other hand, as a Bible that is a sheer pleasure to
hold and read out loud from, the Windsor is peerless.  None of my other Bibles feel as right in the
hand has this one does:  not too heavy,
not too light, not too thick, not too thin, but just right.  Goldilocks would have approved.

In format, the Windsor is double-column, verse-by-verse,
which makes for an excellent preaching Bible. 
I have a slight preference for single-column verse-by-verse Bibles, but
the time delay in locating a verse in two columns of text as opposed to one is
negligible.  I don’t recall any
difficulty in finding my place when I began to read out of the Windsor.

Likewise, because the Bible is verse-by-verse, its verse
numbers are conspicuously placed along the left side of each column.  They look to be in the same Slimbach typeface
as the text, except that they are about 8-point instead of 9.6-point.  Though neither boldfaced nor italicized, they
are perfectly obvious.  Through all of 2
Peter, I didn’t miss a verse citation.

**TEACHING.**  Though I am relying this quarter on the text
of Matthew printed in our workbooks, I did use the Windsor some while teaching,
and it served effectively in that capacity. 
As I’ve said, for me, the size of the Windsor feels perfect.  Its heft is satisfying without becoming
annoying.

The Windsor is a text Bible. 
As such, it doesn’t offer even the light cross-referencing present in
the Crossway Thinline, for instance.  In
theory, I might have wanted cross-references. 
In practice, I didn’t.  When
someone in the class brought up a passage outside of Matthew, my memory rose to
the occasion, and I was able to locate the text in question without
difficulty.  However, those who struggle
to come up with book, chapter, and verse on the spot may benefit from a KJV
with more reference helps.

**STUDY.** A Bible that is a pleasure to hold is a
pleasure to hold, and this was true even within the confines of my office.  It’s a little embarrassing to admit this, but
I found myself opening the Bible to random passages because the sensation of
opening it was so enjoyable.

I don’t think I’ve ever seen a Bible as small as the Windsor
with the same measure of readability. 
Part of this, I think, is due to the unique format of the KJV as a
translation.  Because even the poetic
portions of the Old Testament in the KJV are set in prose, the Windsor has
words where editions of more modern translations have white space, which leads
to a more efficient layout and a smaller Bible.

Second, the typeface in the Windsor is innately
readable.  I don’t think I’ve ever owned
a Bible set in Slimbach before, but it works well for the purpose.  It reminds me a little bit of the ITC
Weidemann in my Zondervan Thinline NASB. 
I think I prefer lighter fonts in Bibles over heavier ones.  They lend themselves to a more elegant
layout.

Third, the Windsor is line-matched.  Let me pause to marvel at the quality of this
setting.  As far as I can tell, whoever
did it did everything right.  Quality
typeface, large text size, line-matching—they didn’t miss a trick.  In my semi-knowledgeable opinion, future
Bible designers would do well to study this one and learn from it.

Once again, the Windsor doesn’t have any study aids
whatsoever, not even any translator’s notes at the bottom of the page, so those
who want such things will have to look elsewhere.  As I was using it, though, I was reminded
that even Bibles with apparently robust cross-referencing won’t have the answer
to everything.

It so happened that the sermon I was writing during the
trial period was on 2 Samuel 7.  The
prophecy in 2 Samuel is certainly referred to in the New Testament, but I was
feeling lazy and didn’t particularly want to cudgel my brains into locating the
text.  Because there are no references in
the Windsor, I leaned over and grabbed my Foundation SCR.  Surely the SCR would help me out, right?

No dice.  None of the
references in the SCR were helpful, so I groaned in my spirit, thought for a
few moments, and located the reference I was after in Peter’s first gospel
sermon in Acts 2.  Incidentally, the SCR
will direct me from Acts 2:30 to 2 Samuel 7:12, but not from 2 Samuel 7:12 to
Acts 2:30.  Perhaps the moral of the
story is that even the most robust cross-reference system will miss a few
things.

The layout of the Windsor works wonderfully for verse
citation too.  As is often the case with
verse-by-verse Bibles, I used the Windsor in my office for two weeks and didn’t
miss a citation.  Generally, the record
of paragraphed Bibles is less impressive.

**CONCLUSION.** The Windsor is an impressive Bible for
preaching, teaching, and study.  Quite
frankly, it’s a joy to use.  It is true
that it doesn’t have cross-references, but to me, the elegance of the page
layout for preaching and study makes up for the absence of references when
teaching a Bible class.

If the Windsor were an NASB, it would probably replace the
SCR as my go-to church-building Bible.  I
generally don’t preach out of the KJV. 
Indeed, the period of this review marks the first time in 10 years of
preaching that I have done so.  However,
the excellence with which the Windsor is executed may lead me to bring it back
into the pulpit even when I’m not reviewing it. 
A Bible like that deserves a 10/10.
