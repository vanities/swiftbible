I think nearly all of us would agree that one of the most
odious aspects of democracy in America these days is the attack ads.  Politicians have learned that it’s more
effective to sling mud at their opponents than to talk about the positive
things that they themselves would do if put in office.  As a result, particularly before closely
contested elections, the airwaves are filled with unflattering black-and-white
photos, red text boxes, and ominous music.

If it’s any consolation, the Bible establishes that smear
campaigns are hardly a modern invention. 
In fact, when the enemies of Jesus realized that they didn’t have
anything as positive as the gospel of the kingdom to offer, they responded by
trying to drag down Jesus instead of defending themselves.  These attacks and Jesus’ response to them
tell us much about the Lord and about the religious climate of His day.  Let’s consider, then, a story about Jesus and
the Pharisees.



**Keeping the Sabbath**

Our text this morning will be Matthew 12, and in it, we’ll
see three main sets of attacks made by the Pharisees.  The first of these sets concerns keeping the
Sabbath.  It first comes up with respect
to **PLUCKING GRAIN.** The story appears in Matthew 12:1-8.  The background here, of course, is the Law of
Moses, which forbade Jews to work on the Sabbath, the seventh day of the
week.  However, there were things that
one might do on the Sabbath that didn’t qualify as work, and apparently the
disciples thought that plucking an individual head of wheat and eating the grains
was such a minimal amount of effort that it fell into this category.  The Pharisees, though, ever alert for
opportunities to defame Jesus, accused them of working and Him of being a bad
teacher.

Rather than arguing the point, which was certainly debatable,
Jesus defends His disciples by pointing to His own presence.  David and his companions broke the Law by
eating the consecrated showbread, and nobody spoke up to condemn the Lord’s
anointed.  Priests butchered animals on
the Sabbath, but nobody condemned them because they were serving in the
temple.

Jesus, however, is Lord of the Sabbath, greater than the
temple, greater even than His ancestor David. 
His disciples were in the grainfields in the first place only because
they were following Him.  If the
companions of David were innocent because they were serving him, and the
priests were innocent because they were serving in the temple, then how much
more were Jesus’ disciples innocent because they were serving the Lord!

Jesus finishes His argument off with a quotation from
Hosea.  The point is not that God didn’t
like sacrifice, any more than the point is that God didn’t care about Jews
keeping the Sabbath.  The point is that
God wanted a people that served Him from the heart rather than clinging to ritual
while practicing wickedness, which is exactly what the Pharisees did.

This is a notoriously difficult text to apply, but this
morning, rather than focusing on what it might allow us to do, I want to focus
on what it warns us against.  In this
congregation, we are zealous to fulfill all the requirements of the law of
Christ in our worship.  That’s good.  We should be. 
However, we have to be careful lest we mistake the means for the
end.  Our assemblies aren’t about
carrying out the five acts of worship. 
They’re about glorifying God. 
Let’s always be careful to keep Him in the forefront of our minds.

The second Sabbath issue in this chapter is the usual
Sabbath flashpoint of **HEALING.** Let’s read together from Matthew
12:9-14.  As we look at this text, one
thing is obvious:  the Pharisees are
starting with the conviction that anybody who disagrees with their view of what
Sabbath-keeping means has to be wrong. 
They get to define what sin is. 
They ask Jesus, then, if it would be wrong to heal on the Sabbath,
apparently believing that if He said yes, He would reveal that He was a false
prophet, because in their view, no true prophet of God would heal then.

In response, Jesus makes two arguments.  The first is that even the Pharisees
themselves acknowledged that there was some wiggle room in the Fourth
Commandment.  If one of them had a sheep
fall into a ditch on the Sabbath, they’d go engage in the obvious work of
dragging it out.  A sheep was worth a lot
of money, after all!  Once again
conceding the debatable point that healing was work, Jesus opines that if one
should break the Sabbath for the sake of a sheep, one should certainly break
the Sabbath for the sake of a man.

Jesus’ second argument is wordless but powerful.  He heals the man with the withered hand, even
though he had probably been put there as bait to get Jesus to heal him.  This is all the validation that anybody could
need that what Jesus had said about the Sabbath was true.  Nobody could heal a man with a withered hand
except by the power of God, so God was with Jesus.

However, the Pharisees next show that they have hearts about
as soft as concrete.  Rather than taking
the healing as proof that Jesus was the true prophet He said He was, they took
it as proof that He was the false prophet they said He was.  In fact, it looks like they were plotting to
kill Him then and there.  They were so
stuck in their own little world that they couldn’t see the truth.  This can happen to anybody.  We need to be careful that it doesn’t happen
to us!

Finally, we see Matthew relate Jesus’ divisive behavior here
to Isaiah’s prophecy of **THE
SERVANT.** Look at Matthew
12:15-21.  This reveals the true object
of His ministry, and it also proves that sometimes the biggest obstacle to
learning more is what we already think we know. 
The common people who sought out Jesus after He withdrew from the
Pharisees weren’t experts in the Mosaic Law like the Pharisees were.  However, they knew enough to know a prophet
when they saw one.  Sometimes, the same
is true today.  It’s a sad fact that
there are an awful lot of people out there who are such religious experts that
they won’t hear the truth.  It’s
similarly true that the easiest people to teach are often the ones who don’t
know anything about the Bible.  They know
they don’t know, so they’re willing to learn, and they don’t reject truth when
they hear it.

Matthew rounds this story off with a quotation from
Isaiah.  It predicts that God’s Servant,
when He comes, is going to be gracious to everybody.  This includes the weakest, most vulnerable
members of Jewish society, but it also includes even the Gentiles.  The Pharisees would probably have lynched
Jesus on the spot if they’d known *that* was
part of His program!  Nonetheless, this
is the picture of Jesus that Matthew leaves us with:  willing to help anybody, even at the risk of
offending the self-righteous, because that’s what God wanted Him to do.

Before the Lord’s Supper, let’s reflect on this.  Before we knew the grace of Christ, every one
of us was a battered reed or a smoldering wick. 
We were just barely hanging on, doomed to an eventual and eternal
failure.  However, Jesus took us and
protected us, even though the price was not merely the anger of others but His
own life.  Now, it’s our responsibility
to make His life our own, not a spiritless round of ritual, but filled with
compassion for others.  Let’s consider
His will for us as we partake.

**Casting Out Demons**

The second flashpoint of controversy between Jesus and the
Pharisees is casting out demons.  We see **THE PHARISEES’ ACCUSATION** in Matthew
12:22-29.  As far as I can tell, this
incident isn’t happening on the same day as the first two things we’ve
considered this morning.  However, it is
definitely a result of that confrontation. 
Jesus has ended up on the Pharisees’ blacklist, so now whatever He does,
the Pharisees have to find some way to tear Him down, even if what He’s doing
is casting out demons.  The best they can
come up with is that Jesus is in league with demonic powers, so He’s using the
power of Beelzebul to cast out demons.

This is not the strongest argument I’ve ever heard, for
reasons that we’ll see in a little bit, but it is an extremely important
accusation.  This isn’t Jesus’ friends
confirming that He is working miracles. 
It is His enemies.  They would
surely have denied Jesus’ power if they could, but they couldn’t.  When Jesus was on earth, everybody, friends
and enemies alike, knew He worked miracles.

Jesus has a number of things to say in reply to this
grotesque accusation.  First, He points
out that if He casts out demons by the power of Satan, then Satan is fighting
against himself and will destroy himself. 
That’s hardly possible.  The devil
is a lot of things, but dumb isn’t on the list.

Second, Jesus brings up the unpalatable truth that it isn’t
only Jesus who is casting out demons. 
Instead, His disciples, some of whom are the sons of the Pharisees, are
casting out demons too.  If the Pharisees
accuse Jesus of working for Satan, they have to tar their own children with the
same brush.  Conversely, if the
Pharisees’ sons are working for God, so too is Jesus, and what He says about
the kingdom of God is therefore also true.

Finally, Jesus points out the actual meaning of His casting
out demons by comparing it to plundering the house of a strong man.  You can’t loot the house unless you bind the
strong man first, so it’s impossible for Jesus to be casting out demons unless
He has power even over Satan.

As Jesus continues His reply, He moves on to the subject of **FRUIT.**Our text here is Matthew 12:30-37. 
This reading begins with the generally sticky subject of the blasphemy
of the Holy Spirit.  This is a text that
sends shivers down the spines of a lot of Christians.  None of us want to commit the unforgivable
sin by accident!  The context, though,
makes clear that we don’t have to worry about that.  In context, Jesus is talking about His
casting out demons by the Spirit of God and the Pharisees attributing that
miraculous work to the devil.  That,
attributing the works of God to the devil, is what blasphemy of the Holy Spirit
is.

What makes blasphemy of the Holy Spirit unforgivable is that
no one who commits it will ever even try to repent.  Think about it.  These Pharisees have just seen an undeniable
miracle, supernatural evidence of the power of God.  However, their hearts are so hard that rather
than repenting and acknowledging that Jesus is from God, they attribute His
mighty work to the devil.  If even a
miracle can’t get them to change their minds, nothing else is going to do the
job either.

In the final section, Jesus tells the Pharisees how they
should have analyzed His miracle and then applies the analysis to them.  Jesus notes, correctly, that you can tell
what a tree is by the fruit that it bears. 
So too with people.  Good
fruit—like casting out demons—can only come from good people, and evil fruit
reveals an evil heart.  As a result, the
Pharisees, in accusing the righteous Jesus of evil, demonstrate only that they
themselves are evil.  The tree is known
by its fruit.

Even though none of us are Pharisees, there’s still a
crucial lesson here for us.  By our words
we too are either going to be justified or condemned.  I think every one of us would like to believe
that we’re good people with good hearts. 
However, we must ask whether that is borne out by what we say.  Do we bear good fruit in what we say to our
families?  Do we bear good fruit in what
we say online?  Do we bear good fruit in
what we say to our friends, classmates, and coworkers?  Or instead, are our words too often
thoughtless, angry, or coarse?  Corrupt
speech doesn’t come from a pure heart, and if we are speaking idly, we will
have to give an accounting for it.

**Signs**

The final portion of this context is about signs.  This begins with the Pharisees’ third attack,
**ASKING FOR A SIGN.** Here, let’s read from Matthew
12:38-42.  By itself, this request seems
reasonable.  In context, it’s
ridiculous.  The whole rest of this
chapter has been about signs!  Jesus has
healed the sick and rescued the demon-possessed.  Even His enemies admit that He is working
miracles.  However, along come these
other scribes and Pharisees who demand a sign like Jesus hasn’t confirmed His
word already.

Not surprisingly, Jesus’ reply is fairly disgusted and
angry.  He refuses to perform on command
like a trained dog because he knows that His interrogators are asking from evil
rather than good hearts.  Instead, He
points forward to the ultimate proof of His mission.  As Jonah was in the belly of the great fish
for three days and came out, He will be buried for three days and rise.

The second part of Jesus’ answer, though, makes the point
that His audience shouldn’t even need a sign to believe in Him.  All they have to do is listen honestly.  After all, when Jonah went to Nineveh, he
didn’t work any miracles, but the men of Nineveh—who were Gentiles--repented
because of his preaching.  Jesus is a
much greater preacher than Jonah. 
Similarly, the queen of Sheba, another Gentile, was astonished at the
wisdom of Solomon, even though Solomon didn’t work any miracles either.  Once again, Jesus is wiser than Solomon.

Both Nineveh and the queen of Sheba, then, judge the Jews of
Jesus’ day by the force of their example. 
They were Gentiles, not part of the people of God, but they knew the
word of God when they heard it.  These
Jews, who are God’s people, don’t know the word of God when they hear it and
whine redundantly for miracles instead.  No
wonder Jesus is fed up!

Similarly, we must be wary of making excuses for why we
aren’t obeying the word of God.  The
preacher’s boring, and the Bible-class teacher isn’t any good either, so I
don’t have time for them!  The other
Christians at church don’t like me, so I’m not going to go there and get
shunned. Besides, the Bible is just an old book that doesn’t have anything to
say to me in 2015!  Friends, those kinds
of excuses never fool anybody but ourselves. 
They might make us feel better for rejecting what we know deep down is
right, but they aren’t going to help us on the Day of Judgment.

Finally, we see **TWO
OUTCOMES** for Jesus’ preaching set out in Matthew 12:43-50.  The first is the path that Jesus says most of
His generation will go down.  They will
be like a man who has a demon cast out of him but prepares his heart to be the
home of that demon and seven more besides. 
This generation has seen the Son of Man come to earth, but Jesus isn’t
going to leave them better off.  Instead,
He’s going to leave them worse off because they have rejected His teaching.

The second outcome, though, is precisely the opposite of
this one, and it is revealed when Jesus’ family comes looking for Him.  If family is important to us today, it was
even more important to Jews 2000 years ago, so when the crowd informs Jesus
that His mother and brothers are waiting outside, the expectation is that He
will stop preaching and go see what they want.

Instead, Jesus reveals that His true family isn’t made up of
the people who are waiting outside, even though they share DNA with Him.  His true family is made up of the people
inside, those who are listening to the word of God because they want to do
it.  Those people are more important to
Jesus than His mother and His brothers put together.

All my life, I’ve heard the saying, “Family first,” and I
agree that it’s a true saying, so long as we apply it to our true family.  I love my flesh-and-blood family.  I love my in-laws, for that matter.  However, if I have to choose between them on
the one hand and my heavenly Father and His family on the other, there’s not
even a choice there to be made.  The most
important relationships in my life aren’t with my earthly family.  They’re with my spiritual family, because
those relationships are eternal.  Let’s
make sure that in our lives, we imitate Jesus in making that family our
priority.
