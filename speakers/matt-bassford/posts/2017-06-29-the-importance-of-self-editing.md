I think it’s beyond anybody to write a good hymn on their
own.  If God gives you the opportunity,
you might.  If He doesn’t, you
won’t.

However, it is also true that the aspiring hymnist has to be
ready to take advantage of the opportunity when it comes.  You can have the most wonderful idea for a
hymn in the world, but unless you have learned your craft, either the hymn will
be less than it could have been or it will fail altogether.  There are a number of virtues that contribute
to success here (love for God and His word, the habit of prayer, humility,
self-discipline, and patience are just a few of the spiritual traits that
matter), but some of the tools of the hymnist are the tools of the secular
writer too.



**The Problem With Writing**

Of these, one of the easiest to overlook is the ability to
self-edit.  It’s much less obvious, for
instance, than the habit of writing.  As
a rule, gifted writers won’t struggle to maintain this habit.  God has created them with the desire to
write, so they do.  The mere habit of
writing, though, is unlikely to produce anything that is useful to the
congregation.

As a rule, it takes me about 100 hours to take a worthy idea
and turn it into a hymn that a congregation might want to add to its
repertoire.  However, all the time that I
spend is not equally productive. 
Typically, I will have a rough draft down in two hours or less (if it
takes me longer than that, I will never complete a draft at all).  If a finished, polished hymn is at 100
percent, a rough draft is at about 75 percent. 
It takes me, then, two hours to get 75 percent of the way to final and
98 hours to travel the remaining 25 percent of the way.

As a result, if all I do is write, write, write, I’m
producing a vast array of hymns that are, at best, 75 percent of the way there,
and most of them won’t even be that good (because the idea isn't strong enough).  Only a few rough drafts have the potential to
become more.

Writing all the time is very satisfying.  It’s exciting to write a hymn!  I get that. 
There have been times in my life when I wrote 3-4 hymns a day.

What it is not, though, is useful.  Unless your tune is fabulous or your song
gets recorded by Chris Tomlin, 75 percent is not good enough to be sung.  Even your best ideas are going to be defeated
by problems with your execution.

Hitting that 75 percent level over and over again, therefore,
accomplishes nothing more than wasting the opportunities God has given you (and
make no mistake about it; a 75-percent rough draft is an opportunity!).  You’ll have a filing cabinet full of
forgotten hymns, and those are not exciting or satisfying at all.  Trust me. 
That’s where all the hymns that I wrote in bunches ended up.

**The Problem With Other Editors**

Hymns need editing. 
Indeed, I think they need editing by others.  No matter how pitilessly self-aware you
strive to be, there will always be problems with your own work that you will
not be able to see.  It’s impossible to
write something that profoundly moves you (which every good hymn must) and then
turn around and regard it with complete detachment.  You need somebody who is more detached than
you are to tell you the ugly truth about your work.  Faithful are the wounds of an editor!

It’s tempting to rely on other editors rather than
developing the capacity to self-edit, especially when (as in the churches of
Christ these days) there is a community of writers who are willing to
help.  However, your editors do not and
will never care as much about your hymn as you do.  As a result, firing rough draft after rough
draft and sketchy revision after sketchy revision at them will exhaust their
willingness to edit.

As with most mistakes a hymnist can make, I’ve made this
one.  I spammed so many hymns and
revisions at people that I reached the point where literally no one replied to
my emails anymore.  This didn’t mean that
I’d gotten good; it meant that I’d gotten tiresome.  By asking others for the editing help I
didn’t need, I cut myself off from the help I did need.

**The Self-Editing Process**

The only solution to the problem is to learn to self-edit,
to spot the flaws in your own work, to spend hours looking for those
flaws.  Sometimes, the hours can stretch
into years.  However, it is much better
to spend five years working on a hymn to get it to 100 percent than to stop working on the same hymn after five months and leave it at 80
percent.  The first one might be sung;
the second one won’t be.

Self-editing must begin with a sense of the standard of good
hymns.  What do the best hymns in the
repertoire look like?  What do they have
in common?  Perhaps most importantly, how
does my rough draft differ from the likes of “Hallelujah!  What a Savior!” and “When I Survey the
Wondrous Cross”?  If I don’t understand
the mark, I’ll never be able to hit it.

This comparison begins at the level of the idea.  There is a vast difference between a hymn
that leaves singers saying, “Yeah; that was nice,” and a hymn that leaves them
with tears in their eyes.  Congregations
will adopt the second kind of hymn but not the first.

Honestly, I’m lousy at distinguishing between the two.  As a result, I’ve taken to trying out my
hymns with a group of sightreaders and polling them about their opinions.  Note, though, that it’s possible to burn out
sightreading groups too!  Consequently, I
try to ask myself whether I can visualize a congregation singing a particular
hymn (and this is a question that calls for brutal self-honesty).  If I can’t, there’s no point in bothering a
group with it.

Second, I evaluate my work at the level of mechanics.  How am I doing with the technical trinity of
rhythm, rhyme, and meter?  Every
technical element that is less than perfect reduces the chance that my hymn
will be sung.

Third, I analyze it for clarity and smoothness.  It’s not all that hard to cram an idea into
rhythm, rhyme, and meter.  However, the
result will sound like it has been crammed. 
Inversions, clichés, and graceless diction will abound, and nobody will
want to sing it.  A polished hymn soars
like a swan; an unpolished hymn tumbles like a brick.

Ironically, hymns that sound artless and effortless are
usually the product of great art and effort. 
The hymnist has invested enough time in self-editing that they have
smoothed out the rough spots and planed away the awkward edges.  I only bother to do this if I think a hymn’s
idea is good enough to carry it, so most of my rough drafts die before this
point.

Polishing takes hours and hours and hours.  When a hymn is nearing its final form, I’m
well satisfied if an hour’s work on it leads me to change a single chord or a
single word.  The closer you are to 100
percent, the subtler the changes you must make will necessarily become.  In my mind’s eye, I envision myself running
the hymn through my hands over and over again until it becomes perfectly smooth.  At the end of the process, there should be
nothing left that will snag the worshipers’ attention and distract them from
the content.

Only when I have carefully considered every word and edited
a hymn to the limit of my abilities will I send it out for formal review.  Usually, by this point, my editors recognize
the time I have already spent on it and are happy to help.  After more editing and more revising, I can
take the hymn final.

**Conclusion**

Even after this, there are still no guarantees that the
church will adopt a particular hymn.  It
is still not in the man who wills or the man who runs, but in the Lord who has
mercy.  Every hymnist needs to own that truth.  Success or failure is not under my
control.  All I can control is that I do
my best with whatever God chooses to give. 
If I invest myself in self-editing, I can be satisfied that I have done
so.
