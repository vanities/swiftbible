A couple months back, the Joliet church was presented with a
financial question.  At that time, David
Girardot, a preacher whom we had supported for years, passed on.  He left behind a widow, Annette, and some
sizable medical expenses.  It was
proposed that the congregation should, in order to help with those medical
expenses, send her the rest of the money that we would have sent Dave for his
support throughout 2016.

This proposal caused some discussion.  There were and are no questions about the
good character of Annette.  Nor were
there questions about what individuals could do.  All of us are free to do whatever godly thing
with our own money that we want to. 
Instead, we had to consider whether financial help extended to Annette
would follow the pattern of the New Testament. 
Derek suggested to me that it might be helpful for everyone for me to
walk through the issue from the pulpit, so this morning, we’re going to study
support and benevolence.



**The Support of
Preachers**

The first and simplest of these issues concerns the support
of preachers.  From the Scriptures, we
learn that preachers are **ENTITLED TO A
LIVING.** Consider what Paul writes
about the subject in 1 Corinthians 9:13-14. 
This actually appears in the middle of a much longer discussion about
the right to support that Paul has as a gospel preacher.  For reasons of his own, he has chosen not to
seek money from the Corinthians, but he insists that he would have the right
to.  Here, we see him comparing preachers
to the priests and Levites who served at the altar in the temple.  When an animal was sacrificed, they quite
literally got their cut.  So too, Paul
says, if you are working as a preacher, you have the right to be supported as a
preacher.

This means that it is lawful for a Christian man to give
himself entirely to the gospel, so that he supports himself and his family as a
full-time preacher.  There are those who
insist that preaching should only be the work of the men of the congregation,
but the Scripture simply doesn’t support that conviction.  “Preacher” is just as much an occupation as
any other.

Furthermore, the money that the preacher receives from the
church is **WAGES, NOT CHARITY.** Look at what Jesus tells the seventy when
He sends them out in Luke 10:6-7.  What
those men are doing is working, and what they receive in exchange for their
labor is their wages.

Sometimes, brethren get the idea that support of the
preacher is a benevolent work, that his salary should be equal only to the
amount that he needs to sustain himself and his family.  That’s not accurate.  When the church gives money to needy saints,
that’s an act of mercy.  It’s giving them
something they have not earned.  By contrast,
the support of the preacher is an act of justice.  It’s giving him something that he has
earned.  He has a right to that money, as
any worker does.

So, for instance, when the congregation was still supporting
Dave, we never inquired whether he was financially destitute.  Instead, we asked whether he was doing the
Lord’s work.  Because he was, we
concluded that it was right for the church to compensate him according to at
least part of the value of the work that he was doing.

Finally, the Scripture teaches us that when a church
supports a preacher, whether he be local or working elsewhere, those funds
should go **TO THE PREACHER DIRECTLY.** Here, read with me from Philippians
4:15-16.  During the time that Paul is
talking about, he’s working with the church in Corinth.  However, the Philippian church sent money to
Paul himself, rather than to the church for which he was preaching.  Nor do we have an example in the New
Testament of a church sending money to some third-party organization, which
then distributed it to preachers.

The Bible doesn’t tell us why this pattern was put in place,
but it probably has to do with the fact that preachers earn their support.  Once again, let’s use Dave as an example for
how this works.  Dave faithfully preached
the gospel and faithfully sent us reports about his work.  Because of these things, we had no doubts
that he was worthy of his wage.  At that
point, Dave had earned the money.  It
wouldn’t be any more right of us to send Dave’s money to the West Allis
congregation than it would be for your employer to send your wages to a third
party who would decide—again—whether you had earned those wages.  Nobody has the right to mess with our money
like that, and no other congregation had the right to mess with Dave’s wages
like that either.

**Benevolence**

Now, it’s time for us to turn our attention to the way that
benevolence works, particularly benevolence directed toward members of a
different congregation.  This time,
rather than asking whether a saint has earned benevolence, we ask whether there
is **A NEED.** Paul sets this principle out in 2
Corinthians 8:13-14.  No ordinary
Christian has a right to expect money from the church.  They haven’t worked for that money.  They haven’t earned it.  Instead, the question is whether they need
mercy from the church, whether their financial situation is serious enough that
the church must step in and help them out. 
This is not about people living comfortably off the Lord’s money.  It’s about them getting the resources they
need to scrape by.

After investigation, the men determined that this kind of
need does not exist with Annette.  It’s
true that Dave was the sole breadwinner for their family, and it’s also true
that during his last days, the medical bills piled up, but Annette is not going
to end up out on the street over it. 
She’s said that she’d appreciate any help that individual Christians want
to send her way—Derek has contact information here—but we couldn’t honestly say
that a need existed in her case.

Assuming that a need does exist, the church leadership must
next ask whether that need can be **MET BY
FAMILY.** Paul raises this issue in 1
Timothy 5:16.  As the text reveals, when
a Christian is in need, the church is not supposed to be the first line of
defense.  Instead, both male and female
believers have the obligation to make sure that their relatives are provided
for.  A little further up, Paul says that
those who don’t provide for their own are worse than unbelievers.  This is a serious matter, and a Christian who
refuses to care for his family should be withdrawn from.  Only in situations where the family is doing
all it can but can’t fill the whole need should the church act.

Here’s how this would play out in Annette’s situation.  Dave and Annette had several daughters
together, one of whom, Sarah, is a faithful Christian who lives with her family
in the Milwaukee area.  If this were a
situation where Annette was going to be impoverished by those medical bills, at
that point, the responsibility shifts to Sarah and her husband, Dale.  Maybe they would need to take Annette under
their own roof, for instance. 
Regardless, the bottom line is plain. 
It’s family’s job to do what they can so the church doesn’t have to be
burdened.

Next up, we ought to ask whether the need can be **MET BY THE LOCAL CONGREGATION.** We see an example of this being done in a
passage we looked at a few weeks past, Acts 4:34-35.  Once again, let’s pay particular attention to
the pattern here.  There is a need—all
these Christians who aren’t native to Jerusalem and so aren’t able to provide
for themselves.  The other Christians,
the natives who do have resources, meet the need by selling their possessions,
taking the money, and laying it at the apostles’ feet.  At that point, the apostles take the pooled
money and distribute it to the needy.  Notice
that there’s no indication that the money goes to all the poor people in
Jerusalem.  Instead, the distribution is
only for the needy saints.

Back to our example. 
Let’s pretend that Annette is in financial need and that the need is so
great that her family can’t bear the whole burden.  Now, this becomes an issue that the West
Allis congregation, where Annette is a member, is supposed to take up.  It’s not right for a congregation with
financial resources to allow one of its members to suffer.  That’s unmerciful, and it’s inconsistent with
the New-Testament pattern to boot.  Like
the church in Jerusalem did, the church in West Allis ought to use its money to
alleviate the needs of those who belong to it.

However, if an individual Christian is in need, and neither
that individual’s family nor that individual’s church can meet the need, at
that point, **OTHER CONGREGATIONS** can
step in.  We see that this is an
appropriate work for the church in 1 Corinthians 16:1-3.  In context, here’s what’s going on.  Paul is writing to the church in Corinth,
probably from Ephesus, about the financial need that Christians in Jerusalem
are experiencing.  We don’t know the
nature of this need, only that it existed. 
Because of this need, Paul tells the Christians in Corinth to take up a
weekly collection.  When they come
together on the first day of the week, they’re supposed to contribute part of
what they’ve earned to this Jerusalem relief effort.  That way, when Paul comes, there will be a
pool of money already collected so that they don’t have to hurriedly scrape up
whatever they can.

This text, of course, is our authority for meeting the needs
of the church via a collection on the first day of the week.  I think it’s right to extend the application
of this passage to matters like building maintenance and preacher support, but
there’s no denying that it’s explicitly about relief for the poor in other
congregations.  If we can collect money
for any purpose, we can collect it for that. 
As a result, if we heard that Annette was in need and that the need was
too great for her family and for West Allis both, this church could send from
its collected funds to her.

Such a collection, though, does not go directly to needy
saints.  Instead, it goes **TO CHURCH LEADERS.** This is the pattern that we see in Acts
11:27-30.  This text is about an earlier
collection for needy saints in Jerusalem. 
Paul is writing in 1 Corinthians 16 about events that take place in Acts
18-21, a number of years later.  In this
case, a prophet predicts a famine, the famine is particularly severe in
Jerusalem, so the church in Antioch takes up a collection.  The resources thus collected are brought by
Paul and Barnabas to the elders of the Judean churches.

This is different from what we saw with preachers.  There, the support went directly to
preachers, but here, the benevolent help goes to the local church, then to the
needy saint.  At a guess, the question
here is one of oversight.  The church
leaders who are actually there have better information than far-off Christians
do.  They’re better able to assess needs
and to distribute funds accordingly.  If
we were going to help Annette, then, the money would need to go from us to the
West Allis congregation.  They don’t have
elders, so it would be up to the men’s meeting to use that money to meet her
needs.
