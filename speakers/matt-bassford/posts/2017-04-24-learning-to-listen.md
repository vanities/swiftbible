The more time I spend in our social-media-saturated world,
the more convinced I become that people don’t become different people
online.  Instead, they merely become more
intense versions of themselves.  People
who behave badly on the Internet will behave badly in real life too.  It’s only that they will be more subtle about
it.

I got to thinking about this while contemplating the
conversation after one of my recent blog posts. 
Most of you would not have seen the brouhaha; it appeared on an
ecumenical hymn-discussion site where brethren typically don’t go.  Dozens of people raised objections to what I
had said, but upon reading the objections, it immediately became obvious that
most of the objectors had read the post title and tagline but not the actual
post.  If they had troubled themselves to
read rather than reacting, they would have seen that I had answered 95 percent
of their objections already.

This highlights, I think, a struggle that all of us face,
not only online, but also in real life. 
We all have a tendency to tune out what others are saying and react on
the basis of what we already think rather than hearing them out.  This morning, then, let’s look at learning to
listen.



**The Problems With Not
Listening**

Lest we underestimate the importance of this issue, let’s
start by looking at the problems that come with not listening.  The first of these is **FAILURE TO LEARN.** Solomon
explores this problem in typically acerbic fashion in Proverbs 17:10.  In real life, none of us have a monopoly on
truth.  We don’t have it all figured out,
and we commonly encounter people who know more about something than we do.  Every one of these encounters is an
opportunity.  Either we take advantage of
that opportunity by learning from what they have to say, or we waste it by not
listening.

If we choose the second of these two options, we may well
have postponed learning rather than avoiding it.  As I’ve been known to observe to my children,
sometimes we listen with our ears, and sometimes we listen with our bottoms.  This is true for adults as well.  Much of the time, if we refuse to listen to
somebody else’s wisdom, life itself will teach us the same lesson, except that
it will be accompanied by considerably more pain.

As Solomon reveals, even at that point, the fool will
continue to not listen and simply spend the rest of his life in unnecessary
suffering.  I know people like that.  They stumble through disaster after disaster
because they don’t listen and they don’t learn. 
It’s frustrating to watch and miserable to experience.  However, we can avoid the whole thing simply
by paying attention to the wise people in our lives who have something to say.

Second, not listening generally leads to a **LACK OF UNDERSTANDING.** Solomon lets loose another zinger on this
subject in Proverbs 18:2.  My father used
to say that understanding is applied knowledge, and we particularly need to
make such applications in our dealings with others.  We need to realize that the things that
people say are inseparable from who they are. 
Jesus Himself said that it is out of the abundance of the heart that the
mouth speaks.  As a result, every time we
have a conversation with somebody, that conversation opens a window into their
soul.

If we’re wise, we’ll look intently through that window.  We can only do this, though, by listening
intently, paying attention to subject matter, tone of voice, and what is left
unsaid.  We can then add what we’ve
learned to the fruit of our other conversations with the same person, so that
we can figure out how to have a good relationship with them and encourage them
to seek God.

Too much of the time, though, people aren’t interested in
these glimpses into somebody else’s heart. 
Rather than being focused on the other, they’re focused on themselves,
on saying what they want to say.  Such
people regard the time in which others are talking as a helpful interlude in
which to compose their next comment.  As
a result, they come away from the conversation with no more understanding than
they had when they entered it.  In
consequence, their interactions with people generally tend to be random.  When they make others happy, they do it by
accident, and when they offend others, they’re surprised.  We need to be wiser than that and pay
attention.

Third, not listening produces **HASTY ANSWERING.** Once again,
let’s look to Solomon, who explains this problem in memorable terms in Proverbs
18:13.  This is when we start revealing
that we haven’t been listening.  Somebody
starts off saying something, we figure we know where they’re headed, so we tune
out everything else they say and start preparing our rebuttal instead.

They pause for breath, we jam that rebuttal in before they
have a chance to say anything else, and by so doing, even though we haven’t
been troubling ourselves to understand them, we give them a whole lot of
insight into us.  First, this kind of
hasty answering makes us look inconsiderate. 
Generally, people don’t say things to us because they want to be
ignored, but they can tell from our words that we were, in fact, ignoring them.  Second, it makes us look foolish.  They’ve been talking along about X, and we
butt in and say, “You haven’t thought about X.” 
Yeah, they did!  We just weren’t
paying enough attention to pick up on it.

It’s tempting to start tuning people out when we disagree
with them, but in reality, times of disagreement are when we need to pay the
closest attention.  In that disagreement,
we can never fall into the trap of believing we are the most important person
in the conversation.  They are.  We already agree with ourselves; they should
be our object.  Only by listening can we
hope to achieve persuasion or at least peace.

Finally, not listening causes **QUARRELING.** Paul warns us
against this in 2 Timothy 2:23.  In life,
disagreements are inevitable.  Quarrels
are not.  Instead, they are generally the
result of the things we’ve already talked about.  Somebody thinks they’ve got it all figured
out, they aren’t interested in trying to understand others and their perspectives,
and their words reflect their arrogance and their indifference.  That, friends, is a combustible mixture that
sooner or later is going to produce an explosion!

It’s tempting for us to consider this and think of the other
people in our lives who act in this way: 
“Yeah, So-and-So can be a real jerk!” 
However, it’s much more useful for us to reflect on the times in our
lives when we have been this person, and all of us have.  When we are that person, it has serious
consequences for our relationships, not least our relationship with God.

What each of us should do this morning, then, is to consider
the quarrels we’ve had with others, and do our best to draw a line between that
quarrel and our failure to listen. 
Usually, we would rather act like we were 100 percent not at fault, but
that’s almost never true.  It may even be
true that we bear the lion’s share of the blame, but we’re paying more
attention to our anger and sense of self-righteousness than we are to
reality.  Unless we acknowledge our own
tendencies to thoughtlessness, the same mistakes will keep coming back to haunt
us.

**How to Listen**

Since listening is so important, we ought to turn our
attention to learning how to listen.  One
of the first keys here is to **DESIRE
UNDERSTANDING.** Solomon explains the
importance of this attitude in Proverbs 23:22-23.  Here, the father of Proverbs is encouraging
his son to listen to him not necessarily out of love for good old Dad, but out
of love for truth, understanding, and wisdom. 
This leads us back to the fundamental question we should ask before we
start any conversation with anybody, “Why am I having this conversation?”  Am I having it for me, because I’ve got these
thoughts and opinions I want to express? 
Or am I having it for them, because I want to know what they think and
feel?  Now, we might say, “Yeah, but
there’s this thing they really need to understand!”  However, even then, we still have to speak
from a place of understanding.

Listening is a great way to understand.  An even better way is to listen, ask
clarifying questions, and then listen to the answers.  The Scripture itself teaches us how powerful
questions are.  We can use them to
confound an adversary, as Jesus often did, to enlighten others, and perhaps
most of all, to enlighten ourselves. 
People hate being ignored, but they appreciate thoughtful questions
asked out of goodwill.  Questions show
that we’re interested enough in what they’re saying to invest ourselves in
figuring it out.  Just as it is obvious
when we focus the conversation on ourselves, it’s equally obvious when we focus
the conversation on others.

Second, we must remember to **SEEK PEACE.** Consider what
James has to say on the subject in James 3:16-17.  In this text, we see two spirits
described:  a jealous, selfishly
ambitious spirit, and a gentle, peacemaking spirit.  James points out, unsurprisingly, that the
first kind of spirit produces disorder and evil, while the second yields a
harvest of righteousness and peace.

One of the surest signs of selfish ambition is that the
selfish person won’t listen to anybody. 
They don’t need to.  After all,
they’ve got it all figured out, they’re dead certain that they’re 100 percent
right, and everybody else just needs to shut up and do what they say!

The peacemaker, by contrast, has to be a listener.  Their goal is not to get their way; instead,
it’s to maintain and enrich relationships, and there’s simply no way to do that
without listening.  After all, listening
has emotional overtones as well as intellectual ones.  It communicates not only interest, but also
care and concern.  It’s a way of honoring
someone’s essential worth as a human being, and every healthy relationship must
be founded on respect.

When we listen, even in the middle of a disagreement, we’re
sending a powerful message.  We’re
saying, “I may not agree with you right now, but you still matter to me, and
you are more important to me than the disagreement.”  That’s the spirit that keeps things from
moving from disagreement to argument.

Finally, we become better listeners by becoming **SLOW TO SPEAK.** This too comes from the pen of James, in
James 1:19-20.  Life is not a game of
Jeopardy.  We don’t win by being the
first to buzz in with an answer.  Unless
we’re literally in the middle of a debate where some third party is keeping
score, there’s no point to crafting our answer while the other person is still
speaking.  In fact, we usually don’t have
to craft an answer on the spot at all. 
In almost every circumstance of life, “Let me think about that, and I’ll
get back to you” is a perfectly honorable thing to say.

Once the pressure to come up with a snappy retort is gone,
our whole perspective on the conversation changes.  Instead of worrying about being quick to
speak, we can focus on being quick to hear. 
For all the reasons we’ve described, quickness of hearing is
valuable.  It reveals both wisdom and
love, and it generates more of both.  We
need to be people who read both the headline and the article, not only
literally, but metaphorically too.  It
may not be as emotionally satisfying to aim before we fire, but we will lead
happier and better lives as a result.
