This cheerful reflection was prompted by [an article](http://www.msn.com/en-us/news/us/lgbt-advocates-seek-to-label-opponents-as-us-hate-groups/ar-BBzOLRL) that popped up in my news feed yesterday. 
Apparently, a bunch of pro-LBGTQ, etc.-rights groups have decided that
they need to work together to come up with a list of socially conservative political
organizations that they see as extremist and hateful.

So far, this umbrella organization, the Eliminate Hate
Campaign, has tagged 50 groups with the “hate group” label.  The list included relatively well-respected,
non-extremist conservative lobbying groups like the Alliance Defending Freedom
and the Family Research Council.  These
are not people who stand by the side of the road with signs reading “F\*GS BURN
IN HELL”, as I once memorably saw Fred Phelps and the Westboro Baptist Church
doing.  Certainly, they oppose the
expansion of gay and transgender rights, but they do so according to the
accepted rules of polite discourse in civil society.

Apparently, that’s no longer good enough.  Now, any group that promotes traditional
morality in the political sphere is considered a hate group *per se*. 
Their claims of Christian conviction are dismissed as a mask for
homophobia.  The Campaign Against Hate
insists that rather than describing such organizations as socially conservative
or religiously motivated, media outlets should consistently brand them with the
“hate group” label.



Now, I’m not terribly impressed with the Campaign Against
Hate.  They don’t even have a website up
yet, so they’ve flunked the Google test of relevance.  However, I think they clearly show which way
the wind is blowing.  We are entering a
phase of the culture wars in which gay-rights crusaders are so convinced of
their own rightness that they refuse to acknowledge that anyone who disagrees
with them can be a person of goodwill. 
Either you’re for me, or you hate me.

There’s absolutely no reason why this conviction will
confine itself to conservative political organizations.  If the Alliance Defending Freedom is a hate
group, what keeps the Joliet church of Christ from being a hate group?  After all, we publicly teach (on the
Internet, even!) that the practice of homosexuality is a sin, and that
homosexuals will not inherit the kingdom of God.  Concerning transgender people, we observe
that “God created them male and female,” so what one feels has no bearing on
what one is.  We believe that if they
want to please God, men must act like men, and women must act like women.

I’m quite sure that the Campaign Against Hate would identify
the above as hatred *per se* also.  Logically, then, any church that agrees with such
sentiments must be a hate group.  Never
mind that we are teaching the same things that early Christians taught 2000
years ago.  It’s already evident that
claims of religious motivation are regarded only as a cloak for bigotry.

Never mind that we don’t behave in ways that are ordinarily
described as hateful.  We won’t alter our
teaching for practicing homosexuals who visit our assemblies (nor for anybody
else either), but we treat them, as we treat everyone, with kindness, courtesy,
and respect.  Not good enough.  Civility does not save the Alliance Defending
Freedom from being tarred with the hate-group brush.

In short, there is no way that we can retain our beliefs in
a way that those who disagree with us will regard as legitimate.  We are not only wrong.  We are also bad, and it is nearly certain
that the coming years and decades will see increased attempts to punish us for
our badness.

It’s terribly ironic that this impulse comes from those who
are customarily described as liberals, whose very name comes from the Latin
word for freedom.  These are the people
who supposedly celebrate personal choice and decry conformity.  In practice, though, their view of “rights”
looks a lot like the “rights” celebrated by the slaveowners of the antebellum
American South.  These are rights for me,
but not rights for you, and this denial of rights to the other is justified by
an attack on the other’s intellectual/moral/spiritual worth.

It’s worth remembering that the advocates of “state’s rights”
were also the architects of the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850, which compelled officials
in the North to cooperate with slave-catchers in recapturing escaped slaves,
even when doing so violated their consciences. 
Surely, we don’t see sexual-autonomy advocates seeking to use the power
of government to compel, do we?  Personally,
any definition of “freedom” that requires the coercion of others strikes me as
more than a little Orwellian.

There’s also plenty of historical precedent for the
cataloguing of the opposition.  It may
not be precedent that progressives want to embrace, but it’s there.  One is reminded of the blacklists of the
McCarthy era, Nixon’s “enemies list”, the terrorist-organization watchlists,
and even Trump’s list of seven Muslim countries from which no one may enter the
U.S.  Once you start making lists, like
it or not, this is the company you are keeping. 
The effect of a list is always to limit, not liberate.

In fact, the article called me to consider why I don’t make
lists.  There are certainly plenty of
people and organizations out there with whom I disagree.  Why not put ‘em all together in one big blog
post, so that readers can see where I stand and where they ought to stand?

Ultimately, I don’t make lists because the people on a list
are people, and the people in the groups on a list are people too.  They’re not my enemies, even if they consider
me theirs.  Like me, they are created in
the likeness and image of God, and we share a common humanity.  I don’t want to be divided from those
people.  I don’t want to exclude
them.  Instead, I want to persuade and
invite them, and nobody who has ever appeared on an enemies list has thought it
was an invitation.

I don’t make lists because lists do nothing to advance my
goals.  I fear, though, that LGBTQ
list-makers make lists because those lists do advance their goals.  List-makers aren’t interested in persuasion
and invitation.  They’re interested in intimidation
and exclusion.  In the end, the Campaign
Against Hate’s list, along with its inevitable successors, will not serve as an
identification of hatred.  They will
serve as hatred’s expression.
