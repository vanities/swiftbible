I try to read articles written by people with whom I
disagree.  It’s healthy to challenge your
own views on a regular basis; otherwise, your mind starts rusting out.  Sometimes, these writers are ranters who
annoy me.  At other times, they have
something worthwhile to say that makes me think, and that’s the best of all.

Take, for instance, [this recent post](http://johnpavlovitz.com/2016/10/12/fellow-white-christian-friends-i-wish-you-really-were-pro-life/) by John Pavlovitz.  I’m not convinced
that his actual intent is to persuade white, conservative, Christian friends of
his (more likely, his aim is to give his white, liberal, quasi-Christian
friends reason to believe that white conservative Christians are hypocrites),
but I think there’s a point here.



If the Republican party is “God’s party” and aims to enact
Judeo-Christian ethics into law, the version of Judeo-Christian ethics that
they aim to enact is a skewed one.  I’ve
made and will continue to make the Biblical case that abortion is evil, but
there’s also a case to be made that many politically conservative positions are
unbiblical too.  The Bible urges us to
love the stranger and the foreigner; Republicans want to limit
immigration.  The Bible instructs us to
care for the poor; Republicans desire to slash welfare benefits.  In short, one of the core principles of
Christianity is compassion, but the political agenda of the Republicans doesn’t
look very compassionate.  In Pavlovitz’s
terms, pro-lifers often don’t seem particularly pro-life once the life gets out
of the womb.

I think that this criticism is something that political (and
moral, for that matter) conservatives must reckon with, but unlike Pavlovitz, I
don’t believe that the solution is to vote Democrat to make the government more
pro-life and more compassionate.  For one
thing, even if *Roe v. Wade* will never
be overturned, and I don’t think it will be, I still can’t bring myself to
support the party that is its greatest supporter.  Pavlovitz claims, “I don’t celebrate when a
woman terminates a pregnancy,” but there are many who do, as the
#ShoutYourAbortion hashtag demonstrates. 
Other Christians may differ here, but that's not an issue where I can compromise.

Even beyond that, though, I simply can’t see that voting for
a particular party is ever an act of compassion.  I’m hardly a compassion expert, but I know
that intervening for good in somebody else’s life is time-consuming, difficult,
emotionally draining, and uncertain. 
Voting is uncertain, but it isn’t any of those other things.  It’s quick, easy, and doesn’t require an
emotional investment, because when you send the government off to be
compassionate, it’s the government doing the work, not you.

Of course, trying to get the government to be compassionate
is kind of like trying to get a rock to sing. 
Compassion is not something that large bureaucratic entities generally
are good at.  They can set up programs,
they can throw money at the problem, but they can’t actually care.  Mitt Romney to the contrary, corporations
aren’t people, and neither are governments. 
They lack the capacity for the individual, emotional involvement in the
life of another.

As a result, government programs that aim to be
compassionate often don’t work very well. 
Giving more money to a school full of failing students usually results
in more expensive failures.  The biggest
problems in such situations are usually not in the schools, but in the
students’ homes.  Government intervention
in health care inevitably results in either an inflationary spiral (which is
what we’ve got now) or care rationing. 
Decades of well-intentioned government efforts to help have often ended
up harming the very people they were intended to aid.  The track record here is more than enough to
give the thoughtful student of politics pause.

And yet, there are still all of those passages that call the
disciple of Christ to compassion. 
As  Christians, we have to engage
with those somehow, or else we truly are the hypocrites the secular liberals
claim we are.  However, I don’t think
that engagement should happen at the ballot box.  I think it should happen everywhere else.

Rock-brained religious conservative that I am, I’m as
suspicious of Christians’ efforts to outsource compassion to churches as I am
of efforts to outsource it to the government. 
To obey the Lord’s command, we ourselves must be moved by compassion and
show mercy, remembering that the very nature of mercy is that it is help given
to those who don’t deserve it.

Sometimes, this is easy. 
In my experience, individual Christians are very good about helping
other Christians when they need financial assistance.  Sadly, though, if all we do is write a check,
that involves us little more than pulling a lever in a voting booth does, and
Christians often struggle with sharing their time, their energy, and their love
with forgotten members who need those things.

This is difficult even when we’re dealing with freshly
scrubbed folks in the pews.  It becomes
even more so when we’re facing those who are not in the pews and probably
aren’t particularly fresh at all. 
There’s been an intriguing series of studies ([like this one](http://research.vtc.vt.edu/news/2014/oct/29/liberal-or-conservative-brain-responses-disgusting/)) over the past several years that reports that the primary mental difference
between liberals and conservatives is that the latter are more prone to feel
disgust.  They see someone who is
ill-clothed and dirty or hear of someone doing something they believe is
morally wrong, and they’re more inclined to say, “Eww!  Gross!” than want to help.

There are many places where Christ calls us to overcome our
fleshly impulses, and I’m certain this is one of them.  It’s hard to lead the lost to Christ if
you’re emotionally repulsed by sinners. 
Believe me; that attitude will communicate itself!  Similarly, if we want the unwashed masses to
go take a shower before we’re willing to help them, we will be prone to
excuse-making rather than acts of mercy.

I don’t know the answer when it comes to politics.  The more I think about it, the more
conflicted I become.  However, I’m pretty
sure I know the answer when it comes to the way we should live.  In the apostle Paul’s words, we need to spend
and be spent for the sake of others. 
This isn’t about the government’s money. 
It’s not about the church’s money. 
It’s not even about our money, really. 
It’s about our lives, our determination as adoptive/foster parents, charitable
volunteers, and freelance do-gooders to make the world a slightly brighter
place for *somebody* than it was
before.  There’s no other way to be a
genuine disciple of Christ.
