Week 1 results:  zero
prayers, one compliment, two conversations with Starbucks management

Last week, I ran across [this post](http://www.ministrymatters.com/all/entry/6670/why-i-offer-free-prayer-in-a-coffee-shop) online.  As soon as I saw it, I said to myself,
“That’s something I want to try.”  Like
the original author, I’m not thrilled with hiding away in my office all week.  I believe Christians ought to be visible, and
we also ought to help others as we can.  There
are lots of things I’m not.  I’m not a
surgeon, a carpenter, or independently wealthy. 
However, I can offer compassion and prayer to anybody who wants them.



Yesterday, then, I gathered my courage, my Bible, and a
“Free Prayer” placard I had made and camped out at the local Starbucks at a
table by the door.  I was there from
10-12 in the morning.  My aim was simply
this:  if somebody wanted me to pray with
them, I would.  I didn’t speak unless
spoken to.  I didn’t have any
church-specific materials to distribute, or even anything that would identify
me as belonging to a particular church (though if asked, I would certainly not
hesitate to supply such identification). 
I worked on the lesson book for next quarter (Ezra, Nehemiah, and
Esther) and waited for somebody to say something.

About a half hour into my vigil, a woman stopped by my table
to say, “I really like your sign.  I
think that’s a great idea!”  I smiled and
thanked her, and she went on her way.

About an hour in, I received my next visitor, the manager of
the Starbucks.  With much discomfort and
apologizing, she informed me that some customers had complained about my
sign.  They found it “offensive”,
particularly given its prominent placement on a table by the door.  I offered to move to a less prominent table
if she thought that would be appropriate.

She didn’t.  Instead,
she opined that she was afraid that my sign was in violation of Starbucks
anti-solicitation policies (even though it wasn’t commercial activity and I
wasn’t talking to anybody).  She asked
me, politely, to take the sign down while she sought clarification from the
higher-ups.  Of course, I complied.

Twenty minutes later, she returned to the table and informed
me that I could put my sign back up (though she’s still supposed to call me
with the final word on whether my sign is allowable on Starbucks
premises).  My guess is that somebody at
Starbucks saw the potential for a lawsuit (not that I was ever going to sue
Starbucks), horrible publicity (admittedly, I was already planning how I would
tell the Internet that Starbucks made me take my sign down), or both.  I thanked her, replaced the sign, and
returned to my study of Ezra and Nehemiah until the time came to go home for
lunch.

This was an interesting experience, though not at all in the
way I had anticipated.  It illustrates
first of all the growing hostility in America toward religious expression.  I didn’t condemn anybody.  I didn’t even speak to anybody.  I and my sign were sitting there minding our
own business, but that bugged at least two people enough that they went to the
manager and complained about me.  What’s
more, the manager initially agreed that the complainers had a point.

I find this particularly interesting in light of what I
found on the Starbucks website once I got home. 
I located policies about online solicitation on Starbucks Internet
properties, but I didn’t find anything about even commercial solicitation
in-store, much less what I was doing, which was certainly non-commercial and
only tenuously soliciting.  However, I
did find this statement on diversity:

“Starbucks actively creates and promotes an environment that
is inclusive of all people and their unique abilities, strengths and
differences, and promotes diversity as a strategic and competitive business
advantage for the company. . . .  We
respect diversity in each other, our customers and suppliers and all others
with whom we interact. Our goal is to be one of the most inclusive companies
globally, working toward full equity, inclusion and accessibility for those
whose lives we touch.”

Perhaps there should be a footnote that reads, “But we’re a
little suspicious of Christians who offer to pray with people.”

As interesting as the socio-legal sidelights may be, the
fact remains that I didn’t accomplish what I wanted to accomplish—praying with
somebody who asked me to pray with them. 
Maybe this is just a doomed effort, original blogger to the
contrary.  However, I wonder if I could
get better results by changing one or more of the following:

**Clothing.** I went to Starbucks dressed in my normal
winter uniform:  quarter-zip sweatshirt,
jeans, work boots, and canvas Carhartt coat. 
I wanted to seem accessible and everyday-ish.  However, the original blogger, who does have
people ask him to pray with them, went for the opposite effect.  He showed up in clerical collar.  I don’t have a clerical collar, and indeed am
opposed to the idea of a clergy class generally, but I certainly could dress up
more.  Would shirt and tie make me look
more like somebody people wanted praying with them?

**Bible.** My wife thinks I’m crazy even to wonder
about this, but I’m a Bible geek and can’t help it.  I spent my two hours studying out of a small
brown Bible.  Once again, I hoped it would
be accessible and everyday-ish.  Would a
different Bible get more people in?

I could use the floppy black Cambridge Wide-Margin Reference
that’s my usual pulpit Bible.  I could
break out my black genuine-leather 2002 Foundation Side-Column Reference, which
is the archetype of what Mark Bertrand calls a Big Black Attack Bible.  Nothing says “authority” like a four-pound
NASB!

I could even deploy my dark-green Schuyler Caxton with the
art gilt and the dry-stamped Jerusalem cross on the cover.  It’s the high-churchingest Bible I own (until
you realize that it’s an NLT, and so contains phrases like “fat cats”).  Or do only Bible geeks pay attention to
Bibles?

**Location.**  It’s everything, or so the realtors say.  It might be that I’m in the wrong coffee
shop, and the only thing I’ll ever accomplish there is to drive down
year-over-year sales.  Should I try
relocating to a different one, maybe one on the premises of a hospital?  Or, conversely, do people need to get used to
my “Free Prayer” sign before they’ll start approaching me?

C’mon, Internet! 
Supply me with some opinions here. 
You’re supposed to be good at that.



Otherwise, tune in next Friday for the riveting Week 2
update of the “Free Prayer” experiment!
