The other day, I ran across [a blog post](http://www.hubcitychurchofchrist.org/resources/preachersblog/2016/11/01/when-going-to-church-stops-being-fun) by my brother Joshuah Ellis entitled “When Going to Church Stops Being
Fun”.  I think Josh makes a number of
good points in the article, but I also believe that he raises an even larger
issue that’s worth exploring.

Why is it that I go to church in the first place?

We live in a society that is big on entitlement.  The Me Generation of the baby boomers has
morphed into a Me Populace.  Americans
these days are most concerned about their rights and their privileges, and
their preeminent concern is to defend those privileges from the encroachment
of others.  How dare you do this to
me???  How dare you treat me like
this???  This attitude is so fundamental
to our worldview that most people aren’t even aware it exists.  They certainly don’t question it.

If I and my rights and my happiness are most important, it
makes perfect sense to expect that church should be fun:  “That preacher better be entertaining; I put
my money in the plate, after all!  The
singing better be good.  The congregation
better be friendly.  If everybody doesn’t
live up to my expectations, I’m going to take my religion elsewhere (or maybe
nowhere)!”  This perspective is
distressingly common.  If you don’t get
what you want out of church, why go?

Even though this perspective on religion seems obvious to
many, it’s far from the only one, and it’s far from a godly one.  Biblically speaking, Christians are not
called to spend much time dwelling on their rights and their privileges and
their entitlements.  Our lives are not
about what others owe us.  They are about
what we owe others.  Paul captures this
well when he says in Romans 8:12, “So then, brothers, we are debtors, not to
the flesh, to live according to the flesh.” (ESV)

None of us stand alone. 
God does not position us at the top of a pyramid in which everybody
else’s job is to make us happy.  Instead,
because of all the ways we have been blessed, we find ourselves at the center
of a web of obligations, and God expects us to define our lives according to
the way we fulfill those obligations.  In
my own case, I owe so much to so many that I will never pay down my debt, and
attending church services is only the smallest of the ways I try to make some return.  For instance:

* I owe my parents.  My
  mother and my father were/are righteous, godly people who unhesitatingly
  devoted themselves to my good the whole time I was growing up.  They gave me every earthly opportunity, they
  saw to it that I was at services every time the doors were open, and most of
  all, they lived lives that were without hypocrisy, wholly devoted to
  Jesus.  Both of them rejoice to see my
  faithfulness.  In return for all that,
  how could I possibly wound them by rejecting the Lord they love?
* I owe my teachers. 
  For decades, I have benefited (and continue to benefit) from the labor
  of Christians, some full-time preachers, most not, who earnestly studied the
  word so that they could help me understand it better.  The value of the time they spent and the
  message they proclaimed is incalculable. 
  If I were to abandon the church they served so well, all of them would
  be disappointed.
* I owe my friends.  I
  have close friendships with brethren all across the country, and to be honest,
  I don’t have any idea what I’ve done to deserve such relationships with such
  amazing people.  For decades, they’ve
  been a wellspring of encouragement and joy and love.  They brighten each day of my life.  If I walk out on the church, I’m walking out
  on them too.  I’m doing evil to those who
  have never done me anything but good.
* I owe my congregation. 
  These are people who have supported me financially, helped and
  encouraged me in my work, and been my spiritual family for almost 11
  years.  I matter to them.  A lot. 
  If I were to leave the faith, that would matter to them too.
* I owe my God.  I owe
  Him so much that it’s impossible to describe my debt in detail.  Even if none of my other debts existed, this
  one would be enough.  My Savior expects
  me to assemble with His people as an expression of my love for them and Him
  both.  Either I do, or I demonstrate why
  ingratitude is a sin.



Basically, I don’t assemble for myself.  I assemble for everybody else.  My moods may change, my tastes may vary, but
my sense of indebtedness does not.  Even
if I’m angry, afraid, or convinced I will get nothing out of assembling, my
emotions don’t alter my responsibility. 
Either I spend my life honoring those debts, or I prove myself ungrateful.

However, this hardly makes my spiritual life grim and
joyless.  Quite the opposite!  In the long term, selfishness never made
anybody happy.  Indeed, the current
American malaise is in many ways attributable to the current epidemic of
selfishness.  The more you take, the less
you have.

Instead, it is selflessness that brings joy, not its
opposite.  The New Testament reveals that
this principle can be carried to dizzying extremes.  As Paul says in Philippians 2:17, “But even
if I am being poured out as a drink offering upon the sacrifice and service of
your faith, I rejoice and share my joy with you all.” (NASB)  Paradoxically, those who are poured out as
drink offerings for others are happy drink offerings!

As a result, I find great joy in assembling.  It locates me as a human being.  I am there because of multitudes who loved me
and helped me.  While there, I love and
help as I am loved and am helped, and I am equipped to love and help others
whom I haven’t met yet.  It is in
selflessness that I find myself.

Is that “fun” like a summer blockbuster or a video
game?  Of course not, and I don’t expect
it to be.  It satisfies a far deeper need
than a craving for momentary pleasure. 
People who seek the Lord with His people because they hope to be
entertained will never find the happiness they’re looking for, either there or
anywhere else.  If, however, we assemble
because of what we owe and seek to give, it is precisely the joy that we aren’t
looking for that we will find.
