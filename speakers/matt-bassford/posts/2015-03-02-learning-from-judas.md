One of the passages we most commonly cite as evidence of the
importance of the Old Testament is 1 Corinthians 10:11.  Today, we understand that the history of the
New Testament is written for our instruction just as much as the history of the
Old Testament was, and we often look to the heroes of the faith under both
covenants for our examples and our inspiration.

However, in context, 1 Corinthians 10:11 isn’t actually
about the heroes of the faith at all.  It’s
about all the ways that the children of Israel failed to serve God in the
wilderness, so that God destroyed them for their disobedience.  In other words, the stories of the villains
of Scripture are written for our instruction and example too, and if we don’t
pay attention to them, we may follow in their disastrous footsteps.  Let’s look this morning, then, at the worst
villain of them all.  Let’s see what we
can learn from Judas.



**Judas the Apostle**

Of course, the story of Judas is not one of unrelieved
disaster from the start.  Instead, it
begins at a high point of spiritual attainment, with Judas as an apostle of
Jesus, one of the Twelve, no less.  The
gospels don’t have a whole lot to say about Judas specifically during the
ministry of Jesus, but we can be certain that the things that the Twelve as a
group saw were **WHAT HE SAW** too.  Look, for instance, at the story of Mark
10:46-52.  Of all of the many miracles of
Jesus, I chose this one because it is one of His last.  We don’t know precisely when Jesus called the
Twelve, nor whether all twelve were present for miracles such as the stilling
of the storm on the Sea of Galilee. 
However, we know for certain that all of them were here for this one,
because it takes place during Jesus’ final ascent to Jerusalem before His
death.

In simple terms, here’s what happens:  A blind beggar named Bartimaeus is sitting
beside the road begging as Jesus and a whole procession of people come trooping
by.  Bartimaeus figures out what is going
on, appeals to Jesus for help, and has his sight restored by the Messiah.  The fact that his name is attached to the
narrative probably indicates that he remained a disciple of Jesus and continued
to repeat this story.

As with so many miracles of Jesus, this one is public,
spectacular, and incontestable. 
Bartimaeus’s problems would have been well known to the people of
Jericho, yet all of a sudden, he can see. 
A miracle is the only explanation, and Judas stood right there and
watched it happen.  He may even have been
able to see the cataracts or whatever vanish from Bartimaeus’s eyes.  It seems incredible to us that a man who had
seen the mighty works of Jesus and knew—knew!—that He was from God would betray
Him in only a couple more weeks, but that’s exactly what happened.  Just as reading the Bible doesn’t make us
proof against temptation, so too witnessing miracles didn’t make first-century
Christians proof against temptation either.

However, **WHAT JUDAS
DID** is what makes his later betrayal of Jesus even more bizarre.  Look at Mark 6:7-13.  This, of course, comes earlier in Jesus’
ministry.  Mark’s account doesn’t include
names, but it is still clearly the story of the calling of the Twelve.  Jesus sent the apostles out to proclaim the
gospel, heal the sick, and cast out demons, and that’s exactly what all of them
did, which must have included Judas.  At
this point, Judas had miraculous spiritual gifts from the Holy Spirit, exactly
like the gifts given to the other eleven. 
What’s more, he used them!  Judas
himself worked miracles by the power of God, but then, perhaps two years later,
fell away so completely that he betrayed Jesus into the hands of His
enemies.  Not only were miracles
insufficient to produce lasting faith in those who saw them, but apparently,
they couldn’t even guarantee faithfulness in those who performed them!  This is mind-boggling to me, but it’s still
true.

If ever there was a cautionary tale for disciples today,
this is it.  I’ve talked before about the
possibility of preachers falling away, of elders falling away, of the most
impressive Christians we know falling away. 
How about an apostle with the power to work miracles falling away?  That is *definitely*an example written for our instruction! 
If Judas wasn’t safe from the devil, then how could any of us ever
consider ourselves safe?  Christianity is
not for the complacent, brethren!  No
matter how spiritually mature we may be, the devil still has a plan to bring us
down, and he will destroy us if we don’t guard against him.  We need to fear that possibility, fear it
enough never to miss a service, fear it enough to spend more time with the
Bible than we do with the television, and fear it enough to pray without
ceasing.  The danger is real!

Still, though, how could it possibly be that the devil got
to Judas?  The Bible gives us the answer
in a story that highlights **HIS FATAL
FLAW.** Let’s read John 12:1-8.  Mary the sister of Martha has just used an
expensive bottle of perfume, valued at 300 denarii, to anoint Jesus,
unwittingly for His burial.  Judas gets upset
about this and makes what sounds like a righteous objection.  That money could have been given to the
poor!  What a waste!  As other gospel accounts make clear, he
really lays into poor Mary for what she’s done. 
However, he’s not actually the least bit concerned about the poor.  He’s concerned about himself, because Jesus
had made him the treasurer of the disciples, and he embezzled from all the
money that passed through his hands.  However,
thanks to Mary, now he’s not going to get a cent of that 300 denarii, and he’s
spitting mad about it!  Jesus knows
what’s going on in Judas’s heart, naturally, but the Lord chooses not to reveal
His disciple’s thieving ways.  Instead,
He publicly defends Mary and publicly rebukes Judas.

This, friends, should remind us of the danger of having that
one part of our lives that belongs to the devil.  As was true for Judas, Satan might have a
grip on our hearts when it comes to money or material possessions.  He might have us when it comes to sex or
lust.  It could be drug or alcohol
abuse.  It could pride, even pride in our
spiritual attainments and how much better we are than everybody else.

It doesn’t matter.  It
doesn’t have to be everything.  It only
has to be that one thing, that one area where we refuse to surrender our lives
to the control of Jesus.  If we give
place to the devil in that one spot, he will surely use our weakness to destroy
us, just like he used Judas’ weakness to destroy him.

**The Betrayal**

The next part of our story concerns the actual betrayal of
Jesus by Judas.  It begins when Judas
enters into **A CONSPIRACY** with the
chief priests.  We’re going to use
Matthew’s account here, in Matthew 26:14-16. 
Note that Matthew tells this story, as does Mark, immediately after the
story of Mary anointing Jesus for burial. 
Even though no one gospel includes every piece of the story, it’s pretty
apparent what the sequence of events is. 
Judas sees 300 denarii slipping through his fingers, gets mad, yells at
Mary about it, but then gets slapped down in turn by Jesus.  Judas then goes straight to the chief priests
with an offer to betray Jesus for 30 pieces of silver.  I don’t think that’s a number that Judas
pulled out of thin air.  Instead, I think
it reflects the extent to which he felt comfortable embezzling.  30 pieces of silver, after all, is 10 percent
of 300 denarii.  Mary’s dramatic action
kept Judas from getting his cut up front, so now he’s going to get it this way.

We often have this mental image of Judas as some kind of
cold, reptilian schemer, but I think that’s exactly wrong.  This isn’t a cold-blooded act.  It’s a very hot-blooded one.  Judas has just been publicly humiliated by
his Master, who has chosen to side with a woman instead of with one of His
twelve closest followers.  As embezzlers
will, Judas has probably also talked himself into believing that he has a
rightful claim on the money in the poor box. 
He deserves his share.  He’s
entitled to it.  Here Mary is, wasting
what belongs to Judas, and Jesus actually praises her for it!  Based on what Judas does later, I don’t think
he intends for Jesus to die.  Instead,
he’s mad at Jesus, he wants to strike back somehow, and here’s a convenient way
to do it and get his money back all at the same time.  Judas is as much angry and dumb as he is
anything else.

To me, friends, that is chilling.  I don’t sit around cold-bloodedly plotting to
harm others.  But have I, in the past,
gotten mad about something I didn’t have any right to be mad about and lashed
out at somebody?  Has my sinful anger
then had consequences far beyond what I anticipated?  Oh, I’ve done that, all right, and I don’t
think I’m the only one in the room who has, either.  The problem with Judas isn’t how different
from us he is.  It’s how much like us he
is, and how easily we can find ourselves acting like him.

Next comes the surreal conversation between **JUDAS AND JESUS.** Let’s read together from John
13:21-30.  What an eerie scene this must
have been!  As we’ve already seen, Judas
hasn’t really thought this one through, but he’s taken the money, and now he’s
committed.  The other disciples are
equally clueless.  They’re aware that
Jesus is upset, that He believes someone is about to betray Him, but despite
the show with the piece of bread, they still don’t know what’s going on.  They think that Judas leaves for some good
reason.

In fact, the only one who fully understands the situation is
Jesus.  He knows that Judas has already
given himself to the devil, even if Judas doesn’t know that yet.  Jesus also knows that in a perverse sense, He
needs Judas, just as He needs Caiaphas, Pilate, and all the rest.  Without their willingness to work evil, the
crucifixion doesn’t happen, and the atoning sacrifice for the sin of mankind is
never offered.

As Jesus does at a couple of different points during His
trial, here too, He gives the final nudge necessary to keep things rolling by
handing the morsel of bread to Judas. 
Even though nobody else gets it, Judas certainly does.  Now he knows that Jesus knows what he’s been
up to.  At this point, Judas has two
alternatives.  He can fall down in front
of Jesus and beg for forgiveness, which would be another humiliation only a few
days after the previous one, or he can stay mad and follow through on his
commitment to the high priests.  The choice
is still there, but Judas chooses the devil over the Lord, as Jesus had known
he would.

Then, in effect, Jesus says to Judas, “Run along to your new
friends.  You’re through with Me.”  As the text says, Judas leaves
immediately.  That’s hardly
surprising.  If you’re Judas, would you
want to spend any more time in the presence of Jesus right now?  It’s hard to imagine what Judas could have
been feeling, but I would suspect that shocked, horrified, defensive, and still
angry are all part of the mix.

This leads directly to the final scene of betrayal in **GETHSEMANE.**Here, let’s go to Mark 14:43-46. 
There are a couple of things that strike me about this narrative.  The first is that it appears that in a sense,
Judas is the one in charge.  He’s the one
who knows where Jesus will be, and he’s the one who knows what Jesus looks
like.  All of this is happening at about
2 or 3 in the morning, so certainly none of the high priests are going to leave
their beds to go thrashing around in the darkness, and apparently they’ve told
the goon squad to grab the guy Judas indicates. 
Judas is having to earn his 30 pieces of silver.

Second, though, this has an amateurish feel to it, like
Judas has been reading too many spy novels. 
He could have simply gone up to Jesus, pointed, and said, “This is
Jesus.  Grab Him!”  Instead, Judas chooses to identify Jesus with
an honorific and a kiss.  It’s like he’s
somehow hoping to keep his role in the whole thing secret:  “Oh, hi, Jesus!  So nice to see You!  But look: 
it’s a whole mob of men coming to arrest You!  Wherever did they come from???”  Once again, Judas has not thought this thing
through.  He’s orchestrating the most
famous betrayal in human history, but he wants to avoid notice.

The impression that Judas leaves us with, then, is if not
sympathetic, at least pathetic.  He
thinks he’s smarter than everybody else, which is probably an embezzler trait
generally, but really, he’s a dupe.  He’s
the high priests’ dupe, but most of all, he’s the devil’s dupe.  There’s a lesson here too, for the times when
we think that we’re going to get away with sin in our lives because we’re
smarter than everybody else.  The fact
that we’re contemplating sin at all should lay that delusion to rest, but if it
doesn’t, we should remember Judas.  Satan
certainly played Judas for a fool, but he will be equally happy to play us for
fools too.

**The Consequences**

Because Judas allowed anger, greed, and pride to lead him
into being spectacularly evil, all kinds of consequences followed.  First, he had to face **HIS OWN REMORSE** and, to say the least, handled it badly.  Look at the story of Matthew 27:3-10.  First of all, this really makes you wonder
what Judas thought was going to happen, doesn’t it?  It’s like he expected the chief priests to
give Jesus a good stern talking-to and then let Him go.  Now, though, Judas realizes that he has
delivered the Messiah into the hands of people who are really, truly, and
actually going to kill Him.  He’s
appalled!

In reaction, he rushes to the chief priests and offers them
their money back, presumably in exchange for Jesus, as though Annas, Caiaphas,
and the gang are the customer-service department at Wal-Mart.  Of course, they aren’t the customer service
department at Wal-Mart.  Judas thought he
was so smart, but the chief priests are true master manipulators.   They’re much better at being sneaky and evil
than Judas ever dreamed of being. 
They’re playing for keeps.  This
is their big chance to get rid of Jesus, and they are certainly not going to
turn loose of Him until they have killed Him. 
One can only imagine their amused contempt for this slimy little traitor
who only now has figured out what he has done. 
They tell Judas to solve his own problems, which he does, sort of, by
throwing the money at them, going off, and hanging himself.

This is what happens, friends, when we allow sin to lead us
into dealings with those who are more practiced at evil than we are.  They’re going to use us, and they’re going to
wreck us.  I can’t help but think here of
the silly teenage girl who offers herself to her worldly boyfriend to prove how
grown-up she is.  He’ll happily take her
up on it, but he’ll always be in it for himself, not her, and when she gets
hurt, too bad.

It reminds me of the card-game saying, “If you can’t spot
the sucker in your first half-hour at the table, you’re the sucker.”  Unless we have made a long career of being
evil, when it comes to sin, we are always the suckers.  Like Judas, we may think we’re in control,
but soon enough, we will realize we were actually the puppet.  I think the devil gets a kick out of letting
us see what we’ve done, too late.

Next, we see **JUDAS’
REPLACEMENT.** Look at the story of
Acts 1:15-22.  Now, notice first of all
that the story of Judas’ end here is a little bit different.  Instead of hanging himself, here Judas is
described as falling and meeting the truly disgusting end of bursting
open.  However, I believe these accounts
are complementary rather than contradictory. 
I know this is gross to think about, but how hard and far would a
healthy man have to fall to split open on impact?  I think you’d have to fall out of an airplane
or off a skyscraper or something like that to hit with enough force—in other
words, nothing that would have been possible for Judas.  Instead, I think this happened after he was
already dead.  He went to the Potter’s
Field and hanged himself, but nobody cared enough about him to cut him down, so
his corpse just hung there until, well, it fell down and kinda splattered.  Yuck! 
According to Deuteronomy 21, a hanged man who was allowed to hang there
was accursed and defiled the land, and that reflects everybody’s opinion of
Judas.

Second in this story, we see the apostles begin to select
the one who will take Judas’ position among the Twelve.  Instead of being crushed by remorse as Judas
was, Peter and the rest have been galvanized by the joy of Jesus’
resurrection.  Judas has abandoned them
and gone to the place where he belongs, so somebody else needs to step up and
take Judas’ part in the ministry.

I think there are two important lessons here for us.  The first is that sin doesn’t make us friends
the way that righteousness does.  Sure,
there are plenty of people who will share in our sin with us, but by definition,
they aren’t truly concerned about us, and we can’t rely on them.  When we truly need help, their selfishness
will lead them away from us.  Second, it
shows that even if we abandon God’s purpose for our lives, that purpose will
still continue.  Even one of the twelve
apostles couldn’t defeat God’s plan by betraying Jesus into the hands of his
enemies.  God was still going to triumph,
and today, God is still going to triumph. 
When we forsake Him, all we do is ensure that we’re going to be on the losing
side.

Finally, let’s consider **THE
WARNING** that all of us should take from this.  I think part of us wants to believe that
Judas’ sin was unique, that none of us could alienate God and wreck our lives
so completely.  However, that’s not what
the Scripture says.  Consider Hebrews
6:4-6.  Judas, despite his pride, wasn’t
a big, spectacular, scary villain like Caiaphas.  Judas was only a little man, but his
little-man foolishness and greed led him to personally betray Jesus into the
hands of His enemies.

Today, we don’t have to be big, spectacular, scary villains
to betray Jesus either.  All it takes is
giving our lives over to everyday, contemptible sins like Judas did.  When we do this, when we fall away from
serving the Lord, we crucify Him again and exhibit Him to the contempt of the
wicked.  That’s not like speeding.  That’s not like accidentally writing a hot
check.  That’s a personal betrayal of
Jesus Christ, and we can be certain that He will take it personally and deal
with us accordingly.

In just a few moments, we’re going to partake of the Lord’s
Supper.  As we do, let’s first of all,
against this backdrop of betrayal and faithlessness, remember the faithfulness
of Jesus.  He stayed true to God’s plan
and to our need, even to the point of surrendering His life on the cross.  Jesus has never let down anyone who put their
trust in Him, and He never will.  Second,
though, let’s ask ourselves whether we are faithful to Him as He is to us.  Judas’ betrayal of Jesus isn’t frightening
because it’s impossible to understand. 
It’s frightening because it’s so easy to understand.  Let’s make sure that we never follow in his
footsteps.
