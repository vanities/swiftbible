*And Isaac loved Esau
because he ate of his game, but Rebekah loved Jacob.*

--Genesis 25:28

One of the fascinating things about the Bible is the sheer
range of human existence that it explores. 
God’s word reveals mankind at its greatest and most sublime, in the
death of Jesus on the cross, and plumbs the depth of human wickedness in places
too depressingly numerous to mention. 
However, not everything in Scripture is built on an epic scale.  Sometimes, even the foremost heroes of the
faith do things that are foolish, shortsighted, and petty.



So it is with the favoritism shown by Isaac and
Rebekah.  Isaac chose Esau to be his
favorite, for no better reason than because Esau was a skilled hunter.  Rebekah, on the other hand, preferred Jacob,
for no reason that is revealed in Scripture—perhaps only because she felt sorry
for him.  From these choices, made
without any particular forethought, all sorts of trouble arose.  Rebekah helped Jacob cheat Esau out of his
blessing, Esau hated his brother and sought to kill him, and when the time came
for Jacob to be a father, his favoritism toward his own son Joseph carried the
cycle of resentment forward into another generation.

It can be no less destructive today for parents to play
favorites.  This rarely comes out
directly in words, but it surely does in actions.  Children naturally look to their parents for
security, and every child carries around a logbook in his head of the parental
behavior that shows he is valued and loved. 
Sadly, children are also good at remembering the behavior that appears
to point out the opposite.  Even an
offhand comment about which child is prettier, or which child is smarter, will
form the basis of that child’s self-image, regardless of what the truth
is.  A beautiful woman whose father told
her she was plain may well never see that she is beautiful.

Of course, when these offhand comments are backed up by an
entire pattern of interaction, the consequences are even more profound.  I can think of more than one father of my
acquaintance whose comments about a particular son or daughter revealed that
that one was his favorite.  These were
intelligent men, and perhaps they failed to recognize the favoritism in
themselves, but what was obvious to me was surely obvious to their other
children too.  The realization that a
sibling is better loved can shatter any child’s psyche.  I’ve known Christians who fell away and blamed
their unfaithfulness on the favoritism shown to a brother.  I’ve talked to elderly Christians who still
harbored bitterness and resentment about the perceived preferential treatment
given to a sibling 70 years ago.

#In such cases, it’s impossible to sort out the truth about
who did what, but one thing remains certain: 
As parents, we must always keep favoritism in check.  Even if we are naturally inclined toward one
of our children, that must never become evident in our words or our
behavior.  Instead, we must love all of
our children impartially, just as God impartially loves us.
