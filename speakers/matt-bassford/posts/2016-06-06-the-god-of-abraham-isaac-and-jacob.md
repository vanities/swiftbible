Last week, I got into a discussion with a friend of mine
about one of the difficulties faced by non-native speakers of *koine* Greek as we try to delve down into
the textual intricacies of Scripture.  It’s too easy for us to build arguments around
subtle variations in verb tense, etc., that simply wouldn’t have meant much to
the original audience.

I identified this as the reason I prefer to preach from
larger rather than smaller sections of Scripture.  When you’re looking at a single verse, little
translation differences can make a big difference in meaning, but the larger
the context you’re looking at, the less significant those differences
become.  No matter what translation
you’re using, Romans 1 means the same thing.

In reply, he semi-sarcastically brought up the discussion of
the resurrection in Matthew 22, where Jesus supposedly hangs His argument on
the tense of a single verb.  It’s
commonly said that this is what Jesus is doing; in fact, I’ve said so
myself.  However, once I started looking
at Exodus 3, I started seeing something different.  Let’s consider, then, what Jesus is actually
drawing from the statement that God is the God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob.



**The Burning Bush**

The first section of our study concerns the first half of
Moses’ encounter with God at the burning bush, which appears in Exodus 3.  Within this section of the story, we must
first address **GOD’S SELF-REVELATION.** We read about it in Exodus 3:1-10.  Our children are familiar with the events of
this story.  Moses is out pasturing the
sheep, notices a bush that appears to be on fire, yet is not being consumed,
and goes to look at it.  Then, God speaks
to him from the bush.

However, this morning, I want to pay attention not to what
Moses does, but to what God says.  After
He tells Moses to take his sandals off, God’s words here divide into two main
sections.  In the first section, He
identifies Himself as the God of Moses’ father, the God of Abraham, Isaac, and
Jacob.  In the second section, He reveals
that He has heard the outcry of the Israelites, has resolved to redeem them
from Egypt and bring them to Canaan, and has chosen Moses to do it.

We’re very, very used to this story, so it’s hard for us to
see it, but there’s something here that doesn’t line up.  If we forget everything we know about the
Bible and take this story at face value, God’s announcement doesn’t make
sense.  Why in the world would a being of
presumably immense power trouble Himself to rescue a group of miserable slaves
from captivity?  I don’t concern myself
with the business of the ants in my backyard. 
Why is God concerning Himself with the business of the Israelites?

That’s a question that needs a good answer.  However, God doesn’t spell it out Himself,
nor does Moses, who objects to everything else, demand an answer.  He never says to God, “God, why should I
believe that You are going to go to such lengths to redeem us?”

Moses never asks this question because he knows God has
already answered it.  He can have
confidence that God will do this because God is the God of Abraham, Isaac, and
Jacob.  This is significant for two
reasons.  First, God has made many
promises to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and even to their descendants.  He will bless them and multiply them, He will
give them the land of Canaan, and in one of Abraham’s descendants, all the
nations of the earth will be blessed.

Second, and equally importantly, in all of God’s dealings
with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, He proved Himself to be faithful.  He made Abraham prosperous, He protected him
from his enemies, and He gave him a son through Sarah at a time when it should
have been impossible.  In short, the God
of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob is a God who makes promises and fulfills them.

However, none of this forestalls **MOSES’ OBJECTIONS.** Let’s
keep going in Exodus 3:11-22.  In this
part of our reading, Moses asks two questions. 
First, he wants to know who he is that he should accomplish such a
redemption.  God replies that it doesn’t
have a thing to do with Moses, but it has everything to do with God.  Just as God was faithful to Moses’ fathers,
He will be faithful to Moses, and Moses will see it when he returns to Sinai
with the whole Israelite nation.

The second question that Moses asks is, again, one of the
things about this text that doesn’t make immediate sense.  First of all, of all the things Moses could
have asked, why does he want to know about God’s name?  Second, why does Moses put the question in
the mouths of the people of Israel rather than simply asking God for his name
directly?

I think that in order to understand what’s going on here, we
have to interpret the question in light of the answer.  This is a long answer.  God doesn’t simply say “Yahweh” and have
done.  Presumably, He knew that such a
long answer was necessary to answer Moses’ real question.

This tells us, then, that Moses isn’t interested in God’s
name.  He’s interested in God’s name as
an insight into His nature.  Moses is
attributing to the people the same question God has already answered for
him.  Who is this God, that we can count
on Him to deliver us?

Logically speaking, God’s reply begins with His declaration,
not once but twice, that He is the God of the Israelites’ fathers, the God of
Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.  As we’ve
already seen, God’s interactions with the patriarchs define His nature and
character.  From this starting point,
though, God announces that He is I AM WHO I AM. 
Our nature is changing and inconstant. 
I can treat my wife wonderfully on her birthday and be a total jerk to
her a day later.  God, though, does not
change and is perfectly constant.  His
nature is utterly certain.

God concludes His answer by pointing out what this certainty
about His nature means for the Israelites. 
Because God is perfectly faithful, they can be sure about what He will
do for them.  He will bring them to
Canaan.  He will overpower the resistance
of the Egyptians.  He will even see to it
that they’re enriched on their way out the door.  He couldn’t do anything else and still be
God.

**Finishing the
Definition**

In the first part of Exodus 4, we see God finishing His
definition of Himself.  The first part of
this is about Moses’ request for **SIGNS.** Consider Exodus 4:1-9.  Here, Moses raises an objection that, for the
first time, strikes us as a reasonable objection.  No matter how great God is, how can he prove
to the people that this great God appeared to him?  In answer, God gives him three signs to
perform:  a staff that turns into a
serpent, a hand that becomes leprous or is healed as Moses puts it inside his
cloak, and turning some water from the Nile to blood.  These things are awfully penny-ante compared
to the upcoming ten plagues, but they’ll be enough for the Israelites.

Notice, though, what it is that God says these signs will be
enough to demonstrate.  They’ll be enough
for the Israelites to believe that the God of their fathers, the God of
Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, has appeared to Moses.  This is the fourth and final appearance of
our formula in the story of the burning bush. 
This is important.  With its first
appearance, God identified Himself as the God who had kept His promises to the
fathers.  With its second and third
appearances, God revealed that this promise-keeping wasn’t a fluke, but in fact
was bound up with the very essence of His nature.  Now, with its fourth appearance, God is
emphasizing that they can know that this perfectly faithful God will be with
them.

Interestingly, this is not only the last appearance of this
phrase in the context.  It is the last
appearance of the phrase in the entire Bible. This story is quoted elsewhere,
but God never again identifies Himself in this way.  His proof of Himself is now perfect and
complete.

This takes us, then, to **THE
STORY’S CONCLUSION.** Look at Exodus
4:10-16.  In the last act, Moses has two
more things to say to try to keep God from sending him to Egypt.  The first is another obstacle that Moses
thinks is insurmountable.  God wants
Moses to talk to the Israelites and then to the Egyptians, but the reality is
that Moses is a rotten speaker.  God
knows perfectly well that he can’t do this. 
In reply, God answers that He knows no such thing.  Now that He has proven who He is, Moses must
acknowledge that He is the Creator too. 
Surely, the One who designed and made the human mouth can get Moses’
mouth to work right!

After this, Moses is out of reasonable objections, so he
outright tells God that he wants God to send someone else.  At this point, not surprisingly, God finally
becomes angry at Moses.  He tells him to
take his brother Aaron, who will do much of the speaking for him, and hit the
road.

There are a couple of important applications of this
story.  I’ll leave it to Jesus to reveal
the big application in the next section, but there’s a second and smaller one
that we need to consider.  There’s a
sense in which all of us find ourselves standing barefooted next to Moses at
the beginning of v. 10.  God has revealed
Himself to us in a perfect and complete way. 
He has called us to do a number of things, at least some of which we
don’t want to do.  It could be that, like
Moses, we aren’t terribly interested in carrying His word to others.  It could be that we would rather be worldly
than godly.  It could be that there is
some sin that we want to cling to.

Regardless, there is certain to be at least one place in our
lives where God says “Do this”, and at least part of us is saying
“Unh-unh”.  What are we going to do about
it?  Like Moses, are we eventually going
to swallow our objections and get going? 
Or, instead, are we going to object to the point where God gets mad at
us too and keep going in our objections? 
That’s an important, indeed life-defining question.  We’re going to have to answer it.  When we do, let’s be sure to answer it from
an awareness of the nature of God.

**Jesus on Exodus**

Now that we’ve worked our way through the whole story of the
burning bush, let’s flip to the New Testament to see what Jesus’ take on the
story from Exodus actually is.  Let’s
begin here by looking at **THE SADDUCEES’
QUESTION** in Matthew 22:23-28.  This
story is actually found in all three Synoptic Gospels, in Mark 10 and Luke 20
as well as Matthew.  However, the first
half of each account reads basically the same, so we can stick with Matthew’s
account and get the point.

This is obviously not a question asked in good faith.  Instead, this is part of the intense
controversy between Jesus and the ruling elite of the Jews that runs throughout
the week preceding His crucifixion.  That
Monday, Jesus had cleansed the temple, putting a big dent in the Sadducee high
priests’ bottom line and accusing them of corruption into the bargain.  At this point, the Jewish religious leaders
are determined to destroy Jesus by any means possible.  They’ll take Him and kill Him if they can,
but they’re afraid of the crowds.  As a
result, they shift to Plan B, which is to take advantage of the publicity of
Jesus’ ministry and somehow discredit Him in the eyes of the people.

This tortured hypothetical about the woman with seven
husbands, then, is not a serious search for truth.  It’s a question intended to embarrass Jesus
by showing the falsity of His beliefs about the resurrection.  The Sadducees’ implied argument is that since
a resurrection would lead to one woman being married to seven brothers simultaneously,
there can’t be a resurrection.

Let’s first look at **JESUS’
ANSWER** to this question in Matthew. 
It appears in Matthew 22:29-33. 
This is a two-part answer.  The
first part, with which we’re not really concerned this morning and which is
substantially the same in all three Synoptic Gospels, serves to dispose of the
Sadducees’ hypothetical.  In the
resurrection, one woman won’t end up married to seven brothers because there is
no marriage in heaven.  Luke further adds
that there will be no marriage because people will be immortal, therefore
having no need for reproduction.

In Part 2, though, Jesus affirmatively proves the
resurrection by arguing from Exodus 3. 
God has said He is the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, He is not the
God of the dead but of the living, so the resurrection must be true.  There are two possibilities for reading
this.  The first is that Jesus is leaning
on the verb tense, taking Exodus 3:5 out of context to prove that God’s offhand
self-identification is spiritually significant.

The second, though, is that Jesus, far from picking a phrase
out of context, is actually relying on the whole argument of Exodus 3 to make
His point.  As we’ve seen, God’s repeated
identification of Himself as the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob is more than
a simple statement of fact.  It’s a claim
to faithfulness based on His faithfulness to those three men.  Jesus’ argument would be, then, that such a
faithful God cannot be the God of the dead. 
If God promised to bless Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and then allowed
their extinction, He would have failed to keep faith.  God’s very nature, then, demands that the
dead are still living and one day will be raised.

It’s certainly true that Matthew’s account allows for either
of these possibilities.  However, **JESUS’ ANSWER IN LUKE** is very
different.  Let’s read from Luke
20:37-40.  Here, we see something that
hopefully all of us have noticed before about the Synoptic Gospels.  Even when they are telling the same story,
they don’t tell it in exactly the same way. 
They don’t quote Jesus as saying exactly the same thing.  This is not a contradiction; instead, it
tells us something important about what Matthew, Mark, and Luke are doing.  They’re not following Jesus around with a
tape recorder capturing His words precisely. 
Instead, they’re capturing the meaning and the sense of His
arguments.  What we have in Scripture is
what God wants us to get out of what Jesus said.

This tells us, then, that if we’re considering the same
story in two or three different gospels, and our reading of it works in one
gospel but not another, we’re probably missing the point.  Let’s see how this works with respect to the
verb-tense argument from Matthew 22.  In
Luke’s account, the verb-tense argument doesn’t work because the verb isn’t
even present.  What is there, though, is
the identification of God as the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God
of Jacob.  Luke’s read on Jesus’ reply,
then, is that it is based on the proven faithfulness of God.

However, there’s another implication to Jesus’ answer
here.  If God is utterly faithful to His
promises, that doesn’t only mean that Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob will be raised
from the dead.  It means that Jesus will
be raised from this dead.  Jesus’ words,
therefore, don’t only function as a refutation of the Sadducees.  They function as a prophecy and a
warning.  Jesus knows that these men are
plotting to kill Him, and in about three days, they’re going to succeed in
doing so.  He wants them to understand
that when they kill Him, He won’t stay dead, because God will not allow His
Holy One to undergo decay.  God is
faithful, so Jesus will rise.

Before the Lord’s Supper, let’s consider the death, burial,
and resurrection of our Lord in this light. 
Let’s remember His perfect trust in the faithfulness of God, so much so
that He was willing even to face death for His Father’s sake.  Let’s remember God’s triumphant proof of His
faithfulness in raising Jesus from the dead. 
Let’s consider the sure hope of eternal life that His death and
resurrection give us as we partake of His supper.
