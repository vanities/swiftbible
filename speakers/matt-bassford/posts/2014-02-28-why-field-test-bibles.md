Since I became interested in physical Bibles, I’ve spent far
too much time scouring the Internet for every Bible-review blog that I can
track down.  I find them to be fascinating reading, and I've learned a tremendous amount from them.  I’m sure that all these
bloggers spend a lot of time with the Good Book; a Bible reviewer who doesn’t
read the Bible is like a car collector who doesn’t have a driver’s
license.

However, with a few exceptions, they don’t talk much about the experience of using a
particular Bible.  These reviews are
careful to specify double-column versus single-column, verse-by-verse versus
paragraphed, goatskin versus calfskin, and so forth, but they won’t tell me,
“This Bible is good for reading but crummy in the pulpit.”  They certainly won’t invest themselves in an
extended, consistent analysis of why.


If there is anything I hope to add to the Bible-review
universe, it is this.  I’m not a Bible
collector.  I’m a Bible user, and I
believe that Bibles are meant to be used, just like cars are meant to be
driven.  If a car reviewer only focuses
on how a car looks sitting in the driveway, he has missed the essence of what
makes that car good or bad.  Likewise, if
we consider only the form of a Bible and not how it functions, we miss the
essence of what makes a Bible worth buying, or not. 

Sure, we can speculate on how well a Bible will fulfill a
particular role.  We can say, “Books for
reading are single-column, but that Bible is double-column, so it won’t be good
for reading.”  I find that
some double-column Bibles are indeed bad readers, but some are quite good, at least in
the three-quarters of the Bible that is prose. 
Generalizations about Bibles often get into trouble in the particulars.

Instead, the best way to tell whether a Bible is a bad or
good reader is to. . . read it, steadily, over a period of weeks, so that its
good and bad characteristics may come to light. 
Experience may well contradict theory, and in the end, it is experience
that matters.

In the Fridays to come, then, I aim to provide my
experiences.  I’m a preacher; Bibles are
the tools of my trade.  I use a Bible
every day, often for hours at a time, for all the things for which Bibles may
be used.  Well, I don’t use them for
doorstops much, but other than that, my pattern of use is comprehensive.  I use them for preaching, teaching, and
study.  I read from them.  I take them on short visits and long
vacations.  Anything I don’t do with a
Bible probably isn’t worth doing.

All of those different uses require different things from a
Bible.  A full-size black-leather monster
may shine in the pulpit, but it probably isn’t the best Bible to toss into the
seat next to me as I go to visit a shut-in. 
Likewise, a petite compact Bible may be the best choice for visiting,
but not for preaching.  However, I’m
going to preach out of the compact and visit with the monster, because both
unlikely successes and unlikely failures emerge when I actually test what a Bible
can do.

Not all my experiences will be universally
generalizable.  Some probably are;
anybody who chooses to use a Bible with 4.5-point font for a daily reader is
likely either Superman or a masochist. 
However, not everybody is going to share my distaste for reading out of
single-column Bibles with a line length in excess of 60 characters. I can only look at Bibles out of my own eyes; somebody else's eyes may well see something different.

Nonetheless, I hope that those whose preferences differ from
mine will still find my test results to be of interest.  My goal is not merely to explain what happened,
but why I think it happened.  That way,
even those who are looking for different things in a Bible than I am will be
able to determine whether an edition I don’t care for will fill their unique needs.

If this fussing over details seems trivial, the goal
assuredly is not.  Bibles are to be used,
and the Bible that best suits a particular use is the Bible that we should
use.  Otherwise, the format of the word
becomes an obstacle to its content.  The
right Bible will make preaching easier, reading more rewarding, and travel more
spiritually beneficial.  If indeed the
Bible is important to us, choosing the right Bibles for our purposes ought to
be important too.
