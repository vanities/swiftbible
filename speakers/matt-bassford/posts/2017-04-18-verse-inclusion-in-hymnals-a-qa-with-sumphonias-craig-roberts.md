Recently, it seems like there’s been a fair amount chatter
online about which verses of a hymn are included in a particular hymnal.  Why leave some out while putting others in,
especially when the decision of previous hymnal editors may have been
different?

This is an important question, especially when it applies to
hymns that we sing regularly.  I have
opinions (surprise, surprise!), but rather than sharing my inexperienced $.02,
I thought it would be better to get Craig Roberts’ take on the matter.  Craig actually has helped edit a hymnal, and
I figured those who use or are considering *Psalms,
Hymns, and Spiritual Songs* (hereinafter *PHASS*)
might be interested to find out some of what went on behind the scenes during
its production.

In the interests of full disclosure, I have worked with
Sumphonia on a number of projects, including *PHASS*, and I will doubtless do so again.  However, I have never had nor will have any
financial dealings with them.  Also, I
agree with them about a lot of things, but certainly not about everything!

My comments and questions are in **boldface**.  Craig’s replies
are in normal Roman type.



**MB:  I wasn’t involved in the verse-inclusion
debates during the production of *PHASS*,
but when I consider the finished product, it’s obvious that the editors had a
strong pro-inclusion bias.  For instance,
“Abide With Me” went from three verses in *Hymns
for Worship (Revised)* to six in *PHASS.* What was the editorial philosophy behind
this?**

CR: The editorial philosophy of PHASS was the standard philosophy
used in professional publications.  It
was nothing fancy, really.  At the core
of this philosophy is the role of editor, which in this publication refers to a
decision editor with similar duties to those of a Senior Editor or
Editor-in-Chief.  The editor must 1)
represent the author and 2) protect the integrity and quality of the publication.  The editor must also understand the
readership.

Regarding inclusion of stanzas in PHASS, the editors
followed several criteria.  Some stanzas
were included because they were vital to the understanding of the hymn.  An example of this is “Nearer, My God, to
Thee,” a hymn whose biblical meaning cannot be fully realized without those stanzas
that borrow from Genesis 28.

Other stanzas were included when they expressed a unique
thought in poetic style.  Examples of these
are the 4th stanza of “Stand Up, Stand Up, for Jesus,” the 3rd
third stanza of “Be Still My Soul,” and many others.  Including these stanzas increased the content
of PHASS without the addition of another hymn, and their quality enabled Christians
to use the hymnal to teach and admonish one another effectively.

In addition to these criteria, stanzas were included when
they captured a biblical thought.  Look
at the stanzas reintroduced into “Abide with Me,” one of which paraphrases I
Corinthians 15, or into “How Firm a Foundation,” which quote passages in Isaiah.  Look also at “'Tis Midnight and on Olive's
Brow,” which includes a beautiful description of Emmanuel alone in the garden
of Gethsemane; it points out the sorrowful irony that at that moment: “God with
Us” was not with us.  He was alone with
an angel.

**MB:  But isn’t six verses of “Abide With Me” or
five verses of “Ah, Holy Jesus” a lot for a congregation to sing?**

CR:  Yes, six stanzas can
be a lot to sing without a “reboot” (stopping to reestablish the pitch and
tempo).  Typically, a congregation will sing
lower and slower as they sing a particular hymn, especially if the music is
slow or the text is lengthy.

Regardless, the editor must fulfill the role of editor—representing
the author while protecting the quality of publication.  If six stanzas represent the hymnist better
than three or four, and if they improve the quality of the hymnal, why not publish
them?  Let each congregation and the worship
leader decide which ones to sing.

Conversely, why keep these stanzas away from the
congregation and the worship leader?  I
speak for many who are now singing these recovered stanzas—I would miss them
dearly, should they be placed back in the archives.

Along these lines, where do we get the idea that what is
published is what must be sung?  As
stated in the Preface of PHASS, “Leaders ... can select the most meaningful
stanzas in a given hymn to focus the message ... [and] should feel no
obligation to sing all stanzas of a hymn simply because the authors wrote them
and the editors published them.”

The production goal of PHASS was to publish a robust
resource of superb content.  The
robustness is caused in part by the stanzas reintroduced, which enables the worship
leader to prepare an effective service.

**MB:  What about circumstances when one verse may
not be quite as strong as others in the hymn? 
For instance, I’m not a huge fan of the final verse of “God of the
Living, in Whose Eyes”.  What’s the
rationale for including such verses?**

CR:  In the production
of PHASS, each stanza was evaluated on a case-by-case basis.  At some point, a judgment call was made by consensus
of five editors. Because all five editors had training in both Bible doctrine
and English hymnody, and because they were independent thinkers and willing to
disagree, I have confidence in those judgment calls.

Regarding the stanzas of “God of the Living,” I don’t
remember the specific discussion.  But discussing
it here will provide a glimpse into the process of stanza selection.

Looking at the hymn today, I can say what I probably said
when these stanzas were under review 5 or 6 years ago—the last stanza is similar
to the first stanza in terms of criteria. 
On the downside, it inverts phrases that can create ambiguity, hopefully
fleeting ambiguity.  But on the upside,
it employs inspired thinking and even inspired wording from Gen 2:7 and Rev
1:18.  It uses wording from the first
book of the Bible (“Breather of Breath”) to form a couplet with wording from
last book of the Bible (“Holder of Keys”); the resulting couplet bookends to
the human, life-to-death experience.

Stanza 5 is typical of Ellerton—rich in content but sometimes
contorted in wording.  Personally, do I
prefer the Ellerton style?  No.  I strongly prefer direct sentence structure, which
facilitates thought-based singing in the congregation.  But when I set stanza 5 alongside the others
in “God of the Living,” I would vote in favor of including it.

Having said that, I would understand if others voted the
opposite.  And I would be okay with the
final vote tally, even if it went against my personal vote, because of the training
and independence of these particular editors.
