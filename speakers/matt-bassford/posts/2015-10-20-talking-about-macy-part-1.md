I’m writing this post at the urging of a friend who
Facebooked last week about her own experience with miscarriage.  Apparently, the 15th of this month
was Pregnancy Loss Awareness Day. 
There’s a sense, then, in which my own writing about stillbirth isn’t
timely, but there’s a much deeper sense in which it is and always will be.



Our increasingly godless society likes to pretend that we live
in an earthly paradise where tragedy never comes.  If earth is heaven, then we have no reason to
long for heaven.  Of course, despite all
our wealth and understanding, tragedy still comes.  Earth is not heaven after all.

Rather than acknowledging the point, we prefer to dismiss
the evidence.  We hide the old and the
sick away in nursing homes and hospitals. 
Talk of suffering and death is taboo, particularly when the suffering is
particularly shocking and “unnatural”, as in the case of pregnancy loss.  The old inevitably die, but babies never
should.

This, I think, is what drives those who ask the woman still
suffering from her miscarriage, “Haven’t you moved on yet?”  Though the question is framed as an
indictment of the sufferer (she’s self-pitying; she’s weak), it says much more
about the questioners.  The pain that
truly bothers them is their own, the pain that comes from confrontation with an
unpleasant reality that they would rather avoid.

I like to believe that I was never so foolish, but whatever
illusions I may have cherished on the subject were shattered on September 19th,
2008.  That’s when my daughter Macy was
stillborn.  She was full-term, a little
past full term, actually.  Her death was
entirely unexpected.  My wife had gone
through all the testing and the monitoring that modern medical science
recommends, but there wasn’t even a hint of disaster until disaster came.  We have never learned why Macy died.  I don’t anticipate that we will ever find
out, at least not this side of Jordan.

To say that I have thought about this would be an
understatement.  It’s one of the defining
events of my life, and by nature, I’m a navel-gazer anyway.  I have thought and thought and thought and
thought, processed and processed and processed. 
I believe, though I may be mistaken, that I’ve reached the point of
peace with my daughter’s death.  I feel
like I’ve answered the questions that arose (and there are many, many
questions) in ways that satisfy me logically and intuitively.

I certainly did not reach this point on my own.  I benefited from the wisdom and love of
countless friends.  Most of all, I
benefited from the presence of God in my life, who is indeed a very present
help in time of trouble.

Indeed, I don’t think I would have reached this point on my
own.  If I had turned in instead of out,
I think that even now, seven years later, I would have found at most an
imperfect peace.  I might have been able
to lock the demons up, but I wouldn’t have been able to banish them, and there
are times in everyone’s lives when all the doors of the heart spring open.

I say, then, to those of you who are still suffering from
miscarriage, stillbirth, or even the death of an infant or small child:  I get where you are.  Everybody’s story is different.  Everybody’s story has its own uniquely
horrible particulars.  My experience
probably doesn’t line up with yours on every point, but I still think there is
a sameness to all of our experiences—that sense of something that ought to have
been one way going terribly and dramatically the other way.  I still hear silences sometimes when my
seven-year-old daughter should have been laughing.

There is a richness to this, of love and sorrow and loss
exquisitely blended, and I don’t think its fullness can be borne alone.  Peace has to be found not around, but
through.  If that’s where you are, in the
middle of through, or even in front of through, and you want to talk, I’m here
to listen.

I don’t have any platitudes or easy solutions, but if you
like, I might have some light to shed on the path before you.  Whatever I’ve got is yours for the
asking.  For those of you who want but
won’t ask, I intend to write in further posts about some of the things I’ve
learned.  There may be a lot of them; I’m
a writer, and sometimes it takes me 1500 words to say “Good morning.”

Ultimately, though, I believe that your answers can’t come
from me.  They have to come from God, who
is the only answer there can ever be to human suffering.  As Abraham Lincoln said in his farewell
address to the people of Springfield, “Trusting in Him who can go with me, and
remain with you, and be everywhere for good, let us confidently hope that all
will yet be well.”  To that same God, who
is still everywhere for good, I commend you. 
May His peace be with you, and may you find your peace in Him.
