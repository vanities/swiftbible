I’m pretty tough to outrage these days.  Nonetheless, against all odds, [this article](https://www.harvardmagazine.com/2020/05/right-now-risks-homeschooling) did the trick.  It summarizes a
law-review article by a Harvard professor, Elizabeth Bartholet, in which she
argues that children have a right not to be homeschooled, and that indeed, the
government should prohibit homeschooling.



As a homeschooling parent, I certainly support the rights of
other parents to send their children to public school if they wish, or to
private school for that matter. 
Homeschooling is not the right answer for everyone.  However, when anyone suggests that my choice
as a parent is illegitimate and should be illegal, I’m likely to get a trifle
tetchy about that.

Ms. Bartholet begins her argument by observing that in much
of the country, homeschooling is essentially unregulated.  In her view, this gives rise to two primary
ills.  First, parents can do a poor job
of educating their children.  Second, the
lack of oversight can allow abusive parents to hide their maltreatment of their
children from the authorities.  In both
of these cases, “can” is a very important word because, due to the lack of
regulation, no one, least of all Ms. Bartholet, has any idea how frequently
these problems occur.

Concerning the first, yes, it certainly is thinkable that
some homeschooling parents might educate their children poorly.  However, we should put this in the context of
the widespread failures of the public-school system, in which a significant
percentage of children (particularly those in impoverished districts) receive a
bad education every year.  Some
homeschoolers might be poorly educated. 
Should we “solve” the problem by putting them in a setting where some of
them are certain to be poorly educated?

As for the second, it looks like a classic
safety-over-freedom argument.  Since some
may be abusing their freedom (again, no one knows how often this is happening),
we should take away freedom from everyone so that no one can abuse it.  We need to subject millions of homeschooling
families to intrusive scrutiny so that, perhaps, some homeschooling abusers
might be unmasked.

Such a regime hardly would eliminate abuse of homeschooled
children, any more than public schooling has eliminated abuse of publicly
schooled children.  However, it would open
a Pandora’s box of regulatory overreach. 
Once the government starts deciding who can and cannot homeschool, it
won’t be long before those decisions start reflecting the values of. . . the
likes of Ms. Bartholet.  “Oh, those awful
parents, clinging to their guns and their God and teaching their children to do
likewise!  Why, that’s practically
abusive!  We need to put a stop to
that!!!”

Speaking of God, it’s clear that Ms. Bartholet has an axe to
grind here as well.  She postulates that
as many of 90 percent of homeschooling parents are conservative Christians who
homeschool for religious reasons. 
According to the article, “Bartholet notes
that some of these parents are ‘extreme religious ideologues’ who question
science and promote female subservience and white supremacy.”

Note the progression of the
ad-hominem here.  Homeschooler = conservative
Christian = flat-earther = chauvinist = racist. 
I don’t think this tells us very much about homeschoolers (or
conservative Christians, for that matter), but it reveals a great deal about
Ms. Bartholet.

She believes that the children of
such people need to be educated, but not merely in a
reading-writing-and-‘rithmetic sense. 
Quoth she, “[I]t’s also important that children grow up exposed to
community values, social values, democratic values, ideas about
nondiscrimination and tolerance of other people’s viewpoints.”  To translate, Ms. Bartholet doesn’t like the
values we homeschooling parents are teaching our children, so she wants to take
them away from us and send them to public school, where they can learn the
values that she believes are important.

Frankly, that’s the single best
argument against public schooling that I’ve ever seen.  If Ms. Bartholet thinks that public schools
are that good at replacing the values of the parents with the values of the
educational system (values that she believes are better), the last thing in the
world that I want to do is to give them the chance!

After fulminating against the
insidious power of the HSLDA for a paragraph or two (side note:  never before in my life have I felt so
strongly motivated to send the HSLDA a check), the article concludes by
conceding that yes, parents do have some rights in deciding how to raise their
children.  I find myself overwhelmed by
Ms. Bartholet’s generosity here.

However, lest we get any ideas,
she goes on to say, “The issue is, do we think that parents should have 24/7,
essentially authoritarian control over their children from ages zero to 18?  I think that’s dangerous.”  Yes, dangerous.  Unlike human governments, which never are
dangerous.  Unlike the schemes of ivory-tower
utopians, which never are dangerous either.

Government intrusion into the most
private and important decisions that families make surely always will be
benign.  Isn’t that what history
shows?  Homeschooling, on the other hand,
is an evil that must be feared and eradicated.

The above quotation concludes, “I
think it’s always dangerous to put powerful people in charge of the powerless,
and to give the powerful ones total authority.”

Yes, Ms. Bartholet.  Exactly.
