One of the nice things about having Facebook friends from a
wide range of religious backgrounds is that periodically, an article pops up on
my news feed with which I disagree passionately.  This is important for every Christian.  If the only challenges to our convictions
that we ever encounter are the strawmen that the teacher sets up in Bible
class, how will we ever learn how to respond to error, or even whether we believe
the truth?

In my case, [this](http://www.drivennails.com/for-everyone/protestant-sacramentalism-an-urgent-call-to-rid-our-churches-of-a-subtle-and-damning-plague872017) is the article I encountered.  It’s long,
so I don’t have time to go through it point-by-point.  In summary, though, the author argues that
many Protestants who think they believe that they are saved by grace actually
believe that they are saved by works.  In
this, they have fallen into the same trap as the Catholics, who also think they
believe they are saved by grace, but actually believe they are saved by
works.  He calls this “Protestant sacramentalism” and compares it to nothing less than the Black Death, which,
admittedly, is fresher rhetorically than the *reductio ad Hitlerum*, though equally tendentious.



To be honest, I have trouble coming squarely at the
arguments in the article because the author’s definitions are wrong.  Most prominently, he takes “works” to mean,
in his own words, “anything you accomplished”. 
Admittedly, there are times in Scripture when “works” takes that
meaning, as in James 2:17, where James observes, “Faith by itself, if it does
not have works, is dead.”  James
certainly believed that the presence or absence of works has an eternal impact
on our salvation!

However, the author of the article wants to impose this
definition of “works” in places where it does not apply, particularly to the
writings of Paul.  However, Paul is very
clear that when he speaks of works in a negative sense, he’s talking about something
much more specific.  He spells it out in
Galatians 2:16, “We know that a person is not justified through works *of the law* but through faith in Jesus
Christ,” and again in Romans 3:28, “For we hold that one is justified by faith
apart from works *of the law*.”
(emphasis mine)

Contextually, Paul isn’t referring to the works of any
generic law (Hammurabi’s Code, the Twelve Tables, etc.), but to the works of a
particular law—the Law of Moses.  He
spends Galatians 2:11-14 complaining about Peter’s hypocritical insistence that
Gentiles have to live like Jews, and he uses the bulk of Romans 3 to prove that
the Law of Moses never saves anyone because no one perfectly keeps it.  In short, Paul is not opposing faith in
Christ to doing anything.  He’s opposing
faith in Christ to earning salvation by perfectly keeping the Law of
Moses.  That’s what faith-not-works means
in Romans and Galatians, and even though Paul doesn’t spell out the whole
argument again in Ephesians, it’s what he’s talking about in Ephesians 2:8-9
too (notice the Romans-esque description of sinful mankind in 2:1-3).

Clearly, Christians today should not seek to justify
themselves by perfectly keeping the Law of Moses (especially by accepting
circumcision, which was Paul’s particular concern).  However, to move from that conclusion to the
conclusion that our salvation is completely independent of anything that we
might do requires a mighty leap of logic.

The biggest problem with this position is that the writers of
the New Testament don’t agree with it. 
They consistently argue, as the author points out, that we are saved by
grace through faith.  Nobody who believes
in salvation through Christ at all will disagree with this.  However, they consistently pair a number
of different works with salvation too.

The first of these works, paradoxically, is faith.  In John 6:29, Jesus Himself tells the Jews,
“This is the work of God, that you believe in Him whom He has sent.”  We’re saved by works in the do-anything sense
of the word because believing itself is something we do, albeit inside our heads.  Those who argue that faith isn’t a work are
arguing with Jesus.

The same thing is true of repentance.  In Acts 2:37, Peter’s audience cries out,
“Brothers, what shall we do?”  The first
word out of Peter’s mouth in answer is, “Repent.”  Repentance is another thing we do inside our
heads.  Additionally, if our repentance is
genuine, it will quickly bring forth fruits in keeping with repentance.

These things are clearly works, even though the author
endorses them.  Oddly, it’s much less
clear that baptism, which the author complains about, is also a work.  Belief and repentance are active.  We do them.

Baptism, by contrast, is passive.  We allow it to be done to us.  Having been on both ends, I will confidently
affirm that during a baptism, it is the baptizer, not the baptizee, who is
working!  The only work that the baptizee
can be doing during baptism is calling on the name of the Lord (see Acts
22:16), which the author acknowledges is necessary for salvation anyway (and
implicitly denies is a work).

Furthermore, *contra*
the author, there are numerous passages that link baptism and salvation.  In 1 Peter 3:21, Peter plainly says that
baptism saves (and I address the arguments of those who insist in the face of
the text that baptism actually doesn’t [here](https://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2017/04/understanding-1-peter-321.html)).  In Acts 2:38, the same man says that baptism
is for the forgiveness of sins.  In Acts
22:16, Ananias tells Paul to wash away his sins by being baptized.  The New Testament offers numerous other
examples.

Ironically, even the last verse the author cites makes the
same point.  In Titus 3:5, the Greek word
translated as “washing” is *loutron*,
which simply means “bath”.  God saves us
by means of a bath of regeneration.  When
we compare this to the other use of *loutron*
in Scripture, Ephesians 3:26 (“having cleansed [the church] by the washing of
water with the word”), it becomes evident that *loutron* refers to baptism.

This interpretive problem, though, points to a larger
problem.  Unless by “works” you mean “the
works of the Law of Moses”, you simply can’t make the New Testament say that we
are saved by faith apart from works.  The
argument requires us to reject the pattern of the first-century
church, dismissing every passage that doesn’t fit our theological biases.

For myself, I’m much less concerned with following Luther or
Calvin (or Augustine, for that matter) and more concerned with following that
pattern.  Whatever the first disciples of
Jesus believed about salvation, that’s what I want to believe.  Whatever they did to be saved, that’s what I
want to do.  If they didn’t know the
truth about the gospel, then nobody does. 
The less we pay attention to the distortion of Scripture in
Reformation-era doctrinal disputes, and the more attention we pay to the
Scripture itself, the better off we will be.
