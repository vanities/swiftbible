**OVERVIEW.**  I loved holding the LPUT, and the larger
print, generous margins, and words of Christ in black were all valuable
features.  However, the low
characters-per-line layout creates problems in the poetic sections of the Old
Testament, the paper isn’t particularly opaque, and the setting isn’t
line-matched.

I put the LPUT through the usual tour of duty:  I used it for my daily Bible reading every
morning for two weeks.  Here’s how it
went:

**SIZE.** Normally, I claim that I prefer reading
Bibles that are 6 x 9 or smaller.  At a
trim size of 6 ½” x 9 ¼”, the LPUT is substantially larger than that.  Well, “substantially” in terms of Bibles, at
least.

However, the size of the LPUT gave me no problems.  Honestly, I think the reason is the
edge-lined binding that I maligned so thoroughly in the opening segment of the
review.  When you’re trying to one-hand a
Bible to read it, that extra flexibility stops being a cute premium selling
point and becomes a practical and useful feature.

Generally, with Bibles the size of the LPUT, I prop them
against my chest to read.  However, with
the LPUT I rarely if ever did so.  Simply
cradling it in my hand felt secure enough. 
That’s not a huge deal, but it did mean that I enjoyed the reading
experience a little bit more.

**LAYOUT.** The LPUT is a center-column reference
Bible with fairly large print and moderate margins.  References, large print, and moderate margins
are all good things, but they all come with a price.  Sadly, in the world of Bible design, there is
no free lunch, and here is where the check arrives at the table.

Generally, I find that the Bibles I prefer for reading have
between 40 and 55 characters per line. 
Those are the Bibles that read most smoothly.  As a result of its other features, though,
the LPUT comes in at about 36 cpl.

That’s not a problem in prose the way that it is with some
of those Bibles with 200,000 references and 30 cpl.  In the reading I used the LPUT for, which
came from Leviticus, Deuteronomy, and Mark, I was able to read without distraction.  I know, though, that in Psalms or Isaiah, it
would have been a different story.  A
glance at the text in those locations reveals enough widows to populate Acts 6,
and those one-word lines slow my reading wayyy down.  That’s just the way it is.  The design choices of the LPUT mean that it
isn’t optimized as a reading Bible, the title of the Allan’s edition of this
setting to the contrary.

As long as we’re piling on, I’ll point out that the LPUT
isn’t line-matched, either.  This is
hardly surprising.  The Bible-design
cognoscenti tell us that line-matching wasn’t really a thing until the release
of the Cambridge Clarion in 2011.  The
setting of the LPUT, by contrast, dates to 2003, long before the concept of a
line-matched Bible occurred to anybody. 
I’m sure that Foundation would set the Bible differently if they were
setting it today, but the decade-old setting isn’t going to be line-matched any
more than any other decade-old setting would be.  It is what it is.

However, there are some layout features of the LPUT that I
do like for reading.  Those moderate
margins do make for generous gutters, even when I’m not holding the Bible all
the way open.  Likewise, this is a
black-letter Bible.  When I consider all
the ways that the execution of red-letter Bibles can go wrong, I can only
applaud Foundation’s decision to go with black letter here.  Advocates of red lettering will be delighted
to hear that in my study, I continued to regard the words of our Lord with the
highest reverence, even though they were printed in black.

Also, the verse-by-verse format really didn’t bother
me.  A lot of Bible bloggers opine that a
verse-by-verse Bible might as well be written in Sanskrit for all the
usefulness it will have as a reader. 
Personally, though, I’ve never found that the verse-by-verse format has
bothered me that much, even though the majority of the Bibles I use are
paragraphed.  From my perspective,
verse-by-verse works fine in the LPUT.

Finally, 10.5-point
ITC Weidemann is great for reading.  As
much as I enjoy reading from the Pitt Minion clan, I will acknowledge that
using them can be a tad bit effortful sometimes.  Not so here. 
It was about like reading a billboard.

**PRODUCTION
QUALITY.** My LPUT does not have a
Jongbloed text block, and you can tell, but the print quality is still decent
to good.  I didn’t notice the print
varying much in darkness from page to page, and even the small letters and
numbers that mark references are crisp. 
They’re not as gorgeous as their Jongbloed-printed equivalents, but they
aren’t bad.

For those who are determined to have a Jongbloed-printed
version of this Bible, though, such an animal does exist.  It’s the Allan NASB Reader’s Edition.  I’ve never seen one, and I suspect my odds of
receiving a review copy from Allan are about the same as my odds of being
appointed as the next Supreme Leader of Iran. 
However, I own enough Jongbloed Bibles to have a pretty good sense of
what one looks like.  If you love the
idea of this Bible and money is no object, the Allan version may well be a good
investment.

Back, however, to reality and to the Bible I actually am
reviewing.  Bible geeks may well recall
reading about the ultra-opaque paper in LPUT’s of days of yore.  Sadly, those days are not these days.  The paper in my China-print LPUT is about
what you would expect from any China-print thinline.  I can read the text on the other side of the
paper, as well as the text on the facing page underneath.  Line-matching would have helped quite a bit
here, but. . .

**CONCLUSION.** I liked reading out of this Bible.  I really did. 
However, it has a number of characteristics that keep it from being an
optimal reader.  Here, I’ll rate it at
7/10.
