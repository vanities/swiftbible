**OVERVIEW.** I was not expecting to like the SCJB as a
reading Bible.  I have not liked reading
from other Bibles like the SCJB. 
However, it far outperformed my expectations.

As I do with every Bible I review, I spent two weeks doing
my daily Bible reading out of the SCJB. 
Here are my impressions:

**SIZE.**I’ve found that the larger the Bible, the larger the print needs to
be for me to be happy with it as a reader. 
I’m fairly happy holding smaller Bibles up like a Kindle reader or
paperback, but I want to prop larger Bibles on my chest or even set them in my
lap.  As a result, I have to be
comfortable with reading the Bible wherever I’m holding it.

The print in the SCJB is fairly small, but so is the physical
Bible.  I didn’t have to think about
one-handing it like a paperback, and it never became heavy while I was doing
so.  There are smaller Bibles out there,
but the SCJB is small enough.

**LAYOUT.** The SCJB has some design elements that I
expected to work well and did, but it also has some features that I didn’t
think would work for my reading preferences, yet did anyway.  When I’m testing a Bible for reading utility,
I focus more on characters-per-line count than just about anything else.  Usually, I prefer to read from Bibles with a
CPL count between 40 and 55—double-column text Bibles, single-column reference
Bibles, and the like.

The SCJB, well, it has a CPL count of something like
87.  I’ve never tested a Bible with
longer lines.  It makes sense that this
would be a trait of the SCJB. 
Single-column Bibles are longer than double-column Bibles (more white
space in the Psalms, etc.).  If you want
your single-column Bible to be of a manageable size, then, you must either make
it 2000 pages long and print it on super-thin paper (like the Schuyler Caxton),
make it a great big Bible with lots of lines per page (like the Foundation
Side-Column Reference), or put lots and lots of words in each line.  Crossway couldn’t print the SCJB on thin
paper and still have it be any good for writing in, they didn’t want to make it
larger than the squarish journaling-Bible form factor, so they went
wholeheartedly through Door Number Three.

I should have hated reading out of the Bible, but instead, I
found that I enjoyed it.  My best guess
is that the CPL count is so bad that it’s good. 
I’m a fairly fast reader, and I suspect that when I’m reading, I read in
chunks, about 50 characters at a time. 
When I’m forced to stop and pick up an extra 10-15 characters on the
same line, it slows me down and distracts me from the text.

However, with a CPL count of nearly 90, I’m simply reading
the SCJB in two nearly full chunks per line. 
What can I say?  It works.

Of course, with lines that are so long, the poetry in the
SCJB looks great.  I glanced through
Psalms, but I think I only saw one widow. 
This is a Bible that keeps the benefits of single-column settings while
dodging the problems, at least for me.

The gutter margins of the SCJB are also problem-free.  In most Bibles, ½” is a generous inner
margin.  The inner margins of the SCJB,
by contrast, are ⅝”.  They’re almost big enough to write in, even
though you don’t have to.  That much
white space down the middle does an admirable job of keeping the text from
curling into the gutter.

I also like that the SCJB is black-letter.  I don’t attach any particular devotional
significance to having the words of Christ printed in red, and the red
lettering often makes the layout worse. 
Here, Crossway avoided the whole problem by printing all the text in one
color.  To me, this is optimal.

Next, the SCJB is a line-matched Bible.  In other words, the text on one side of a
page lines up with the text on the other side of the page (and the text on all
nearby pages of the layout).  In just
about any Bible currently in print, that text on the other page is still
visible, so the effect is of gray bars underneath the text you’re reading
(line-matched) as opposed to a formless gray blob under your text (not
line-matched). For some reason, this heightens contrast and is easier to
read.  Even though the paper in the SCJB
is decent enough that it probably didn’t have to be line-matched, this still
helps.

Finally, the print in the SCJB is a little smaller than I
typically prefer, but I found that I didn’t mind.  Crossway reports that it’s set in 7.5-point
Lexicon No. 1, rather than the 8-point font that is normally my minimum for
comfortable reading.  However, the SCJB
benefits from generous leading, as do many of Crossway’s more recent
designs.  This is in part to accommodate
the true-superscript verse numbers, but whether by accident or design, it also
makes the text more readable.

I found this to be true in the SCJB.  According to my in-wallet point gauge that my
photographer gave me, the text is leaded at about 9.5 points.  That’s a lot of white space between lines,
and it keeps the eye from confusing a given line with the line above or below
it.  Even though the SCJB has smaller
print than the Holman KJV Notetaking Bible (7.5 versus 8), I actually found the
former easier to read.

**PRODUCTION
QUALITY.** This one is on a par with
many of the China-print Crossway Bibles I’ve reviewed recently.  Generally, the print is crisp and clear, but
every so often, you’ll find some ink speckles on a page.  That doesn’t interfere with the readability
of the text, but it does show that Chinese printing facilities still have some
way to go to catch up with the likes of Jongbloed.

The paper is fine. 
It’s not as good as really awesome, opaque Bible paper (like the paper in the Foundation In Touch from days of yore), but it’s good
enough to write on without disastrous bleed-through.  That means it’s good enough to read from too,
especially with line-matching.

**CONCLUSION.** I expected the SCJB to be a disaster as a
reader, but it proved to be anything but. 
I find myself wondering, in fact, if I want to use it for a readthrough
of the ESV next year.  I give it a
9.5/10, for an extraordinarily high overall score of 28/30.
