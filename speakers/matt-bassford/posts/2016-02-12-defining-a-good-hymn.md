A couple of weeks ago, I wrote a post about the need for
great hymns in the future.  Among a
number of interesting comments, I attracted this one from Tony Esolen:

*A good hymn should:*

* *Be tuneful, and be
  singable by a congregation -- men and women, old people and young people and
  children.*
* *Be a mature reflection
  upon the Christian life, the life of Christ, the Scriptures, the promises of
  God, the glory of God and the beauty of His works, and so on.*
* *Be orthodox, theologically.*
* *Be an example of
  decent poetry at the very least -- no embarrassing solecisms, no office
  memoranda language, no clodhopping terminology, no political patois. The
  writers should be striving for what any poet strives for, at the LEAST.*



*If you can't fulfill
all four criteria, scrap it and start over.*

I liked Tony’s definition so well that I asked to borrow it,
to which he graciously assented. 
Preacher that I am, I couldn’t resist the opportunity to boil it down to
four one-word bullet points.  A good
hymn, then, should be:



**Tuneful.** In other words, the hymn should have a
tune that is both abstractly stirring and evocative, and will sound stirring
and evocative when sung by a congregation of average musical gifts.

The first half of this is really more a formal than a
practical consideration.  Nobody needs to
be told to avoid hymns with bland, uneventful music.  They do that anyway.  Over time, this results in such hymns
vanishing from the repertoire unless the lyrics are really, *really* good.

The second half is a more serious problem.  It’s entirely possible to write a hymn that
sounds great when you play it back on the piano or in Finale, yet is so
difficult musically that the tune will always be a barrier for the congregation.

Sometimes, the musical problems are driven by the
lyrics.  Praise songs with varied and
broken meter are always going to cause problems for the congregation.  The tune has to match the unpredictable text,
which forces the composer to use all sorts of dotted eighth notes, tied/slurred
sixteenth notes, and various other rhythmic expedients.  When this jerry-rigged rhythm appears on the
staff, most ordinary worshipers, even ones who can read music, cannot sightread
it correctly.  The congregation can
listen to a soloist or praise team perform such a hymn, but it can’t sing the
hymn together.

At other times, though, the fault lies entirely with the
composer.  I’m fond (overly fond?) of
telling the composers with whom I collaborate, “No hymn has ever been rejected
because the arrangement is too simple.” 
There are all sorts of wonderful, rich harmonies that you can contrive
and enter into Finale that the congregation simply isn’t going to hear and be
able to sing.  Congregational music has
to be simple music, particularly if you want the congregation singing in parts.

**Thoughtful.** This is the point where I usually camp
out.  There are all too many hymns and
praise songs out there that don’t say anything untrue because they don’t say
anything.  This is not acceptable.  This is the Joel Osteening of worship.  In Hebrews 5:12, the writer complains, “For
though by this time you ought to be teachers, you have need again for someone
to teach you the elementary principles of the oracles of God, and you have come
to need milk and not solid food.” (NASB)

His argument is predicated on the belief that only
spiritually mature Christians should be teachers.  If you haven’t matured, you can’t be a
teacher.  When the Bible tells us that
hymns are supposed to teach too, it follows that a good hymn will reflect
spiritually mature thinking.  Too often,
though, hymns and praise songs reflect only an elementary understanding of the
mysteries of the faith.  We don’t turn
our pulpits and Bible-class lecterns over to new converts, but somehow, we
think it’s acceptable and even desirable to sing spiritual songs that a new
convert could have written.

Over the past couple of weeks, I’ve crossed paths a couple
times with the Gordon Fee quotation that reads, “Show me a church’s songs, and
I’ll show you their theology.”  What
happens when a church’s songs are written by the doctrinally uninformed?

**Truthful.** Simply because a hymn is deep and well
written doesn’t mean that it’s true.  A
lot of this is going to be tradition-specific. 
I’m not a Methodist, and that means that I’m not going to be comfortable
with some of the things that the Wesleys wrote, even though both brothers were
intelligent, thoughtful, and sincere men.

I don’t belong to a Reformed denomination or tradition, and
I’ve read some magnificently written Reformed hymns that I can’t sing in good
conscience because I don’t agree with what they teach.  Ironically, it is the very depth, clarity,
and skill with which these hymns are written that make the doctrinal problems
apparent.  Vague and pointless hymns don’t
ring anybody’s alarm bells.

This is true in the opposite direction as well.  I’ve written hymns about the Bible as the
single authoritative guide to faith and practice.  My religious tradition is strongly committed
to that belief, but I know that my Catholic friends don’t share it.  Issues of hymn quality aside, thoughtful
Catholics are going to choke on singing a hymn that says such things.

Of course, there are hymns that travel well across
traditions.  I don’t know of any church
that would have a doctrinal issue with singing “Amazing Grace.”  However, not all hymns do, even good hymns,
and I believe that my tradition, at least, should sing some hymns that don’t
travel well.  Doctrinal distinctiveness
should appear in songs as much as in sermons.

**Graceful.**  I admit that there’s a fair amount of
subjectivity to this point, but I believe that good hymns have to be written by
people who know how to write.  This theme
of skilled service appears throughout the entire Bible.  Exodus 31:1-2 shows that only those who had
been given skill and understanding were to work in constructing the
tabernacle.  1 Peter 4:11 reveals that
Christians are to serve according to the gifts they have, not the gifts they
don’t have.

Indeed, Peter even supplies us with the justification for
this practice:  “. . . so that in all
things God may be glorified.”  God
deserves only the best, so we ought to be offering Him only the best.  When we offer Him the obviously imperfect,
that diminishes the glory we are giving Him.

The possible imperfections are legion:  grammar that your high-school English teacher
would have red-penned, slangy language, conspicuous forced rhymes that reveal a
lack of poetic aptitude, and so forth. 
If those things show up on Top-40 radio, it’s not a big deal, but the
solemn assembly of the people of the Lord ought to have higher standards than a
Top-40 radio station.

**Sorting It All Out**

Not all of these factors are equal in importance.  Hymns with overly difficult tunes are only
problematic if your determination to use them leads you to adopt performance-style
worship.  If you don’t, they’ll crash and
burn a few times and then stop appearing.

Likewise, there’s probably going to be at least one
curmudgeon in every congregation who will complain about hymns that don’t line
up with the congregation’s convictions. 
In real life, neither tune nor truth is going to be a consistent issue
(though they do belong in the definition).

Even when it comes to the two most likely problems,
thoughtless hymns are much worse than graceless hymns.  Some writers of the Southern gospel era were
masters of the clichéd rhyme.  They could
string three meaningless verses together as smoothly as you please (Wherever
you find “roam”, you can be sure that “home” isn’t far away).  I never got much out of singing hymns like
that, and I don’t mourn their departure from my tradition’s repertoire.

However, a hymn with unlovely writing can still have
something meaningful to say.  Exhibit A
here is Mosie Lister’s “Where No One Stands Alone”, particularly the beginning
of the second verse.  The technique of
the hymn is far from superb, but its content and passionate sincerity make it
well worth singing anyway.



Exceptions to the rule, though, are no reason to reject the
rule.  “Where No One Stands Alone” does
not work because of its problems; it works despite them.  Generally, it is hymns that don’t present
problems that are most likely to succeed. 
They provide the best spiritual foundation for a congregation and will
continue to do so far into the future.
