Throughout my life, the churches where I attended/was a member
have used six different hymnals.  Every
time I changed hymnals, whether because the church in question bought new books
or because I moved, the repertoire changed too. 
I remember singing “Sing to Me of Heaven” for the first time in New
Jersey, because *Songs of the Church* contained
the hymn, and *Great Songs of the Church*
didn’t.  When my dad retired and we moved
to Missouri, I was introduced to *Sacred
Selections* and literally hundreds of Southern-gospel hymns I’d never sung
before.

A couple years later, *Sacred
Selections* was replaced by the original *Hymns
for Worship*, and in the next year, the congregation learned 100 new hymns
out of it, everything from “Be Still, My Soul” to “Because He Lives”.  *Hymns
for Worship, Revised* introduced more new hymns, but the next big change
occurred when the Joliet church purchased *Psalms,
Hymns, and Spiritual Songs.* We’ve
learned around 150 hymns from it, with dramatic effects on our repertoire.  It’s not uncommon for four of the eight hymns
in a Sunday worship service to be hymns that I did not know 10 years ago.  They may well be hymns that did not exist 10
years ago.



It’s probably true that my experience is atypical.  I think that my first congregation was late
in abandoning *Great Songs of the Church*
(which, if memory serves, they did sometime around 1988), and I know that my
current congregation was among the first adopters of *Psalms, Hymns, and Spiritual Songs.*

However, what I have gone through is nothing more than an
accelerated version of the changes that happen everywhere.  Even in a very conservative religious
tradition like the churches of Christ, over the course of a lifetime, the
content of the hymns in widely used hymnals will change dramatically.

There is a core of great hymns that remains constant.  Of the first 10 hymns of *Great Songs of the Church*, the Joliet church still sings five of
them.  However, outside that core, which
only increases slowly, everything else is in flux.

When it comes to hymns (and everything else), I have an
undeniably conservative perspective. 
However, I believe that this shift in repertoire is not only inevitable
but good.  Indeed, I see several
advantages to it:

**It gets rid of bad
old hymns.** Though it happens slowly
instead of dramatically, the winnowing of the repertoire by decade after decade
of hymnal and PowerPoint-deck editors is like a forest fire.  It appears destructive, but in reality, much
of what gets destroyed is the underbrush and deadwood that was cluttering up
the place.

Every hymnal I’ve mentioned, and every other hymnal besides,
contains hymns that should not be sung.  *Great Songs of the Church* is arguably
the greatest and certainly the most influential hymnal that anybody associated
with the churches of Christ has ever produced, but it contains its share of
misses.  Handel’s “Hallelujah Chorus”,
lovely though it is, is not congregational fare.  Later editors were right to discard it.  Most hymns left out in successive generations
of hymnals match its lack of usefulness while not attaining to its merits.

This is important.  In
my opinion, retaining a bad old hymn is even worse than adopting a bad new hymn.  At least the bad new hymn possesses the
virtue of timeliness.  Christians enjoy
singing it because they remember it from Bible camp or heard it on the
radio.  It’s as culturally relevant as
anything we do in worship.

However, the bad old hymn has ceased to be culturally
relevant.  It has outlived the people who
could remember hearing it on the radio in the 1930’s.  It doesn’t sound timely.  It sounds dated, and it’s unable to transcend
its time.  If it could, it wouldn’t be a
bad hymn.  A hymnal is supposed to be a
library for living worship, not a mausoleum in printed form.  Like dead people, expired hymns ought to be
buried and left decently alone.

**It allows for
correction.** “But wait!” some of you
are crying at the previous point.  “What
about when hymnal editors exclude hymns that are good?”  This certainly happens too.  To my dying day, I will never understand why *Sacred Selections* omitted “Lord, We Come
Before Thee Now” in favor of page after page of quartet-style trash.

However, exclusion from the latest hymnal does not doom a
hymn to oblivion.  A good hymn will
survive not having been sung for 20 years. 
It may survive not being sung for 150 years.  If it’s good enough, sooner or later, someone
will remember/discover it, and it will be used. 
“Lord, We Come Before Thee Now” reappeared in *Hymns for Worship*, and it continues to be a congregational
staple.

More recently, the editors of *Psalms, Hymns, and Spiritual Songs* chose to include the minor-key,
African-American-spiritual version of “A Shelter in the Time of Storm”, which
dates from 1880.  As far as I know, it
had never before appeared in a hymnal used by churches of Christ, yet after
only a few years, it has become solidly entrenched in the repertoire at
Joliet.  My kids wander around singing
it.

By contrast, if the repertoire is frozen at any moment in
time, these wrongs will never be righted. 
I was a technical editor for *Psalms,
Hymns, and Spiritual Songs* (translation: 
I did lots of work but made no decisions), and I think highly of the
hymnal, but with a moment’s thought, I could come up with a list of a dozen
hymns that I believe should have been included but weren’t.  No editorial team will always make the right
decision about every hymn, and only repertoire shift can address those errors.

**It introduces good
new hymns.** While I have a great
appreciation for the tradition of English-language hymnody, I do not venerate
it.  I do not believe that every hymn
that the people of God ought to use in worship has already been written.  Indeed, I believe the opposite is true.  I’m heavily involved in the work of writing
new hymns myself, and though I do not uncritically accept the body of
contemporary praise songs (again, quite the opposite!), I believe that each of
them ought to be evaluated on its merits instead of the merits of the company
it keeps.

In any era, most new hymns that are introduced will fail,
and they should fail.  Maybe they’re
poorly written.  Maybe they’re too
difficult.  Maybe, despite skillful composition, they fail to touch the heart of the worshiper (I’m an expert in writing
hymns like this).  Editors do their best
to anticipate what will work and what won’t, but nobody is terribly good at
predicting this.  In the end, it is the
songleader and the congregation, not the editor, who decide what will survive.

There’s a sense, then, in which the job of any hymn
introducer to throw stuff up against a wall and see what sticks.  Most of it won’t, but some of it will, and it
should.  10 years ago, I had never sung “In
Christ Alone”, but now that I know it, it has enriched my spiritual life
immeasurably.  A repertoire that is set
in stone will reject the good as surely as it rejects the bad, and that’s a
problem.

In any religious tradition, repertoire shift is a fact of
life.  Generally speaking, I think that’s
a good thing.  Every period has its
excesses.  The current flood of
contemporary songs that seeks to replace the traditional repertoire reminds me
strongly of the dominance of radio-era hymns in *Sacred Selections*.  Christians
aren’t immune to faddish enthusiasms simply because they’re Christians.

However, time has a way of tidying up the excesses of the
past even as it throws up new excesses, and the net trend is positive.  We don’t sing the same body of hymns as our
grandfathers or even our fathers.  Instead,
our hymn tradition is richer and more diverse, and we are the better for it.
