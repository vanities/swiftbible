Last week, my wife went to Texas for several days to sing
for a hymn-recording session.  This was
something that Lauren and I had discussed several months before, and to which I
had agreed.  This is in the middle of her
winter break in graduate school, she has always loved being part of a chorus
and doing recordings, and if (as we plan) she starts working full-time next
year, she may never have another opportunity to do something like this.  Plus, the trip down south also gave her the
chance to spend a day or two with her folks.

Of course, as a result of this decision, I was going to be
entirely responsible for my children from Wednesday afternoon through Monday
evening.  I was, in effect, going to
become a single father.  I do a fair
amount of kid-wrangling normally, so I figured that though it might be a little
difficult, it wouldn’t be too much different from my normal existence.



What I learned instead was that in setting up a two-parent
family as the standard, God was very, VERY wise.

I had never realized before what a difference it makes for
me to be able to tag-team child-watching with Lauren, that if I need to throw
Zoë and Marky at her, to say, “Deal with them; there’s something I need to work
on,” I can do that.  Not so for the past
five days.  Instead, I was on parental
duty 24/7, and as a result, my attention was, shall we say, fractured.

This certainly showed up in my preaching work.  Because I was never off-duty, I couldn’t get
away to my sanctum at the church building and focus on sermon-writing.  Instead, I had to cram writing in whenever
the kids weren’t around and bothering me. 
I always keep an extra sermon in my back pocket in case my world blows
up in the next week, but with Lauren gone, I finished that extra sermon on
Saturday afternoon instead of on Thursday morning, which is my norm.  I did plenty of other writing too, and indeed
wrote unusually well (as always, misery makes for good emotional fuel), but
that also had to get crammed into odd corners of my life.  Rather than being leisurely and reflective
(which I prefer), my writing was frantic.

“Frantic” is actually a pretty good description for the way
I spent those days generally.  Because I
had to be working when not actively minding the Dynamic Duo, I never had an
opportunity for down time.  Life was a
blur of bathing and meal preparation and rushing out the door.  When I wasn’t breathlessly minding children,
I was breathlessly trying to accomplish grownup stuff, whether writing or
washing dishes.

This was draining in a couple different senses of the
word.  First of all, it was mentally
draining.  I think most who know me would
describe me as a self-disciplined, high-motor kind of guy.  Ten years ago, I had the mental toughness to
push through and get a law degree from a Top 15 law school.  However, the experience of being The Parent
exhausted my emotional stamina in a way I’d never experienced before.  I can remember one moment Sunday when I said
to myself, “I know I need—need!—to get this done right now, but I simply can’t
make myself do it.”  So I sat down and
dinked around on the Internet instead.

Single parenthood was also draining in terms of, for lack of
a better phrase, “household capital”. 
Lives and households need to be maintained the same way that a car needs
to be maintained.  You have to pick up
after yourself so you can find things later when you need to.  You have to plan and prepare ahead so that
the getting-out-the-door routine is a routine and not a screaming match.

The problem was that just like a family with a financial
deficit might stop maintaining the car, I had a willpower deficit, so I stopped
maintaining the household.  I stopped
putting things away.  I didn’t plan.  I didn’t prepare.

In the short term, that’s a gain, just like it’s a financial
gain not to change the car’s oil at 5,000 miles.  However, in the long term, it’s a losing
strategy, just like bad car maintenance will lead to a non-functional car.  Mess has a way of piling up until it
paralyzes.  The panicked scramble to
locate Zoë’s hair towel is more exhausting than taking two minutes to put the
towel away would have been.  What’s more,
last-minute heroics tend to cascade, so that soon you have no time for anything
but last-minute heroics.  That’s the
result of completely depleted household capital.  I hadn’t gotten there yet when Lauren got
back, but I could tell I was headed there.

From all of this, I learned two worthwhile lessons.  First, it taught me that God’s way
works.  My sense of unbearable strain, of
pushing myself past my limits, evaporated once my wife came back and pitched
in.  I still help quite a bit, as I
always have, but “helping quite a bit” is worlds away from “doing
everything”.  There is no substitute for
the two-parent family of God’s design.

However, it also gave me considerably more sympathy for the
struggles of those who haven’t followed God’s plan (though obviously, there are circumstances in which someone may find him- or herself a single parent through no fault of his or her own).  Sadly, many Christians don’t have a great
deal of compassion for single parents, especially single mothers.  Prune-mouthed disapproval and sour jokes
about how aspirin is the best form of birth control are much more common.

Now, obviously it would have been better if single mothers who are single mothers as the result of fornication had
never had sex and gotten pregnant in the first place.  Premarital sex is a black-letter sin.  However, who of us is so righteous as to claim
that we would never—no, never!—have done such a thing?  As it happens, I didn’t, but I attribute that
much less to my own spiritual strength and much more to the grace of God, who
preserved me from temptation greater than that which I was able to bear.  Who am I to sneer at those who failed in a way
I easily could have?

Of course, the sneering does not end at conception.  One of the predominant images in our national
discourse is that of the welfare queen, a hard-eyed (minority) woman who
cold-bloodedly churns out child after child in order to maximize her government
payout.  There may well be people like
that out there, though I’ve never met one.

I now suspect, though, that a sizable number (at least) of
the women on the welfare rolls are there not because of calculation, but
because of disaster and exhaustion. 
Single parenthood is necessarily a life on the margin, and when you live
on the margin, all it takes is one blow to send you over the edge.

Even though I struggled with work during my five days in the
wilderness, I actually had much more flexibility than most.  I’m a preacher, which means that during the
week, I pretty much get to set my own schedule. 
That is emphatically not true for the minimum-wage legions who toil at
McDonald’s and Wal-Mart.

If one of my children had vomited spectacularly all over the
living-room floor on Friday, I could have stepped away from the computer to
tend to my ailing darling for a couple of hours.  If Minimum-Wage Mom gets a spectacularly
vomiting child an hour before her shift starts, she has to call in.  Enough vomit-induced call-ins, and she
probably gets fired.  All sorts of other
things start going wrong then too—the same cascade of failure I experienced,
writ large.

Even if she’s able to recover and track down a second job in
two weeks, what happens when the next stomach-flu epidemic strikes?  How much strength does it take to keep
getting back up off the mat, knowing full well that the next punch will put you
right back down?  It’s easy to condemn
that single mom who doesn’t want to work, doesn’t want to try.  It takes a little more thought to ask what
made her that way.

This is not a political statement.  I am certainly not a political animal.  I am suspicious that the easy answers offered
by the electoral process are a way to avoid the hard work required by God of
the individual.  What God would have us
do is this:  be merciful.  Think mercifully of others, speak mercifully
of others, and when opportunity offers, show mercy to others.



For by your standard of measure, it will be measured to you
in return.
