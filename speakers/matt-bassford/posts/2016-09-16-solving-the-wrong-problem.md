Isaiah 55 is one of the most exultant chapters in the entire
Bible, but its opening contains a pointed negative question.  In Isaiah 55:2, God asks, “Why do you spend
money for what is not bread, and your wages for what does not satisfy?”  His concern, of course, is not physical
nutrition, but spiritual nutrition.  The
Israelites had problems and knew it.  In
fact, they were even trying to solve them. 
However, they were looking to themselves rather than to God, so their
solutions left them hungry.

We can easily fall into the same trap.  We’ve got this problem, and it’s *really* got us down.  Maybe we have a whole slew of problems.  We try to work our way through them, but it
doesn’t work.  It fact, it may well be that
our efforts have left us in a worse position than we began.



We’re a little downcast about it, but we pick ourselves up,
dust off the seat of our pants, and try again. 
More!  Harder!  However, more and harder doesn’t seem to be
working either.  The very urgency with
which we are attacking the problem is pushing the solution away.

It’s like when a teenage boy starts worrying that his
girlfriend is going to dump him, so he gets clingy and suffocating.  Not surprisingly, this produces the very
result he fears.  We often aren’t a whole
lot smarter in the way we approach our own problems.

After enough iterations of this cycle, even the most
determined of us get discouraged.  At
that point, we start trying to dump the blame on God.  Where’s the help He promised us?  Where’s the way forward?  Et cetera.

Sometimes, though, if God were to give us the way forward
that we want, it would be akin to me handing my five-year-old son the keys to
our Corolla.  An opportunity that we
haven’t yet grown enough to handle isn’t actually an opportunity.  It’s a disaster in waiting.  Paradoxically, the answer we’ve been asking
for may well be the last thing we need.

Instead, there will be times when the way forward lies in not moving.  “Be still,” the Creator says, “and know that
I am God.”  As with children, our
flurries of activity may reveal an unquiet spirit.  If I want my children to rest, I don’t leave
them in a brightly lit room filled with toys and a super-loud TV.  Instead, I take those things away from them
(even though they like them), so that when outward calm is enforced, inward
calm can follow.

We don’t like it when God takes our toys away either, but we
must be careful not to miss the message. 
All those things we’re trying to do to protect ourselves and care for
ourselves?  That’s not our job at
all.  It’s God’s job.  Either we trust Him to do that or we don’t,
and if we don’t, it’s the lesson we most need to learn.

Our problems are never about all the external things that
are clamoring for attention.  Eventually,
all of those things are going to work out. 
It may be on the other side of Jordan, but they’re going to work
out.  God hasn’t exactly made a career
out of forsaking the righteous.

The real problem, the problem that we must solve, is the
tendency of those external things to divert our attention from Him.  The secret to joy lies in stillness, not
striving, trust, not self-sufficiency, and faith, not fear.  What is more, all of these can only be found
in God.  Once we have them, our problems
will disappear, whether we have found a solution or not.
