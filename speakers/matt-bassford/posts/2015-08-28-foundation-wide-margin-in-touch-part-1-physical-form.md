**OVERVIEW.** The Foundation Wide Margin In Touch
hardback is certainly too large to be a good carry Bible.  Its hardback cover won’t last as long as a
leather cover will.  However, in terms of
print and paper quality, it probably has a better text block than any other
mass-market Bible available today.



Among Bible geeks, the In Touch has attained a
semi-legendary status.  Its coterie of
admirers insists that the calfskin version of the In Touch has one of the
finest calfskin covers ever put on a Bible, and its paper, print, and layout have
a similarly exalted reputation.  Sadly,
Foundation no longer prints the In Touch, and the calfskin and genuine-leather
versions have long since sold out.  They
can still be had for cringe-inducing prices on the secondary market, but that
does me no good.

I decided, then, to ask for a review copy of the hardback
version.  Even though the cover is made
out of cardboard rather than calfskin, I believed that the text block would be
the same as in the more upmarket versions, and I was interested in seeing it
for myself.  Foundation graciously sent
me one to review, and here are my thoughts:

**VITAL STATISTICS**

**Translation:**  New American Standard Bible (1995)

**Cover:**  Hardback

**Binding:**  Sewn

**Trim Size:**   6 ½” x 9 ¼”

**Text Block Width:**  1 ⅜”

**Weight:**  3 pounds

**Column:**  Double

**Layout:**  Verse-by-verse

**Font:**  10.5-point Times New Roman

**Words of Christ:** Black

**Cross-References:**  No

**Ribbon Marker:**  0

**SIZE.** This is a big ole Bible, make no mistake
about that!  At 6 ½” x 9 ¼”, it’s larger
than the Crossway Legacy, and the same trim size as a wide-margin Foundation
Side-Column Reference or a Foundation Giant-Print Reference.  At 1556 pages with impressively opaque paper,
it’s also fairly thick and heavy.  Even
Goliath couldn’t put this one in his hip pocket!

I didn’t bother putting it through the glove-box test
because I knew it would fail and I didn’t want to damage the hardback cover by
shoving too hard.  If you like small
Bibles, this is not the Bible for you. 
Other than true behemoths like the Schuyler Quentel and the full-size
ESV study Bible, this is about as large a Bible as you’re likely to find.

**COVER.** It’s a hardback, just like the textbooks
you had in elementary school.  The design
on the cover is. . . OK, I guess, with “Holy” in “Holy Bible” written in a
scripty typeface and the In Touch Ministries logo beneath it.  I know nothing about In Touch Ministries
besides what Wikipedia tells me, but I don’t particularly care about what logo
appears on the cover of a Bible, so long as the logo owners don’t mess with
what’s between the covers.  In this case,
In Touch Ministries didn’t.  All in all,
though, I’d rather have a plain black or blue buckram cover on a hardback
Bible.

In terms of durability, well. . . it’s a hardback.  Because its construction is the same as an
elementary-school textbook, over time, its fate will be the same as that of an
elementary-school textbook.  I carried
the In Touch with me everywhere for two weeks. 
I was as careful with it as I ordinarily am with Bibles, but the cover
is starting to show wear in ways that a leatherbound Bible would not.  The corners of the spine are a little bit
crushed, there are a few scratches in the glossy material of the cover, and so
forth.  I’m sure that in a couple of
months, the nice sharp corners on the cover would get bent down too.  If I wanted to protect it, I’d probably have
to make a cover for it out of a brown grocery sack (elementary school, again).  Now THAT would look magnificent in the pulpit!

More seriously, the endpapers are just ordinary paper, and
in a hardback Bible, the endpapers generally come under tremendous stress.  This is not a Bible that will stand up to
sustained daily use, unless you happen to be as careful with your Bibles as my
mother was with her hardback Bibles.  She
could make a glue-bound hardback Bible last for 10 years of daily study.

That is clearly not an ability that she passed down to
me.  I know from experience that if I
were to adopt a hardback In Touch as my daily reader , within a year or two,
one of the endpapers would split completely, leaving the cover to flop around
uselessly.  It’s not like the In Touch is
a shoddier hardback than other hardbacks; it’s just that the whole tribe is not
a particularly durable one.

**TEXT BLOCK.** The text block, on the other hand, is
much more impressive.  Like all
Foundation Bibles other than compacts and pew Bibles, the In Touch hardback is
sewn.  In fact, because the text block
hasn’t been sanded, rounded, or gilded, you can still see the zigzag of the
signatures when you look at any of the edges of the block.  It’s kinda cool-looking, actually.  Certainly, the cover of a hardback In Touch
will fail long before the binding will.

Like many mass-market Bibles, the In Touch has a little bit
of cockling.  It crackles slightly when
you open it, and the typical dimples in the gutter are occasionally
visible.  I think this is the result of
the binding thread being drawn a little too tight during the binding process,
though, rather than of the Bible being printed cross-grain.  When I look at the signatures from the top
and bottom of the text block, they have the slight waviness that is
characteristic of Bibles that are printed with the grain.  Cross-grain Bibles, by contrast, show
waviness in the page edges opposite the spine. 
However, because the paper in the In Touch is so heavy, it doesn’t
exhibit any problems with curling like a lot of with-the-grain Bibles do.

**VALUE.** As it often seems to be, value is a
tricky question here.  I don’t think I
could get away with using an In Touch hardback as my primary Bible.  I think I am too careless and would kill it
in a few years.  Nor is this a
particularly cheap Bible.  Even a
hardback In Touch retails for $40.

And yet.  By my count,
I’ve received 11 Bibles as review copies since I started reviewing Bibles.  I’ve given most of them away but liked a few
well enough to hold on to.  Of them all,
though, there is only one Bible I liked well enough to order another with my
own money for myself, and that’s the In Touch hardback.

Here’s why.  My In
Touch hardback has a printing date of 1999. 
I suspect all the In Touch hardbacks do. 
They’re USA-print Bibles from the glory days of Bible printing, back
before all the mass-market Bibles got offshored to China, back before the EPA,
etc., cracked down on the super-polluting paper mills that produced
high-quality Bible paper.  I am glumly
confident that there will never again be a mass-market Bible with paper and
print this good.  I also don’t know of
any other in-stock Bibles that are dinosaurs like the In Touch hardback.

I didn’t buy another In Touch to use.  I bought it as the raw material for a
rebind.  There are businesses out there
that will happily round the corners and gild the edges of a hardback Bible text
block.  There are others that will
happily put a text block of your choosing inside a calfskin or goatskin
cover.  Ain’t nobody, however, who can
make you another text block this good, unless you want to pay $100 retail or
more for a Jongbloed-print premium Bible. 
Even then, though, to my eye, the paper in the In Touch hardback looks
slightly more opaque than the 38-gsm Tervakoski Thinopaque that appears in my
Cambridge Wide Margin.

I’m not looking at doing a rebind for a while.  In fact, I’m pretty happy with my
aforementioned Cambridge WM.  However,
once my eyesight starts going—and it will—my In Touch hardback will undergo a
metamorphosis, and I wanted a pristine one so that it would be worthy of such
treatment.

**CONCLUSION.** Yes, it’s huge.  Yes, it’s a hardback.  But that text block!  Even with all the dings against it, I still
think the In Touch hardback deserves a 7/10 for physical form.
