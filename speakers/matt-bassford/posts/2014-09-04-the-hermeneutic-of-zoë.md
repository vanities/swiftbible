My daughter, Zoë, is 4. 
Because she is genetically related to me, she has a strong will and a
lot of opinions.  Many of these opinions
concern what she should and should not eat for dinner.  Sadly for Zoë, I have opinions too, and one
of them is that children should not be allowed to subsist on a diet of
peanut-butter sandwiches.  This
divergence of views is the starting point for a lot of dinner-table
conversation.

For instance, we had chili for dinner yesterday.  Zoë does not approve of chili.  As I expected, we ended up making a Deal, in
which I agreed to sprinkle cheese on top of her chili in exchange for her
promise to eat it.  This, of course, did
not resolve the problem, but it did give me more room to cajole and
threaten.



I spent the rest of dinnertime elaborating on the ghastly
consequences that would befall Zoë if she failed to honor her word and keep her
end of the Deal.  Among other things, I
noted, “If you do not eat your chili, there will be no bedtime snack.  You will eat nothing else until
morning.”  Eventually, wisdom prevailed,
Zoë ate her chili, and I forgot about the whole thing.

A few hours later, as we were returning from Wednesday night
Bible study, Zoë said to my wife, “Daddy promised me a bedtime snack!”

Lauren, knowing my views about bedtime snacks, said
confidently, “No; he didn’t.”

I overheard the conversation, thought for a moment, and
interjected, “Wellll. . . I kinda did.”

Zoë confirmed my suspicions by insisting indignantly, “I ate
my chili!”  She had heard me say that she
wouldn’t get a bedtime snack if she didn’t eat her chili, so she reasoned that
if she DID eat her chili, she WOULD get a bedtime snack.  I never made any explicit promises about
bedtime snacks, but Zoë recognized and remembered the implicit promise in what
I had said.  I acknowledged the validity
of her argument, and she got what she wanted.

This is the way that language works.  We convey meaning not only by what we say,
but also by what we imply.   Sometimes
this meaning may not be what we intend—I certainly did not mean to promise a
bedtime snack in exchange for chili consumption!—but it accompanies our words
nonetheless.  Whenever we listen to
someone else, we are listening to the unsaid as well as to the said.  There is nothing esoteric or irrational about
this process.  As Zoë has proven, it’s
something that a smart 4-year-old will do.

And yet, it is something that many refuse to do when it
comes to the Bible.  There are many
things that God does not explicitly say in the Scripture.  No passage says, “Don’t use instruments of
music in worship.”  No passage says,
“Don’t band together in denominations.” 
No passage says, “Churches can build and own church buildings.”  None of these things are stated, but they are
all logically implied.

God is smarter than I am. 
He has never yet said anything without recognizing every implication of
His words.  We may be certain, then, that
every logical implication of Scripture is one that God intends for us to
recognize.

Of course, this principle can be abused.  People draw conclusions from the Bible all
the time that the text does not warrant. 
However, the basic principle remains. 
We can and should reason from what God has said to what He has left
unsaid but intends.  Otherwise, we may
find ourselves in the position of the Sadducees in Matthew 22.  They failed to consider what the word implied
about the resurrection.  Jesus told them,
“You are mistaken, not understanding the Scriptures nor the power of God.”  May we all avoid similar mistakes!
