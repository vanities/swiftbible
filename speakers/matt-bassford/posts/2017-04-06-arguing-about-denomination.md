Bible geek that I am, I follow all the various Bible
publishers and merchants on Facebook.  A little while back, Evangelical Bible (which is both a merchant and a
publisher, incidentally) put up a Facebook poll that asked, “Which denomination
do our customers belong to?”  I know that
brethren buy a lot of Bibles from Evangelical Bible (typically, purchasers of
premium Bibles like to read and study the Bible a lot), so I glanced down the
list to satisfy my curiosity.  Sure
enough, there it was:  “Church of
Christ”, listed as a denomination.



My first impulse was to message the Evangelical Bible
customer-service rep (she’s a friend of mine; no big deal) and point out that
as one who has always been associated with the churches of Christ, I regard
myself as belonging to a religious tradition, not a denomination.  My second impulse, which I ultimately
followed, was to let it go.  It’s kind of
like when the funeral-home director calls me “Pastor”.  He means well, he won’t understand the reason
for the correction, and by next year, he’ll have forgotten anyway and be
calling me “Pastor” again.

However, the incident did remind me of the simmering
controversy among brethren about whether the churches of Christ are a
denomination or not.  On one side, you
have more traditional Christians who regard our distinctiveness from “the
denominations” as an important doctrinal point. 
On the other, you have less traditional Christians, often those who are
unhappy with the status quo in their congregations, who insist that there is a
“Church of Christ” denomination, and that “we” also teach as doctrine the
commandments of men (I put “we” in quotation marks because churches that have
“Church of Christ” on the sign out front engage in a staggering diversity of
practices, so much so that I’m not sure who “we” is).

In support of these various positions, people introduce
various definitions of “denomination”, some of which seem to fit, some of which
don’t.  Edward Fudge [writes,](http://edwardfudge.com/2012/03/is-church-of-christ-a-denomination/) “Any group of Christians larger than a local assembly but smaller than the
universal body of Christ, which takes a name that sets it apart from other
Christians who are not part of it, is a ‘denomination’ by my definition. This
definition, it seems to me, usually fits the “Church of Christ” which descended
from the Stone-Campbell restoration movement.” 
Conversely, Donald A. Nash [argues,](http://www.thecra.org/files/WhyNotDenom.pdf) “Now, a denomination is a group of professed followers of Christ which
divides itself from other professed Christians by adopting procedures over and
above those found in the Scriptures, thus dividing the church universal.”  I endorse neither of these arguments; I
merely cite them as representative.

My own convictions on the matter are twofold.  I don’t use the word “denomination” to refer
to the churches of Christ, but I also think that the denomination-or-not
argument is a distractor from what’s really important.

Let’s tackle the first conviction first.  In all spiritual matters, my goal is to
imitate the beliefs and practices of the New-Testament church.  I know that first-century Christians pleased
God, so I want to be as much like them as possible.  As part of this, I strive to use language that
mirrors the language of the first century. 
Even though people in the world call me a pastor, I’m not a pastor.  Scripturally speaking, I’m not qualified.  Instead, I’m a preacher, like the preachers
of the first century.  Similarly, I
prefer “Lord’s Supper” to “Eucharist” and “assembly” to “worship service”.

“Denomination” is not a word that appears in the Bible.  Why use it when “tradition” and “brotherhood”
do appear?  If, by contrast, I find
myself participating in something that these Scriptural terms are inadequate to
describe, that’s probably a sign that I’ve departed from the Scriptural
pattern.

However, I’m not going to get terribly exercised about
whether somebody insists that I belong to a denomination or not.  The “denomination” label is nothing more than
a mask over what’s truly important.

Let me illustrate. 
Let’s say (and I believe this to be the case) that I belong to a
congregation that in all things devotes itself to faithfully following the word
of God.  We don’t elevate human traditions
to the level of Scripture, we don’t join in inter-church organizations, and we
spend the Lord’s money the same way that brethren did 2000 years ago.  However, somebody wants to point a finger at
us and say, “You’re part of a denomination.” 
What difference does that make? 
Does it in any way diminish our faithfulness or make us less pleasing to
God?  It’s a Scripturally irrelevant
issue.

On the other hand, let’s say that I belong to a church with
a very different orientation.  We have
human traditions that are just as important, or even more important to us, than
the Bible is.  We cooperate with other
churches as first-century churches did not. 
We spend money on all sorts of various projects, some Biblical, some
not.  However, we proudly insist that we
don’t belong to a denomination, and nobody argues with us.  Does the lack of the label make our practices
any more correct according to the Scripture? 
Again, it’s a distractor from what matters.

All my adult life, I’ve been suspicious of religious arguments
that are based on magic words, and I see the same problem here.  A church is not made good or bad by whether
somebody calls it a denomination or not. 
Faithfulness to God is evaluated according to the Scripture, not
according to a term that doesn’t even appear in it.  Rather than getting caught up in wrangling
about words (I seem to recall having read about that someplace), let’s instead
devote ourselves to discerning God’s will and following it.
