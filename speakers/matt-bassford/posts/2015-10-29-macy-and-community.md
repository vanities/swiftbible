Unusually for me, I have not planned my various posts on
Macy to cohere together, but it is clear to me in retrospect that some themes
have emerged.  Chief among them is that
of aloneness:  being apparently alone in
wrestling with child loss as a father, leaving my wife alone because I was too
absorbed with my own grief.

However, there was one thing I did right, and one way in
which I was not alone.  From the very
beginning, Lauren and I were surrounded by the love of other Christians, and
both of us chose to accept that rather than pulling away from it.



Even today, I remain humbled, floored even, by the love we
experienced.  Brethren came from hundreds
of miles away to be there for Macy’s funeral. 
Christians from all around the country sent us thousands of dollars,
even though we hadn’t asked for a dime. 
We still have a tub filled with sympathy cards that well-wishers, 99
percent of whom were Christians, sent us. 
My favorite:  a card that said
only, in all caps about half an inch high, “TRUST”.

Closer to home, the members of the Joliet church did more
than we could have asked.  Brethren often
respond to grief in a loved one by throwing food at the problem.  In our case, they brought us enough meals
that we were literally eating them for months thereafter.  From time to time, various of the men would
corner me and subject me to searching inquiries:  “How are you? 
No; how are you *really*?”

Perhaps the most telling incident of all, though, came when
a couple of men from the congregation visited our hospital room a day or two
after Macy had died.  They said, “We have
no idea what to say, but we love you, so here we are.”

From time to time, I’ll see these lists online:  “15 Things Not to Say to Grieving People.”  They turn the offering of sympathy into a
kabuki dance, where you have to say and do everything just so, or you will give
horrible offense.  I don’t buy it.  I think there’s one rule, and it’s
simple.  Don’t try to fix things.  Don’t try to fix the mourners.  Just love them, and what you do will be
right.  So far as I can remember,
everybody who interacted with us nailed it.

Really, losing Macy was two life-changing events, not
one.  The first was her death
itself.  The second was the way that our
Christian family reacted to it.  The
first changed me in a whole bunch of ways, some bad, some good.  The second made me long to help others the
way that others had helped me.  I’m in
debt, and I intend to spend the rest of my life paying on it (more on that in a
little bit).

Of course, none of this was coincidental.  It wasn’t like hundreds of loving, helpful
people popped up in my life like dandelions in my front yard.  Instead, their appearance was the result of
the way I had chosen to live—not as an individual, but as a part of a community
of believers.

Just like Americans don’t do grief well, they don’t do
community well.  In the 15 years since
Robert Putnam wrote *Bowling Alone*,
the fragmentation of US society has only increased.  Membership in everything from churches to Lions
Clubs is declining.  Causes range from
work-life balance issues to the prevalence of the Internet.  If you can find a group of 50 people online
who are exactly like you, why bother cultivating real-life friendships with
people who aren’t?

The problem is that establishing and maintaining membership
in a group is a skill, and it’s a skill that fewer and fewer of us have.  From time to time, Lauren and I will find
ourselves remarking on people who don’t seem to get community.  They visit the Joliet church a few times, or
even become members for a short while, but they don’t appear to understand how
to connect with others, how to belong. 
Because they never form bonds with anybody, in time, they drift off.  By contrast, others have a knack for
connecting and in six months have melded seamlessly with the church.

This is not an introvert-extrovert issue.  Extroverts may be better at developing dozens
of surface friendships, but introverts are better at nurturing a few close
friendships.  People who don’t know how
to connect at all are neither extroverts nor introverts.  They’re non-verts.

There are two problems with being a non-vert.  The first is that non-verts don’t have a
support structure when things in their lives go terribly wrong, as they always
do sooner or later.  When members of a
group encounter disaster, they have a choice. 
They can indulge the inward turning of grief, or they can turn outward
to receive support and consolation from the other members of their group.  People who don’t belong don’t have a
choice.  Inward they must go, and no
matter how much professional help they seek, counselors will always remain a
poor substitute for the fellowship of kindred minds.

I intentionally describe this problem in non-religious terms
because I’m not sure that this kind of group strength is a purely religious
phenomenon.  It may be, but my personal
experience with non-religious groups is limited enough that I don’t feel that I
can say either way.

The second problem, though, is purely religious.  If you don’t belong to a group of Christians,
you will never experience Christ.  Here
is the fatal flaw in our cultural contempt for “organized religion.”  Here is the contradiction in saying, “I’m
spiritual, but I’m not religious.” 
Unless you are religious in the sense of being bound together with
others, you can’t be spiritual either.

All of these posts about Macy have been
self-revelatory.  When I wrote above that
the love of other Christians put me in the position of paying on an unpayable
debt, that’s something I’ve obviously felt for the past seven years but never
articulated.  It surprised me.  “Wait!” I said when I read what I had
written, “Am I supposed to be saying things like that about anybody but Jesus?”

As I thought about it, though, I realized that I was talking
about Jesus.  In talking about myself as
the recipient, I was talking about Jesus in the very highest sense of Matthew
25.  What anybody did for me, they did
for Him.  In talking about other
Christians as the givers, I was talking about Jesus, who loved me and gave
Himself for me.  In talking about the
overwhelming grace shown me by my brethren, I was talking about the
overwhelming grace of Jesus, of which their actions are an echo.

The experience of seeking Christ with a group of other
believers is like trying to listen to a distant radio station.  The signal isn’t very strong, so the sound is
garbled and staticky.  You grind your
teeth and fiddle with the tuning knob, but the result is generally more
frustration than anything.  Sometimes,
though, when the atmospheric conditions are just right, the signal comes in
true and clear, and you understand what you were looking for all along.

By contrast, seeking Christ without a group of believers is
like trying to listen to a distant radio station without a radio.  You don’t have to put up with any static, but
you’ll never hear a sound.

Even when you most desperately want to.
