In the adult class recently, we’ve been studying the book of
Daniel.  One of the main themes of that
book is the struggle of men such as Daniel, Shadrach, Meshach, and Abed-nego to
retain their identity as Jews as captives in the middle of a hostile
empire.  Even when their lives were on
the line, they insisted on behaving like the people of God.

In many ways, our situation is parallel to theirs.  We too find ourselves in a great nation whose
values are not ours, where others constantly influence and even pressure us to
behave in ungodly ways.  We too have to
fight to maintain our identity as God’s people, and our motivation is much the
same as Daniel’s.  It’s because we are
God’s people.  We belong to Him;
therefore, we have to live for Him.  With
this in mind, let’s look this morning at the Lamb’s call to holiness.



**1 Peter 1:13-25**

The text in Scripture that best describes this call is 1
Peter 1:13-25, so let’s turn there for the first part of our study today.  Within this connection, we first see Peter
link **OBEDIENCE AND HOLINESS**.  This link appears in 1 Peter 1:13-16.  Peter opens this section with a stirring call
to obedience.  In context, this appeal is
all the more powerful because Peter is making it to Christians who are beset by
persecution and suffering.  In stern
first-century fashion, Peter is telling them that their difficult situation
doesn’t matter.  They don’t get to lapse
back into the evil practices that the world around them tolerates.  Instead, they must devote themselves
completely to God.

This is a lot to ask for, but Peter justifies his
exhortation by citing the nature of God. 
Because God is holy, we are responsible for being holy ourselves.  Throughout Scripture, we never see God
sin.  We never see Him waver in his
character or His actions.  His goodness
is unmarred by the slightest hint of evil, and He cannot tolerate evil in His presence.  He is holy. 
If we are truly His people and desire to dwell in His presence, we have
to be the same way.  We can’t be of two
minds, behaving one way during church and another way the rest of the
week.  We must be holy too.  If we aren’t, we demonstrate that we aren’t
actually the people of God either.

Next, Peter examines the connection between **REDEMPTION AND HOLINESS.** Let’s look here at 1 Peter 1:17-19.  Here, Peter is relying on a human
universal.  We value things according to
the price we pay for them.  As any
landlord can tell you, there’s a vast difference between being a home-renter
and a homeowner.  Renters will put holes
in walls, randomly pull the tiles down off shower walls, and trash the carpets.  Owners usually don’t do those things.  Why? 
Because the owner paid a lot of money for that house, and the renter didn’t.

In the same way, we need to evaluate our relationship with
God in light of the price that was paid for us. 
We weren’t redeemed from our sins by silver or gold or any other earthly
thing.  Instead, God bought us with the
precious blood of Christ.  The death of
His Son is the value He assigned to us. When He has paid such an
incomprehensible price for us, and we show our contempt for that price by
willful sin, we are certain to anger Him. 
Therefore, says Peter, we must conduct ourselves in fear.  The ransom of Christ’s death obliges us to be
holy.

Third, Peter connects **HOPE
AND HOLINESS.** Here, let’s read 1
Peter 1:20-25.  We are obedient not
merely because we are the people of God, nor because we have been purchased
with the blood of Christ.  We are also
obedient because we have a hope.  This is
a hope that Christ offers only to His people, and no one else can claim
it.  We know that everything on this
earth is passing, but the promises of God in His word are eternal, and they
inform us that if we are faithful, God will raise us from the dead and give us
glory just as He did Christ.

That’s a big “if”, though. 
It doesn’t apply to Christians who fall away, nor even to those who
lackadaisically go through the motions. 
Instead, because we have this hope, we should learn from it to purify
not only the outward man, but even our souls. 
We have to bring every thought into captivity to the obedience of
Christ.  We can’t regard our brethren
with indifference or even vague good intentions.  We must love one another fervently from the
heart.

We all know what it means to strive and sacrifice for a
goal.  When I was preparing to go to law
school, I knew that my score on the LSAT would have a huge effect on where I
went and what kind of scholarships I got when I went there.  As a result, I studied for the LSAT a couple
of hours a day for six months.  I went
through book after book of practice LSAT’s. 
Why?  Because the goal was worth
the work.  Brethren, the prize before us
is incomparably better.  Doesn’t it make
sense for us to put in the work that we need to attain it?

Before the Lord’s Supper, let’s reflect on these
things.  Let’s remember first of all the
blood of Christ, the blood that makes our entrance into heaven even
possible.  If Jesus had not died on the
cross for us, every one of us would be doomed. 
However, now that we have been sprinkled with that blood, let’s examine
ourselves and make sure that we’re living according to the price that was paid
for us.  There’s no way to God but
holiness.  Let’s consider this as we
partake.

Somewhat to my surprise, my streak of sermons from requests
continues.  A couple of weeks ago after
services, Kate Norris mentioned that she had been studying Job recently and
asked me to preach on it.  Of course, I
don’t have a problem with preaching on anything in Scripture, but Job strikes
me as a particularly useful topic. 
Contrary to all the health-and-wealth nonsense, we know that the life of
even the most faithful believer contains plenty of hardship, and we need to understand
how to handle that.  With this goal in
mind, let’s see what lessons we can learn from Job.

**The Frame Tale**

Structurally speaking, the book of Job is divided into two
parts, the poetic discussions that make up the vast majority of the book, and two
little prose bookends at the beginning and end. 
These bookends are called a frame tale, and we’re going to start by
considering it.

The first thing that the frame tale tells us is that **LIFE IS INCOMPREHENSIBLE.** We see one reason why this is so in Job
1:6-12.  In this section, we catch a
glimpse of the spiritual interaction between God and Satan.  It’s almost like they have a wager on how Job
will act if his blessings are taken from him. 
Satan thinks he’ll reject God, but God thinks he will remain faithful.

Two things are worth noting about this.  The first is that even though we are
presented with this encounter early on in the story, Job has no idea that it
takes place, and indeed, throughout the entire book, never learns that it has
happened.  In the same way, friend, there
are almost certainly spiritual events that will have a massive effect on our
lives that we don’t get to know about either.

Second, we must also recognize that this story raises more
questions than it answers.  Sure, it
makes sense that bad things happen to Job because God allows Satan to do them,
but why does God allow them in the first place? 
All we can say is that God’s thoughts are not our thoughts.  For this reason, attempting to assign any
meaning to the events of our lives is often impossible.

Similarly, the story teaches us that **PROSPERITY IS PRECARIOUS.** Read
with me from Job 1:13-19.  Presumably,
Job is wise as well as good.  He’s done
everything he can to ensure security and prosperity for himself and for his
family, but then, all of a sudden, wham! 
He goes from rich to penniless in a moment, and all 10 of his children
die in a single disaster.  He has lost
basically everything on earth that he values, and he’s lost it more or less
instantaneously.  As the story continues,
he even adds his good health to the list of his losses.

In the same way, friends, we must acknowledge that our
earthly blessings are no more certain. 
No matter how prudent and thoughtful we may be, there is nothing we can
do to guarantee that the good times will continue to roll.  You can be amazing with money and still end
up bankrupt.  You can be a health-food
and fitness nut and still get cancer. 
You can be the helicopteriest mom out there and still lose your
children.  Anything we have is something
we can easily lose.

This is a grim point, but it shouldn’t be a call to
despair.  Instead, it should be a
reminder to set our hopes on the certain blessings of the spirit.  Only those who trust in Christ will not be
disappointed.  If we build our lives
around anything or anyone else, we’re riding for a fall.

Third, we learn that **TRIAL
DOES NOT EXCUSE SIN.** Consider Job’s
attitude in Job 1:20-22.  Even though he
faces extreme, life-destroying sorrow, he doesn’t allow that trial to turn him
away from God.  As we’ll see in the next
section, he goes through all sorts of emotional gyrations in his relationship
with God, but he never sins in what he says or what he does.  In fact, the eventual good outcome of the
story is due to Job’s determination to stay faithful.

Sadly, even though our trials are much less severe, we all
too often allow them to lead us into temptation.  We act like the difficulty of our situation
gives us the license to sin.  My spouse
hasn’t been treating me right, so that excuses my porn habit or my affair.  My brother in Christ was inconsiderate to me,
so I’m allowed to blow my top at him. 
Life has been too hard recently, so I get to spend my family into bankruptcy
or use alcohol as a painkiller.  The list
goes on and on.

As reasonable as these justifications may sound to us, they
are never anything more than excuses for sin. 
Serving God is not about doing the right thing when every circumstance
is in our favor and it’s easy.  It’s
about doing the right thing all the time, regardless of circumstances, even
when it’s the most difficult thing in the world to do.  When the way is hard, we don’t get to turn
aside to the passing pleasures of sin. 
We have to take up our crosses and follow Jesus.

Finally, the frame tale teaches us that **GOD IS FAITHFUL.** We see the
outcome for Job in Job 42:10-13. 
Everything he lost, he got back. 
Now, we shouldn’t expect the faithfulness of God to show up in our lives
on such a literalistic level.  God was
faithful to Jesus too, but Jesus had to die to see the good stuff on the other
side.  The same may be true for us.

However, we shouldn’t allow that to obscure the point.  In the end, it will be well for the people of
God.  It will be so amazingly,
overwhelmingly well that not a soul will complain about the path it had to
travel to get there.  Sometimes, we may
get some of our own back in this life. 
Periods of earthly trial may well be concluded by seasons of earthly
blessing.

Even then, though, that’s not the point.  The best moment of all of our lives on earth
is not worthy to be compared to the worst moment of life in heaven.  That’s why we’re Christians:  not because we hope that God is going to
bless us out of his earthly goody bag, but because we have our eyes on the real
prize.  If we endure, we can be certain
that He will perform what He has promised.

**When in Trial**

In the final part of our study this morning, I want to
consider the non-frame-tale part of Job, which actually makes up the vast
majority of the book, to see how we can or should behave when in trial.  There is so much to be said here that I am
going to skip right over many important lessons, but we do have time to look at
three of the most important ones.

Of these, one of them is certainly that **QUESTIONING GOD IS OK.** Consider,
for instance, Job’s behavior in Job 23:1-5. 
Not surprisingly, Job doesn’t understand why he’s going through what
he’s going through, and he wants answers! 
We’ve all heard people say similar things before, but what’s interesting
about Job is that we know what God’s perspective on his questioning is.  All the way through this, Job never sinned
with his lips, so his questioning can’t be sin either.

In many ways, this conflicts with our sense of how
Christians are supposed to behave.  We
have this idea that come what may, we are supposed to face the trials of our
lives with sweet, pious resignation.  If
something happens that we don’t understand, maybe even something that shakes
our faith, it’s wrong to think about that, and we have to bottle it up and hope
that God doesn’t notice.

If the book of Job teaches us anything, it should teach us
that that isn’t true.  We serve a God who
is mindful of our frame.  We serve a
Savior who became flesh and dwelt among us, who experienced the trials and
hardships that we experience.  Even Jesus
didn’t meet the trials of His life with serene acceptance.  Why would God expect us to do what His Son
didn’t?  We can’t bottle ourselves up in
our relationship with our Creator.  We
need to bring those struggles to Him.

Second, and even more provocatively, Job shows us that **GETTING MAD AT GOD IS OK.** There’s really no other spin we can put
on his words in Job 30:20-23.  Job’s
pretty blunt.  All of these bad things
are happening to him because God is treating him unfairly.  As we know from the frame tale, he’s wrong
about this.  God isn’t the one who blew
his life up.  It was the devil.  However, even in criticizing God for
something He didn’t do, Job still didn’t sin with his lips.

There are certainly times when getting angry at God isn’t
appropriate.  As the saying goes,
“Everything happens for a reason, but sometimes the reason is that you make bad
decisions.”  When you stick your arm in
the woodchipper, don’t blame God for the result!

However, there are times when we say along with Job, “I
didn’t deserve this.”  There’s no obvious
link to sin or foolishness on our part; we just get hit by the lightning bolt
out of the clear blue sky.  In situations
like that, even the best Christians are prone to get mad at God, and God is
perfectly capable of handling that.  I
don’t take it too much to heart when my kids get mad at me and declare that I’m
the worst dad ever.  Doesn’t God have at
least as much patience as we do?  In our
dealings with Him, we don’t have to pretend to be anything other than what we
are.

Finally though, we must learn from Job **NOT TO GIVE UP ON GOD.** Job
clearly didn’t, as we see in Job 19:25-26. 
Even when Job was questioning, even when Job was mad, He still continued
to look to God.  Sometimes, though,
people in trial do give up on Him.  They
decide that if He is allowing them to go through this, either He isn’t real or
He isn’t worthy of their love.  At the
other end of the spectrum, they can decide that this must be about them, that
God is punishing them because they’re so worthless, and they can never expect
anything good from Him again.

As appealing as those feelings may be sometimes, we must
remember that they offer us neither comfort nor hope.  When we go down the path that Job didn’t,
when we do curse God and die, it might give Satan the giggles, but it doesn’t
do anything useful for God or us either one. 
If we are faithful to Him, God is not going to forsake us.  He’s never given us any reason to believe
that He will.  Through whatever we face,
then, let’s make sure we face it with Him.
