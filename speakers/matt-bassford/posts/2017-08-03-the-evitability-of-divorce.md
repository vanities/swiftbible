There are all sorts of plans to “make America great again”
being thrown around Washington these days. 
However, I haven’t seen any of them mention what is actually one of the
most serious problems our nation faces: 
the decline of the American family. 
The further we move from the Biblical ideal of one man, one wife, for
life, the more trouble we get into.



This is true even in a purely economic sense, especially for
women.  All the protestations of the
feminist movement aside, the path to prosperity for women still looks like
this:

* Don’t have children out of wedlock.
* Get married.
* Don’t get divorced.

Women who manage all three of these things will generally
also find their way to financial stability. 
By contrast, divorce is one of the worst financial disasters that can
befall anyone, and single motherhood is strongly correlated with poverty.  Of course, this is a generalization, not a
prophecy.  Exceptions to the rule abound,
but the rule is still the rule.

The intangible harms caused by the disintegration of the
family are equally great.  Back in the
day, while divorce was shifting from taboo to acceptable, proponents of divorce
frequently argued that it was actually best for the children for a husband and
wife who couldn’t get along to go their separate ways.

Nobody says that anymore because we now have decades of
social-science research that demonstrate that it isn’t true.  Across the board, children of divorcees fare
worse in life than children who come from intact families.  For instance, [this study](http://www.huffingtonpost.com/2011/01/24/divorce-and-suicidal-idea_n_812456.html) found that men brought up in broken homes were three times more likely to
commit suicide than men who weren’t. 
Three times!  Other studies have
found significant increases in drug abuse, alcohol abuse and mental illness
among children who experience the end of their parents’ marriage.  In sum, divorce leaves behind wreckage that
families will stumble over for generations.

As a result, it is with great sadness that I read articles
like [this one](https://www.yahoo.com/news/why-first-marriage-failed-yours-235821185.html).  I foresee lots of pain ahead for everybody
who contributed to the list.  Worse
still, as I was reading through the list of reasons for failed marriage, I kept
thinking to myself, “It didn’t have to end like that!”

There were, of course, exceptions.  The woman whose husband serially and
unrepentantly cheated on her didn’t have a whole lot of choice.  That marriage was done for whether she
actually filed for divorce or not.  Some
spouses are determined to be evil.

In the other cases, though, I saw a lot of pain and a lot of
ignorance about how marriage is supposed to work.  It may well be that many of these people are
children of divorcees themselves, and whether our marriages are good, bad, or
cratered, we are assuredly teaching our children how to be married.  They will most likely live out the patterns
they learn from us.

Regardless of where their confusion came from, though, it’s
obvious that all the divorcees in the article were confused.  In particular, I think their confusion came
from three things:

**“Marriage Is About
Me”**

This theme underlies many of the specific reasons
cited.  It’s behind the women who
complained about losing themselves in the marriage and the men who shied away
from emotional commitment.  Ultimately,
their marriages failed because they were focused on themselves rather than
their spouses.

Here, I can’t help but be reminded of the Lord’s words in
Matthew 16:25, where He says, “Whoever would save his life will lose it, but
whoever loses his life for My sake will find it.”  This paradoxical truth is at the core not
only of Christianity but of life in general. 
If we are concerned primarily about ourselves, if we are interested primarily
in our rights and our desires, we will surely end up lonely, unhappy, and
unsatisfied.  By contrast, if we are
invested in others, in treating them as we ourselves want to be treated, we
will never lack for love and friends.

As a result, Paul observes in Ephesians 5:28 that he who
loves his wife loves himself (and the same is true, of course, of the woman who
loves her husband).  A policy of
selflessness is not only godly.  It is
wise.  When we spend our marriages
defending the selfish happiness we can’t hold onto anyway, we lose.  When we put our spouses first, not grudgingly
and resentfully, but from a genuine desire to seek their good, we win.  It’s true that some people manage to remain
obdurate jerks in the face of the kindest treatment imaginable (in which case
we still have a responsibility to treat them kindly anyway), but we can improve
nearly every relationship in our lives, including marriage, through
selflessness.

**“Marriage Is About
Compatibility, Not Commitment”**

I think these contributors have a fatalistic view of
marriage.  The way they see it, behind
the door of marriage is either a lady or a tiger, and you don’t know which
until you get married and open the door. 
Sometimes, you luck out and get the lady—a compatible partner.  Other times, you get the tiger, and there’s
nothing you can do about it.  Better keep
your lawyer on speed dial!

Don’t get me wrong; compatibility is nice.  It’s important to me that I’m married to a
wife who is as devoted to the Lord as I am. 
It helps that both of us are fascinated by people and place a high value
on friendships and relationships.  It
even matters that both of us are interested in current events and enjoy batting
around the political issues of the day together.

However, our compatibility is not the reason why we are
married and will stay married.  It is our
commitment to one another and to the institution of marriage, and that has to
be true in every marriage.  For one
thing, no two people will ever be perfectly compatible.  My wife has learned to enjoy watching college
football with me, but she will never share my interest in camping or the Civil
War.  I think there are things in every
marriage that can become divisive if one or both spouses allow them to be.  To such people, because marriage isn’t the
mind-meld that they imagined it could be, divorce is the only recourse.

Second, I think that most marriages will go through
stretches where compatibility becomes impossible.  We certainly did.  When our first daughter died, both my wife
and I invested so much emotional energy in grieving that we had little to spare
for one another.  For about a year
afterward, our relationship was straight-up rocky, and if either one of us had
been open to the possibility of divorce, we could have found all the reason we
needed right then.  However, both of us
were committed to our marriage, we stayed married, and over time, things got
better, and we repaired our relationship.

I’ve read studies that indicate that this is the rule.  Couples who hit a rough patch and stay
married anyway will generally find their way to marital happiness again.  Only in extreme cases, like repeated adultery
and physical abuse, does this not usually happen.  Compatibility doesn’t keep marriages
together.  Commitment does.  Indeed, I am convinced that two people who
are committed to God and to one another will have a successful marriage, and
that’s true regardless of how compatible they are.

**“Marriage Doesn’t
Take Work”**

Maybe sometimes it doesn’t. 
I’ve read that maybe 10 percent of marriages actually are storybook
marriages, where circumstances and common interests allow two people to float
blissfully down the stream of life together. 
I think my parents’ marriage was like that.  At least, they claimed it was.

The other 90 percent of us, though, are going to have to
exert more effort, and many of the contributors to the original article
lamented the effort they didn’t invest. 
No date nights.  No emotional
intimacy.  No daily insistence on
reconnecting.

For about 10 years now, I’ve been proclaiming that one of
the great forgotten virtues of Christianity is perseverance, and I think it’s
particularly relevant in marriage. 
Sometimes, we face the temptation to only be diligent when we can’t get
away with laziness.  I come in to work
every day because otherwise my boss will fire me.  I pay my bills every month so ComEd doesn’t
turn the electricity off.

In marriage, by contrast, the harms that come from not being
diligent are not as immediately obvious. 
If I don’t run that load of dishes, if I don’t spend a couple hours this
evening talking with my wife, neither one of those things is likely to provoke
an immediate blowup.  Neglecting them is
the path of least resistance.  However,
over time, this kind of neglect will corrode the fabric of a marriage, and in
many cases, it leads to divorce.  As
disciples, we are called upon to carry our crosses daily, to consistently do
what is hard because it is right.  We
should do this no less in our marriages than anywhere else.

**Conclusion**

The Yahoo! article contains 16 anecdotes of failed
marriage.  In almost every one of them,
there is a sense that failure was inevitable. 
We got divorced because there was nothing else we could do.  I think this is a tragic mistake.  If both spouses are willing to save a
marriage, there is always something they can do, and when divorce is so
disastrous, they have every reason in the world to do it.
