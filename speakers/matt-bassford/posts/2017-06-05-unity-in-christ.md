It may be that I’m getting old enough to get grumpy, but it
seems to me that the world is a much less optimistic place than it was 20 years
ago.  People look into the future, and
rather than feeling hopeful about what they anticipate, they feel
threatened.  They believe that unless
they take action to defend what they regard as rightfully theirs, it’s going to
be taken away from them.

This paranoia exists equally on the left and the right, and
where hope tends to draw people together, fear tends to pull them apart.  As a result, people in our country
increasingly associate with those whom they consider to be like them and
increasingly avoid those whom they do not.

This is problematic on many levels, but it is particularly
problematic when it comes to the church. 
Christ didn’t establish one church for every little grouplet out
there.  Instead, He established one
church, and He expects all of His people to come together in it.  This morning, then, let’s consider the
importance of and our responsibility to our unity in Christ.



**Challenges to Unity**

Let’s open our study this morning by looking at the
Scriptural witness about the challenges to our unity.  The first of these is **TRIBALISM.** We see it rearing
its ugly head in Acts 11:1-3.  As far as
the party of the circumcision was concerned, even by sharing a meal with some
Gentiles, Peter had done wrong.  Later,
when they learned that Gentiles could become Christians, they insisted that
they had to become Jews too.  Their
vision of righteousness excluded everybody else.

Today, we must make sure that our concept of fellowship
isn’t limited to those who are racially, economically, and socially like
us.  Honest hearts don’t come in
colors.  There are no tax brackets that
define righteousness.  In fact, it should
be true that when we come to church, we are confronted with more variation than
we see in any other area of our lives. 
Joliet is a diverse community, and if the church in Joliet doesn’t
reflect that diversity, we’re doing something wrong.

What’s more, we must make sure that we are equally open in
our friendships and relationships within the church.  It’s comforting for us to take our closest
Christian friends from our demographic peers. 
However, we’re most likely to learn and grow not from our encounters
with similarity, but from our encounters with difference.  There’s a whole symphony of human existence
out there, but when we listen only to those who are like us, we hear only a
single note.  Every brother and sister
here has something to teach us.  We need
to be willing to learn.

Second, we must beware of **DOCTRINAL DIVISION.** Consider
Paul’s warning about this in 1 Corinthians 1:10-12.  Even though Paul wrote this centuries before
the advent of denominationalism, I think we see the beginnings of the
denominational spirit here.  If everybody
in the church in Corinth had defined themselves by saying, “I am of Christ,”
there would have been no division. 
However, once they started defining themselves by claiming to follow the
teaching of different men, even men who didn’t want to be faction leaders,
division arose.  Not surprisingly, when
there were no longer apostles around to quell this sort of bad behavior, the
church splintered into a million different fragments, each one based around the
teaching or name of some man.

There’s a lesson here for us, and it’s not that we need to
wag our fingers at those who teach as doctrine the commandments of men.  It’s that we need to be careful to avoid the
same trap ourselves.  There’s nothing
wrong with reading things that people write about the Bible.  I wouldn’t maintain a blog if I thought there
were!  However, there is a danger
involved with so doing.  Particularly if
the author is not a Christian, the more we read from them, the more we will
imbibe concepts that aren’t Biblical, and this can happen even if the author is
a Christian.  The true source of division
isn’t studying the Bible.  It’s studying
things that aren’t.

Third, we must watch out for **INDIFFERENCE TO OUT-GROUPS.** Though
it certainly isn’t the last, the first instance of this happening in the
history of the church appears in Acts 6:1. 
In order to appreciate what’s going on here, we have to understand what
Luke means when he designates this particular group of widows as
“Hellenistic”.  These widows were Jewish,
but they weren’t from Jerusalem, nor even from Judea.  Instead, they were from someplace where the
dominant cultural influence was Greek, and so they spoke Greek and probably
adopted some Greek customs.  Because they
didn’t fit in as easily, they were overlooked.

Today, we must pay particular attention to those in our
congregation who are likely to be overlooked. 
If you look around, it’s pretty obvious what the in-group at Joliet
looks like.  It’s made up of married
couples in their 30s or early 40s.  Both
spouses in these families are faithful Christians.  They have children, but all of them are under
the age of 18.  They have relatives
outside of their nuclear family who are also members here.

Now, there’s nothing wrong with being somebody who checks
all of these boxes, but we must beware of behaving as though somebody who
doesn’t is a second-class Christian.  An
older sister who comes to church by herself is every bit as precious in God’s
sight as the head of a white-picket-fence family.  Let’s make sure that these people get our
attention and respect too.

Finally, let’s be wary of **SELF-ABSORPTION.** We see that
it can have a direct effect on unity in 3 John 9-10.  Diotrephes was concerned with his own status,
so he decided to throw his weight around by rejecting Christians whom he should
have welcomed.

This can certainly happen today, but I think that quieter
forms of self-absorption are more likely to be a problem.  We don’t have to be the church bully to live
a self-centered life.  Instead, it’s just
as self-centered to come to church only concerned with what we can get rather
than what we can give, or even not being all that interested in our fellow
Christians at all.

It’s a commonplace to observe that in the church we all need
each other, but I think the problems with inward focus go even deeper than
that.  We don’t need each other because
we need to be built up.  We need each
other because we need someone else to build up. 
Jesus defined discipleship as being made of selflessness and acts of
service.  Certainly, when we help someone
else, they benefit, but we benefit even more, because in that experience, we’ve
drawn nearer to Him.  When we’re only
interested in ourselves, we never serve, and we never grow.

**Christ’s Prayer for
Unity**

We might be tempted to dismiss failures of fellowship as an
insignificant problem, and indeed in practice many Christians do dismiss
them.  However, before we get too blasé
about the subject, we ought to consider the perspective that the Lord expresses
in His prayer for unity.  We find this
prayer in John 17, and because the whole prayer is a sermon in itself, we’re
going to focus particularly on the parts where Jesus appeals for oneness in His
people.

Let’s begin by considering His discussion of **UNITY AND AUTHORITY.** Look with me at John 17:9-11.  There’s a lot to study in these three verses,
but two particular points are especially worth exploring.  Even though Jesus spoke 2000 years ago and
did so in Aramaic that was translated into Greek that was translated in to
English, His sincerity here comes through perfectly.  It is vital to Him that His followers be
united.  When we consider the subsequent
centuries of religious disaster that have come from His people not being
unified, we have no trouble understanding why.

Also, note that Jesus specifies how He wants the Father to
make His people one.  He believes that
unity can only result if God keeps them in His name.  As usual, this doesn’t mean that Christians
are people who go around calling themselves by God’s name.  Instead, it means that unity and obedience to
God’s authority are inextricably linked. 
We cannot be united unless we all accept that authority, and if we are
not united, then somewhere, we are not being obedient.  Unity and fellowship with our brothers aren’t
a social extra.  They’re at the heart of
the gospel.

Second, Jesus highlights the connection between **UNITY AND EVANGELISM.** Here, let’s read from John 17:20-21.  First of all, Jesus specifies here that he’s
not only talking about the disciples present on that night.  Instead, He’s talking about all the disciples
who will ever believe because of the first disciples’ testimony about His
resurrection.  He wants all of these
people to be one because only then will they be in Christ.  Dissension, backbiting, and even apathy and
indifference are not a sign, brethren, that we are abiding in Jesus!

Jesus then reveals that when we abide in Him, others will
notice us and believe in Him as a result. 
Why?  Because unity and love among
Christians who are very different from one another are not traits that appear
in the world.  When visitors to our
assembly see brethren of different ages, races, backgrounds, and social classes
talking to one another, worshiping with one another, and caring for one
another, they see something that the wisdom of the world tells them is
impossible.  And in fact, in worldly
terms, it is impossible.  Such unity is a
sign of the power of Christ at work within us. 
The Lord is in the midst of a united group, and it leads others to
believe in Him.

Finally, look at the Lord’s thoughts about **UNITY AND GOD’S GLORY.** Read on in John 17:22-23.  Interestingly, here Jesus talks about God’s
gift of glory to us, not His authority, as the basis of Christian unity.  The connection here isn’t obvious, but it’s
profound.  Ultimately, the root of all division
is selfishness.  We’re too fearful,
proud, or self-satisfied to be interested in reaching out to others.

In turn, though, selfishness is rooted in a focus on this
life and a fear of death.  We are selfish
and refuse to give of ourselves because we believe life is about scarcity
rather than abundance.  If I give, I
won’t have.

Our hope of glory in Christ, however, sets us free from all
that.  In Christ, life isn’t about
clinging to everything good that I have until death renders it all futile
anyway.  Instead, life is about freely
giving of myself, because I know that on the last day, God will give back to me
far more than I could ever possibly surrender. 
Life isn’t about scarcity; it’s about infinite abundance.  When we consider one another from this
perspective of abundance, we will happily share ourselves and do all the things
that are the building blocks of unity.

This surrender of ourselves to one another in the hope of
God’s abundance is, of course, our reflection of the selfless love of Christ
for us.  When outsiders see this in our
congregation, they don’t merely come to believe in Christ.  They come to know Him.  God is love, and wherever His love is
revealed, He is present.  Our unity and
our desire to bring others into it is the most tangible evidence an unbeliever
will ever encounter that God loves him.

Before the Lord’s Supper, let’s remember the context in
which Jesus said all of these things. 
This prayer didn’t take place on some Galilean hilltop in the early days
of His ministry.  Instead, my impression
from the text is that Jesus literally prayed this while He was on His way from
the upper room to the garden of Gethsemane on the night of His betrayal.  These are the things that were most on His
mind then.  Puts a whole new spin on what
He was hoping to accomplish by His suffering and death, doesn’t it?

Above all else, Jesus loved us and desired to be one with
us.  Unity with us was so important to
Him that He was willing to offer His life on the cross to make it
possible.  He died both as our sacrifice
and as our example.  If unity was so important
to the One we call Master, how far should we be willing to go to make unity in
our congregation possible?  Let’s
consider as we partake.

**Building Unity**

Finally this morning, let’s consider three things we can
work on to build unity in our congregation. 
The first of these is **HOSPITALITY.** Let’s look here at Hebrews 13:1-2.  I don’t think we have to be worried about
angels these days, but showing hospitality to one another is still of vital
importance.  The unity that we’ve been
talking about throughout this lesson is a unity of the mind and the heart and
the spirit, and such unity becomes far easier when we get to know one
another.  In some ways, being in a church
where the people are strangers to one another is like being married to a
stranger.  If you want the relationship
to continue, the stranger part can’t.

Obviously, this doesn’t only mean that we have the people in
our families over for family dinners.  It
doesn’t only mean that we spend time with the best friend we’ve had since
forever.  It isn’t even only limited to
the people in the congregation who are like us. 
Instead, true hospitality is about inviting people we don’t know into
our homes so that we can get to know them.

This is not easy.  In
fact, the struggles that some Christians have with practicing hospitality
remind me of their struggles with evangelism. 
I wonder if in both cases the real problem is bringing people into our
lives whom we don’t already know.  We
simply don’t want new relationships.  If
true, this is tragic.  Building relationships
is at the heart of the gospel.  We are
called to bring others near to God as we ourselves have been brought near, and
hospitality is a key part of that.

Second, we can develop unity in the church through **RECIPROCAL CONCERN.** Consider Paul’s description of how the
church should operate in 1 Corinthians 12:21-25.  As Paul observes, every part of our bodies
has a function, and we don’t consider one part of the body less important than
another because its function is different. 
I’ve never seen my kidneys, and neither has anybody else, but I’m sure
glad they’re down there filtering junk out of my blood!

In the same way, brethren, there are members of the body of
this congregation that do work that is more visible than the work of
others.  My work, for instance, is
probably the most visible of all, and it’s important work.  Every church needs somebody to preach and
teach.  However, every church also needs
somebody who’s willing to teach children’s classes, to visit the sick and
shut-ins, and to notice when people aren’t around and check up on them.  Those functions are not as obvious as the
work of the preacher, but without them, the church will get sick and die.

Because everybody is equally important, we need to make sure
that we’re treating everybody as equally important.  Whenever we encounter a brother or sister
engaged in some quiet work of service, we need to express our appreciation to
them.  We need to listen to their
concerns.  We need to make sure that
through every means available to us, we convey to them that they matter.

Finally, we develop unity by developing **THE MIND OF CHRIST.** Paul
explains what this means in Philippians 2:3-5. 
It’s a fact of life that in every human organization, there is a
tendency toward chaos, and one of the biggest organization-wreckers out there
is selfishness.  From kingdoms to
companies, countless groups have been destroyed because the people in them
couldn’t get along and started fighting one another.

Of course, this happens in churches all the time too.  If we in the Joliet church start insisting on
getting our own way in everything, it won’t be a whole lot longer before we’re
gone and our building gets converted to a bar or a pizzeria.  In fact, we don’t even have to work very hard
to get there.  All we have to be is our
natural, worldly human selves.

This is why Paul’s encouragement to put others first is so
important.  We don’t naturally think this
way; instead, it’s something we have to learn from Jesus.  He is our example of humility, of absence of
worldly ambition, and of putting others’ interests before our own.  Only when we are willing to sacrifice our
wants to the good of the whole will we build up rather than tearing down.

I suspect every Christian here would agree to this in
principle, but churches are split all the time because brethren don’t put it
into practice.  In particular, we must be
aware of the danger of doctrinal disagreements becoming a stalking horse for
personal enmity.  There’s a church not
too far from here that split because one of the members didn’t like what the
preacher’s daughter was wearing.  Issues
like that need to be worked out quietly and lovingly rather than brewing up
into an explosion that takes the whole church down.  90 percent or more of the time, what somebody
thinks is a necessary stand for the truth is an avoidable conflict based on a
personal difference that Jesus wants us to resolve anyway.
