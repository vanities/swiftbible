**OVERVIEW:** The LPVT performed credibly everywhere I
used it.  Its most unusual and annoying
feature was the complete lack of cross-references.

This is a Bible that got quite a workout.  I preached three sermons out of it, taught
four auditorium Bible classes, held two in-home Bible studies, and used it
heavily in my office for two weeks. 
Here’s what I thought:

**PREACHING:** This is a Bible that flunks the first
test to which I put preaching Bibles. 
It’s got 5/16” outer margins, which isn’t nearly enough room to put a
sermon outline in, and the paper is so thin (more on this) that you wouldn’t
want to write on it even if there were room. 
However, I know full well that not every Bible can be a wide-margin
Bible, so I broke out the sermon notebook and pressed on.

Like every other Bible larger than a Pitt Minion, the LPVT
didn’t coexist well with my notebook in the pulpit.  There isn’t room to have both notebook and
Bible open right next to each other.  The
price I pay for asking for a small pulpit to better fit on a cramped stage, I
guess.  However, because the LPVT is
comparatively light, I didn’t have great difficulty with juggling the two.

I did miss a citation during one sermon, but it wasn’t the
Bible’s fault.  I wanted Matthew 22:23
but found and started reading Matthew 21:23, my confusion only heightened by
the similarity of the two verses.  I
don’t recall missing any verse numbers, though, which is hardly surprising in a
Bible with relatively short lines and relatively large (though still
superscript) verse numbers.

**TEACHING:** The size of the LPVT worked well here
too.  I was able to float around where I
wanted in the front of the auditorium with my Bible still in my hand.  It didn’t strike me as weighing a whole lot
more than my lesson book.

However, I did notice the absence of any sort of
cross-references from the LPVT.  This is
extremely unusual for a Crossway Bible. 
Even Crossway’s “text” Bibles are, in my experience, almost always
tweener Bibles.  They’ll contain
references for Old-Testament prophecies cited in the New Testament and
citations for parallel accounts in the gospels. 
This is true of, for instance, Crossway’s normal Value Thinline and
Large-Print Compact.  It’s a feature I
like and find useful.

However, this wasn’t the case in the LPVT.  My guess is that the decision-makers at
Crossway told the designers, “Make a Bible with 10-point type as small (and
therefore cheap) as you can possibly make it.” 
As a result, even the minimal cross-references in the Value Thinline
went, leaving only translators’ notes behind. 
This is a true text Bible.

On the one hand, this makes for an extremely clean, pretty
layout, enough so that I find myself wondering a little bit whether Crossway
has plans for it.  On the other, teaching
a Bible class with a text Bible can leave you feeling lonely.  If somebody asks you a question, either you
know where the verse is off the top of your head, or you’re not going to know
it.  Your Bible will not rescue you.  I don’t think I found myself in any jams
where a reference Bible would have helped me out, but the experience did feel a
little bit like coasting down a mountain with the brakes out.

**STUDY:** The size was fine here too.  The Bible’s not at all hard to hold up, and
it fits easily in my lap when I’m sitting at a computer typing.  The moral of the story, I think, is that the
distance from my wrists to my elbows is longer than eight inches, so the Bible fits
into the space I’m leaving already.

This is another place, though, where I missed
cross-references.  In particular, I
missed the cross-references to parallel passages in the Synoptic Gospels.  One of the sermons I wrote was a comparison
of Jesus’ answer to the Sadducees about the resurrection in Matthew, Mark, and
Luke.  As is often the case, I know the
Matthew reference off the top of my head (Matthew 22), but I had to go hunting
for the accounts in Mark and Luke (Mark 12 and Luke 20, by the way).  A cross-reference at the bottom of Matthew’s
version would have saved me all the flippage.

I did notice, though, that the LPVT retains a
back-of-the-Bible concordance, albeit a modest one.  That isn’t true, for instance, of the Crossway
Single-Column Journaling Bible.  This
makes me wonder a little bit about whether Crossway actually cut the
cross-references to save space.  Maybe
something in the layout made bottom-of-the-page notes difficult.  Maybe Crossway is experimenting with a true
text-Bible layout to see how people like it. 
I don’t know.  I’m intrigued, yet
puzzled.

I didn’t miss any citations, at least that I noticed, in
anything that I wrote during the trial period. 
I did, however, notice a citation that I missed in the lesson book that
I’m teaching out of currently.  Sigh.

**CONCLUSION:** I liked using this Bible.  I would have liked it better had it been
smaller and had more cross-references. 
As always, in my subjective evaluation system, Bibles that offer features
I don’t care about (like larger print) are going to fare worse than Bibles that
exclude them.  Bible design is always a
series of compromises, and sometimes, those compromises are tilted in a
direction I don’t like.  However, this is
far from a bad Bible.  I give it a 7.5/10
in preaching, teaching, and study.
