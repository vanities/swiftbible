Even in the free-for-all of online religious discussion,
some things are sacred.  As a result, it
was with great surprise that I saw [this article](http://thefederalist.com/2017/06/28/vacation-bible-school-drove-millennials-away-church/#.WVRG1vrX9-Q.facebook) in my Facebook news feed yesterday.  A
writer for *The Federalist*, of all
things, criticizes the American tradition of Vacation Bible School as being
responsible for the decline of religious interest among millennials.  My stars and garters!  Next week, I guess the author is going to
follow up with a hatchet job on Mom and apple pie.


Once we get past our knee-jerk defense of a cherished
practice, though (and if I felt my knees jerk, I suspect most people did), the
author has some valid points to make. 
He’s not opposed to VBS per se (indeed, he writes VBS material himself);
instead, he’s opposed to VBSses that are more about culture than about Christ.  He lampoons the habit of theming this year’s
VBS after last year’s summer cinematic blockbuster, of being more concerned
about hipness and relevance than spiritual depth.

Not surprisingly, the article generated a great deal of
discussion in the Facebook thread where I saw it.  Some had observed the same problem; others
defended their VBS as being both Scripturally sound and evangelistically
effective.  At a guess, both sets of
commenters are correctly describing their experiences.  Like any other work of the church, VBS can be
done in a useful and edifying fashion, or it can be done in a way that creates
the illusion of usefulness while actually accomplishing no spiritual good.  The problems aren’t in the concept.  They’re in its implementation.

In fact, I think the author’s critique can be applied
profitably to every aspect of our congregational service to God.  On the one hand, cultural relevance is
important.  Paul’s sermon on Mars Hill in
Acts 17 is a brilliant example of adapting the gospel to a particular cultural
context.  Enthusiasm and joy are equally
important (even if we tend to be a suspicious of emotional display in the
churches of Christ).  The first-century
church was emotionally as well as intellectually vibrant.

However, even though cultural relevance and enthusiasm are
important (indeed, arguably necessary), they are hardly sufficient.  As the author of the original article notes,
all around us are churches that have no higher goal than being relevant and
exciting.  Joel Osteen is all about
connecting with the culture.  The problem
is that when salesmanship takes center stage, the gospel gets shoved into the
wings.  “How can I draw people in?” and
“How can I draw people to Christ?” are two very different questions.

It’s easy to turn VBS into an answer to the first of those
questions.  Bright colors, peppy music,
games, crafts, and snacks all appeal to children.  Free daycare appeals to their parents.  Sprinkle enough Jesus on top to allow you to
justify holding it in a church building, and you will certainly draw a
crowd.  However, the attendees will leave
enthused but unchanged.  

By contrast, if you make God’s word the focus of your VBS,
if you include culturally relevant and exciting elements in order to draw
children and their parents deeply into the word, that kind of activity can
change lives.  As do many others, I know
people who came to the Lord because of their experience with VBS as
children.  It’s tempting to assert that *this* is what we’re doing with *our* VBS (nobody wants to admit that
they’re running a dog-and-pony show in the guise of teaching children about
Jesus), but we must be brutally honest with ourselves about our methods, our
content, and our results.  Otherwise, the
devil will get us right where he wants us: 
thinking that we are serving God while actually only serving the flesh.

This same unflinching self-examination is spiritually
significant in any number of other areas. 
We need to ask the same questions about our preaching and teaching.  Especially, we need to ask them about our
song worship.  I think it’s true that
song worship, as with VBS, is particularly dependent on accessibility and
enthusiasm.  As a result, because these elements
are so important, it’s tempting for us to leap to the conclusion that accessible,
enthusiastic worship is all it should be.

Once again, though, “Does this singing draw people in?” and
“Does this singing draw people to Christ?” are two very different
questions.  A song that has a little
Jesus sprinkled on top is not the same thing as a song that is a profound and
meaningful exploration of spiritual truth. 
The former might make people happy; the latter will change their
lives.  

Here too, there is a strong tendency to conflate the two
questions.  Christians are extremely reluctant
to admit that a song they enjoy singing has the spiritual depth of a mud
puddle!  Again, the only cure for the
disease is brutal self-honesty.  We can
be certain that Satan’s goal for all of us is to have us offering vain worship,
and he is constantly and actively trying to nudge us toward his goal.  Lack of vigilance here will cause the same
problems it causes everywhere else.

Basically, we all need to work on being less impressed with
our own wisdom and more impressed with God’s wisdom.  We act like the success of the church and the
gospel are dependent on the clever things we can come up with to draw people
in, and if we succeed in that, we’ve succeeded in doing God’s work.

In reality, of course, our success and failure in any work
of the church are dependent on one thing: 
our faithfulness in presenting God’s word.  We have a promise from Him that His word will
not return to Him void, without accomplishing the work He has intended for
it.  If we get the word out there, it
will achieve the results He wants.  If we
don’t, if we instead replace the centrality of the word with things that are
culturally appealing and exciting, even though the results may please us, they
will surely disappoint Him.
