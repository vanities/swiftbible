Unlike most Christians I know who homeschool, Lauren and I
didn’t consider moral issues much in our decision.  Instead, we focused entirely on where we
thought our children would receive the best education.  Neither one of us felt like we had been well
served by public school, particularly elementary school, so we concluded that
our children, who are likely to share our quirks, would benefit from
nontraditional schooling.



Having said that, some of the trends I’ve been seeing in
public schooling recently concern me.  I
think the times are changing in a way that makes the old concerns less
significant than the new ones.  Take peer
influence, for instance.  I feel some
uneasiness about my children being exposed to worldly children with very
different values, but I figure that they’ll have to learn to deal with worldly
people sooner or later, and besides, influence works both ways.  As many Christian advocates of public
schooling observe, you can’t be a light to the world if you aren’t in the
world.

The same thing is true about the creation/evolution
conflict.  I can remember learning about
the theory of evolution in science class. 
However, this debate has always been a very abstract one.  It doesn’t make much of a difference in daily
life, and my suspicion is that people on both sides believe what they want to
believe.  Whatever.  As long as I let my daughter check out
science books from the library, we’re going to have conversations about
evolution anyway.

I find that kind of thing to be annoying but
manageable.  I’m genuinely alarmed,
however, by what appear to me to be attempts by schools to teach morality to
children in the place of parents.  My
read on Ephesians 6 tells me that teaching morality to my kids is my job, but there
are an awful lot of authority figures in the world of education today who want
to promote a very different vision of right and wrong.

Take, for instance, [this fact sheet](http://hrc-assets.s3-website-us-east-1.amazonaws.com//welcoming-schools/documents/WS-WhoCanMarryWhom-FINAL.pdf), promulgated by, among
others, the National Education Association.    I don’t know how widely adopted the thing is,
but it’s certainly got a lot of organizational muscle behind it.  It promotes values like, “Marriage is about
love, commitment, and responsibility.” and “People can fall in love and want to
be in a relationship regardless of gender.” 
[This sample lesson plan](http://hrc-assets.s3-website-us-east-1.amazonaws.com//welcoming-schools/documents/WS_Lesson_What_is_a_Family.pdf) from The Human Rights Campaign Foundation encourages positive conversation about families with same-sex and transgender
parents in classes as young as kindergarten. 
There’s definitely a system of values about marriage being pushed on
children here, and it doesn’t have a thing to do with Genesis 2 or Matthew 19.

I don’t like the
secular treatment of the issue at all.  Yes,
it is true that people do things contrary to the will of God.  Yes, it is true that in some cases, those
sinful things are legally permissible. 
It is even true that we should treat others with respect and love
regardless of their choices.  However, that’s
a far cry from celebrating those choices as equally legitimate, and that’s
exactly what these NEA standards seem to me to be doing.

I see two harms here. 
The first is that as our children’s parents, it is our right to decide
when and how to introduce such sensitive subjects.  In a world where there isn’t enough time and
funding for art classes, schools shouldn’t be expanding their reach into
values-based instruction that has never been part of the traditional concept of
secular education.  That looks
suspiciously like a strongly held agenda in search of victims.  I think schools should stick to the things
they ought to be doing and aren’t, rather than interfering with what I should
be doing.

Second, these mixed messages have the potential for doing
significant harm to children.  Whether to
get married and whom to marry is probably the second most important decision my
children will ever make.  For their
souls’ sake, they must do so in a way that honors God.  As many parents can testify, it’s hard enough
to get children to do that even if all the teaching they get on the subject is
good.  If they’re presented with
unbiblical teaching about marriage from kindergarten on up, how difficult will
their parents’ task become?

Of course, it’s unclear how these things are being
implemented in any given classroom.  In
my eyes, though, that uncertainty doesn’t help. 
It’s not exactly the thing to interview your kindergarten teacher about his/her
moral views at the open house, and you may not get a straight answer even if
you make the attempt.  Just as I consider
it my responsibility to teach the gospel to the lost, I don’t have any trouble
imagining a secularist teacher who thinks it’s his/her duty to indoctrinate
kids “most at risk”.

Maybe you don’t get somebody like that; maybe you do.  However, our society’s changing views on the
role of teachers certainly leave the door open for it.  Even if a teacher doesn’t seek to usurp the
role of parents in teaching morality, the fact that it’s now socially
acceptable is problem enough.

I’m not one to make blanket statements about
homeschooling.  The most that I will say
about it is that for us, right now, I think it’s the best choice.  For some other family, or indeed for us five
years from now, the answer may well be different.  However, I think that to homeschool or not is
a decision that Christian parents should make thoughtfully and
prayerfully.  Even for the most
determinedly academic family, I think we’ve reached the point in America now
where morality must always be a part of that calculus.
