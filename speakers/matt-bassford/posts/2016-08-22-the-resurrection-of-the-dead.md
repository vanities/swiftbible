One of the biggest problems I have as a preacher is that I
don’t know what others don’t know.  I was
raised by parents who talked about the Bible all the time, I have a number of
friends who do the same, and I spend all week long reading, studying, talking
about, and writing about the Bible. 
There’s always more to learn, but I’ve had as much opportunity to learn
as anybody.

As a result, I can unthinkingly assume that others are
familiar with some Scriptural teaching that they aren’t actually familiar with,
and unless they clue me in by asking a question, I may never know the
difference.  I had this experience last
week when I was studying with Chris Widlacki. 
We talked about some questions he had asked in Bible class, and I soon
realized that he’d never heard a complete explanation of Scriptural teaching
about the afterlife before.  We were able
to remedy that pretty quickly, but there may well be others here who are in
Chris’s former position.  Let’s look this
morning, then, at one of the main texts that addresses the topic:  1 Corinthians 15 and its discussion of the
resurrection of the dead.



**Christ Is Risen!**

In this extremely important chapter, Paul begins his analysis
of the topic by proclaiming that Christ is risen.  This starts with the details of **HIS RESURRECTION** itself.  Here, let’s read from 1 Corinthians
15:1-11.  This is probably the most
important apologetic text in the whole Bible. 
If you want to defend your faith to an unbeliever, this context is the
first place you go.

Here’s why.  Most
agnostics and atheists don’t know very much about the New Testament, so they
treat it as a series of myths rather than a collection of historical
documents.  They justify their unbelief
by hypothesizing that Jesus was nothing more than a good man who died on the
cross and stayed dead, but hundreds of years later, a legend arose that He was
really the Son of God and rose from the dead like Osiris, Adonis, and other
mythical figures.

However, this plausible-sounding story doesn’t stand up to
scrutiny.  Due to archaeological
evidence, we can date 1 Corinthians pretty precisely, to about 53 AD.  That’s only 20-25 years after the crucifixion
of Jesus.  It’s simply not enough time
for myths and legends to develop, precisely because the events of the
crucifixion and its aftermath were still within living memory.  Nobody’s going to convince me that some
miracle happened in 1991 because I remember 1991 and know perfectly well that
there were no miracles in it

Within this short time frame, though, Paul is already saying
that Jesus rose from the dead.  Worse
still, he cites literally hundreds of witnesses, some named, most not, who also
claimed to have seen Jesus after His resurrection.  In fact, they believed in the resurrection so
strongly that they risked their lives by proclaiming it in the face of
persecution.  Far from being a legend or
a myth, the resurrection of Jesus is actually one of the best-attested events
in all of ancient history.  Because our
faith is based on the resurrection, it rests on solid ground.

It’s a good thing that the resurrection is so well attested
because **THE IMPLICATIONS** of Jesus
being raised, or not, are so profound. 
Paul lays this out in 1 Corinthians 15:12-19.  Apparently, there are some skeptics in the
Corinthian church who are questioning the teaching of the general resurrection
of the dead, likely because “everybody knows” that dead people don’t rise from
the grave.  Paul shows the fallacy of
this thinking by pointing out that if there is no resurrection, not even Christ
has been raised, and if Christ has not been raised, then the whole Christian
system of faith falls apart.  The
apostles are a bunch of false teachers, our religion is meaningless, all the
Christians who died in faith are actually dead like Rover, and the Corinthians,
along with all of us here this morning, are only wasting our time.  Nothing is left.

This should clue us in, friends, to how important the
resurrection is.  When I was growing up,
I don’t remember studying the resurrection very much.  Instead, the focus was more on Christ dying
on the cross for our sins.  That’s
obviously important, but think about this: 
without the resurrection, how would we even know that Christ died for
our sins?  The Romans crucified lots of
people, and it’s possible to make up wild stories about any of them.  The only thing that makes Bible teaching
about Christ more than a bunch of wild stories is His resurrection.  Just as Jesus’ healing of the lame man proved
that He had authority on earth to forgive sins, so His physical resurrection
from the dead proves our spiritual resurrection when we are buried with Him in
baptism.

In a few moments, we’re going to partake of the Lord’s
Supper.  When we do so, though we should
think about His death, let’s be sure that we aren’t thinking only about His
death.  As Paul’s words earlier in the
chapter reveal, our first-century brethren thought about the death, burial, and
resurrection of Christ as a unit.  It’s a
continuation of the same event.  If we
cut things off when Jesus dies, we’re remembering a funeral rather than the
greatest victory ever won.  Let’s not
make that mistake this morning.  Let’s
remember His suffering and His glory both.

Second, though, let’s consider ourselves.  We’re supposed to be living a life of
resurrection, as those whom Christ has rescued from the death of sin.  Are we? 
Let’s reflect as we partake.

**Resurrection, or Not**

In the next main section of the text, Paul contemplates the
consequences of Christ being resurrected, or not.  First, he describes **THE END** if Christ has indeed risen. 
Let’s look here at 1 Corinthians 15:20-28.  As Paul observes, because Christ has risen,
there are several significant therefores that follow from the
resurrection.  By His own words, Christ
didn’t rise only for Himself.  He rose
for everybody.  Just as Adam brought
physical death into the world, so Christ brought physical resurrection.  In fact, that physical resurrection is
nothing less than the purpose for which Christ was given all authority and
power.  The Scripture testifies of Him
that He will reign until all His enemies have been put under His feet.  Jesus has already defeated Satan and sin.  The only enemy remaining on the list is
death.  The Lord has already won the
first battle in that war, and the day will come when He will win the war by
bringing life to all of us.

Then, when the last enemy has been defeated, Christ will do
something that we don’t often think about. 
Once everything and everyone has been subjected to Him, He will return
the authority that the Father gave Him to the Father.  Then, all the people of God will belong
wholly to Him.

This is a particularly relevant text when we consider the
false doctrine of premillennialism. 
Among other things, premillenialists argue that when Christ came to
earth, He was supposed to reign as an earthly king but was defeated by His
enemies.  However, they say that when He
returns to earth the second time, then He will take up the earthly kingship in
Jerusalem that He was supposed to have thousands of years ago.  In reality, though, Christ’s first coming was
not a defeat but a victory.  In dying on
the cross and establishing the church, Jesus did everything God wanted Him to,
and He reigns as King even now.  In fact,
His return in the clouds (though not actually to the earth) will mark not the
beginning of His reign, but its end.

By contrast, if Christ is not risen, we won’t rise either,
and we are living **A HOPELESS LIFE.** Paul explains in 1 Corinthians
15:29-34.  The first thing that appears
in this text is the contentious issue of baptism for the dead.  I don’t think this is about being baptized on
behalf of dead relatives.  Instead,
despite the plural, I think it’s about a single dead man—Christ.  After all, if the dead are not raised, not
even Christ is raised, and if Christ isn’t raised, baptism is pointless.  As Romans 6 explains, when we are baptized,
we are symbolically united with Christ both in His death and in His
resurrection.  If Christ did not actually
rise from the dead, the whole metaphor collapses, and none of us actually rose
to walk in newness of life either.

Similarly, the Christian walk itself is also pointless in
the absence of resurrection.  If we have
no more hope of eternity than the animals do, we might as well live like
animals.  Why not seek out every passing
pleasure of sin?  After all, we’re as
passing as they are.  Conversely, all the
suffering that Paul underwent and we undergo for the sake of Christ is
pointless.  If the dead are not raised,
our devotion to the Lord gains us nothing. 
We’re a bunch of chumps.

However, Paul points out, if we do accept the resurrection,
that has serious consequences for the way we live.  Just as Christianity is pointless if the dead
are not raised, worldliness is pointless if the dead are raised.  If the pleasures of sin are passing but I am
not, I’m an idiot if I pursue them.  I’m
wasting my shot at eternal life for the sake of something that can’t even
really make me happy here!  We need to
get our friendships and our lives in order, so we can focus on what truly
matters.

**The Nature of
Resurrection**

Finally in this context, Paul explains the nature of
resurrection.  He begins this explanation
with a discussion of **DIFFERING
GLORIES.** It appears in 1 Corinthians
15:35-41.  Paul here is imagining a
dialogue between himself and a hypothetical questioner.  Generally, Paul doesn’t make a habit of
insulting even the questioners he invents, so I think it’s fair to conclude
that he imagines questions like this coming from bad faith.  This person who’s asking about the nature of
the resurrection body isn’t genuinely seeking information.  Instead, he’s using the question to attack
the idea of resurrection generally.  It’s
sort of like the skeptic’s parable about the cannibal who eats the missionary
but later becomes a Christian himself. 
In the resurrection, who is going to be raised from the dead when the
missionary’s flesh has been incorporated into the cannibal’s?

Paul begins his answer by pointing to the boundless variety
of creation.  Not even things that are
essentially the same maintain the same appearance.  A grain of wheat doesn’t look a whole lot
like a mature stalk of wheat, even though the one came from the other.  Similarly, earthly things are different from
one another in appearance, just as they differ from the heavenly bodies, which
themselves also differ from one another.

The point, I think, is that when we try to box God in by
what we think He logically can or cannot do, we’re wasting our time.  It is not only God’s power that is
boundless.  His wisdom and inventiveness
are too.  If indeed there is a penitent
cannibal out there who has eaten a missionary, I am quite confident that their
unusual relationship to each other will not prevent God from raising them both
from the dead.  God has already proven
His ability to solve worse problems than that!

Indeed, Paul goes on to explain, it is God’s imagination
revealed in His creation that gives us our best insight into what **OUR SPIRITUAL BODIES** will be like.  Look at 1 Corinthians 15:42-49.  The discussion here is a little too subtle
for even our best translations to capture. 
The NASB has Paul referring to our “natural” bodies, but the word here
isn’t “fleshly” or anything like that. 
Instead, it’s *psychikos*, which
in English would literally be “soulish”. 
Adam became a living soul, so we now have soulish bodies like him.  Christ, by contrast, became a life-giving
spirit, so we will have spiritual bodies like Him.

Our current bodies are earthy, literally “made of dust”, but
they will be heavenly, not only, I think, located in heaven, but made from the
substance of heaven.  This heavenly body
will be far superior to our earthy bodies. 
Now we are perishable; then we will be imperishable.  In death, we are dishonorable, but we will be
raised in glory.  Now we are weak; then
we will be powerful.

However, just like the grain of wheat and the wheat stalk,
even though we will be very different, what we will be still will have an
essential relationship to what we are now. 
I’ve known more than a few Christians who don’t believe in the bodily
resurrection from the dead.  They think
that when we die, our spirits drift off to the Day of Judgment and leave our
bodies behind forever.

However, that’s simply not what I see the Scripture
saying.  Jesus wasn’t raised as a
disembodied spirit; instead, His body itself was raised and came out of the
tomb.  He is the firstborn from the
dead.  In His experience, we see what our
own experience will be.  In John 5, Jesus
says that all who are in the tombs will hear the voice of the Son of God and
come forth.  When we die, our spirits are
separated for a time from our bodies, but on that day, they will be reunited.

The last thing that Paul discusses in this chapter is **A MYSTERY.**Paul illuminates it in 1 Corinthians 15:50-58.  This is a reading that really divides into
three parts.  In the first, Paul answers
an obvious question.  If the dead will
rise and be transformed when Christ returns, what will happen to those who are
still alive?  In response, he reveals
that even though the living on that day aren’t dead, they will still be
transformed, receiving the same kind of spiritual bodies the dead have.

Second, Paul points out what the effect of this
transformation will be.  We die because
we have earthly bodies that are subject to deterioration and eventually
failure.  However, the spiritual bodies
into which everyone’s body will be changed aren’t subject to corruption.  As a result, those who are still living on
the last day will never die.

This connects back to what we saw in the first section about
Christ’s reign continuing until He has defeated all His enemies.  Once the dead have been raised and the living
made immortal, death, which formerly had power over everyone, will now have
power over no one.  This is how Christ is
going to defeat death, not by engaging in hand-to-hand combat, but by rendering
it impotent.  Once death has been
defeated, then His work will be done forever. 
This will be the ultimate victory not only for Christ but for every one
of us who has trusted in Him.

Then, Paul gets to the therefore.  I think this is one of the most powerful
therefores in the Bible.  Because we can
have confidence in this great victory that Christ will win and share with all
of us, it logically follows that we should live in a certain way.  Because of the resurrection, we can be
certain that everything that we go through for Christ is worth it.  It’s worth it for us to get pushback from
friends and family members who don’t understand.  It’s worth it for us to choose assembling
with the saints over our work, our families, or our leisure.  It’s worth it for us to contribute generously
to the Lord’s work even if we’d like to spend that money on ourselves instead.  It’s worth it, if necessary, for us to suffer
persecution or even death for the Lord’s sake.  Why?  Because our toil is not in vain in the Lord.
