Last Sunday was our bimonthly singing at Joliet.  It so happened that we had half a dozen
visitors in attendance who were members of other churches of Christ, people who
were used to congregational a-cappella singing. 
However, just about every one of them commented to me after the assembly
was over about how wonderful the singing was and how much they enjoyed it.

Part of that was happenstance.  I myself had thought that the singing was
unusually good, better than the Joliet norm. 
However, I think that the song worship at Joliet is normally
strong.  I may be biased here, but I can
think of few congregations that can match us for wholehearted, enthusiastic
worship.  In a country where the custom
of congregational singing seems to be on the decline across many religious
traditions, I think the reasons for our success are worth exploring.



**A familiar
repertoire.** Most people can’t read
music, even in religious traditions that value congregational singing.  These non-sightreaders are comfortable and
confident singing hymns they know by heart, but when confronted with an
unfamiliar hymn, their comfort and confidence vanishes.  Give them one song they don’t know, and
they’ll struggle through it.  Give them
half a dozen, and they’ll check out.

Sadly, this is precisely what praise-band religious culture
does.  In an effort to be relevant, many
churches have ditched the traditional worship repertoire in favor of material
that mimics the sound of Top 40 radio. 
However, this material doesn’t merely borrow its sound from secular
culture.  It borrows its turnover.  Both genres generate a plethora of new songs
every year because people like listening to new songs.  High-school students today don’t listen to
the same music I listened to when I was in high school 20 years ago (though
part of my brain is convinced that Portishead and Alanis Morissette are still
cool).  On with the new, right!

The problem is that people like listening to new songs, but
they don’t like singing them.  They might
sell CD’s in the church bookstore, but they don’t invite the congregation to
worship.  Any church that relies on
novelty for musical impact is going to have performers performing alone while
the congregation sits back and passively listens.  By contrast, because the Joliet church sings
many hymns that most members have known all their lives, its involvement in the
singing is robust.

**A refreshed
repertoire.** However, simply because
people prefer to sing the familiar doesn’t mean that they won’t benefit from
being stretched!  Even the best-loved
hymns will grow stale if they are endlessly recycled without variation.  Too much novelty deadens the mind, but
novelty in appropriate amounts challenges and stimulates it.

There are hymns and praise songs from the past 50 and even the
past 10 years that are powerful, Biblical, and well worth singing.  It’s not wise for a church to accept
contemporary worship music as a whole simply because it’s new, but uncritically
rejecting it for the same reason is no better. 
Instead, the key is for us to use good judgment in what we can
adopt.

This change should be evolutionary rather than
revolutionary.  The body of traditional
English hymnody served diverse congregations all over the world well for centuries.  If some new musical trend is so different
that it’s not compatible with traditional worship, we do well to consider it
with skepticism.  Nor should the object
be to overwhelm the congregation with novelty. 
In the Joliet church, we make the effort to learn about 12-20 new hymns
each year (outside of the assembly so that unfamiliarity does not detract from
worship), and to me, that pace seems about right.

**A simple
repertoire.** I’m generally the one in
my congregation who is responsible for teaching new hymns.  However, because of my duties as a preacher,
I’m generally not one of the men who actually does the leading, and I certainly
don’t interfere with hymn selection.  As
a result, I’m an interested spectator to the process of hymn adoption.

One trend that I’ve noticed is that of the hymns and praise
songs I teach, the ones that are most often selected and most enthusiastically
sung have folk-music DNA.  For instance,
last year we learned “A Shelter in the Time of Storm”, not the major-key Ira Sankey
abomination, but the minor-key African-American spiritual.  There’s nothing fancy here.  The arrangement we use is mostly block-chorded
and has about three different chords in it. 
However, the members at Joliet love to sing it, and when we sing it, I
can hear even the small children in the congregation joining in.

Basically, hymns that best stir up congregational singing
have simple, beautiful melodies with limited ranges (generally an octave or
less) and simple, intuitive harmonies that even non-sightreaders can feel.  Any increase in complexity above that level
(even for beauty’s sake, and there are many beautiful, complicated tunes out
there) will lead to a decrease in congregational involvement.

**Leaders who
care.** I am aware that there are many
religious traditions that won’t put a guy up front during worship, but we
will.  I’ve seen others argue that this
will inevitably turn the leader into a soloist, but in practice, I can rarely,
if ever, recall having seen that happen. 
Rather than seeking to impress others with his wonderful, amazing voice,
the object of the song leader should be to draw the congregation into worship,
and to that end, one of his most powerful tools is his own emotional
involvement.

When Christians see a man forgetting himself and pouring out
his heart before the Lord, it inspires them to forget themselves and pour their
hearts out before the Lord.  Who cares if
you can’t sing?  God is great; worship
Him anyway!  In my congregation, we’re
blessed with four song leaders who, although they are all very stylistically
different from one another, all care about glorifying God.  That makes worship work.

**Leaders who pitch
correctly.** In any religious
tradition that uses musical instruments, this won’t be an obstacle to
worship.  For a-cappella churches, it can
be a significant obstacle.  Even simple
hymns with limited melodic ranges will become stumbling blocks if they’re
pitched three steps too high or (more commonly) three steps too low.  I think it’s great when song leaders beat
time, use dynamics, and employ all the other whiz-bang song-leader tricks.  All that helps.  However, if you want your congregation to
sing, the song leader has to know how to start hymns on the right note.  If he doesn’t know how, the result is more
likely to be depressing than uplifting.

Much more could be
said on this subject, but these things seem to me to be the five essential ingredients in
the recipe for participatory, congregational worship.
