This is a Matthew 7:7 Bible. 
I asked, and I was given, in this case by the good people of the
HarperCollins marketing department, who sent me a review copy without notifying
me that it was coming.  Hopefully, I
shall be able to avoid joining the ranks of Bible reviewers who have perjured
their immortal souls for the sake of free poly-cover thinlines.  If I appear to have become corrupted, let me
know.

As with all the Bibles I review, this one has been put
through the Bible triathlon:  two weeks
with me at the church building, where I use it to preach, teach, and study, two
weeks at home as my daily reader, and two weeks traveling around with me as I
visit shut-ins and engage in kitchen-table studies.



**Translation:**  NIV, 2011

**Cover:**  Polyurethane (“Italian Duo-Tone”)

**Binding:**  Sewn

**Trim Size:**  5 ¼” x 8 ¼”

**Text Block Width:**  ⅘”

**Weight:**  1 pound, 4 ounces

**Column:**  Double

**Layout:**  Paragraphed

**Print Size:**  8

**Words of Christ:**  Red

**Cross-References:**  No

**Ribbon Marker:**  1

The first thing that merits comment about this Bible is not
the Bible itself, but its packaging.  For
some, at least, of its lineup (not for the Zondervan Thinline NASB,
tragically), Zondervan has developed a novel method of packaging.  It is neither a normal nor a clamshell
box.  Instead, it consists of two
open-spined boxes, one of which nests inside the other.  The outer box is decorated with the usual
photos of people doing inviting things with Bibles and testimonials from girls
named Sarah.  The inner box is much
plainer.

I’ve handled NIV’s in boxes like this at the bookstore
dozens of times, but I failed to realize the truth until I actually had this
Bible at home.  The inner box is a
slipcase!  Mark Bertrand opines, and I
agree fervently, that slipcases for Bibles are wonderful things.  All those stacks of goatskin-clad tomes on
the Bible-geek sites look so impressive, but I find that the reality is much
less appealing.

For one thing, my stacks consist of genuine leather and
bonded leather and poly, all of which lack a certain majesty.  For another, stacks are a dreadfully
inconvenient way to store working Bibles. 
I’m always shifting five Bibles over to a different stack to dredge up
the one at the bottom.  It’s much easier
to grab a slipcased Bible off a row on the shelf.  Admittedly, the Zondervan slipcase has a
plastic window along the sides, which isn’t so classy as a Cambridge slipcase,
for instance, but it’s still a slipcase.

**COVER.**  As I observed above, this particular Thinline
NIV has a poly cover (and I don’t think Zondervan makes a straight Thinline NIV
with either a genuine- or premium-leather cover), but it is a reminder that not
all poly covers are created equal, even among Zondervan products.  The poly covers that came on my Zondervan Thinline
NASB’s were miserable things, easily the flimsiest poly covers I’ve
handled.

The cover on the Thinline NIV, by contrast, are respectably
sturdy.  It isn’t as thick as some
Crossway poly covers, which feel like they must have come from a synthetic
rhinoceros.  However, it doesn’t feel
cheap either.  I don’t flex it and say to
my self, “Oh, so THAT’S where the cover is going to fail!”  The endpapers are medium-weight paper rather
than the leatherette I prefer, but I’ve never had a softcover Bible fail at the
endpapers.

I also like the appearance of the Thinline NIV.  My Thinline NASB’s shared a color scheme with
my daughter’s bedroom—chocolate and carnation pink.  That doesn’t exactly match my sentiments
about the dignity of the word of God. 
The Thinline NIV fares much better in that respect.  It is predominantly a sober charcoal gray
with an inch-wide strip of burgundy at the ends of the covers.  All the imprinting (spine text and ISBN, no
goofy designs) is blind-stamped.  The
perimeter and seams are stitched in white. 
I would feel ridiculous preaching out of a pink Bible, but the Thinline
NIV has enough gravitas to appear in the pulpit.

**TEXT BLOCK.** As choirs of angels sing hosannas, I
reveal that the Thinline NIV is indeed a sewn Bible.  Most things in the Zondervan lineup these
days are, as helpfully though indirectly shown by the “Lay-Flat” logo on the
Bible box.  My Editing professor from
journalism school would doubtless insist that it should be “LIE-Flat” (“What’s
the Bible laying?  Eggs?”).  However, I imagine that Zondervan’s marketing
department shied away from putting “LIE” in all caps on the packaging for the
word of truth, so “Lay-Flat” it is!

Regardless, I appreciate Zondervan’s willingness to adopt
sewn bindings for mass-market Bibles. 
Crossway and Foundation are also to be commended here.  Bibles with glued bindings are only useful
for people who don’t read them.  Maybe we
should start calling glued Bibles “hypocrite’s Bibles”—for carrying to church,
not using!

Within, the paper of the Thinline NIV is about what you
would expect from a thinline.  It’s
whiter than the paper in either my Thinline NASB or Thinline ESV, but it isn’t
any more opaque.  You can’t quite read
Proverbs when the Bible is opened to Psalm 1, but it’s close.

This is another words-of-Christ-in-red Bible.  As usual, the execution is indifferent.  “Words of Christ in orange” might be closer
to the truth.

**CONCLUSION.**    Of course, we aren’t talking about the
latest triumph of the binder’s art from Allan here, either.  This Bible retails for $27.99 at the moment,
and also at the moment, it can be purchased at christianbook.com for
$18.99.  For less than $20, this is a
decent Bible.  I wouldn’t hesitate to pay
that for a Bible constructed like this for one of my children.  As value for money, it doesn’t jump out like
the ESV Value Thinline does, but Zondervan doesn’t have anything to blush
about, either.



**PHYSICAL FORM
RATING:  4/5**.
