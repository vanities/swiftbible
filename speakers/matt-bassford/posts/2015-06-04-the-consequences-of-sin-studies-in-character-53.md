*“Judah, you are he
whom your brothers shall praise; Your hand shall be on the neck of your
enemies; Your father's children shall bow down before you.”*

*--Genesis 49:8*

As we see throughout the book of Genesis, in the ancient
Middle East, it was customary for the eldest son to receive his father’s
blessing and inherit the lion’s share of his property.  However, when Jacob is near death, he gives
his blessing not to his eldest son, Reuben, nor his second son, Simeon, nor
even to his third son, Levi.  Instead,
Jacob passes all the way to his fourth son, Judah, before imparting the
blessing.



As unusual as this was, it was hardly capricious.  In fact, Jacob explains his reasoning in
poetic form in what he does say to the disinherited elder brothers.  Reuben is in many ways a sympathetic
character.  Way back in Genesis 37, he
was the only brother who cared for the life of Joseph.  However, that was hardly the only aspect to
his personality.  Before that, while
Jacob was away burying Rachel, he lay with his father’s concubine, and
eventually Jacob caught him out.

Nor are Simeon and Levi wholly unsympathetic figures
(although they’re close).  The story of
Shechem’s rape of their sister Dinah reveals their concern for her.  However, they expressed that concern by
tricking the men of Shechem into being circumcised, and then attacking the town
and killing all the men in it while they were still incapacitated.  Not only was this an evil, treacherous thing
to do, but it made trouble for Jacob with his neighbors.

Judah, on the other hand, was no shining example of
righteousness, but he did succeed in keeping out of trouble serious enough to
lead Jacob to disinherit him too.  This
had far-reaching consequences.  Because
of Jacob’s blessing, the great king David and his heirs eventually arose from
the tribe of Judah, and from them in turn, the Christ was born.

Like many of the stories in Scripture, this one should help
us appreciate the long-term consequences of sin.  There’s no evidence in Genesis that Reuben,
Simeon, and Levi realized that they were endangering their inheritance through
their wicked acts.  Instead, they did
what they wanted to do without thinking it through, and it caught up with them
later.

This is how sin works in our lives too.  When we sin, we gain some short-term
satisfaction from it, but it always leads to greater long-term suffering.  If the devil laid the terms out for us when
first he tempted us, none of us would say “Wow, Satan, that’s a fair deal!  Sign me up for that!”  Instead, he uses trickery to lure us in,
highlighting the short-term gain and concealing the long-term pain.  In our dealings with him, we must always
remember that the worm hides a hook.  If
we sin now, we may well pay for it years or decades down the road.  We will be far better off if we follow the
example of Judah and abstain instead.
