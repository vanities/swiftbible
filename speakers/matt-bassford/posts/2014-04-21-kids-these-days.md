One of the great preoccupations
of the Internet recently has been the so-called millennial generation.  Roughly defined, this age cohort embraces
those from 10 to 30 years old.  From a
religious perspective, almost everything I read about millennials is dispiriting.  Polls reveal that millennials are less likely
to believe in God, read the Bible, attend worship services, and accept
traditional moral values than any generation before them.  Commentators on all sides of the political
spectrum agree (whether jubilantly or glumly) that millennials are likely to
usher in a new age of American secularism.

However, as Jesus notes in
Matthew 7:13-14, discipleship has always been the exception, not the rule, and
some of the exceptions I see are exceptional indeed, particularly in the world
of hymnody.  I like to believe that I
have a pretty good idea of who among the churches of Christ is writing
what.  In particular, I try to keep an
eye out for the truly gifted among brethren, hymnists who either show the
capacity for writing an enduring hymn or (in my view, at least) already have
done so.

More of these writers are
millennials than any other generation.



This is remarkable.  For one thing, it’s HARD to write a lasting
hymn.  Of the tens of millions of devout
men and women who lived in Victorian England, for instance, only a tiny handful
wrote at this level of quality.  Most
Victorian hymnists, like most writers of spiritual songs today, produced hymns
that were either mediocre or quite frankly bad. 
If we applied this same ratio to modern churches of Christ, much less
“non-institutional” churches, we would expect to see only one or two writers
with real skill.  The number of
millennial hymnists alone is enough to defeat this expectation.

Second, it’s even more unusual to
see such a measure of ability and interest among those who are so young.  Most hymnists begin writing in their thirties
or even later, once they have reached the level of spiritual maturity necessary
to glorify God in song.  The millennial
hymnists, by contrast, seem to have grown up awfully fast.  They don’t write the Jesus-is-my-boyfriend
silliness that prevails in the wider religious world.  Instead, their work reveals a depth of study
and spiritual perception that puts many older Christians to shame.

I see two possible and perhaps
complementary explanations for this hymnic profusion.  The first is that it is enabled by the
Internet.  After all, the Web enables us
all to seek out whatever is in our hearts, whether it be pornography or the
training we need to improve our hymn-writing skills.  I’m in regular contact with younger hymnists
from all across the country, and contrary to millennial stereotype, they’re not
looking for affirmation.  They’re looking
for critique.

Sure, some would-be writers
vanished from my inbox and my Facebook-message queue once they realized that I
wasn’t going to pat them on the head and tell them how wonderful they
were.  A number of others, however,
regard frank feedback not as an insult, but as a blessing.  They will happily subject a hymn to 10 or 20
cycles of editing, not even with the goal of preparing That Particular Hymn for
publication, but simply for the sake of better understanding.  None of these people live near me, so we rely
on the Internet for communication.

However, as appealing as this
explanation is, it is inadequate.  Why
have all of these dedicated young writers appeared now?  Why not 10 years ago?  Why not 15 years ago?  After all, we had the Internet then too.  By and large, though, young Christians 10 and
15 years ago didn’t have the same passion for and commitment to the hymn work
that is so evident in several today.

By elimination, then, if man
isn’t doing it, God must be.  Romans
12:4-8 reveals that non-miraculous spiritual gifts are also bestowed by the
same Giver.  For whatever reason, God
appears to have decided that this particular generation needs a
disproportionate number of gifted hymnists in it.

I am neither a prophet nor the
son of a prophet, but I still wish to speculate about why this might be.  First, it may be that the direction of wider
religious trends requires it.  150 years
ago, hymnists regarded sacred music as participatory.  They wrote not only for the organist or the
choir, but also for the congregation.    As a result, we can easily use hymns from
this era in acapella congregational worship.

Today, this is no longer
true.  By and large, the crafters of
modern praise-and-worship anthems do not write for the congregation.  They write for the band, and they conceive of
the congregation not as participants, but as audience.  In consequence, many contemporary hymns are
difficult to adapt for congregational use. 
Contemporary quirks from irregular lyrical meter to lengthy instrumental
passages adapt poorly, if at all, to an acapella setting.  As brotherhood editors of hymnals and
supplements can testify, it’s difficult to find worthy contemporary hymns that
translate well.

In consequence, churches of
Christ can no longer rely on denominational sources for the new hymns that are
the lifeblood of any tradition of sacred music. 
Instead, we must look to our own, to writers who understand the virtues
of contemporary hymns, incorporate those virtues in their own work, and reject
the accompanying vices.  The result will
be a body of hymnody purpose-built for the acapella church.

Second, it is probable that, in
the words of every Pentecostal “prophet” ever, hard times are coming.  Our nation is indeed becoming more secular,
and even if in some ways this makes it a more welcoming environment for the
gospel, in many other ways, it is becoming more hostile.  If the Lord’s church is to survive and
flourish in the future, it will need a rallying point, and there are few more
likely candidates than our tradition of worship.

Our Lord chose well when He
dictated that we should teach and admonish one another in song.  Music is one of the most powerful emotional
forces known to man, and a well-crafted hymn stirs the intellect as deeply as
it does the passions.  Our hymns are our
heart-deep answer to an increasingly godless world, our affirmation of all that
we believe, our defiance of everything that it stands for.  In the spiritual battles that lie ahead,
then, the hymnists of the church may well be its shock troops.

Of course, none of us can know the
unrevealed mind of God.  However, I do
believe that He bestows His gifts according to need, and if He has seen fit to
bless us with all of these younger writers, I am certain that one way or
another, we will need them.  I am
thankful that we have them among us, and I pray that they and all who join them
will indeed fulfill His purposes.
