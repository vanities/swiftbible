Several weeks ago, I was asked about the places in Scripture
where a household is described as having been baptized, most notably the
household of Lydia in Acts 16:15, the household of the Philippian jailer in
Acts 16:33, and the household of Stephanus in 1 Corinthians 1:16.  Because many households have children, do
these mass baptisms provide first-century evidence for the practice of infant
baptism?



It’s certainly a legitimate question, and I think the best
way to answer it is to look at other things that the New Testament describes
households as doing.  According to
Scripture, households can also:

* Believe (John 4:53; Acts 16:34, 18:8)
* Fear God (Acts 10:2)
* Be devoted to ministry (1 Corinthians 16:15)



An infant can’t do any of these things, and yet we see
households doing them (indeed, we see both the household of the jailer and the
household of Stephanus doing them). 
There are two possible explanations for this.  First, those households may not have included
children.  Certainly, there are plenty of
households today that don’t.

Second, it may also be that the definition of “household” in
the New Testament embraced only adults and not children.  Even today, we sometimes use the word
“family” in this way.  For instance, I’ve
been known to say that everybody in my grandmother-in-law’s family is a
Christian.  In this, I don’t mean to
include any of my own children, the oldest of whom is 5.  Instead, I’m referring to her, her children,
their spouses, her grandchildren, *their* spouses,
and all of her great-grandchildren who are old enough to have obeyed the
gospel.  There are enough believers among
her descendants to outfit a fair-sized congregation by themselves, preachers,
elders, deacons, and all!  My point is
that everybody in that family who could be a Christian is, and it may well be
that first-century writers used the word “household” in the same way.

Which of these two inferences is correct doesn’t
matter.  They serve only to point out
that there are other reasonable explanations for whole-household baptism than
the baptism of infants.  We may suppose
that the first-century church baptized infants, but we can’t prove it from the
Scriptural record, either from the "household" passages or any other.

This is a key distinction. 
We can suppose a great many things about the first-century church.  We can suppose that they used instruments of
music in worship, used church funds to provide for the needs of the
non-Christian poor, or joined together in a formal denominational structure.

However, if our goal is to be like the first-century church,
what matters is not what we can suppose, but what we can establish.  If we follow instead what we suppose, we make
our own imaginations the authority and not the Bible.  There are certainly many examples in
Scripture of men who did what was right in their own eyes, but never once is it
said of them that they glorified God in so doing.  If we want God to have the glory, we will do
only the things that He has taught us are right.  The baptism of infants is not on that list.
