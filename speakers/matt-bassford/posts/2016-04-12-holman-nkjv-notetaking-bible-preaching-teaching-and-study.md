**OVERVIEW.** This Bible is a delight in the
pulpit.  It should perform nearly as well
in a teaching setting, and it has some attributes that are useful in the office
too.  Any preacher who uses the NKJV, has
good eyesight, and wants to write in his Bible should take a look at this one.

As always, I used the Notetaking Bible as my primary
church-building Bible for two weeks. 
During that time, I preached two sermons (actually the same sermon
twice) and prepared sermons, lesson books, and blog posts.  I didn’t use it to teach any classes because
the class I’m teaching now is the preschool class, which isn’t really very
text-centric!  Regardless, here’s what I
think.



**PREACHING.** The first question I ask these days about
a pulpit Bible is, “Can I write in it?” 
The answer here is a resounding yes. 
Before I tried using it, I had some misgivings.  A sermon outline (with points of emphasis)
takes up a fair amount of marginal space, and the Notetaking Bible isn’t very
big or very thick.  I wasn’t sure there
would be enough room next to the text for me to say everything I wanted
to.

As it turned out, I had no problems fitting in my
outline.  I hadn’t reckoned with the
combination of two of the Notetaking Bible’s unique features:  lined margins that are two inches wide.  My Cambridge NASB Wide-Margin Reference has
outer margins that are nearly as wide (about an inch and a half), but I rarely
use all of them because a) I don’t have to and b) without lines, my handwriting
starts to straggle.

With the lines in the margin of the Notetaking Bible, by
contrast, I could write confidently all the way across, so that none of my
notes took up more than two lines.  There
was plenty of room to say everything I wanted.

In addition, because the Notetaking Bible is single-column,
I never had to deal with the obnoxiousness of writing in the gutter
margin.  Most double-column wide-margin
Bibles are designed with outer margins that are wider than gutter margins.  I understand the aesthetic logic behind this
(wider gutter margins would break up the flow of the design), but in practice,
it’s a pain.  Some of the inner margin
isn’t usable at all because it’s too close to the spine, and even to get at the
rest, you have to squish one side of the Bible down, push the other side up,
etc.  Sigh.

By contrast, single-column wide-margin Bibles don’t need a
wide inner margin.  You only have the one
column to write notes about, and you can put all of those notes in the outer
margin, where the writing is much easier. 
I’d never written my sermon outline in a wide-margin single-column Bible
before, and I greatly enjoyed the comparative ease with which I could do so.  I’m nearly ready to declare that all
wide-margin Bibles should be single-column for that reason alone.

In the pulpit, the comparatively small size of the
Notetaking Bible was a joy.  The only
other single-column wide-margin Bible I own is a 2002 Foundation SCR, and that
Bible weighs three and a half pounds!  I
agree that it’s a beautiful thing, but I’m not interested in holding it for 45
minutes.

By contrast, my impression of preaching with the Notetaking
Bible was that its smaller size encouraged me to be out of the pulpit even more
than I normally would.  When you’re
preaching from a Bible with a negligible weight, why not just hang out off to
the side of the pulpit?  A small
wide-margin Bible freed me to move where I wanted.

In times past, I’ve complained about verse citation in
single-column paragraphed Bibles like the Notetaking Bible.  In wide-margin Bibles, though, that’s not a
problem.  I habitually mark the beginning
of all of my readings in such Bibles with brackets.  If I’m marking the thing up anyway, why
not?  As a result, I didn’t miss any citations
in the Notetaking Bible.

I did notice some problems during my practice sessions with
keeping my eyes focused on the right line (the Notetaking Bible has
comparatively long lines and not much in the way of leading), but those same
problems didn’t emerge in the pulpit.  As
far as I’m concerned, problems that disappear with rehearsal aren’t really
problems, because anytime you’re doing anything in the assembly of the Lord’s
people, you ought to be rehearsing first!

**TEACHING.** Again, I didn’t actually teach any
classes with the Notetaking Bible, but my list of wants in a teaching Bible is
simple enough that I don’t have much trouble predicting how I would have liked
it.  I would have liked the small size
that left me free to roam.  I might not
have liked the lack of cross-references. 
However, in a wide-margin Bible, if you want cross-references, you can
always write in as many as you care to. 
All the white space offers you the opportunity to build your own Bible.

**STUDY.** Here is a setting where I’m not too fond
of the single column.  Even though the
Notetaker’s Bible is set in 8-point Bible Slab and my Cambridge NASB Pitt
Minion is set in 6.75-point Lexicon No. 1, I find the latter slightly easier to
read.  I think my eyes get lost a little bit
in the long, fairly small-print lines of the Notetaker’s Bible.  This wasn’t a serious problem, merely an
annoyance.

However annoying the Bible may have been to use, it didn’t
cost me anything to use it.  So far as
I’ve been able to locate, I didn’t miss any citations during the two-week trial
period.  I don’t recall wanting to use
any cross-references, either.

**CONCLUSION.** Sure; it doesn’t have
cross-references.  Sure; it could stand
to have print that’s a little bit larger, and probably some more leading
too.  However, if the Notetaking Bible
were available in NASB, I’d give serious thought to switching from my Cambridge
WM to it.  It’s not as lovely a Bible,
but man! that setting is great for preaching from.  I think it deserves a 9/10 for preaching,
teaching, and study.
