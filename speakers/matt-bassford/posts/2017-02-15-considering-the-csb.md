Bible geek that I am, I’m always interested when word starts
going around about a translation update or a new translation altogether.  2017 is apparently going to be a rich year
for this.  There’s a fine-tuning of the
NASB due out sometime this year, and only about a month from now, the CSB is
slated to be formally released.



For those who aren’t up on their translation genealogies,
the CSB (short for Christian Standard Bible) is a descendant of the HCSB (or
Holman Christian Standard Bible).  I’ve
used the HCSB, and I still read from it sometimes (though more because I like
my particular copy of the HCSB than because of my fondness for the
translation).  Its claim to fame is a
translation philosophy of “optimal equivalence”, which supposedly stands
somewhere between formal equivalence (“word-for-word” translation) and dynamic
equivalence (“thought-for-thought” translation).  Of course, there is no such thing as a purely
word-for-word or thought-for-thought translation, but there’s something to be
said for the ideal of combining word-for-word precision with
thought-for-thought readability.

Sadly, my impression of the HCSB was that it missed the
mark.  It had a whole bunch of quirks as
a translation (using “Yahweh” rather than “Lord”, marking hard-to-understand
Biblical terms in the text with great big unsightly bullets, putting the text
of signs in Scripture inside boxes so that they looked like actual signs), but
basically, it read like a slightly different version of the NIV.

There were also times when I felt like the translation’s
roots were showing.  Holman is owned by
the Southern Baptist Convention (hence the joke that HCSB stands for “Hard Core
Southern Baptist”), and there are places where the HCSB reads with a distinctly
Southern-Baptist slant.

Of these, the most egregious (that I’ve noticed, at least)
is Acts 22:16.  The HCSB renders the
verse as, “And now, why delay? Get up and be baptized, and wash away your sins
by calling on His name.”  That’s awfully
convenient if you’re a Southern Baptist and don’t believe that baptism washes
away sins, and it’s also far outside the translational norm.  Of the 14 English translations on
blueletterbible.org, the only one that follows the HCSB in interpolating “by”
is the NLT, which is about as loose and interpretive as you can get and still
be a translation.  I don’t think the HCSB
is on the same level as the New World Translation or anything, but the apparent
bias in it is enough to keep me from relying on it.

Enter the CSB.  As its
creators present it, the CSB isn’t so much a revision of the HCSB as it is a
new translation in the HCSB family.  It
stands in the same relation to the HCSB as the NASB does to the ASV.

Overall, the word is that the CSB translators took it in a
much more conservative direction.  Many
of the quirks of the HCSB are gone (“Yahweh” went back to “Lord”, for
instance), and the translators don’t seem as interested in departing from the
translational mainstream.  A few days
back, one of my Bible-geek friends announced that he’d received an advance copy
of the CSB, so I asked him to check Acts 22:16 for me.  He faithfully did so, and he reported that
the offending “by” is now gone.

That got me excited.  Yesterday,
I discovered an online version of the CSB at csbible.com and received an
advance copy, courtesy of another Bible-geek friend of mine (thanks,
Paul!).  And what an advance copy!  I’m still not going to imperil my soul for
the sake of a fine Bible, but I do love me some edge-lined goatskin.  I will spend *a lot* of time considering your new translation if that’s the
wrapper it comes in.

So far, I’ve read the CSB version of Romans online and 1
John, Ephesians, and Mark through the sixth chapter in my advance copy, and I
intend to read the entire New Testament before too much longer.  I’ll have more to say once I finish, but my
initial impressions are extremely positive.

The CSB literature argues that the translation fits between
the NKJV and the NIV on the formal-versus-dynamic continuum, and I think that’s
about right.  I’m no Greek scholar, but I
have spent my adult life studying the Bible, so I’ve acquired some sense of the
nuances of the text.  That’s what makes
the NIV so frustrating to me.  I think
most of the NIV renderings are wonderful, but every so often, the translators
simplify something down too far or miss the point.

The CSB doesn’t feel like that.  I started reading it in Romans because Romans
is usually where dynamic-equivalent translations go to die.  The unfortunate decision of the NIV84
translators to render “flesh” in Romans as “sinful nature” did more to kill the
translation among churches of Christ than anything else.  However, the CSB translation team nimbly
avoided such pitfalls.  I thought Romans
in the CSB was significantly simpler and clearer than Romans in the NASB, but I
didn’t feel like the translators sacrificed nuance to get there.  Compare, for instance, Romans 8:5-8 in the
two translations:

*For those who are according to the flesh
set their minds on the things of the flesh, but those who are according to the
Spirit, the things of the Spirit. For the mind set on the flesh is death, but
the mind set on the Spirit is life and peace, because the mind set on the flesh
is hostile toward God; for it does not subject itself to the law of God, for it
is not even able to do so, and those who are in the flesh cannot please God.*
(NASB)

*For those who live
according to the flesh have their minds set on the things of the flesh, but those
who live according to the Spirit have their minds set on the things of the
Spirit.  Now the mind-set of the flesh is
death, but the mind-set of the Spirit is life and peace.  The mind-set of the flesh is hostile to God
because it does not submit to God’s law. 
Indeed, it is unable to do so.  Those
who are in the flesh cannot please God.* (CSB)

Those two versions mean pretty much the same thing, but I think the
CSB’s rendition is a whole lot easier to understand.  The key is the CSB’s deft transition from
“have their minds set” to “mind-set”, a modern American colloquialism that
nonetheless does a marvelous job of distinguishing the attitude of the godly
from the attitude of the ungodly.  Though
the NASB is precise, its prose (both here and generally) is about as deft as a
drunken elephant.

So far, the biggest hurdle to adoption of the CSB that I see
is that it is modestly gender-neutral. 
It adopts gender neutrality in Psalm 1:1 (“How happy is the one who. .
.”) but keeps masculine pronouns in places where they make a significant
difference, such as Hebrews 2:6-9.  Quite
frankly, I think the NIV11’s use of plural, gender-neutral pronouns in that
text is a disaster and a good reason not to use the translation.  If the CSB has similarly booted any decisions
about gender-neutrality, I haven’t seen the wreckage yet.

In places like Psalm 1:1, though, I'm not terribly concerned about it.  We invariably read the gender-neutral pronoun into the passage anyway (unless somebody wants to argue that it only applies to males).  Why not put the universal understanding of the passage into the text?

All in all, I think the CSB shows a great deal of promise as
a translation.  I want to see what Bible
students who do know Greek have to say about it before I declare it the best
thing since computerized concordances, but I would not be surprised to find myself
using it as my primary translation a year from now.

In my preaching, I’m not terribly worried about reaching
mature Christians, the kind of folks who speak fluent NASB. 
They’re probably on the right track and will remain so even if I start
using a translation in Swahili.  Instead,
I’m most concerned about connecting with visitors, young people, and immature
Christians, those for whom Bible study is a strange and intimidating
experience.  If I can sacrifice a little
bit of textual precision to make the word of God much more accessible to people
like that, I think that’s a trade well worth making.
