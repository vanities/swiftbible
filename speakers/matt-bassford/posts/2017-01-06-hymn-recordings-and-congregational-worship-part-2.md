As preachers like to say, [my response](http://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2017/01/hymn-recordings-and-congregational.html) the day before yesterday
to Jeremy Hodges’ comments on worship “ran a little over”.  OK; a lot over.  I was at 1300 words with no end in
sight.  I decided, then, to truncate the original
post and deal with Jeremy’s second concern separately.

To summarize it (and you can read his original Facebook
comments back in Part 1), Jeremy fears that a focus on beautiful-sounding
groups like the Sumphonia singers (and in my opinion, this year’s recording
does indeed sound lovely!) will lead us to emphasize singing at the expense of
worship.  If it sounds good, we’re happy,
regardless of whether all the pretty singing doesn’t make it any farther than
the ceiling.



Once again, the concern that Jeremy raises is a valid
one.  Singing together is one of the most
emotionally powerful activities that a group of people can engage in.  In fact, I think that’s the reason why God commands
us to sing.  He wants us to be
emotionally involved as we praise Him.

However, as with any other powerful force, the emotion
generated by singing hymns can be misdirected. 
After all, the emotional component to singing is present no matter what
the subject matter of the song is.  Every
one of us has had an emotional experience singing along with the radio in the
car, even though the song we’re singing is about a thousand miles away from
being religious (and may not even make sense—20 years after Oasis released (*What’s the Story) Morning Glory?*, I
still don’t know what a wonderwall is).

In the same way, it’s entirely possible for us to have, in
the middle of a worship service, an emotional experience that has nothing to do
with God.  The song just sounds so
pretty, and we had such a great time hitting that final note, and wow!  We might come away from a “worship” like that
feeling happy and fulfilled, but it leaves God feeling disgusted.

However, I think that the root of the problem here is not an
emphasis on sound and technique.  It’s
the lack of emphasis on content.  As I
discussed in [this post](http://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2016/12/emotion-and-christian.html) a few weeks ago, “heart” in the Bible doesn’t mean the same thing as it does in contemporary
English.  It’s not about the seat of pure
emotion; instead, it’s about the place where our thoughts and emotions come
together, a mind-and-heart.

Necessarily, then, the instruction of Colossians 3:16 to
sing with thankfulness in our hearts to God demands an intellectual as well as
an emotional response.  However, once
we’ve managed to combine the two, we’ve succeeded in fulfilling God’s design
for worship.  When our intellects are
occupied with thoughts of His greatness and our emotions are soaring as we
sing, that’s what worship is supposed to be. There's nothing else to add.

It’s entirely possible, though, for hymns to provide both
intellectual and emotional obstacles to worship.  Normally, the latter of these isn’t much of a
problem.  There are certainly plenty of
boring, uninspiring hymns out there (and I’ve written more than a few of them),
but when Christians encounter these, they don’t sing them because they're boring.  Problem solved!

Sadly, content-free hymns that don’t engage our minds are
far more common.  They say nothing, but
they’re fun to sing, so brethren sing them anyway.  Of course, it’s possible to worship with any
hymn that doesn’t actively teach false doctrine.  However, some hymns and praise songs
certainly make intellectual involvement very difficult.  Trying to think about them while you’re
singing them is kind of like trying to eat air. 
There just isn’t a whole lot to sink your teeth into.

I think this is a serious problem, and I see at least a
couple of different possible solutions. 
The first is straightforward—teaching on the importance of mental
involvement in worship.  I do a lot of this,
both in person and online, and I have to say that the results from so doing
have been. . . mixed.  Once Christians
develop an emotional attachment to a hymn, it’s extremely difficult to lead them to understand that the hymn should not be sung because of its poor content.  Over the course of a year of blog posts, I
may have convinced somebody who originally believed otherwise that “Sanctuary” is not suitable for worship in the
assembly.  However, I sincerely doubt it.

The second approach that we might adopt is to beat the
emotionally stirring, content-free hymns at their own game—to supersede them
with hymns with strong content and strong feeling.  Almost always, when I ask a Christian to
identify their favorite hymn, they will name a hymn that is good.  Content plus emotion is more powerful than
emotion by itself.

Hymns like this don’t guarantee God-pleasing worship.  It’s totally possible to tune out mentally
while singing “It Is Well With My Soul”. 
However, they do make the genuine sacrifice of praise as easy and as
likely as possible.

So, then.  Let’s say you’re
got a hymnal full of hundreds of new hymns that have really strong content,
hymns that will help brethren fulfill the Biblical design for worship.  How are you going to get brethren to sing
them?  Are you going to put up a bunch of
dry lectures on YouTube that explain the characteristics of a good hymn
(“Irregular meter and inconsistent rhythm in hymns distract the worshiper and
interfere with worship.”) and allow the church to draw its own
conclusions?  Or, instead, are you going
to produce recordings of skillful singers worshiping with these hymns,
recordings that make brethren want to worship with them too?

Again, I’m not speaking for Sumphonia here, and I don’t have
any inside knowledge of the board members’ decision-making processes.  However, I guarantee that when it comes to
hymns, their first concern is strong Biblical content that leads to
God-glorifying worship.  They like pretty
singing as much as anybody, maybe more, but the recordings are nothing more to
them than a means to the end of helping the church worship better.  If we overlook the goal, we’re missing the
point.
