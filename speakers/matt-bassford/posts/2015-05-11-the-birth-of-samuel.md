As Christians, we spend much of our lives on earth anticipating the life to come in heaven.  Indeed, that hope is at the heart of our faith.  We recognize that only Jesus can promise us eternal life, so we devote our lives to Him in order to inherit that promise.

However, even though our faith is most relevant when it comes to the things of the spirit, it is important in our lives on earth as well.  Even though our world is fatally flawed by sin, and God never promises us freedom from earthly sorrow, it is still true that He loves His children and answers our prayers when we appeal to Him for help.

There are many stories in Scripture that illustrate God’s concern for His people, but this morning, let’s consider the help that He offered one of His faithful children with a problem that is all too common even today—the problem of childlessness.  From studies like this, we gain the confidence necessary to offer our prayers in faith.  Let’s look, then, at the birth of Samuel.


**Hannah**

Even though the story of Samuel’s birth is extremely important to our understanding of the direction his life takes, he isn’t even really the main character in the story.  Instead, that honor belongs to his mother, Hannah.  We quickly learn that Hannah has A PROBLEM.  The story unfolds in 1 Samuel 1:1-8.  Really, Hannah has two problems.  The first is that she is childless, which isn’t any picnic even today, but 3000 years ago was a disaster.  Back in Bible times, a woman’s whole worth was bound up in her ability to bear children, and if she was incapable, she was worthless.

Hannah’s second problem, though, is that she is in a polygamous marriage.  This was something that God tolerated in that day because of the hardness of men’s hearts, but it was never part of the divine plan, and the facts of this story show us why.  Hannah happened to be the preferred wife, but Peninnah, her despised co-wife, took advantage of Hannah’s childlessness to make her miserable in a way that only a woman with a grudge can.  It’s hard to be too harsh on Peninnah, though; who of us today would want to be the odd person out in a marriage?

By contrast, Elkanah, who is the husband of the story, is a much less sympathetic figure.  We see him demonstrating here that husbands were still clueless 3000 years ago.  When Hannah becomes upset because of Peninnah’s needling, he gets offended by it.  After all, when Hannah has a hunk of awesomeness like him for a husband, who needs children?

This sad situation should remind us of the problems that accompany any deviation from God’s plan.  Polygamy is out of favor these days, though maybe not for long, but the abuses of marriage that are common in our culture are no less devastating.  Pornography use, adultery, divorce, and remarriage all come with their cargo of pain for the innocent and the guilty alike.  If we choose God’s way in our marriages instead, both righteousness and happiness will result.

Hannah responds to her problems with PRAYER.  Here, consider 1 Samuel 1:9-11.  There are stories in the Bible that just make your heart hurt to read, and this is one of them.  Elkanah and his family have come to offer sacrifice and eat a festival meal at Shiloh, which is where the tabernacle was located then.  Hannah is literally sick because Peninnah is harassing her so mercilessly, so she leaves the feast, stumbles to the tabernacle, and racked with weeping, prays for a son in words that only God can hear.  She is guiltless, yet her suffering is intense.

However, even in her despair, Hannah provides an example for us.  In her intense suffering, she doesn’t claw Peninnah’s eyes out.  She doesn’t become an alcoholic.  She doesn’t close herself off emotionally and die inside.  Instead, she brings her burdens to the Lord.

Today, every time we face a great trial, that trial is going to have one of two results.  Either it’s going to bring us closer to God, or it’s going to pull us away from Him.  We can’t decide whether we undergo trial or not, but we can decide which of those two paths we’re going to take.  Only if we seek God through prayer will we find Him.  Any other choice will lead us away.

The final section of this first part of the story, and arguably the most frustrating, is the subsequent conversation between HANNAH AND ELI.  Look at 1 Samuel 1:12-18.  Eli is the high priest at this time, and he is sitting by the door of the tabernacle as Hannah comes staggering in.  He sees her behavior--the bitter weeping, her lips moving soundlessly—and jumps to exactly the wrong conclusion.  He concludes that Hannah is drunk and yells at her for being drunk.  If I were in Hannah’s sandals, I might have slugged the high priest, but Hannah responds in a distinctly feminine way.  Even though she isn’t drunk, she practically starts apologizing to Eli too as she explains the situation.  Eli, mollified by this, prays with her and sends her on her way much restored.

This is perhaps the best example in the entire Bible of an extremely common problem:  the problem of judging others before we know the facts.  Eli could have spent a whole fifteen seconds asking Hannah what was going on, but he was so angry and self-righteous about what he thought he was seeing that he couldn’t wait to lay into her instead.  Likewise, friends, we must be careful never to assume the worst about anyone.  Like Eli, we can get all righteoused up about a brother who hasn’t been to services recently, or a friend who greets us brusquely, or an ambiguous text message from a spouse.  Before we let somebody have it, though, we should always give them the opportunity to explain.  It’s the godly thing to do, and it will spare us a lifetime of looking foolish!

**Samuel is Born**

Next, the text turns its attention to how Samuel is born.  This begins with GOD’S ANSWER to Hannah’s prayer.  Here, look at 1 Samuel 1:19-20.  What’s interesting to me about this part of the story, at least, is that it could just as easily happen today.  Elkanah and Hannah come together, God remembers her, and she gives birth to a baby boy.  We don’t see any flash-bang miracles here.  The only way that we know God is involved in Samuel’s conception and birth is that the text tells us so.  A skeptic could easily deny that God intervened here at all.  When we look at it from Hannah’s perspective, it also raises some questions about God’s purpose.  If God was going to intervene, why didn’t He do it years ago and spare Hannah all the pain of childlessness?

These things should remind us, friends, that in our relationship with God, not everything will be spelled out for us.  Not every question will be answered.  Sometimes God will allow us to suffer for reasons of His own, and we may never find out in this life why it was necessary or important.  Many people use this as an excuse to reject God, to turn away from Him.

However, if we are people of faith, our reaction will be the opposite.  We need to come to Him forthrightly like Hannah did, seeking in faith the blessing that we want.  When God grants our petition, we need to be thankful, especially when His handiwork is not obvious.  When He doesn’t, we need to continue to live as people of faith and trust that God does not deny us needlessly.

However, once Samuel is born, HANNAH DELAYS in handing him off to Eli.  1 Samuel 1:21-23 explains.  Even though Hannah has vowed Samuel to the Lord “all the days of his life”, she holds on to him until he is weaned and doesn’t even attend the sacrifice lest someone misconstrue her intent.  The text offers no condemnation of Hannah for this, which certainly makes sense.  After all, Baby Samuel would hardly have been much use to God!  Better to let him develop first.

This points to a larger principle.  Even in our lives, serving the Lord should wait until we have developed enough to be useful servants.  I think it’s great when Christians grow.  I thought it was so encouraging two weeks back when Tom did a Bible reading in the assembly and Omer offered the invitation, both for the first time.  However, we shouldn’t push our brothers and sisters to serve before they’re ready, any more than we should push children to obey the gospel before they’re ready.  Of course, if some man has been a Christian for 20 years, but he still doesn’t know enough about the Bible to explain God’s plan of salvation from the Scripture, that’s somebody who needs to be encouraged to get ready, but we still shouldn’t set brethren up for failure.

This is perhaps most important when it comes to elders.  It’s no secret to anyone here that I long for the Joliet church to have elders.  However, it’s better for a congregation to have no elders at all than it is for a congregation to have so-called “elders” who aren’t actually qualified to fill the role.  Just because a man has two children who have been dunked and still show up at services doesn’t mean that he’s ready to serve.  Instead, he has to have learned the skills of spiritual servanthood and leadership before he should be entrusted with leading an entire church.

Finally in this section, though, we see HANNAH’S COMMITMENT of Samuel to God.  Here, let’s read from 1 Samuel 1:24-27.  Once Samuel has been weaned, Hannah brings him to the tabernacle and entrusts him to the care of Eli as she had promised she would.  When the text speaks of Samuel as “dedicated to the Lord”, in conjunction with Hannah’s promise that a razor would never come near his head, we can conclude that Samuel was a Nazirite from birth, as in Numbers 6.

Now, God doesn’t call anybody to be a Nazirite today, which is just as well—I enjoy eating my oatmeal with raisins in it!  However, there is still a sense in which Christian parents today are responsible for dedicating their children to God.  In Ephesians 6, Paul famously instructs fathers to bring their children up in the discipline and instruction of the Lord, and in 2 Timothy 2:1, we learn about the role that Timothy’s mother and grandmother had in helping him to be a man of God.

There are many things that we might want for our children:  a good education, a nice career, a happy marriage and family, and so on.  However, the goal of raising our children to be godly is more important than all those other goals put together.  Even if they don’t get any of those other things but they go to heaven, their lives will have been a success.  If they get every one of those things, if they taste everything the world has to offer, but they are lost, they will have failed.  I think every parent here would acknowledge that God must come first.  Let’s make sure, then, that God does come first in the lives of our children:  before school, before homework, before band, before sports, before anything else.  If sacrifices must be made, let’s make those other things the sacrifices.

**The Prayer of Hannah**

The final section of Scripture that we’ll be studying this morning contains the prayer of Hannah.  This begins with Hannah EXULTING IN THE LORD.  Consider 1 Samuel 2:1-2.  Before we get into content, indulge me in a hymn-geek moment.  In the hymn “Holy, Holy, Holy,” the third line of the third verse reads, “Only Thou art holy; there is none beside Thee.”  I’d always thought it was a cool line, but it got even cooler once I realized that Reginald Heber had lifted it from 1 Samuel 2:2.  The hymn is actually teaching us the Bible without us realizing it.

This is important not just for the hymn-geekery of it, but for the content of this line, and indeed of this two-verse snippet of Hannah’s prayer.  There is nobody we should rejoice in like God because there is nobody who is like God.  God delivered Hannah, He promises to deliver us today, and He is going to follow through on His promise.  No other being in heaven or on earth is going to do the same.  Keeping my promises is important to me, but sometimes I fail.  Sometimes keeping the promise has become impossible.  Sometimes I forget.  Sometimes I just don’t do it.  God, by contrast, is utterly powerful and utterly reliable.  I would hope that most of you would regard me as  a trustworthy man, but nobody should commit their earthly lives and their eternal destinies to me.  I can’t bear up under a burden like that.  Only God can shoulder that load for us.

Second, Hannah uses the bulk of her prayer to explore THE WORKS OF GOD.  We read about these in 1 Samuel 2:3-7.  This whole section of Scripture is about the way that God engineers reversals of fortune for those who trust in Him.  The mighty are weakened, but the feeble are strengthened.  The rich are impoverished, but the poor are enriched.  It’s obvious, though, where Hannah’s heart is, and it’s in the line about the barren woman giving birth to seven while the woman with many children languishes.  Remember; all of this is happening during the festival at Shiloh.  Peninnah is right there while Hannah is saying this, along with Elkanah and the whole rest of his family.  Every time Hannah has come to the festival before, Peninnah has used the opportunity to make her life utterly miserable because of her childlessness.  Now, Peninnah can’t do that anymore because Hannah has a son.  So far as we know from the text, Hannah never even said a harsh word to her spiteful rival.  Instead, she trusted in God, and God has now shut Peninnah up permanently.

This, friends, is the Bible’s prescription for how we should handle the people who trouble us today.  The wisdom of the world says that we should repay them in their own coin.  If they are nasty and spiteful, we should be nasty and spiteful back.  The wisdom from above, however, says that we should never let somebody else’s wickedness drag us down to their level.  Instead, we’re supposed to trust in God, pray to Him for deliverance, and wait on Him.  It’s His problem to solve, not ours, and we can be certain that on the Day of Judgment if not before, He will make things right.

Finally, Hannah discusses the importance of PREVAILING WITH GOD.  Let’s read here from 1 Samuel 2:8-10.  Implicit in her words here is the understanding that there’s no third way.  Everybody is on one side or the other.  Either you are with the Lord, and you can trust Him to deliver you, or you are opposed to the Lord, and you can count on Him to defeat you.  Of course, we live 3000 years after Hannah did, but the same principle still holds true.

There’s one last feature of the text, though, that will open up to us the final point that we need to make here.  It appears in the last two poetic lines of the prayer.  Here, Hannah is talking about the king and the Lord’s anointed, which is awfully strange because at this point of Israel’s history, there is no king and there is no anointed, at least not in this sense.  At this point, Samuel is still just a toddler, and he doesn’t anoint Saul as the first king until he’s an old man.

We might be tempted to dismiss this as an anachronism, a line inserted by some later scribe, except for one thing.  In all of Scripture, there is one text that above all others resembles this one.  It too is a prayer of thanksgiving by a mother about her baby boy, but in the second case, the mother is Mary and the child is Jesus.  Her prayer, which is commonly known as the Magnificat, appears in Luke 1:46-55.  We don’t have time to look at it this morning, but it is clearly intended to make us look back to Hannah’s prayer, which in turn must be intended to make us look forward to Jesus.  Hannah never gave birth to a king or to the Lord’s anointed, but Mary did, and from a millennium before, Hannah by insipiration was anticipating that event.

Before the Lord’s Supper, then, let’s reflect on Jesus as the greatest fulfillment of everything we’ve seen in 1 Samuel 2.  Through Jesus, God overturns everything that the world values.  In Christ, it is no longer the mighty, the wealthy, and the proud who are on top.  Instead, it is the lowly, the poor, and the humble, not because the mice somehow grew fangs, but because God has delivered them.  As we partake of this memorial meal that celebrates the death that did deliver us, let’s remember our Savior.  Against Him, we cannot prevail.  With Him, we cannot fail.
