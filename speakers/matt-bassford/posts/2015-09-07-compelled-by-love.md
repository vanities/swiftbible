I’ve been known to say that if the Bible weren’t
well-written, I wouldn’t be a Christian. 
I’ve always been someone who loved words and who loved to write, and
back when I was a teenager, one of the things that led me to appreciate the
Bible on my own was the beauty of its language. 
Conversely, if the Bible weren’t well-written, if its language had all
the grace and the beauty of the instruction manual for your lawn mower, that
would be a sign to me that it didn’t come from God.

However, even within the Bible, I still have passages that I
think are particularly beautiful.  One of
my favorites is the section of Scripture running roughly from 2 Corinthians 4
through 2 Corinthians 6.  It’s beautiful
not merely because of Paul’s command of *Koine*
Greek, but because of the beauty of the concepts it expresses.  As we consider these concepts, they’ll remind
us of what it means to be a Christian. 
Let’s consider, then, what Paul has to say about being compelled by
love.



**The Light of the
Gospel**

In the first section of this text, Paul discusses the
implications of the light of the gospel. 
He begins here by explaining **HIS
MINISTRY.** Let’s read together from 2
Corinthians 4:1-6.  There are a couple of
points here worth exploring.  The first
concerns Paul’s conduct.  Here, he tells
the Corinthians that because he has received the ministry of the gospel, he has
to live in a certain way.  He has to
renounce shameful things, crafty behavior, and adulterating the gospel.  In other words, Paul doesn’t live to please
himself or other people.  He lives to
please God, and the honest simplicity of his God-pleasing behavior shows
everybody what kind of man he is.

This is the way that we ought to live too.  After all, “minister” isn’t Bible code for
some kind of clergyman.  Instead, it
simply means “servant”, and every one of us who has obeyed the gospel is
supposed to be a servant of God.  We too,
then, have to live to please God, rather than ourselves or other people.  If we do, Christ will be plainly seen in us
as He was plainly seen in Paul.

Second, let’s look at what Paul has to say about the light
of the gospel.  If we imitate Paul’s
example, we can’t help but shine, any more than Paul could.  We won’t get any glory from it, but God
will.  And yet, even when we do
everything right, we’re still going to encounter the same thing that Paul
did.  We’re going to encounter people who
don’t seem to get it and don’t want to follow. 
Paul tells us here that the problem with those people is that Satan has
blinded them to the truth.  To me, this
explains a lot.  How is it possible that somebody
can look at the dozen-plus passages in Scripture that command baptism for the
forgiveness of sins, yet deny that they have to be baptized to be saved?  This is how. 
God is still working today, but sadly, so is the devil.

From here, Paul explains that the treasure of the gospel is
contained in **EARTHEN VESSELS.** Consider 2 Corinthians 4:7-15.  First of all, this text should temper our
expectations about what being a Christian will mean in earthly terms.  This is a far cry from all of those big-name
preachers out in the religious world with their helicopters and private
jets.  For Paul, being a Christian
doesn’t mean health and wealth.  To the
contrary, it means carrying about in his own body the dying of Jesus.  Christ suffered, so God reveals Christ in
Paul through Paul’s suffering.  The only
possible reason why Paul would undergo such things was because he believed
Jesus was the Christ.

Likewise, friends, we should act in ways that can only be
explained by our faith in Christ.  We
need to be people who don’t lie, even though there’s an obvious advantage to
lying.  We need to be people who don’t
swear, even when we’re under great emotional stress.  We need to be people who would rather keep
our word and be ruined financially than break it to financial benefit.  We need to be people who do the right thing
because it is the right thing, because it’s what God wants.

In the next portion of the text, Paul reveals why he’s
willing to be delivered over to death for the sake of life in others. His faith
isn’t only that Jesus is the Son of God. 
It is that Jesus is the resurrection and the life.  He anticipates the day when he will enjoy the
resurrection.  What’s more, other people
are so important to him that he is willing even to suffer in this life so that
they can be raised along with him.  He
seeks only the good of others and the glory of God.

Finally in this section, Paul goes into greater detail
describing **THE ETERNAL WEIGHT OF GLORY** that
he hopes to experience.  Here, let’s read
from 2 Corinthians 4:16-5:5.  Paul’s
words here are all the more striking in light of what we know about his
life.  A little later on in 2
Corinthians, Paul lists all the trials he has faced for Christ, everything from
stoning to shipwreck.  And yet, he
dismisses all of those things, things that we’d probably be boo-hooing about
for years, as “momentary, light affliction”! 
The only reason that they’re insignificant to Paul is because nothing on
earth can be significant to Paul.  He’s
so focused on the joys of heaven that nothing that life can throw at him will
even register on the scale.

In fact, Paul says, the life that he hopes to enjoy with God
is so real to him that it makes life on earth unreal.  His home isn’t on earth.  His home is in heaven, and all that he can
think about on earth is the joy that he will experience in heaven.  His suffering only makes him long all the
more.

This, friends, needs to be the way that we too look at earth
and look at heaven.  In Hebrews 11, the
Hebrews writer reports that the heroes of old confessed that they were
strangers and exiles on the earth.  Is
that us too?  Do we stand out as people
who have our hearts set someplace that is not here, or do we blend right in
with our worldly friends and neighbors, with the people who do claim this world
as their home?  Only if we truly long for
heaven will we end up in heaven.

**The Love of Christ**

In the next section, Paul examines the appeal of the love of
Christ.  As we consider his thoughts
here, this will also help us to prepare our minds for the Lord’s Supper.  Within this context, Paul first addresses the
subject of **WALKING BY FAITH.** Let’s read here from 2 Corinthians
5:6-10.  Once again, let’s notice the
close connection between this text and what we looked at in the last
section.  Paul’s longing for heaven isn’t
merely something that he talks about. 
Instead, it transforms the way that he lives his life.  Even if he dies, well, that just takes him where
he wanted to be all along!  His only goal
is to do the things that please God.

To make a poker analogy out of this, Paul is all-in on
Jesus.  He has shoved all of his chips to
the middle of the table.  If Christ is
faithful, he will be rewarded beyond his wildest dreams.  If Christ is unfaithful, he’s going to be
utterly ruined.  Only his complete faith
justifies the risk.

Sadly, it’s too often the case that we aren’t willing to go
all-in on Jesus ourselves.  We’re pretty
sure that He will reward us, but we’re not convinced.  As a result, we hedge our bets.  We try to walk the line of keeping Jesus
happy on one side while sinning a little bit here and there on the other side.  We’re willing to suffer a little bit, but
we’re not willing to turn our lives upside down.

Paul points out the problem with this half-hearted
Christianity in the last verse of this reading. 
Every one of us is going to stand before the judgment seat of
Christ.  If we lived our lives with any
other goal than pleasing Him, we can’t hope to hide that.  Paul is an all-in Christian because all-in
Christianity is the only kind that makes sense. 
That’s the kind we need to be too.

Paul next reveals, though, that obedience isn’t just about a
rational calculation.  It’s about **THE LOVE OF CHRIST.** Here, let’s give consideration to 2
Corinthians 5:11-15.  In appealing to the
Corinthians, Paul isn’t merely doing the minimum necessary to get God off his
back about it.  Instead, he’s driving
himself crazy trying to figure out a way to reach them.  Why? 
Because he feels compelled to do it by the love of Jesus.

I’m fond of talking about my 500 favorite passages in the
Bible, but 2 Corinthians 5:14-15 might be my favorite favorite.  This, to me, is what Christianity is all
about.  It’s about confronting the
reality of the fact that Jesus died for me and acknowledging that because He
did, I don’t get to go on being the same person.  Instead, I’m so deeply in debt to Him that
I’m never, ever going to get out from under it. 
I am so wholly overwhelmed by His love that it leaves me with no choice
but to go out and be the person that He wants me to be, instead of the person
that I want me to be.  When I’m face to
face with Jesus on the cross, His example doesn’t allow me to do anything less.

This, friends, illustrates better than anything else what
we’ve gotten ourselves into as Christians. 
Without a word, the suffering and love of Jesus demand that we surrender
ourselves completely to Him.  The stop
line, the standard for when we’ve given enough, is when our lives look just
like His life, when we are obeying Him perfectly in everything.  I can’t even get my mind around what that
would mean for me, much less do it. 
Until we get there, though, our job is to keep trying.

Finally in this section, Paul urges the Corinthians, and
indeed us, to accept **THE RECONCILIATION**
that God is offering.  Here, let’s read
from 2 Corinthians 5:16-21.  Paul says
first of all that the Corinthians should regard him as a new creature.  He has been transformed by his encounter with
the love of Christ.  Because this is what
Paul has become, essentially Christ’s puppet, the Corinthians need to recognize
that it isn’t really Paul who’s reaching out to them.  It’s Christ.

There’s a camp song out there that says, “The gospel in a
word is love.”  I know they’re getting
that from Romans 13, but another good one-word gospel summary is
“reconciliation”.  Before Jesus died, we
were estranged from God by our sin. 
Jesus, however, took that sin upon Himself so that we could be counted
righteous.  Now that that barrier of sin
has been removed, we can be reconciled to God and enjoy fellowship and unity
with Him.  However, as with Paul, that’s
not where the work of reconciliation is supposed to end.  Instead, through Jesus, God is now holding
His arms open to everybody, and we’re supposed to do what we can to bring them
to Him.

Before the Lord’s Supper, then, let’s reflect on
reconciliation in our lives.  Jesus paid
such a heavy price to open that door for us! 
The blessings of reconciliation are many; the benefits of continued
rebellion are nonexistent.  Have we
embraced God as fully as Jesus allows us to? 
Do we do our best to lead others toward Him too?  Let’s ponder these things as we partake.

**Come Out From Among Them**

The final portion of the text that we’re going to be
examining this morning contains Paul’s appeal to the Corinthians to come out
from the world.  This begins with his
description of what it means to be **A
SERVANT OF GOD.** Look at 2
Corinthians 6:1-10.  The list from v. 3
to v. 10 isn’t my favorite favorite passage of Scripture, but it’s definitely
in the Top 500 list.  In places, the
thoughts of the Bible have an almost musical sound to me, and this is one of
those places.  It starts off as a list of
bad stuff, then morphs to a list of good stuff, then wraps up with a list of
paradoxes.

In doing so, the context raises the point that Paul’s life
can only be summarized by paradox.  He
was persecuted yet possessed the power of God. 
He was poor, yet shared unimaginable riches with others.  His life was filled with sorrow and
suffering, yet he rejoiced.  Indeed, this
directs us to a larger truth:  the
paradox that dominates the story of the whole Bible.  It is paradoxical that in the death of Jesus,
we should find life.  It is paradoxical
that the greatest victory Satan ever won was simultaneously his greatest
defeat.  And so on.  Sometimes, the paradoxes start linking up
into whole chains of opposites that shouldn’t go together, but in the Bible
somehow do.  In fact, it’s probably not
too great a claim to assert that paradox is a sign of the working of God.

If paradox is so important Biblically, such a central part
of the discipleship of Paul, it should be a central part of our lives too.  We should be people who are freed from the
slavery of sin, yet bound to the service of Jesus.  We should reply to curses with blessings, to
hatred with love.  We should be so
resolute in proclaiming the truth that other people start telling lies about
us.  For us too, paradox is the sign that
God is active in our lives.  God’s people
are paradoxical people.

Now that Paul has presented what he does, he tells the
Corinthians that they need to **REJECT
WORLDLINESS.** Here, let’s read from 2
Corinthians 6:11-18.  The point that Paul
is making here is that the Corinthians have gotten so close to unbelievers that
they aren’t able to receive Paul, and, for that matter, to receive Christ.  The solution to the problem is for them to
get rid of these worldly relationships that are dragging them down spiritually.

Let’s start by looking at what this passage does not
say.  It does not say that it is a sin
for a Christian to marry a non-Christian. 
First of all, we’ve just examined three chapters of Scripture leading up
to this passage.  Nowhere in any of it
has Paul been talking about marriage.  He
does not mention marriage here.  To say,
then, that this passage is about marriage is a stretch.

Second, even to say that Paul would have had an opinion
about who Christians should marry is an anachronism.  Today, by and large, we do choose whom we
marry, but 2000 years ago, the vast majority of marriages were arranged.  As is still the custom in many parts of the
world, your family told you whom you were going to marry, and you didn’t have
much choice about it.  In that setting,
advice to choose a Christian spouse makes as much sense as advice to choose
Christian parents!

Instead, Paul’s point is that we should avoid and even
discontinue voluntary relationships with people who are bad for us
spiritually.  This is not something we
can break down into bright-line advice like, “Only have Christian friends, and
only marry Christians.”  That advice
oversimplifies things on both sides of the line.  A non-Christian friend is not necessarily
going to corrupt us.  Conversely, just
because we marry somebody who has been baptized does not mean that we’re
actually marrying a good person.  All of
us have known “Christians” who proved to be terrible husbands and terrible
wives.

However, we do have to be honest about the relationships
we’re forming.  Just because we like them
or even love them does not mean that they will be spiritually good for us.  Of course, if we’re married to them, we’re
married to them for good or bad.  Short
of that, though, it’s our responsibility to separate ourselves from worldly
people so we can draw closer to God.
