In late March, my co-worker Clay made a meme.  It was based on a picture of the COVID virus,
and it bore the inscription, “Pray for a Vaccine.”  I liked it and approved of the message, as
did everyone else at Jackson Heights.

In early May, someone took a picture of the meme on our sign
and uploaded it to a Facebook group for people who are opposed to
vaccination.  They descended en masse on
our Facebook page, leaving dozens of comments on the “Pray for a Vaccine” post even
after we asked them to stop.  They also
began spamming the church email account, filling up the church voice mailbox,
and leaving slanderous negative reviews (“This church promotes abortion!”) on
the page. 

Attempting to police the mess quickly became a full-time job
for the church preachers, secretaries and Web admins.  In an attempt to diminish the distraction
(which eventually ended it), Clay ended up removing the meme from our sign and
Facebook page.

I don’t tell this story to bring discredit on those who are
morally opposed to vaccination.  Though I
disagree with them, I respect their right to form their own judgments and to
live according to their own conscience.  However,
I think the conduct of these anti-vaccination extremists is worthy of further
examination.

I am sure that in some corner of the Internet, they exchanged
electronic high-fives and celebrated their “victory”.  Deeper consideration, though, reveals that it
is perhaps not what they would wish it to be.

First, it came at the cost of ungodliness.  Many of these people claimed to be
Christians, and Christians are called to persuade, not bully.  Contrary to the claims that some of them
made, I don’t find the word “vaccine” in the Bible, but there are many passages
that warn against contentiousness and strife. 
Perhaps I’m wrong, and praying for a COVID-19 vaccine is sinful.  Their behavior certainly was sinful.

Second, their behavior changed electrons but not
hearts.  Clay didn’t take down the meme because
they persuaded him.  He took it down
because their evil conduct was interfering with the work of proclaiming the
gospel.  In fact, I’m quite sure that
they didn’t persuade anybody.  It’s
ironic that those who want others to believe that vaccinations are wrong didn’t
change anyone’s belief.

In fact, their actions had the opposite effect.  Even though we didn’t waste time arguing with
them, they gave us a powerful incentive to find flaws with their
arguments.  I will admit that never
before had I engaged with the claim that it’s wrong to vaccinate because (some)
vaccines are made using tissue from aborted fetuses.  I have now, and it has left me more convinced
that vaccination is morally acceptable than I was before. 

There is, of course, a much broader application, for all of
us who discuss our views with others. 
Especially online, we all know the temptation to drown out those who
disagree with us, to bury them beneath a landslide of text.  Though our efforts to do so may vary in scope
from those of the anti-vaccination extremists, they do not vary in intent.

Before we click “Post” on that latest polemic, then, we
should ask whether our means truly are suited to our ends.
