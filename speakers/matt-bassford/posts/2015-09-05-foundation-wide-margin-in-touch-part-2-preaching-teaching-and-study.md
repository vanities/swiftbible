**OVERVIEW.** It’s a large, heavy Bible, and it doesn’t
have cross-references.  On the other
hand, the paper is phenomenal, and it has more room for writing than anything
short of an LCBP Note-Taker’s Edition.  I
care more about the second two qualities than the first two.

**INTRODUCTION.** Anybody who reads my reviews will
probably be able to anticipate what I put the In Touch through.  For two weeks, I used it daily in study and
sermon preparation.  I also used it to
preach three sermons and teach three adult Bible classes.  Here are my impressions:



**PREACHING.** As I’ve said throughout the review so
far, the In Touch is a big Bible, and it didn’t somehow magically shrink in
size just because I stepped up to the pulpit. 
This has both its minuses and its pluses.

Let’s start with the former first.  I’m used to preaching out of a fairly
good-sized Bible.  My Cambridge NASB
Wide-Margin has a comparable trim size, and it weighs a substantial 2 pounds,
10 ounces.  The In Touch has about six
ounces on it, and I can tell the difference. 
A three-pound Bible is really a bit bigger than I want to tote around
for 45 minutes, and there were times when I left the In Touch in the pulpit and
wandered away on my own.  Hopefully, my
straying from the word of God was literal rather than metaphorical.  I’m definitely more comfortable with the size
of the Wide-Margin.

However, the pluses are equally impressive.  First of all, even though the In Touch is a
heavy Bible, all sorts of good things come along with that weight.  It truly is a wide-margin Bible, and
not all Bibles that proclaim themselves to be wide-margin Bibles actually are.  However, such Bibles are my weapon of choice
in the pulpit these days.

Indeed, there is more room to write in the margins of the In
Touch than in any other Bible I’ve ever used. 
The margins themselves don’t look unusual.  They’re a fairly standard 1 ¼” on the outside
and ⅞” on the inside.

However, because the In Touch is a moderately large-print
Bible (10.5-point Times New Roman as opposed to, say, the 8-point-ish Lexicon
No. 1 of the Cambridge Wide-Margin) and is set verse-by-verse rather than in
paragraphs, each verse gets more margin space than it would in a different wide-margin
Bible.  For instance, John 21 in the
Cambridge NASB Wide Margin is allotted about 13 inches of margin space.  In the In Touch, the same chapter has about
18 inches, and that is a big difference. 
Also, I was able to use the space at the ends of verses to add further
notes.

All of this had an impact. 
One of the sermons I preached was a pretty tight exposition of the end
of James 3 and the entirety of James 4. 
I had a lot to say about an unusually small (for me) number of
verses.  I was able to fit all the notes
I needed to include in the In Touch, but I’m not sure I would have been able to
do so in my Cambridge Wide-Margin.

Part of the heft of the In Touch, then, comes from its bountiful
stretches of white space.  The rest of it
comes from the paper, that beautiful, opaque, illegal-to-manufacture-now
paper.  Good paper is heavy, and there’s
no way to avoid the problem.

However, that same paper is also a Bible-writer’s
dream.  I used the same red Pigma Micron
005 pen on the In Touch that I use on my Cambridge Wide-Margin, and once again,
my impression was that the In Touch suffered less from bleed-through than the
Wide-Margin does.  The Wide-Margin is
certainly usable, but the In Touch is better.

Also, the format of the In Touch lends itself well to
preaching.  As I said above, it’s
double-column and verse-by-verse, with verse numbers in what looks like
10.5-point Arial.  It’s some contrasting
sans-serif font, anyway.  If you miss a
citation while using the In Touch, it’s your own fault.  I didn’t miss any.

Basically, then, what you have in the In Touch is wide
margins, great paper, and the text: 
everything you need to preach a sermon, and nothing you don’t.  This is an outstanding Bible for the pulpit.

**TEACHING.** At the risk of sounding repetitive, I
will once again note that the In Touch is a big Bible, and that it once again
affected the way that I used it.  When
teaching our auditorium Bible class, I often left it on the lectern and roamed
around clutching my lesson book instead. 
It’s not like the Bible was ever more than a few steps away, but I would
still prefer to have a Bible light enough to be comfortable to carry the entire
time.

Also, as I observed above, the In Touch is a purist’s
Bible.  It has translators’ notes but no
cross-references.  That’s perfectly fine
in a sermon—if I need a cross-reference, I’ll just write it in myself—but it
can be more problematic in a Bible class, where I don’t necessarily know what
cross-references I’ll need beforehand. 
This time around, I didn’t have any trouble coming up with book,
chapter, and verse on my own for the inevitable out-of-left-field comments and
questions, but it’s conceivable that I might have.  If I were to adopt an In Touch as my primary
Bible, I would certainly spend a few hours at least writing in the citations
for Old-Testament prophecies cited in the New Testament.

**STUDY.** Once again, there’s some bad news here,
but there’s also some good news.  The bad
news is that, like all Bibles before the advent of the Cambridge Clarion about
five years ago now, the In Touch isn’t line-matched.  The good news is that the paper is so opaque
that it doesn’t have to be line-matched.

That, combined with the relatively large font and crisp USA
print job, makes it extremely readable, even in large chunks.  As I was preparing to write a lesson book, I
read the entirety of Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and Song of Solomon in one
sitting, and the In Touch didn’t give my eyes any trouble.

Cross-references I didn’t have, but I didn’t want them much
either.  I only remember wanting one on
one occasion, and I got up out of my chair, walked across the room, and looked
it up in an SCR.  Also, I’ve lost a
little bit of weight over the past two weeks. 
Coincidence?

Once again, when it comes to ease of citation, the In Touch is as good as it
gets.  I’m fairly certain that I didn’t
miss any while putting together sermons and lesson books for two weeks.

**CONCLUSION.** Basically, I loved everything about the
In Touch, except that I wished it weighed less, and that it had
cross-references.  I don’t want much, do
I?  Every Bible is the product of a
series of harsh compromises, and a wide-margin Bible has to make more
compromises than most.  The designers of
the In Touch opted to prioritize paper, print size, and margin space at the
expense of everything else.  I think
that’s an extremely defensible design decision that makes a strong statement
about what a Bible is and what it ought to be used for.  I find the purity of the concept appealing,
and I largely agree with the sentiments behind it.  If you want a study Bible, make your own,
right down to the cross-references that you have discovered for yourself in
your own study.

I think the In Touch deserves a 9.5/10 for preaching,
teaching, and study.
