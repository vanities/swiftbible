**OVERVIEW.** This is a nicely proportioned little
carry Bible.  The polyurethane cover is
well constructed and attractive, and the text block is sewn.  At $28 retail, the Large-Print Compact isn’t
a fabulous deal, but it’s competitive with other mass-market, poly-cover
Bibles.

\*             \*             \*

Like most of the Bibles I review these days, my LPC is a
review copy.  Obviously, I write about
Bibles because I like them, but I still feel responsible for evaluating all the
Bibles I review as objectively as possible, highlighting both their strengths
and their weaknesses.  Here’s my take on
the physical characteristics of the LPC:

**VITAL STATISTICS**

**Translation:**  English Standard Version (2011)

**Cover:**  Polyurethane (“TruTone”)

**Binding:**  Sewn

**Trim Size:**   4 ½“ x 6 ½“

**Text Block Width:**  1”

**Weight:**  15 ounces

**Column:**  Double

**Layout:**  Paragraphed

**Font Size:**  Standard (8-point Lexicon No. 1)

**Words of Christ:** Red

**Cross-References:**  No

**Ribbon Marker:**  1

**SIZE.** My misgivings about the name of the LPC
aside, this is a handy little Bible. 
Though thicker than a Pitt Minion, it’s actually a little narrower and
substantially shorter.  I can’t put it in
my hip pocket, but it will fit anywhere else I would want to carry a
Bible.  It will rattle around in any but
the most overstuffed of glove boxes and fits easily into the side pocket of a
backpack, laptop case, or suitcase.  I
could take it on vacation without a qualm because I would always have some
place to put it to keep it safe.  Because
of its readable standard-sized print, the LPC makes an excellent one-and-only
candidate for those who prefer text Bibles to reference Bibles.

**COVER.** My LPC has a polyurethane cover, and none
of Crossway’s LPC’s are available with a cover above poly, unless you count
bonded leather as a superior product. 
Personally, I’d just buy the poly version and be content with that.  I simply don’t care for bonded leather in
Bible covers.

Unlike some Bible-design mistakes of years past, the cover
of my LPC is subtly and tastefully designed, in what Crossway calls a
“portfolio” design in two shades of brown. 
The perimeter of the cover and the borders between differing shades of
brown are stitched.  As with many
Crossway Bibles, the spine contains about fifteen repetitions of some form of
“ESV” (actually three).  However, because
they’re all dry-stamped, they’re not as busy as they would otherwise be.  The spine also bears half a dozen dry-stamped
decorative bands.

As poly covers go, the LPC’s cover doesn’t have the
flimsy-feeling softness of some other poly Bibles, but neither does it feel
rigid and inflexible like some of Crossway’s low-end value-poly covers.  As is usual for Crossway poly Bibles, the
endpapers are thick paper rather than leatherette.  Generally speaking, leatherette endpapers are
more durable than the paper variety, but I used a Crossway Personal Size
Reference Bible with paper endpapers daily for five years and still didn’t
reach the failure point.  Conversely, the
leatherette endpapers of my photographer’s Crossway Legacy split down the hinge
after less than a year of use.  In short,
I don’t foresee any durability problems for a poly-cover LPC, provided, of
course, that it is kept away from heat and direct sunlight.

I like my LPC.  In
fact, I like it a lot, enough to add it to my permanent collection as the
representative of the ESV.  The poly
doesn’t have a nice leathery smell to it because it’s poly and not leather, but
this is still an attractive, dignified Bible. 
I don’t have any qualms about being seen in public with it or even
preaching from it.  If I were going to
adopt the LPC as my one-and-only Bible, I would probably invest in a rebind,
but the poly will hold up just fine if I’m only using it for occasional reading
and reference.

**TEXT BLOCK.** As with nearly everything in Crossway’s
lineup, the LPC has a sewn binding. 
Without this, the durability of the cover would be irrelevant because
the Bible would start shedding pages and chunks of pages in about six
months.  However, I can see signatures
from the top of the spine and thread in the gutter, so this is a Bible that
will stand up to regular use.

Like most mass-market Bibles, the LPC appears to have been
printed cross-grain, probably to reduce page curling from the (spoiler alert!)
very thin paper used.  As is usually the
case, this leads to a measure of cockling, but the cockling in the LPC is
slight.  I hear crackling when I open the
Bible, and I feel bumps when I run my finger down the gutter, but I can’t look
at the gutter and see the bumps. 
Cockling at this level certainly isn’t enough to deter me from using the
Bible.

**VALUE.** As I mentioned above, the LPC retails for
$28.  This is slightly cheaper than a
Zondervan NIV ReadEasy Compact (retail price $30) and basically the same price
as a Foundation (NASB) Large-Print Compact ($27).  The price of the Crossway LPC, then, neither
impresses nor horrifies me.  It’s easy to
find one online for less than $20, and that’s a fair price for this kind of
Bible.

**CONCLUSION.** There’s simply not a lot to dislike about
the Large-Print Compact.  The size is
great, the cover is well designed and made, and the binding is sewn.  Anybody who buys this Bible will be getting a
good mass-market Bible.  I think it
deserves a 9/10 in Physical Form.
