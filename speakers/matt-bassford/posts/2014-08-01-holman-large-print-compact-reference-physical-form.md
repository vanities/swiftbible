This Bible came to me as a review
copy.  After spending hours on
christianbook.com studying excerpts of every NKJV they have for sale, I
concluded that Holman’s Large Print Reference Bible line showed more promise
than most of them.  I requested and
received a review copy in genuine brown cowhide.  Anyone who worries that my independence may
be compromised by the receipt of a review copy should probably read the rest of
the review.

**VITAL STATISTICS**

Translation:  New King James
Version (1982)

Cover:  Cowhide

Binding:  Glued

Trim Size:  3⅞” x 5⅝”

Text Block Width:  1⅛”

Weight:  14 ounces

Column:  Double

Layout:  Paragraphed

Type Size:  8

Words of Christ:  Red

Cross-References:  Yes

Ribbon Marker:  1



As any Bible geek knows, different translations have different
strengths and weaknesses in physical form. 
Crossway is very innovative in coming up with new formats for the
ESV.  The NASB has a couple of excellent
text blocks.  Even the venerable KJV has
some decent layouts paired with sumptuous covers.  The NKJV, on the other hand. . . well. . .

As I understand it, back before I became interested in Bibles, there
used to be nice NKJV’s.  You had the
Nelson Signature Series, which were nice Bibles with premium bindings.  I’ve seen some really intriguing pictures
online.  However, Nelson decided to stop
making premium Signature Series Bibles (although you can still find them with
Mossy Oak covers—literally, a photo of an oak tree trunk on the cover of your
Bible--if you’re so inclined) in favor of legions of bonded-leather glue-bound
Bibles with stodgy verse-by-verse, center-column-reference layouts.

I’ve always thought this was a shame. 
The NKJV is a fine translation, and its very name has branding allure
that money can’t buy.  I go to church
with people who use the NKJV because it’s the New *King James Version*.  It
sounds like the Bible that everybody has heard of.  I wouldn’t be surprised if a lot of NKJV fans
end up using the translation on their iPads because the print versions are so
shoddy.  I’ve taken to saying that I
don’t know of a single mass-market edition of the NKJV that I would
recommend.  This review is the story of
how the Holman LPCR came so, so frustratingly close to changing that—and failed.

**BINDING.** The LPCR impressed right out of the
box.  I knew it was a compact Bible, but
I didn’t know *how* compact until I
actually held it.  It’s the same width as
a paperback novel, an inch shorter, and only about an inch thick.  “Stubby” would be a good descriptor for
it.  So would “chunky”.

However, like a six-year-old boy puffing out his chest for the camera,
the LPCR is proud of its size.  The spine
especially has some dramatic design elements: 
an outsized NKJV logo and massive spine ribbing that would be prominent
on a Bible twice this size.  I normally
like my Bible covers subtle and understated, but the brashness of the LPCR’s
spine works for me.

The cowhide of the cover itself is soft, fairly thin, and flexible.  It reminds me of the leather in a nice pair
of women’s gloves.  However, I don’t have
any concerns about its durability.  I
suspect it would scuff up pretty impressively with prolonged use, but I don’t think
it’ll start falling apart.  The copyright
page won’t tell me, but from what I’ve seen, I suspect this is an ABBA
binding.  It does have that hand-bound
look and feel to it.  Within, the LPCR
has a standard paste-off binding with leatherette endpapers, much like you
would expect to see in any fine Bible that isn’t actually edge-lined.  All in all, it is a delight to touch and
hold.

**TEXT BLOCK.** Let’s start with the nice things
first.  Believe it or not, Holman went to
our Danish friends at 2K (the designers of the Schuyler Quentel series, among
other projects) and had them set the text. 
Even though I will have much more to say about this in the later
sections of the review, I think the layout is outstanding.  It’s certainly the best-laid-out NKJV I’ve
seen, and it’s one of my favorite Bible layouts for any translation, period.

The opacity of the paper in the LPCR isn’t great, but, in another sign
of the cleverness of 2K, the text is line-matched.  This ought to be celebrated.  I’m so glad to see another mass-market
publisher besides Crossway getting the memo about the importance of
line-matching.

But.  When it comes to the LPRC,
there is a fatal flaw to this tragic hero, a skeleton at the wedding
feast.  For reasons beyond my
comprehension, Holman commissioned a new, impressive setting of the NKJV, bound
it in an attractive cover, did everything that they needed to do to make a
truly top-notch Bible. . . and then glued the text block.

My emotions concerning this cycled from dismay to elation to dismay in
about twelve hours.  When I opened the
box, the first thing I did was to admire the cover.  The second was to peek under the headband for
signatures.  No signatures to be
seen.

My heart sank, but because I couldn’t believe that Holman would glue
this Bible (Surely they wouldn’t, would they?), I started checking inside for
evidence of sewing.  There, I saw a
series of regular indentations in the gutter like you would expect from a sewn
Bible.  I was delighted.  Maybe the signatures got squashed flat in the
printing process or something!

However, things just didn’t add up. 
The Bible was stiff and unwilling to open flat.  No matter how I stared at the headband and
tailband, I still didn’t see signatures. 
Finally, I got an X-Acto knife and started poking at those gutter
indentations.  I discovered that they
were filled not with thread, but with glue. 
They were just notches cut in the text block so the glue in the spine
would hold better.

I have to be honest.  This
hurts.  I would almost rather be
reviewing some bonded-leather clunker with no redeeming qualities other than
the truth it contains.  I’ve used Bibles
like that before, and my expectations for them are so low that I almost cannot
be disappointed.  The Holman LPCR,
however, does so many things *right*.  Covers are hard to do right.  Its cover is done right.  Settings are *really* hard to do right.  Its
setting is done right.

Then, the Bible’s designers got to something easy—sewing the
binding.  I have half a dozen friends who
produced a hymnal a few years back. 
They’re smart folks, but they aren’t professional designers or
publishers.  And yet, this collection of
amateurs knew enough to Smyth-sew the binding. 
That’s not a hard decision for any book that’s going to see repeated
openings.  It’s a no-brainer.  I’m sure it added something to the cost, but
the hymnal retails, sewn binding and all, for $13.95.

Holman was faced with the same decision for its entire
brand-spanking-new, wonderfully designed Large Print Reference series.  Again, I’m sure a sewn binding would cost a
little bit more, but Crossway’s Value Thinline has a sewn binding and retails
for $16.99.  Zondervan’s full-featured
Thinline NASB, which is sewn, retails for $29.99.  A sewn binding doesn’t exactly price you out
of the market.

However, Holman decided not to sew the LPCR, and, like taking the road
less traveled, it makes all the difference. 
Sure, you can buy this nice cowhide Bible for $5 or $10 less than you
might have paid for a sewn version, but now this nice cowhide Bible is going to
start shedding pages like dandruff after six months of use.  I would gladly have paid $50 for a sewn
cowhide LPCR (gorgeous setting!  gorgeous
setting!) to use as an NKJV reader, but I’m not going to pay anybody anything
for any Bible with a glued binding, period.

In the last portion of this review, I’m going to appeal directly to
Holman.  I don’t know if it’ll do any
good, but they did send me a free Bible that retails for $40, so they may as
well get something out of their investment. 
Please, pretty please, with a seraph on top, start publishing this Bible
with a sewn binding!  I suspect that
somewhere in the B&H publishing empire, there is a market analyst without a
soul, and this soulless market analyst has concluded that most Holman Bible purchasers
do not read their Bibles, have not learned that use wrecks a glued binding, and
so will not pay more money for a product they will not use.

The market analyst with no soul may be right.  I don’t care. 
Don’t listen to him.  For the sake
of pride in your product (because this could be a wonderful product), for the
sake of people like me, who read their Bibles and want them to last, for the
sake of God’s word between those two covers, sew the binding.

And maybe, just maybe, if you produce a Bible that is meant to be
used, you will find that more people use it.

**PHYSICAL FORM RATING:  2/5**. 
The Bible probably deserves better than this, but for me, the glued
binding, like a cockroach in a bowl of cherries, ruins the whole experience.
