In just two more days from now, I’ll be turning 37.  To many of you, at 37, I still probably look
like I just got out of diapers, but from my perspective, at least, I’ve been
around long enough to see some things. 
The world I experience today is very different from the world I remember
from high school.  Back then, I never
imagined that I would see a government official jailed because of a stand she
took on the basis of her faith in Christ. 
To us, her profession of faith seems inconsistent with the way she
lives, but I see no reason to believe that the federal judge would have treated
a more consistent believer any more kindly.

However, even though nations may change, the word of God
doesn’t.  In fact, as our day continues
to darken, texts that might not have seemed very applicable 25 years ago now
have taken on renewed relevance.  Texts
about persecution certainly fall into this category, but so too do passages
about God’s coming judgment and what that will mean for the righteous and the
wicked alike.  For an example of just
such a passage, let’s turn our attention to the book of Micah.



**The Coming of the
Lord**

The first part of the book warns about the consequences of
the coming of the Lord.  This begins with
**GOD’S WITNESS** against His
people.  Here, let’s read the entirety of
Micah 1.  Unlike many other prophetic
books, Micah does not concern itself with pronouncing judgments against all the
nations around God’s people.  Instead,
everything in it concerns Israel and Judah. 
In fact, we can date the book using the two kings of Judah mentioned in
the first verse.  Both Ahaz and Hezekiah
reigned from about 750 to 700 BC, so we can conclude that Micah wrote then too.

This was a time of tremendous political upheaval in the area
of Palestine.  This was just about high
tide for the Assyrian Empire, which was coming down out of the north like a
tidal wave, destroying everything in its path. 
Micah first of all warns that God would not rescue Israel from this
menace.  Instead, Samaria, the very
capital of Israel, was going to be destroyed. 
The idolatry of Israel had become too much for God to endure, so the
nation was going to be exiled.

After this blanket promise of devastation, Micah gets a lot
more specific about what’s going to happen to Judah.  From 1:8 to 1:15, we see a list of woes
pronounced against various obscure towns in Judah.  However, there are two things interesting
about this list.  First of all, it’s a
list of puns.  All of the bad things said
to these towns are in some way related to the name of the town.  For instance, “Beth-le-aphrah” means “house of
dust”, so they are instructed to roll in the dust.  “Shaphir” means “pleasant”, so they’ll face
shameful nakedness.  And so on.

Second, all of these towns are found in a very specific
place.  They’re all in the Philistine
plain, on the Mediterranean side of the hills that run down the middle of
Israel and Judah.  This is significant
because when the Assyrians invaded Judah, that’s the way they went.  They conquered the Philistines, took Lachish,
the second most important city of Judah, and wiped out everything else along
the way.  Here, Micah is precisely
correct in predicting the immediate future.

After this, we come to a description of **THE SINS OF THE PEOPLE.** It
appears in Micah 2.  This is not a
flattering portrait!  Instead, it vividly
depicts just how rotten the people of Judah have gotten.  They’re so eager to do evil that they can’t
sleep at night for plotting it.  They
tell the prophets to shut up because they don’t want to hear about the
unpleasant things the future has in store for them.  They plunder strangers.  They persecute defenseless women and
children.  In conclusion, Micah says, if
a liar started telling them about how glorious it is to get drunk, that’s the
kind of prophet they would listen to. 
This is a thoroughly debauched, depraved people!

Despite their debauchery, however, this is a people that God
wants to save rather than destroy. 
They’ve wandered far away from God, but He wants to bring them back to
Him like a flock of lost sheep back to the fold.  God still hasn’t given up on His people.

Today, this should be every bit as reassuring to us.  Hopefully, none of us are quite as wicked as
the people of Judah were, but no matter how wicked we may become, God still
wants us back too.  God will be the God
of judgment if we force Him to be, but He really wants to be the God of
mercy.  He loves us with an everlasting
love, and He yearns to forgive us, if only we will let Him.

In the next section, we see Micah detail **THE SINS OF THE LEADERS.** They are recounted in Micah 3.  As bad leaders often do, these men exploit
and ruin the common folk.  The judges can
be bribed, the priests distort the Law for money’s sake, and the prophets will
prophesy good things about the people who pay them and bad things about people
who don’t.  What’s worse, they cloak this
greedy behavior in the name of the Lord, as though they are doing His work.

Today, of course, we don’t have any shortage of corrupt
officials and money-loving religious leaders either.  I’m quite certain that God feels the same way
about them today as He did about their counterparts 2700 years ago.  They think that they can go on doing what
they’re doing without consequence, but judgment is still coming.  Remember how Micah 2 ended with God gathering
up His wayward sheep?  By contrast, Micah
3 ends with a promise that Jerusalem will be destroyed.  God doesn’t have as much sympathy for
misleading leaders as for misled people.

However, the story of this prophecy doesn’t end in Micah
3.  Instead, it shows up again about 150
years later, in Jeremiah 26.  There,
Jeremiah is on trial for prophesying that Jerusalem will be destroyed, and in
his defense, some of the elders point out that Micah had done the same thing in
the time of Hezekiah.  Because Hezekiah
repented, God saved Judah from the Assyrians. 
One verse delivered a whole nation. 
Let’s make sure that we allow the word to work in our lives too!

**A Bright Future**

After all that time spent on the grim present, Micah next
turns his attention to a bright future for God’s people, a future which we
happen to inhabit.  The first of these
hopeful texts concerns **THE MOUNTAIN OF
THE LORD.** Here, let’s look at Micah
4.  There are really two parts to this
chapter.  The first addresses the distant
future; the second addresses the near future.

Let’s take the first one first.  Here, Micah uses language that’s familiar to
us from elsewhere in the Bible.  The
prophet Isaiah, who lived at around the same time as Micah and was also around
for the Assyrian invasion, says basically the same thing in the beginning of
Isaiah 2.  Apparently, in the middle of
such a conflict-filled, tumultuous time, God’s people needed to hear that the
time would come when God’s mountain would be a mountain of peace.

As time would reveal, this mountain wasn’t the hunk of rock
in Palestine.  Instead, it was the church
that Christ would establish with His own blood. 
We see the essence of what this means in vs. 6-7.  God would take the misfits of mankind and
make them into His own people.

How wonderful a thought this is!  I suspect that from time to time, every one
of us struggles with feelings of unworthiness. 
Why would God want someone as inadequate and sin-filled as I am?  When we get to feeling this way, though, we
need to remember that it’s not about us. 
It’s about God, who is able to make anybody who turns to Him into what
He wants them to be.

However, before Judah reached the first prophecy, it had to
go through the unpleasant stuff in the second prophecy.  God would redeem His people, yes, but they
would have to go into exile in Babylon first. 
This is particularly interesting because at this point in time, the big
power in the Middle East is not Babylon. 
It’s Assyria.  In fact, the
Assyrians were about to conquer and destroy Babylon in 20 years or so.  However, Micah knows that by the time Judah’s
spiritual decay is complete, the Babylonians will be back on top again and the
instruments of God’s vengeance.

Next, Micah predicts the coming of **A RULER IN ISRAEL.** Consider
Micah 5.  Once again, this chapter really
has two parts.  The chronologically
first, which runs from vs. 5b-15, concerns how God is going to deliver Judah
from the Assyrians.  All of those things
certainly happened.  We know that the
reign of Hezekiah was marked by the destruction of idols.

Second, though, and more important to us, is the prophecy of
an eternal ruler of Israel who would come forth from Bethlehem.  This doesn’t fit any of the other kings of
the line of David, nor even David himself. 
Not even the most powerful Israelite monarchs were great to the ends of
the earth.  Instead, this is predicting
an event that not very many people noticed at the time—the birth of Jesus in
Bethlehem.  Jesus, of course, went on to
become all that was predicted here and more.

Before the Lord’s Supper, though, let’s zero in on one part
of this prophecy in particular—the prediction that Jesus would be peace for His
people.  As Jesus Himself said, this
peace is not a peace as the world gives. 
Instead, it’s the peace we share in with one another and with God.

**Following God**

In the final section of Micah, we see the prophet’s
presentation of what it means to follow God. 
This begins with his explanation of **THE
LORD’S REQUIREMENTS.** This appears in
Micah 6.  Structurally, this chapter has
four parts.  In the first, God explains
His intention:  to indict the Jews for
their sin.  Next, God explains what His
expectations are.  Third, He points out the
ways that the people have fallen short of those expectations.  Fourth and last, he describes the punishment
that they will face.

Within this, there are two contexts in particular that I
want to focus on.  The first is the
quasi-judicial language with which God opens the chapter.  Here, we see God holding Himself forth almost
like a prosecuting attorney.  His people have
wronged Him, so He’s going to lay out the charges against them.  He’s been watching, and He has the evidence
He needs to prove His claim.

When we recall that God is always with us, this should be a
thought that we either find extremely comforting or extremely terrifying.  He’s always nearby to help, but He’s also
always nearby to see all the evil that we do. 
We should never think that we can hide from Him.

Thankfully, though, we don’t have to worry about God’s
standard of judgment, because He explains quite clearly what He wants to see
from us.  In this context, it appears in
vs. 6-8.  Incidentally, this is the first
passage of Scripture that I ever set out to memorize of my own volition, and
even today, I still find it both beautiful and spiritually profound.  We can’t hope to buy God off with any kind of
external sacrifice, even one so extreme and gruesome as our firstborn
children.  Instead, God wants us, our
hearts and our lives.  If we give Him
these things, He will be pleased.

Finally, Micah explores **THE
REDEMPTION OF GOD.** Consider Micah
7.  In this text, Micah identifies two
kinds of oppression.  The first is the
oppression that Micah experiences because of the wickedness of the people
around him.  The second, though only
implied, is the oppression that Judah experiences at the hands of the wicked
nations round about.

In the face of both of these challenges, Micah expresses an
attitude of hope and trust in the Lord. 
God is going to hear and deliver him. 
If Micah falls, God will help him get back up.  If Micah ends up in the darkness, God will be
his light.  God will save His servant and
His people.

Today, brethren, we should be filled with this same
unquenchable, unconquerable hope.  If we
walk faithfully with God, there is a sense in which our lives are like a
superhero movie.  No matter how scary
things may get in the middle, no matter how triumphant the villains may seem to
be, we still know how it’s going to turn out in the end.  God is the superhero, and He’s going to win.  No matter how great our sin, no matter how
dark our circumstances, they can’t defeat God.

We can expect this blessing from God because of His
nature.  As Micah reports, God doesn’t
delight in unchanging anger.  He delights
in unchanging love.  It’s not we that He’s
going to stomp down under His feet.  It’s
our transgressions.  It’s not we that
He’s going to sink to the bottom of the ocean. 
It’s our sins.  God is sure to
bless us because He is a faithful God, because He is a loving God, and because
He’s our God.  We can safely entrust our
lives to Him.
