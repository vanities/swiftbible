**OVERVIEW.** First off, unlike Holman’s Large Print
Compact Reference Bible (this Bible’s little brother), the binding of the LPPSR
is sewn.  The cover is soft cowhide, and
I enjoy the aesthetics of the cover design. 
Size-wise, the LPPSR is a little bigger than a typical thinline, but
it’s still small enough to pass the glove-box test.  Basically, this is a nicely designed
low-premium Bible.

Like most of the Bibles I’ve reviewed recently, this one is
a review copy.  I asked; Holman
sent.  Unwittingly, I asked for a thumb-indexed
copy, which is not normally my preference in Bibles.  However, in all the other aspects of text
block and binding, the thumb-indexed version is identical to the
non-thumb-indexed version.  If you like
indexing, buy the version with indexing. 
If you don’t, don’t.



**VITAL STATISTICS**

Translation:  New King
James Version

Cover:  Cowhide

Binding:  Sewn

Trim Size:  5 ¼ ” x 8
⅛”

Text Block Width:  1⅛”

Weight:  1 pound, 12
ounces

Column:  Double

Layout:  Paragraphed

Print Size:  12

Words of Christ:  Red

Cross-References:  Yes

Ribbon Marker:  1

Like most better-quality Bibles, the LPPSR (my goodness, but
that’s an ugly acronym!) came in a box of heavy, non-corrugated cardboard.  However, I am so enamored of slipcases as a
Bible-storage solution that I resolved to try making a DIY slipcase from the
box.

I razored the appropriate sides out of the inner and outer
boxes, trimmed a strip from one of the razored sides to go between the bottom
sides of the inner and outer boxes (originally, they had about a sixteenth of
an inch of clearance between them), glued the whole together, and clamped it
until it dried.  The result, though not
beautiful (though I could cover the cut edges with strips of white paper to
make it look better), is serviceable. 
Now, I can store the LPPSR upright on a shelf without fear that I will
crumple the binding.

I’ve fussed before about the paucity of quality options
available to fans of the NKJV.  Even
though I don’t have the same sentimental attachment to the NKJV that I do to the
NASB, I’ve always thought this was a shame. 
The NKJV has a great “brand name”, shares in some of the literary
quality of its illustrious predecessor, and is a well-executed
formal-equivalence translation.  Surely a
translation like that deserves better than to be glued together and bound in
cardboard!

I thought that I had found the solution to the problem in
Holman’s Large Print Compact Reference, which is actually a shrunken version of
the setting of the LPPSR.  However, much
to my chagrin, I realized that the former Bible had a glued binding.  So close, and yet so far away!  The LPPSR, by contrast, succeeds where its
little brother failed.  Let me explain:

**SIZE.**  I can’t explain it, but the LPPSR passed the
glove-box test.  I was able to close the
door on my minivan’s glove compartment with the LPPSR inside, and the Bible
emerged with the cover neither mangled nor dented.  This is strange, especially considering that
the LPPSR is essentially the same size as the Cambridge Standard Text REB,
which only passed half of the glove-box test.

There are two possible explanations here.  The first is that somehow, something actually
came out of the glove box.  Considering
that said glove box probably contains car-insurance cards from 1996, this seems
unlikely.

The second is that the differing results are based on
differing cover flexibility.  My REB has
a poly cover, and like all single-piece poly covers, it’s fairly stiff.  At the time, I noted that the REB failed the
glove-box test because the cover got stuck up in the car innards.

By contrast, the cowhide cover of the LPPSR is extremely
soft and flexible, to the point where I wonder how it will stand up to prolonged
use.  However, such a cover certainly
isn’t going to poke up where it’s not wanted. 
It may well be that the extra flexibility was just enough that I could
insert the Bible, close the door, open it, and remove the Bible without any
obvious ill effects.

When I was trotting around actually carrying the LPPSR, it
didn’t give me any trouble.  I have been
able to drag both children into and out of various church buildings (my son and
I departed the meeting site in some haste after he tried to tear down the projection
screen behind the pulpit) with the Bible tucked under an arm.  It’s not as convenient to carry as a
thinline, but it’s not far off.

**BINDING**.  The LPPSR makes a good impression right out
of the box.  It has pretty much the same
design elements as its compact equivalent. 
The cover is made of brown cowhide, which is fairly thin but soft and
flexible.  The quality is high enough
that I feel no inclination to rebind the text block.  Both front and back cover are entirely blank.  The spine bears only one NKJV logo, at the
top (other Bible publishers, take note!), “Holy Bible” in the middle, and the
Holman logo at the bottom.  Each of those
three elements is bracketed by two ribs. 
The Large Print Compact Reference, which had the same spine elements in a
much shorter space, looked a little over-the-top.  The LPPSR, by contrast, strikes a nice
balance between boldness and refinement. 
This is a pretty Bible.

As you would expect from a Bible at this price point
(without thumb indexing, the cowhide version retails for $60), the LPPSR has
leatherette endpapers.  They’re a
matching shade of brown, and they don’t feel like they’re going to split or
separate from the cover anytime soon. 
The combination of cover and endpaper is extremely flexible, maybe too
much so.  I get the feeling that this
Bible might be more vulnerable to edge-on crumpling than, for instance, a
genuine-leather Foundation NASB. 
However, I think that’s a reasonable tradeoff for much greater softness
and flexibility than any mass-market Foundation product will offer.

**TEXT BLOCK.** The LPPSR is a sewn Bible.  Those who have journeyed through the NKJV
wilderness for the past 10 years will greet this news with astonishment and
joy.  No more cracks in the spine!  No more pages falling out!  No more having to use bar clamps to hold the
thing open!  It may be that poly versions
of this Bible are glued ($20 retail is awfully cheap for a sewn Bible), but the
cowhide definitely isn’t.  After my bad
experience with the Large Print Compact Reference (I know referring back and
forth like this is confusing, but there ain’t no other way to tell the story),
I poked in the gutter of the LPPSR until I saw bindery thread myself.

I don’t know why the compact version isn’t sewn, but I am
sure glad this one is!  To paraphrase *It’s a Wonderful Life*, every time a sewn
Bible comes off the production line, an angel gets his wings.  Most NKJV publishers have been doing their
best recently to keep the angels grounded, but Holman is finally making a Bible
with the quality the translation deserves.

The text block also has the standard corner rounding and
page gilding that one would expect.  Like
most mass-market Bibles, the LPPSR is printed cross-grain (cross-grain Bibles
will often have waviness in the gilding opposite the spine; with-the-grain
Bibles will have waviness at top and bottom). 
However, the printers did an excellent job in minimizing the problems
commonly associated with this.  Though
the LPPSR doesn’t open as quietly as the Cambridge REB or the TBS Windsor (both
of which are with-the-grain Bibles printed by Jongbloed), it’s quieter than
almost any mass-market Bible.  Also, I
had to look hard for any visible evidence of cockling.  For once, the decision to print cross-grain
didn’t lead to any noticeable diminution in quality.

This is all the more impressive because the LPPSR is printed
in China.  Over the past decade or so,
Chinese Bibles have acquired a reputation for being shoddily printed, and it’s
hard to deny that many publishers saw a dramatic decline in quality once they
switched to a printer on the far side of the Pacific.  However, the past few years have seen Chinese
printers really start to figure things out. 
I think the latest Foundation SCR’s are very well-executed (yes, no
French-milled paper anymore--I know), and the LPPCR has nothing to be ashamed
of despite its Far-Eastern origin.

Speaking of figuring things out, the LPPCR is
line-matched.  This feature used to be
reserved for premium Bibles like the Cambridge lineup, but recent years have seen
mass-market publishers such as Crossway adopt it for their newest
settings.  It’s great to see Holman join
the trend.

In the particular case of the LPPCR, line-matching is
especially important because of the thinness of the paper.  At slightly more than an inch, the LPPCR is
not a particularly thick Bible, but it still boasts nearly 1600 pages.  Bible dorks know what that
means—not-very-opaque paper.  To my eye,
the opacity level appears about the same as in my collection of thinlines.  I’m OK with that.  Especially with the line-matching, greater
opacity strikes me as a reasonable tradeoff for reduced thickness and
weight.  However, those who can’t abide
ghosting, even in line-matched Bibles, probably should look elsewhere.



**CONCLUSION.** Some months ago, I clobbered the Large
Print Compact Reference for being a nicely produced NKJV with a glued
binding.  By contrast, the LPPSR is a
nicely produced NKJV with a sewn binding. 
I can’t very well scream and throw things about the former (which I
did), without being generous to the latter. 
NKJV lovers, if you want a well-made mass-market Bible in your preferred
translation, this is it.  9/10.
