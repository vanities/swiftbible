Last week, I wrote about Colossians 3:16 and what it implies
about the hymns we ought to be using in worship.  Even though I believe that the Colossians
3:16 standards are important, other verses are equally significant to the
Scriptural ideal of the good hymn.  One
of these is 1 Corinthians 14:15.  In the
NASB, it reads:  “What is the outcome,
then?  I will pray with the spirit and I
will pray with the mind also; I will sing with the spirit and I will sing with
the mind also.”

Paul here means to remind us that song worship is a both-and
proposition, not an either-or proposition. 
Our spirits should undeniably be involved.  Our hearts should be exulting within us as we
sing about God and His word.

However, song worship is not merely an exercise of the
spirit.  It is an exercise of the mind
also.  A hymn that touches our hearts but
does not engage our minds cannot help us to fulfill God’s goals for our
worship.  Obviously, there’s a sliding
scale here.  Singing a hymn in a language
we do not understand (without a translation provided) cannot be mindful.  However, even a hymn in English can fail to
involve our minds fully in worship.

This does not mean that every hymn we sing has to be laden
with intellectual complexity like a doctoral dissertation!  In fact, I believe that “Jesus Loves Me!”,
surely one of the humblest hymns in our repertoire, is nonetheless a hymn that
we can use mindfully.  It possesses
several characteristics that make it valuable for mindful worship.



**Structure**

The first of these attributes is its logical structure.  “Jesus Loves Me!” advances an argument (the
Bible teaches us that Jesus loves us), supplies information that supports the argument
(Jesus was kind to children during His life, He died for us, and He cares for
us today), and reaches a conclusion (because of Jesus’ love for us, we should
live for Him).

There’s nothing remarkable about this structure.  It’s the same five-paragraph essay structure
that every one of us learned in high-school English, the most logically
straightforward way to explore any topic, whether spiritual or not.  There’s no unfocused writing in “Jesus Loves
Me!”, no verses about the Israelites, God the Father, or any other spiritual
subject that, though worthy, belongs in a different hymn than this one.  Instead, every verse is on point.

**Fresh Writing**

Likewise, the writing in “Jesus Loves Me!”, though extremely
clear and simple, is fresh and free from clichés.  Clichés are uniformly considered to be bad
writing because they are mindless writing. 
They say the same old thing in the same old way, so that they do not
engage the intellect of the reader.  By
contrast, “Jesus Loves Me!” offers food for thought for even the most mature
Christian.  Consider the opening couplet:

*Jesus loves me!  This I know,*

*For the Bible tells me
so;*

My four-year-old uses every word in that couplet, but it
presents one of the foundational truths of Christianity:  that we can learn about the love of Jesus
from the Bible.  No other hymn in our
repertoire puts it so clearly.  The value
of this concept cannot be overstated, and we could easily spend hours
meditating on its implications.  Writing
that leads us to do that is good writing.

**Text-Tune Match**

Finally, “Jesus Loves Me!” has a mentally useful text-tune
match.  CHINA, the tune to “Jesus Loves
Me!” was written by William Bradbury, who, as far as my religious tradition’s
repertoire goes, was unquestionably the greatest hymn-tune composer of all
time.  His strong melodies and simple
harmonies are perfectly suited for congregational a-cappella worship.

CHINA is no different. 
It matches the lyrics to “Jesus Loves Me!” so perfectly that it’s
scarcely possible for us to mentally separate one from the other.  This is true from the opening interval.  SOL-MI (from the fifth degree of the major
scale to the third degree) is the first interval that elementary music teachers
teach their students.  No interval is
more childlike than SOL-MI.  It’s no
accident that Bradbury’s tune for a hymn about children opens with precisely
that interval.  Little touches like that
make the difference between a tune that can accompany a text and a tune that
clarifies and enhances its meaning.

**Conclusion**

Remember, though, that we’re still talking about what is
primarily a Bible-class song for children.  All of these intellectual elements work for my
four-year-old and my five-year-old, both of whom have quite a bit of mental
development ahead of them!  “Jesus Loves
Me!” is far from the most thought-provoking hymn in our repertoire, which is
plain when we compare it to a “Be Still, My Soul” or a “Great Is Thy
Faithfulness”.  That’s fine.  It still has its place.

Not every hymn has to be a “Great Is Thy Faithfulness”, but
every hymn should be at least a “Jesus Loves Me!”  If “Jesus Loves Me!” makes sense even to
small children, then who is the intellectual target for hymns that offer less
to the mind than it does?  Many such
hymns exist.  They don’t have the logical
coherence of “Jesus Loves Me!”, they are riddled with mind-deadening clichés, or
the tune appears to have nothing to do with the text.  These hymns were written with a purpose, yes,
but their purpose is not to conform to 1 Corinthians 14:15, and that’s a
problem.  It’s hard to sing with
understanding when a hymn is not written to appeal to the understanding.

I propose, then, the “Jesus Loves Me!” test for hymns in our
repertoire.  **If a hymn offers less for the intellect than “Jesus Loves Me!” does, it
interferes with God’s goals for our worship and so should not be used
congregationally.**  It is hardly
snobbery to insist that a hymn for adults ought to offer at least the same
level of intellectual challenge as a song for four-year-olds!  If a hymn falls below the intellectual bar
established by a children’s song, we cannot contend that such a hymn is truly
offering spiritual meat for our adult understanding.

Maybe we’re really emotionally involved in it.  Maybe we’re having a great time singing
it.  However, it’s not enough for a hymn
simply to appeal to the spirit.  It must
appeal to the mind too.  God requires
both.
