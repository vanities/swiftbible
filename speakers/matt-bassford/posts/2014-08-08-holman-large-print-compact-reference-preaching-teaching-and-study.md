One of the great joys of being married is not having to play
the dating game anymore.  Back in the
day, I encountered a few girls who radiated trouble but were too attractive to
avoid.  You knew it was going to end
badly, but you couldn’t help yourself.

So too it is with the LPCR. 
I think this Bible is beautiful, but because of the glued binding, I
know it’s going to end badly.  If I were
to use the LPCR for any length of time, it would eventually break my
heart.  Of course, that would happen
because I would break its spine.  I guess
the dating analogy has to fall apart somewhere.



Regardless, though the smallest of all the Bibles I have yet
reviewed, the LPCR spent two weeks with me at the church building, going
through the usual course of preaching, teaching, and study.  Here’s how it fared:

**PREACHING.** Because of its stubby text block, the
LPCR is nowhere near the size of the Bibles I normally prefer to use in the
pulpit.  I traditionally fold my sermon
outlines in half, tuck them into the cover of the Bible, and carry them up that
way.  Trying to put a half-sheet inside
the LPCR would have been an exercise in absurdity.  The Bible is barely big enough to contain a
quartered sheet of paper, so quarter the outline I did.  Honestly, though, the extra fold in the paper
didn’t bother me as much as I thought it might.

Typically, I prefer to preach out of Bibles that are
single-column and verse-by-verse.  The
LPCR is double-column and paragraphed. 
Sadly, its time in the pulpit demonstrated why double-column,
paragraphed Bibles are less than ideal for preaching.  In particular, during the second sermon of
the testing period, I failed to spot two verse numbers and continued reading
for longer than I should have on both occasions.  The congregation may or may not have noticed,
but it certainly bothered me.

Also, the LPCR’s glued binding demonstrated that it isn’t
only a longevity problem.  Glued Bibles
don’t open as widely as Bibles with sewn bindings, and this is particularly
annoying with the LPCR.  It shares a
setting with Holman’s larger Large Print Personal Size Reference Bible and
Giant Print Reference Bible.  This was
clever on Holman’s part; they only had to pay 2K for one setting instead of
three.

However, the decision to use the same layout in the LPCR,
which is 4 ¼” x 6 ¼“, as in the Giant Print Reference Bible, which is 6 ¼“ x 9
¼“, forced some compromises on the designers. 
A ⅜” gutter margin for the LPCR, which is typical for compact Bibles,
would have become a space-eating, weight-adding ⅝” gutter margin in the
GPR.  Instead, the designers appear to
have selected a margin that would be more normal for the GPR, which leaves the
LPCR with a gutter margin of mayyybe a quarter-inch.

When combined with the tightness of the glued binding, the
miserly gutter margin made reading out of the Bible difficult despite its
apparently generous 8-point print.  My
photographer was present the first time I preached out of it, and he said he
had to fight the church giggles as he watched me bring the Bible closer and
closer to my face while I was reading.  I
wasn’t aware of it at the time, but apparently squinting into the chasm the
LPCR calls a gutter is hard work!

**TEACHING.** I’m teaching our middle-school class at
the moment, and I haven’t been getting up much recently, so I didn’t notice the
LPCR’s size or lack thereof much while I was using it in that setting.  Likewise, the cross-references in the LPCR
never came up.

What did become an issue, though, was, once again, the
gutter.  One Sunday, I was attempting to
explain Acts 20:26 to the class, and I decided to use the famous watchman
passage from Ezekiel as an illustration. 
I turned there in the LPCR and invited one of the students, “Why don’t
you read Ezekiel 2:16-21”?  She looked
down at her Bible and then back up at me with a quizzical expression on her
face.  “There is no Ezekiel 2:16-21,” she
replied.

Puzzled, I examined the LPCR and realized what had
happened.  The glued binding was so tight
and the gutter so ungenerous that the whole chapter number for Ezekiel 3 had
vanished into it!  The most recent chapter
number I could see was Ezekiel 2, so I assumed that the watchman passage
belonged to Ezekiel 2 rather than Ezekiel 3. 
I had to flex the gutter back with my thumbs before the missing chapter
number became visible.

**STUDY.** Here, I must confess that I failed in my
purpose.  I did not use the LPCR in my
study for two full weeks, being instead seduced by my old standby, the
Foundation SCR.

It is true that I was weak, but the layout of the LPCR
encouraged my weakness.  This wasn’t the
result of a designer compromise, but rather a designer decision.  The LPCR, like the other two Holman Bibles
that share the 2K setting, doesn’t have a center column of references.  This is promising, because reference columns
are the bane of many double-column setting. 
Their presence on the page requires narrower columns of text, which
chops the poetic sections of the Old Testament into a mishmash.

The LPCR doesn’t have the deadly center column, but it does
have something else—large print.  All
other things being equal, large print is a good thing.  It’s easier to read (unless it’s being sucked
into the gutter, apparently).  However,
in Bibles, all things are never equal. 
To get large print, you have to give up something else, and the Holman
setting gives up the generous number of characters per line that is usually
associated with double-column text Bibles.

Most double-column text Bibles have 40-45 characters per
line, and they handle the OT poetry OK. 
The LPCR has about 35 characters per line, which means that any poetic
line beyond the shortest is going to get chopped.

The first sermon I prepared with the LPCR was on Paul’s
sermon in Acts 13.  That’s prose, and it
gave me no problems.  The second sermon
was on Malachi, which the NKJV treats as poetry.  That Monday, as I began work on the sermon, I
opened to Malachi in the LPCR and. . . stared blankly at the page.  The poetic lines in Malachi had been diced
into so many fragments that I literally couldn’t get them to make sense in my
head.  After about 15 seconds of
befuddlement, I gave in and grabbed the SCR (which, incidentally, treats
Malachi as prose).  Once I started using it,
I couldn’t make myself go back to the LPCR.

**CONCLUSION.** There’s a very real sense in which this
review is unfair.  Compact Bibles aren’t
intended for preaching, and despite its larger-than-normal print, the LPCR is
still a compact Bible.  Criticizing it
for not being useful in the pulpit is kind of like criticizing me for not being
an NBA center.   I’m sure one of its
bigger brothers would have fared better.

Having said that, the LPCR, despite its promise, gave me
trouble for reasons beyond its small size. 
The short lines and glued binding made using it less than a pleasure on
several occasions.  I must reluctantly
award the LPCR a 2.5/5 for its usefulness in preaching, teaching, and study.
