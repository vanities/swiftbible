A couple days ago, my friend Randy Brown ran [this post](http://biblebuyingguide.com/choosing-bible-pastor/) on his blog, Bible Buying Guide, entitled “Choosing a Bible for Your Pastor”.  Randy’s main point is that a preacher limiting himself to one Bible is about like a carpenter limiting himself to one saw.  There are many general-purpose Bible designs out there (the equivalent of a hand saw that you can use for lots of different things), but there are also many Bibles that are adapted to particular uses.  Preachers ought to choose among these specialist Bibles based on their needs, their circumstances, and their preferences.  They shouldn’t feel bad about swapping out Old Faithful for a design that’s better suited for a particular use.

Here’s the suite of Bibles that I’m running right now.  I’ve written extensively about all of these Bibles, so if you want many thousands of words of information about them, all you have to do is search!

**Expository Preaching:  Cambridge NASB Wide-Margin Reference.**  This has been my standby for exegetical sermons for more than a year and a half now.  From my perspective, it has two attributes that particularly suit it for this role.  First, it has wide margins that enable me to preach an entire sermon using only the notes in my Bible.  This frees me from the pulpit and makes me a more engaging speaker.  Second, it’s a comparatively small member of the hefty wide-margin tribe.  At somewhere around 2 pounds, 12 ounces, it’s light enough that I can comfortably hold it for long periods of time without returning to the pulpit to set it down.

**Topical Preaching:  Cambridge NASB Pitt Minion.**  A couple years back, the Joliet church shifted to a much smaller pulpit that doesn’t have much surface room.  For topical sermons, I inscribe my notes in a small notebook that I have in the pulpit with me, so space up there is at a premium.  The Pitt is small enough that I can have it open on the pulpit without jostling the notebook.  Admittedly, it’s a small-print Bible, but my pulpit is brightly lit and my eyes are still good enough for close work that I can make out the text easily.

**Bible Classes:  Cambridge NASB Pitt Minion.**  The Pitt has two features I particularly value for this use:  small size (so that I can wander away from the lectern holding my Bible for 20 minutes at a time) and cross-references.  This past quarter, I’ve been learning rather than teaching, and I’ve found that I use the references more in my seat than up at the front.  When I’m teaching, I don’t have the luxury of running down some Biblical bunny trail that the discussion of the class has brought to mind.  However, in the pew, that reference column is awfully useful for locating some obscure passage.

**Office Study:  Cambridge NASB Pitt Minion.**  It’s already out on my desk, it’s light and handy, and it has cross-references.  Why not?

**Personal Studies:  Crossway ESV Thinline.**  In my mind, the Thinline has one immeasurable advantage.  It has the same design as the Crossway ESV Value Thinline, which is the best giveaway Bible in the world.  As a result, it shares page numbers with the Value Thinline.  This means that when I’m studying with someone who doesn’t know Genesis from Revelation, I can hand them a giveaway, tell them to turn to Page 931 or whatever, and show them what they want to know.  Normally, I’m an NASB guy, but there’s no NASB on the market that combines the high quality and low price of the Value Thinline, and my leatherbound Thinline is a semi-necessary adjunct to it.

**Reading:  Crossway ESV Thinline/Foundation Side-Column Reference.**  I’m pretty loyal in a lot of my other Bible preferences, but I will switch reading Bibles a lot.  I enjoy having a respectable number of Bibles (a dozen or so) that I already know I like as readers, so that when the mood takes me, I can switch format and even translation.  At the moment, that’s taken me to the Thinline.  Like most double-column Bibles, it does poorly in the poetry of the Old Testament, but it handles prose quite well.  When I do my readings at home, this is the Bible I’ve been grabbing, at least for the past several weeks.

At the church building, I’ve gotten into the habit of reading from my Side-Column Reference.  The one I’ve been using is a 2002 edition that I inherited when my uncle passed on.  It’s a great big beast of a Bible (6.5” x 9.25”, 3.5 pounds), but the paper and printing are top-notch.  Also, it is one of the few single-column Bibles with a text column that has proportions that work for me.  As a result, where the Thinline chokes in Psalms and Proverbs, the SCR excels.

Note, by the way, that even though several Bibles on my list are premium or premium-equivalent (I put the ’02 SCR in that class because copies of it sell for north of $100 these days), it’s entirely possible to stock a similar arsenal using only mass-market Bibles.  NASB users will have trouble doing this (I’m not aware of an in-print NASB wide-margin that I can recommend), but the Holman lineup of NKJV’s and the Crossway collection of ESV’s offer quality Bibles for nearly any use.  This is particularly useful for preachers, but I think that any Christian who is a diligent Bible user will benefit from switching things up.

If you are a preacher, though, you ought to be spending the whole week in the word, and there’s no reason to try to make the same generalist Bible do everything.  Sure, you can make the same Bible work, but you can build a house with a handsaw as your only saw too.  Why make the effort when there are so many better tools available?
