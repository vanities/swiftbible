I typically
keep one Bible at the church building, and I use it for all of my preaching,
teaching, and study during my office time there.  If I could invent a perfect Bible for these
purposes, here is what it would look like. 
Yes, I am aware that some of these descriptions are mutually
contradictory.  I can’t insist on a small
Bible with references for teaching a Bible class and then expect it to have a
large font for private study! 
Nonetheless, in a perfect world, here is how things would be.



**Preaching**

My perfect preaching
Bible would have the following attributes:

**SIZE.** By preference, I always
leave my Bible in the pulpit when I’m preaching.  I return to the pulpit to read passages, and
I don’t wander around waving my Bible in the air when I’m not reading.  As a result, small size is not a big deal to
me in a preaching Bible.  So long as I
can hold the Bible comfortably in one hand when I’m reading from it, which
typically means a Bible no larger than 6” x 9”, I’m fine with it.

In fact, a
smaller Bible can be an annoyance.  I
keep my sermon notes on a sheet of copier paper folded in half.  If my preaching Bible is smaller than the
dimensions of that half sheet, 5½” x 8½”, the edges of the sermon notes will
stick out around the edges of the Bible when I’m carrying it up to the
pulpit.  No one else may notice this, but
it sure bugs me.

**LAYOUT.** I prefer for my pulpit
Bibles to be in verse-by-verse rather than paragraphed format.  In a verse-by-verse Bible, all the verse
numbers are tidily arranged on the left side of the column.  That makes them prominent and easy to
locate.  In a paragraphed Bible, on the
other hand, they’re scattered throughout the paragraph.  In the pulpit, that means that I have to
think about how to locate them and then spend a second or two actually doing
it.  I would much rather devote that
brainpower and time to figuring out what I’m going to say next.

It is true
that paragraphed Bibles help with reading comprehension, particularly
throughout a larger context, but if I’m still trying to figure out the text in
mid-sermon, something has gone horribly wrong!

Also, I think
single-column Bibles are better suited for preaching.  Verse-by-verse helps a double-column Bible,
but you still have two sets of numbers to scan through instead of just one.

**Teaching**

The attributes
of my perfect Bible for teaching Bible classes would be these:

**SIZE.**  I don’t spend much time at the lectern when
I’m teaching.  Instead, I prefer to float
back and forth across the front of the auditorium so that I can make eye
contact with particular students when I’m talking with them.  It’s rare for me to do readings myself;
instead, I invite others to read, and I carry my Bible with me so that I can
follow along with them as they do.

As a
consequence, Bible size, which isn’t terribly important to me in preaching, is
very important to me in teaching.  I want
a Bible that is small and light enough that I can hold it for 45 minutes
without it becoming a distraction.

**STUDY AIDS.**  Cross-references
are meaningless when I’m in the pulpit. 
I’ve never attempted to run one down in mid-sermon.  By contrast, a lively Bible class with
thoughtful students may well throw me a curveball that I hadn’t considered
during my preparation.  At that point,
cross-references can be very helpful indeed! 
However, I don’t want canned answers from a study Bible.

**Study**

I define
study as the close examination of small portions of Biblical text, as opposed
to reading, which is the more casual perusal of large portions.  Most of what I do in my office throughout the
week is study.  I write sermons, I
prepare for Bible classes, and I write Bible-class workbooks.  Here is what my perfect study Bible would
look like:

**SIZE.** When I study, Bible size
isn’t very important.  I don’t want a
dictionary-sized Bible that I have to set on a desk or prop on a reading stand,
but as long as I can set it in my lap or hold it against my chest, it’s small
enough.

**READABILITY.** My eyes are decent for close
work, but when I’m glancing back and forth between a Bible and a computer
screen for hours at a time, it’s nice to have a Bible and a monitor both that
will minimize eyestrain.  This means that
I prefer Bibles with large, readable fonts, opaque paper, and line-matching if it’s
available.  However, this isn’t really a
terribly big deal to me.

**STUDY AIDS.** I don’t really need anything
in the Bible but the text.  Marginal
references aren’t much use when I have many more powerful search tools at my
fingertips.  I don’t write in my Bibles,
so I don’t need wide margins.  I find
so-called study Bibles to be worse than useless.  I don’t want anything between the covers of
my Bible but the word of God.  The words
of man can wait over there on the shelf on the off chance that I’ll decide to
consult them.

**LAYOUT.** When studying, I prefer
verse-by-verse to paragraphed Bibles. 
Both my sermon texts and my workbooks contain many verse citations, and
the harder verse numbers are to see and locate, the more likely I am to miss a
citation by a verse or two.  Because
verse-by-verse Bibles display verse numbers more prominently, they help me to
create a more polished product.

Paragraphed
Bibles do provide a much better sense of context than verse-by-verse Bibles do,
it is true, but in study, I am rarely concerned with figuring out a context
much larger than a chapter.  If I am
reading a book of the Bible in preparation for writing a workbook about it, I
will usually turn to a dedicated reading Bible for that.
