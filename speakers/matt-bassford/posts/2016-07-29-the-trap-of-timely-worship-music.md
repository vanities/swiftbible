Just this morning, I discovered a worship blogger I’d never
encountered before, Chris Marchand at [postconsumerreports.org](http://postconsumerreports.org/).  I haven’t had the chance yet to read more
than a few articles, but from what I see, even though Marchand doesn’t agree
with me about everything (he comes from a different worship tradition, for one
thing), he does thoughtfully consider modern worship culture.

In [this article](http://www.postconsumerreports.com/2016/07/worship-musics-good-old-days-featuring.html),
for instance, he discusses what were to him the “good old days” of worship
music:  the time in the 1990s when he
came of age and first discovered Christian contemporary music that sounded like
the pop music of the day.  He rattles off
a whole list of bands that were the whole world to him 20 years ago but since
have vanished from the scene, along with 99 percent of the secular pop music
from the same era.  He recounts the first
time he heard the bands that dominate Christian contemporary music today
(Hillsong, etc.) and reacted with shock and horror.  “This is terrible!” he cried, because it came
from a different musical style than he had grown up listening to.



His point here, which I think is a valid one, is that all of
our tastes in worship music are shaped by our experiences.  Whatever we hear and use in worship at a
formative time in our lives is what we think of as “the golden age” of church
music.  What we conceive of as good is in
reality merely familiar.

He’s right, but I think there’s also something else to be
drawn from his description of his exposure to different eras of music.  Bands like Vineyard Music and Delirious? were
popular in the ‘90s not because their repertoire was a timeless expression of
the truths of faith, but because it wasn’t. 
It had a sound that was distinctly modern and cutting-edge, and people
liked it because it captured the spirit of the time.  It was trendy.

However, as the closets of nearly everybody over the age of
40 can testify, trends come and go.  This
is true in music just as much as it is in fashion.  My polo shirts from 1995 look kinda funny
these days, and sacred song that captures the spirit of 1995 sounds now like it
belongs in 1995.  The problem with
seeking timeliness, either as a writer or a worship leader, is that even if you
find a hymn for today, you will certainly not have found a hymn for tomorrow.

If a repertoire of sacred song is based around hymns for
today, it can only divide rather than unify. 
My coming-of-age music came from the ‘90s too.  It is nothing like my dad’s coming-of-age
music from the ‘50s, and it will be nothing like the coming-of-age music my
children adopt 10 or 15 years from now. 
If we all insist on repertoires that are timely to us, we will
inevitably insist on different repertoires.

There are several ways this can play out, and they’re all
bad.  I can be stuck in church listening
to sacred music that isn’t timely for me and be grumpy and upset about it.  I can demand that the church provide a
special service that caters to my musical tastes, with the result that worship
actually introduces divisions into a congregation that is supposed to be one in
Christ.  A church that doesn’t worship
together isn’t together.  Finally, of
course, I can simply leave that congregation altogether because I don’t like
the worship.  Timeliness is appealing,
but it’s also polarizing and disruptive.

Every church, then, should strive for a repertoire that isn’t
founded on timeliness.  We shouldn’t be
concerned about whether a given praise song captures the spirit of our
time.  We should care whether it captures
the spirit of faith.  A truly good hymn
isn’t merely timely (though it may be timely when first produced).  It’s universal.  How many languages has “Amazing Grace” been
translated into?  In how many different
countries has it been sung?  If you are a
Christian, you can buy into “Amazing Grace”.

A hymn that is able to overcome the cultural barriers that
divide the U.S. from India from China from Ghana from Argentina is also able to
overcome the cultural barriers between generations.    People
can sing “Amazing Grace”, and they can sing it together.  It is no less relevant to me than it is to my
dad than I hope it will be to my children. 
Hymns like these are unifying rather than divisive.

The point here is not that our repertoire should be frozen
in time.  New, good hymns are written
every year, just as they were 200 years ago. 
I believe that “In Christ Alone” is a universal hymn.  It depends for its appeal on the truths of
Scripture, not on a timely sound. 
Rejecting it because it is new makes no more sense than rejecting “Amazing
Grace” because it is old.

However, it’s a terrible mistake to revamp a congregational
repertoire every 15 years (or sooner).  We must not exchange the good for the timely. You
might be able to catch the wave of cultural relevance, but when the tide ebbs,
you’ll find yourself stranded.
