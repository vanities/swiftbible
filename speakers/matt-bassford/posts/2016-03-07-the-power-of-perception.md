Every
once in a while, I will run into somebody, usually online, who is just as hurt
and angry as they can be about their experiences in some church of Christ
someplace.  Whenever I encounter one of
those horror stories, I’m generally taken aback.  I’ve been a Christian for 25 years now, I’ve
been a member at seven different churches, and I’ve never seen anything like
that happen.

Part
of this massive variation has to do with the equally massive variations across
churches.  No human being can possibly
accumulate the experience necessary to make confident declarations about how
the church is like this or the church is like that.  It’s like the fable of the blind men and the
elephant.  We draw different conclusions
because our experiences are different.

However,
there’s another factor at work:  our
subjective perceptions of the churches where we are.  No matter what we like to believe, none of us
see the world as it really is.  Instead,
every one of us sees the world through a filter of our own experiences, beliefs,
desires, and fears.

The
effect of that filter is about like applying a filter to a photograph in
Photoshop.  Use one filter, and you’ve
got a picture of a gloomy, gray, nasty day in autumn.  Apply a different filter, and suddenly the
same picture is ablaze with life and color.

Whether
we admit or not, every one of us is doing that to the world around us all the
time.  This has a profound impact on the
way we see reality, the way we treat others, and even the way we shape their
lives.  We need to get a handle on
this.  Let’s consider, then, the power of
perception.




**Jesus and
Perception**

Let’s
begin this morning by considering how our Lord’s perception of those He
encountered changed their lives.  There
are many stories in the gospels where this happens, both for good and ill, but
this morning, let’s consider the story of Zacchaeus.  **THE
BACKGROUND** for this story appears in Luke 19:1-4.  The first thing that this text reveals to us
about Zacchaeus is that he was a chief tax collector.  We might think that we don’t like the IRS
today, but our antipathy toward the tax man is nothing compared to the way that
first-century Jews felt about tax collectors. 
For one thing, taxes were extortionately high.  In bad years, they could pose a threat to the
survival of the Jewish population.  For
another, they didn’t even go to a Jewish ruler. 
Jericho was in the Roman province of Judaea.  All the taxes Zacchaeus and his underlings
collected went straight to Rome and did not benefit the Jewish people in any
way.  Basically, this is nothing more
than legalized theft, and Zacchaeus is in charge of it.  He’s a Jew, but he’s probably the most hated
man in Jericho.

Second,
at this point of the story, Zacchaeus is not in any way a disciple.  Note that he doesn’t climb the tree because
he’s eager to see Jesus.  He climbs the
tree because he wants to find out who Jesus is. 
He’s short and can’t see, so for all he knows, Jesus is the vanguard of
a traveling circus.  This is not
religious devotion on Zacchaeus’s part. 
This is idle curiosity.

However,
Zacchaeus’s life changes dramatically because of **JESUS’ INVITATION.** The story
continues in Luke 19:5-6.  This section
has two parts, Jesus seeing Zacchaeus and Jesus instructing Zacchaeus, and both
are equally remarkable.  The text only
says that Jesus looked up, but when He who searches the hearts looks at you, in
a glance He understands everything about you that there is to know.  Jesus doesn’t speak because He is ignorant
that Zacchaeus is a chief tax collector. 
He certainly knows that.  He
speaks because He is able to see past the fact that Zacchaeus is a chief tax
collector.  He knows that this loathsome
Roman collaborator has a good heart.

Second,
Jesus speaks.  At first glance, this
looks like Jesus is demanding that Zacchaeus do him a favor:  “Go on, Zacchaeus!  Time to roll out the red carpet for
Jesus!”  In reality, Jesus is offering
Zacchaeus mercy.  How many people in that
crowd do you think would have willingly associated with Zacchaeus?  How many of them would have gone to his house
and helped him feast on his ill-gotten gains? 
Ordinary Jews would have treated Zacchaeus like a leper, but here is
Jesus, an important person, who is willing to treat Zacchaeus like a
friend.  It’s both heartwarming and sad
that Zacchaeus is so excited because somebody respectable is being nice to him.

After
this, we see **THE CROWD’S REACTION**.  Look at Luke 19:7.  This is entirely predictable.  These are Zacchaeus’s neighbors, exactly the
people who have known him and been shunning him for years.  They can’t believe that Jesus is going to
stay with this slimeball!  Unlike Jesus,
they can’t see the heart that can be reached. 
All they can see is the chief tax collector.

It’s
all too easy for me to see myself here, and I think that most of us, if we’re
being honest, will have to admit the same. 
All of us can be put off by what we see on the surface of somebody’s
personality and not bother looking any deeper.

This
can happen in our dealings with people in the world and even with other Christians.  We see some trait that we don’t like, so we
write them off.  Maybe, like Zacchaeus,
they’re part of some group that we despise. 
We assume that because we “know” what that group is like, we know what
this person is like too.  Maybe they have
a difficult or an off-putting personality. 
At all times, we must remember that Jesus didn’t write people off.  He showed mercy.  We should do the same.

Finally,
we see both **ZACCHAEUS AND JESUS** speaking.  This concludes our story in Luke
19:8-10.  At this part of the story,
Zacchaeus obviously hears the grumbling. 
My guess is that the people of Jericho weren’t terribly concerned about
sparing his feelings.  Zacchaeus is
worried, but not about them.  He’s
worried about Jesus.  He’s worried that
Jesus is going to hear them and say, “You know, they’re right!  I shouldn’t be eating with a pariah like
Zacchaeus.”

In
an instant, Zacchaeus concludes that if being a sinner means that he can’t have
Jesus in his life, he’s not going to be a sinner.  He does something he would never have
considered half an hour ago—giving away the greater part of all the wealth he
has accumulated.  Jesus never tells him
to do that.  Zacchaeus already knew what
he was doing wrong.  However, Jesus’ act
of mercy makes him want to be merciful too, so Jesus will stay with him.  So it is that Jesus seeks, finds, and brings
back to God someone who was lost, all without saying a single word of
condemnation.

What
a beautiful story this is!  How clearly
it shows the character of Jesus!  Here, Jesus
briefly surrenders His standing in the eyes of the crowd to redeem
Zacchaeus.  To redeem us, He surrendered
much more than that!  However, His goal
on the cross was exactly the same as His goal on the streets of Jericho—to seek
and save the lost.  As we partake of the
Lord’s Supper in a few moments, let’s first of all remember the depth of His
compassion for us at our least lovable.

Second,
let’s remember what His example should mean for us.  It’s easy to be a Pharisee:  to love the lovable, to associate with the
respectable, and to shun the despicable. 
Following Jesus, on the other hand, is hard, because it means showing
grace to people who don’t deserve it. 
Are those truly the footsteps in which we are walking?  Let’s think about that as we partake.

**Perception
Problems**

Now
that we’ve seen how Jesus’ ability to understand Zacchaeus changed the tax
collector’s life, let’s consider some problems with our own perception.  These are spiritually problematic attitudes
that keep us from seeing opportunities and lead us to see threats that aren’t
there.  The first of these negative
filters is **FEAR.** Consider God’s warning to Jeremiah about
fear in Jeremiah 1:17.  Fear certainly
has its place in the life of the Christian. 
After all, fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom.  However, when we allow fear of other things
to rule us, it becomes a problem.

For
many people, the most prominent fear in their lives is fear of being hurt.  They’ve had bad experiences with a friend or
a loved one, and those bad experiences color their whole worldview.  Another common fear in Christians is fear of
confrontation.  We fear that speaking up
boldly about the truth will cause conflict, so we try to avoid it by hiding the
light of Christ.

Over
time, I’ve noticed one great irony about fear. 
As was true for Jeremiah, when we are guided by our fears, the result is
often that we are overtaken by the very thing we fear.  Let’s say that we’re afraid that our friends
will reject us if we take a strong stand for Christ, so we hang our heads
instead of speaking up.  The irony here
is that people, even in the world, will usually respect strong, sincere
convictions in others, but they have no respect for those who talk out of both
sides of their mouths.  Our
double-mindedness will actually expose us to the contempt we were so afraid of.

Similarly,
**BITTERNESS** can warp the way we view
the world.  The Hebrews writer points out
this spiritual problem in Hebrews 12:14-15. 
Bitterness is a toxic mixture of fear and anger, and it can easily
become the lens through which we see not only the past, but the present and
future.  Bitterness is particularly
tragic when the embittered person has truly been wronged, but he can’t see that
his current attitude is equally wrong, and that his bitter behavior perpetuates
the wrong.

This
is especially problematic when the bitterness is directed at Christians or at
the church.  I know brethren who have
been so wounded by the behavior of past church leaders that they have vowed
never to enter a church building again, even if the congregation there has
nothing to do with the men who wronged them. 
That may be emotionally satisfying, but it makes about as much logical
sense as refusing to eat honey because you were stung by a bee once!

Of
course, Christians don’t have to be absent to be bitter.  It’s entirely possible for them to stick
around and poison the congregation instead. 
You want them to believe the best about you?  Fat chance! 
Instead, they’re going to watch you like a hawk, waiting for the first
sign that you’re going to betray them like that whoever-it-was long ago.  As soon as they see that sign, or think they
see it, the counterattack will begin. 
They’re going to put your actions and motives under the microscope,
conclude the worst about you, and broadcast that evil opinion as widely as
possible.  If you’re the one who actually
did wrong them, there will be no forgiveness from them for you, ever.  No wonder that the Hebrews writer describes
bitterness as a source of defilement for many!

Finally,
many Christians suffer from the negative effects of the filter of **SELF-RIGHTEOUSNESS.** Consider the parable of Luke
18:9-14.  Self-righteousness generally
has two parts:  a blindness to our own
faults and a fascination with the faults of others.  In this parable, the Pharisee’s spiritual
resume sounds pretty good, but he clearly refuses to acknowledge all the ways
he has fallen short of the glory of God. 
Similarly, he’s quick to assume that his neighbor is ungodly, while
failing to recognize the humility in him that is the foundation of
reconciliation with God.

Sadly,
self-righteousness has not vanished since the days of the Pharisees.  It can crop up in our dealings with
outsiders, as we sneer at their sin-filled lives.  Then, when we offer them the truth from our
lofty pinnacle of spiritual superiority, for some reason they’re not
interested.

It
can equally be a problem in our dealing with brethren.  We say to ourselves, “Boy, my church is sure
blessed to have me as a member!  Too bad
I can’t say the same about anybody else.” 
Without much trouble, we construct these massive lists of all the things
we do for the Lord and equally massive lists of all the things that others
aren’t doing.  If we’re feeling really
holier-than-thou, we might sit some hapless brother or sister down and tell
them about how they don’t measure up. 
Strangely, those conversations don’t go much better than our
conversations with non-Christians!

Self-righteousness
might be very self-satisfying, but it doesn’t do much for our relationships
with anybody else, including God.  In
fact, if the gospels are any guide, self-righteousness bothered Jesus more than
just about any other sin of the heart He encountered.  Let’s work to avoid it.

**Purifying Our
Perceptions**

When
perception problems are so dangerous, it’s important that we work on purifying
those false perceptions and replacing them with godly ones.  The first cure for the disease is to learn to
**VALUE OTHERS.** Notice the argument that Paul uses in 1
Corinthians 8:9-11.  It’s easy for us to
insist on our rights and our freedoms when we’re focused on how important we
ourselves are.  However, once we turn our
focus to how important others are, particularly other Christians, those same
arguments start sounding pretty weak. 
The Bible doesn’t leave us in much doubt about how much Christians are
worth.  The least important, the least
mature, the least lovable Christian out there is still someone whom Jesus died
to save.  Their worth is literally
infinite.

Now
*that* is a filter that we should learn
to incorporate!  Every time we make eye
contact with a brother or sister, every time we greet one of them, we should
think to ourselves, “Jesus thought this person was worth dying for.”  If we keep that in mind, we suddenly develop
trouble stomaching a whole lot of evil conduct. 
I can’t be unmerciful to somebody who has received the mercy of
Christ.  I can’t overlook somebody whom a
King bled to save.  I can’t be embittered
toward somebody whom Christ has forgiven far more than I ever could.

To
a lesser extent, the same analysis applies to outsiders.  Jesus’ death may not have saved them yet, but
we know that He did gladly die to offer them the opportunity.  Nobody is a nobody.  In Christ’s eyes, everybody is important.  Let’s see them the same way.

Second,
let’s learn to **TRUST GOD.** Here, let’s turn to one that’s on the
“500 Favorite Passages” list, Ephesians 3:20-21.  I suspect that this is one that the
prosperity-gospel people love to abuse. 
They probably say things like, “You never dreamed that you could live in
a 5000-square foot mansion, but God can do more in you than you ask or think,
provided you put enough in the plate.”

That,
of course, is hardly the point.  I should
not expect God’s help in becoming a multimillionaire or a Super-Bowl-winning
quarterback.  However, when it comes to
the things that glorify Him, that is when God is able to do things in us that
exceed our imaginations.

So
many times, our failures to serve are due to fear.  I’m afraid to give up this sin that I’ve
clung to for decades because I can’t imagine life without it.  I’m afraid to talk to my co-worker about the
Lord because I wouldn’t know what to say and he’d hate me and a whole bunch of
other stuff will go wrong that I don’t even know about.  In other words, we act as though our fears
have the unimaginable power instead of God. 
It’s a failure of faith.

The
best way to learn to trust instead is through study of the word.  There are lots and lots of stories of
righteous people in the Bible, from Genesis all the way through
Revelation.  How many of them did God
ever betray?  How many of them did God
ever fail?  Maybe they didn’t enjoy the
whole journey, but nobody yet has been disappointed by the destination.  God makes the same promise to us.  If we will only trust Him and live
accordingly, He won’t let us down either.

Finally,
we must learn to **BEAR WITH AND FORGIVE**.  Here too, we come to another one of those 500
favorite passages, Colossians 3:12-13. 
There are few filters that will have a greater effect on our lives than
this one.  When we consider our lives
with an attitude of forbearance and forgiveness, the whole world changes.  As all of us know, people aren’t necessarily
easy to get along with.  If we’re looking
for one, just about all of us can find an excuse to be angry with everybody we
know.  God, however, wants us to have
peace in our hearts rather than conflict.

This
begins with bearing with.  This is when
people do something that rubs us the wrong way but isn’t sinful.  It’s weird, annoying, thoughtless, or
frustrating, but not actually wrong.  In
these instances, the Lord wants us to understand that the problem lies not with
them, but with us.  Who are we to get
angry about something trivial when God has shown us mercy for being evil?

Second,
this passage tells us how to react when someone is actually evil to us.  There are other texts that suggest that we
ought to forgive even when the one who has sinned against us is
unrepentant.  I don’t think that’s the
focus of Colossians 3, though.  Instead,
this text defines how fully Jesus wants us to forgive—as fully as He has
forgiven us.  There are no asterisks in
the forgiveness of Christ.  There are no
reservations.  There are no plans to
bring up the sin the next time He’s unhappy with us.  Instead, Christ forgives completely and
forever.  So too should we.
