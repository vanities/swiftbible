Sometimes I forget, but as a rule, anytime somebody comes to
me and requests a sermon on a particular topic, I will oblige them within the
next several weeks.  So it is with the
origin of this sermon.  John Meyer and I
were talking after services a couple of Sundays ago, and he noted that he had
never heard me preach a sermon on fellowship and asked me to do so.

I was happy to oblige him because fellowship is one of those
topics that’s more important than is readily apparent in our English
Bibles.  “Fellowship” in our Bibles is
usually a translation of the Greek word *koinonia*.  However, *koinonia*is also translated as “participation”, “sharing”, or even “contribution” in
addition to “fellowship”.  As a result,
it’s an idea that is much more prevalent than we think it is, and it links
together ideas that we might not necessarily recognize as going together.  With these things in mind, let’s embark on a
study of the Biblical concept of fellowship.



**Fellowship with God**

In the Bible, there are three main categories of passages in
which the word *koinonia* is used, and
the first of these categories is our fellowship with God.  This is a fellowship that must be **BASED ON THE WORD.** This is evident from what John writes in
1 John 1:1-4.  As John makes clear here,
his desire is to bring his readers into a double fellowship:  fellowship with John and the other disciples,
and also fellowship with God.  To this
end, John is describing his own personal experience with Jesus.  He heard Jesus, he saw Jesus, he examined
Jesus, and he held Jesus with his own hands.  John is certain that Jesus has bodily been
raised from the dead, and he writes so that everyone else can know the truth
and become sharers in Christ too.  If we
are going to come to God at all, we can only do so through the testimony and
instructions of the inspired writers.

This highlights the problem that many in the religious world
have.  They think they know God, but they
haven’t come to know and follow Him through His word.  What they have is not fellowship in the
Biblical sense because it isn’t based on the truth.  If we want a relationship with God, it has to
start with the Bible, and the more time we spend with the word, the closer our
fellowship with Him will be.

However, fellowship with God is also **DEPENDENT ON HOLINESS**.  We
see this in the language of 2 Corinthians 13:14.  We don’t share in the fellowship of the
Inclusive Spirit.  We share in the
fellowship of the Holy Spirit, and the Spirit cannot indwell us unless we are
holy.

This holiness must begin with the grace of Christ.  None of us can be holy on our own
merits.  We depend on His blood to
cleanse us, purify us, and bring us into fellowship with God.

Once we have shared in this blessing, though, we are obliged
to live according to the will of God. 
Paul informs us in Romans 8 that only those who are led by the Spirit of
God are sons of God.  In context, this
doesn’t mean that we have a supernatural Jiminy Cricket sitting on our
shoulders telling us what to do.  Instead,
as Paul explains earlier in the context, it means that we have a mind set on
the Spirit.  It is not only true that we
know what God wants us to do.  Instead,
we also do it, and indeed, we delight in doing it.  We live our lives under the influence and
control of the Holy Spirit.  Then, and
only then, is fellowship with a perfectly holy God even possible.

If we want to maintain this fellowship, we must seek it **THROUGH SUFFERINGS.** Paul explains this in Philippians
3:7-11.  Here, Paul explains the way he
sacrificed everything that he used to value for the sake of Christ.  Because of his faith, he went from respected
Jewish leader to hunted social outcast. 
In this, one of his goals was to have fellowship with the sufferings of
Christ.

I think Paul means this in a double sense.  First of all, his fellowship with God, and
indeed ours as well, is only possible because Christ suffered for us.  Christianity is not founded on a fellowship
of prosperity and ease.  It is founded on
a fellowship of suffering.

We strengthen that fellowship, then, when we suffer for the
sake of Christ as He suffered for our sake. 
It means leaving behind our old lives as Paul left his.  It means enduring persecution and the
vindictiveness of our adversaries without striking back because that’s what
Christ did.  It means dying completely to
ourselves so that we can be united with Christ’s resurrection.

Here is where the differences between the genuine gospel and
the counterfeit gospel that so many preach start becoming dramatically
evident.  Discipleship is not about
seeking earthly comfort.  Instead, it may
well require us to leave behind every earthly comfort, everything that is dear
to us.  It’s not a strategy for success
in this life.  It’s dying to this life
for the sake of the life to come.

That’s a huge investment on our part, but it’s worth making
because fellowship with God is **CERTAIN**
if we do.  Consider Paul’s comment in 1
Corinthians 1:9.  Why are we willing to
sacrifice everything, if need be, for the sake of fellowship with God through
Jesus?  Because the God who has called us
is faithful.  No human being is faithful
like God is.  All of us make commitments
that we don’t keep, sometimes because we forget, sometimes because we choose
not to, and sometimes because we do our best but can’t get it done.

None of those things ever happen to God.  He remembers every promise He has made to us,
His very nature is to keep those promises, and His boundless power ensures that
what He has promised, He will perform. 
As a result, if we seek fellowship with God, both in this life and in
the life to come, we can be certain that we will find it.

**Fellowship with One
Another**

The second main category of *koinonia* relationships in Scripture has to do with our fellowship
with one another.  If we think of our
fellowship with God as vertical fellowship, this is horizontal fellowship.  Of course, the connection is closer than
that.  Our fellowship with one another is
**BASED ON FELLOWSHIP WITH GOD.** This couldn’t be clearer than it is in 1
John 1:5-7.  God is light without
darkness.  As we’ve already seen, if we
walk in darkness, we can’t have fellowship with God.  However, if we walk in the light, that is, if
we practice righteousness rather than sin, we are necessarily with God, and
that also brings us into fellowship with one another.  We are spiritual sharers in the same God.

This tells us something critical about the churches around
us.  I have many friends who belong to
denominational churches.  I suppose that
those churches are filled with sincere people who are trying to serve God in
the way that seems best to them.  They
probably do many things that appear good to us. 
However, we can’t involve ourselves with those churches because they
lack something critical.  Because they
don’t follow the word, they don’t have fellowship with God, and we cannot be
misled by their apparent goodness into thinking that they do.

Similarly, our fellowship with one another is **IMPOSSIBLE WITH THE WICKED.** Look at Paul’s argument in 2 Corinthians
6:14-18.  Here, Paul’s great concern
appears to be worldliness.  Corinth is a
famously wicked city, and apparently the new Christians there are being tempted
to return to their old friends.  Paul’s
point here is that first of all, that kind of close contact is always going to
have an effect.  Second, he wants the
Corinthians to see that such contact shouldn’t happen.  God has called them to come out from the
people of the world, not to remain in the world and be as corrupt as the
worldly.

Obviously, there are limits to what Paul says here.  After all, in 1 Corinthians, an earlier
letter to the same church, he tells them not to divorce unbelieving
spouses.  It’s not a sin to be married to
a non-Christian.  However, we need to
consider our worldly connections with a careful eye and ask ourselves how
they’re influencing us.  If we keep on
hanging out with our pre-Christian friends after obeying the gospel, they’re
not going to help us to follow Christ! 
Our spiritual sharing has to be limited to other Christians.  Only if we are a holy people together can we
all belong to God.

Third, our fellowship with one another is **APPARENT IN OUR WORK.** Consider Paul’s account in Galatians
2:7-10.  The meeting that Paul describes
here takes place during the famine-relief visit of the last part of Acts
11.  Paul believes that God has called
him to preach the gospel to the Gentiles, so he meets with Peter, James and
John to seek confirmation of his revelation. 
In response, the other apostles extend to him the right hand of
fellowship.  Even though they aren’t
going with him on what will be the first missionary journey, they support and
encourage him.

Even though I’m not a Mormon nor approve of Mormonism, there
are several things that I see the Mormons do that I like.  One of them is their adoption of the beehive
as a symbol.  By doing so, they’re making
a statement.  They’re saying, “The work
that we do, we do together.”

That’s how we ought to be too.  In addition to doing our own work for the
Lord, we need to be a force for good in the work that others are doing.  If we know that somebody is studying with a
friend, we need to talk about it with them and encourage them in it.  If somebody has a useful idea for serving
God, our name needs to be on the sign-up sheet. 
The more fellowship we have with one another in our work, the more
successful in doing that work we will be.

Finally, our fellowship is **EXPRESSED IN OUR WORSHIP.** For
instance, consider 1 Corinthians 10:16-17. 
There’s both a general point and a specific point to be made here.  The first is about what fellowship when
Christians come together looks like. 
Biblically speaking, it doesn’t have anything to do with church-building
kitchens and coffee and donuts.  Instead,
what we often call acts of worship are also acts of fellowship.  In this text, the Lord’s Supper is identified
as an act of fellowship.  Our word *koinonia* is translated twice in v. 16 as
“sharing”.  Indeed, any act of worship we
engage in together, be it congregational singing, prayer, or study of the word,
is also going to be a spiritual sharing. 
In that moment, we are united with God and one another.  Even though other churches don’t use the word
this way, it is our auditorium that is the true fellowship hall.

That’s the general point. 
The specific point is about fellowship with one another during the
Lord’s Supper.  In many church buildings
where I’ve worshiped, I’ve seen “This do in remembrance of Me” carved on the
front of the Lord’s-Supper table.  That’s
an accurate statement of our purpose here, but it’s not complete.  The Lord’s Supper isn’t only about
Jesus.  It’s about us too.

As Paul says here, our partaking together of the one bread
is a symbol that we are one body.  The
Lord’s Supper isn’t only about vertical fellowship.  It’s about horizontal fellowship too.  As we eat, we are not only honoring our
Lord.  We are expressing our unity and
our love for one another.

Today as we share in the Lord’s Supper, then, let’s think
about both sides of this.  Let’s consider
not only our Lord, but also our brethren too. 
Is each one of us doing what we can to work toward fellowship in this
one body?  Let’s consider this as we
partake.

**Financial Fellowship**

Earlier, some of you might have been wondering why I didn’t
list the collection for the Lord’s work as one of our acts of fellowship.  In truth, this wasn’t an oversight.  Instead, it’s because the topic gets a whole
sermon segment of its own.  Scripturally
speaking, the third main use of *koinonia* has
to do with financial fellowship, particularly with ordinary Christians sharing
in the work of preachers because they support them in their work.

The first thing that we must appreciate about this is that
it is **A GOOD WORK.** Consider Paul’s description of it in
Philippians 1:3-6.  Here, we find another
stealth *koinonia*.  It’s the word that the NASB translates as
“participation” in v.5.  When Paul talks
about their participation in the gospel, he doesn’t mean that they traveled
down to Corinth to help him preach. 
Instead, he means that they sent him money that enabled him, as Acts 18
says, to devote himself fully to the word.

Sadly, thanks to corrupt false teachers, the good work of
supporting preachers has taken on a bad name. 
In many ways, the transition is similar to what has happened to
sex.  What God intends to be a good and
holy thing when rightly used in the right context has become something dirty
and sordid.  As a result, this kind of
spiritual sharing is something that Christians don’t like talking about.

Brethren, in both cases, we need to get past that.  Certainly, it’s shameful when a man uses the
gospel as a means of gain, but it’s honorable to share in the work of those who
stand for the truth.  In a slightly
different way, it’s equally honorable to share our abundance with brethren who
are in need.  We need to be as ready to
do both these things as we are to engage in any good work.

Let’s take a little more time to examine each one.  With respect to financial sharing with
preachers, Paul emphasizes that it is **SPIRITUALLY
IMPORTANT.** Here, let’s read from
Galatians 6:6-8.  The last two-thirds of
this reading is a very familiar text, but for whatever reason, we tend to want
to take it out of context.  We want to
say things like, “You’d better read your Bible every day instead of watching
filthy movies, or else you’re going to fall away and lose your soul.”  I don’t dispute the general applicability of
the principle, but Paul’s talking about one particular form of sowing to the
flesh or the Spirit.  He’s talking about
sharing good things with preachers.

As you probably have figured out already, where the NASB has
“share” in v. 6, that’s the verb form of *koinonia.* Therefore, when a Christian shares in all
good things with the one who teaches him, he is *kononia*-ing.  Paul says he is
sowing to the Spirit and will reap eternal life.  By contrast, when a Christian fails to share
in all good things with the one who teaches him, he is sowing to the flesh and
from the flesh will reap corruption.

Of course, the application here is not limited to the local
preacher.  In Paul’s discussion in
Philippians 4 of the way that the Philippians sent money to him in Corinth, he
also describes that as *koinonia* and
tells the Philippians that it’s going to work to their spiritual benefit.  When we support preachers in other places as
well, the same will be true for us.

Finally, financial fellowship is **ENCOURAGING.** Rather than
looking at this in the context of support due preachers, though, let’s look at
it in the context of help for needy saints. 
Particularly, let’s consider 2 Corinthians 9:12-15.  This comes at the end of Paul’s great
discussion of the collection for the saints in Jerusalem in 2 Corinthians 8-9,
and it provides one more reason why the Corinthians need to join in.  This is yet another passage with a hidden *koinonia*.  This time, it’s “contribution” in v. 13.  The point is that when the Corinthians
contributed to that collection, they were sharing with the needy brethren who
were going to benefit from it.

Obviously, this contribution would produce the tangible
benefit of providing for poor brothers and sisters in the Jerusalem
church.  Paul argues, though, that it
would produce other benefits too.  It
would lead those Jewish Christians in Jerusalem to yearn for and feel connected
to Gentile Christians all over the Mediterranean.  Second, it would lead them to thank God and
glorify Him for their deliverance accomplished through the collection.

Even today, both of these things happen.  These days, it seems like whenever a natural
disaster wipes out the Christians in some community, like the floods in Baton
Rouge earlier this year, other Christians are quick to rise up and help
them.  Not surprisingly, such help also
bears the same two spiritual fruits. 
Christians who may live thousands of miles apart now feel much closer to
each other, and as happened 2000 years ago, those whose sufferings are relieved
glorify God.  When we use our money to
freely engage in spiritual sharing, we inevitably accomplish great good.
