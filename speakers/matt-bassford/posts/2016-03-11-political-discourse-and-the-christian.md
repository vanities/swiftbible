The past couple of months have been a dispiriting time on
social media.  I will admit to watching
the rise of Donald Trump with some concern. 
However, the behavior of Christians arguing about Trump has me
considerably more concerned.  In place of
the thoughtful, courteous, and loving dialogue that should occur between
brethren, I’ve seen political controversy degenerate far too often into
contention and name-calling.



There are several problems with this.  First, contentiousness is a black-letter
sin.  Along with incest and refusal to
work, it is one of the few sins explicitly cited in Scripture as grounds for
withdrawal, in Titus 3:10-11.  Paul wants
us to understand that a man with a factious spirit is a danger not only to
himself, but to the whole congregation. 
After all, someone who is prone to controversy in political matters is
likely to stir up trouble when it comes to matters of faith too.  Our Lord expects us to pursue peace in every
area of our lives, not conflict.

Second, strife among brethren on a public forum like
Facebook can only bring shame upon the church. 
In 1 Corinthians 6:7, Paul speaks of lawsuits between brethren as a
defeat.  They have engaged in ungodly behavior
before a court of unbelievers, so that no matter who wins the suit, Christ has
already lost.

Much the same principle applies to all our public
speech.  Christians need to remember that
what they say in public will either honor the Lord (if it is dignified and
wise) or dishonor Him (if it is hotheaded, rash, and foolish).  Every one of us is responsible all the time
for upholding the glory of His name.

It is particularly embarrassing, then, when two Christians
so forget themselves that they begin to quarrel with one another in a thread
that hundreds of people, believer and unbeliever alike, are reading.  How disgraceful it is for those who claim the
name of Christ to show that they do not share the mind of Christ!  Who could blame the outsider who sees such a
display and says, “Whoever those people are, I don’t want any part of
them”?  If our disputatious behavior
causes others to lose their souls, we may rest assured that we will have to
give an account for them as well as for ourselves.

Finally, such passion over politics on the part of the
Christian reveals badly misplaced priorities. 
Christ has told us, “My kingdom is not of this world.”  We are strangers and exiles on the earth who
are seeking a country of our own.  Our
priorities should be these:

* How can I better glorify God?
* How can I help my family better glorify God?
* How can I help my church better glorify God?
* How can I help my neighbors better glorify God?



Politics has nothing to do with any of the items on this
list.  Even when a political goal is in
accordance with the spirit of Scripture, governmental coercion is no substitute
for the obedience of faith.  There is no
human law that can change the sinner’s heart. 
Only the word of God, presented with love by the people of God, can do
that.

Politics, then, should be nothing more than a sideshow to
the disciple.  We shouldn’t be arguing
about politics because politics isn’t worth arguing about.  If political disagreement elicits more than a
shrug from us, we have invested far too much of ourselves in the things of this
life.  Stirring up my brother to love and
good works is worthy of my passion. 
Leading the lost to salvation is worthy of all the conviction I can
summon up.

Donald Trump, be he ever so obnoxious, is not.
