**Tl;dr:  Hymns require different copyright treatment than sermons because they require much more time and care in order to be successful.**



Last week’s hymn-related blog post, on the interaction
between hymns and copyright law, generated far more reaction and discussion
than I had ever imagined it might.  Many
commenters on various Internet fora raised a number of interesting questions
and points.  One of the most interesting
responses of all, though, came from Marshall Reid.  In part, he wrote:

*You have presented what I believe to be a
valid argument in appealing to Christians to obey the law of the land. So technically,
all individuals/churches should follow the outlined procedures in securing
permission to use a song. But then again, if what the songwriters are wanting
is just to glorify God and help other Christians do the same thing, then why
worry about whether it's done the legal way or not?*

*. . .*

*Is the issue of hymns
any different than sermons? As a preacher, and I'm sure you fully understand
this, there are many times that I hear or read a sermon and think, "I need
to preach that!" I would never advocate a preacher preaching someone
else's sermon almost word for word and trying to pass it off as his own, but I
have most definitely used other preacher's sermons. I will incorporate points
they made, and maybe even a statement that they made because I don't think it
can said any better. I don't ask permission before I do so (maybe I should?).
However, if I'm directly quoting from someone else, whether it's another
preacher or a commentator, I will give that person credit. Is there a
difference in this scenario and that of hymns?*

Elsewhere in his comment, Marshall made quite clear that he
does NOT believe that Christians should ignore copyright law.  He simply wanted to raise a question that he
thought others would be asking, at least to themselves.  Why, when our attitude toward the
intellectual property of sermons is so relaxed, should we be so concerned about
copyright when it comes to hymns?  That’s
a question that deserves a thoughtful answer, and I think the answer has to do
with both the different requirements and purposes of sermons as opposed to
hymns.

It takes me maybe five or six hours to write a sermon from
scratch (about 3700 words or so).  I put
together a slide presentation to go with it if I think the slides would be
useful, run through it, edit it, run through it a couple more times, and I'm
ready to go on Sunday--about 10 hours' work, all told.  I preach it, and then I put it aside, likely
never to be preached again, at least by me.

On the other hand, I would estimate that writing a hymn
requires something like 200 or 300 hours of work. An initial burst of writing
is followed by an endless process of editing and revision.  During that process, I will often stare at a
couplet that I don't like for five or six hours, devising and discarding dozens
of wording variations.  By the end of
that six hours, I will be exhausted, but if I have succeeded in finding a
couplet that satisfies me, I consider it time well spent.

Likewise, I spend countless hours on the arrangements that I
write, listening to them over and over again. 
I commonly slow down the playback in Finale to 10 or 15 beats per
minute, so that I can listen to each chord change and make sure that each one
is precisely right.  I will sing through
the parts hundreds of times, to the point where I know the bass lines to my own
arrangements better than anything I sing in the assembly.

Then, once the hymn is as good as I can get it, I put it out
for review.  I post it on the Hymnody
group on Facebook, on the e-mail listserv for alumni of the Hymninar, and I often
submit it for private review elsewhere as well. 
Usually, the first review exposes a number of flaws that I had
overlooked, which sends me into another cycle of text and arrangement
revision.  Then, I put it out for a
second round of semi-public critique, and another, and another after that if
necessary, until my reviewers see nothing more they would like me to work on.

After that, if the hymn not only passes long-distance review
but elicits a number of enthusiastic comments during the process, it might
become one of the two or three hymns I take to the Hymninar in a given
year.  At the Hyminar, the hymn will be
examined and then tried out by a group of brethren with more than a century’s
worth of combined experience in writing and editing hymns.  Usually, this tryout session will highlight
more places where the hymn is not as strong as it could be.

If the revisions recommended are sufficiently extensive, I
may well go back home, rewrite, and bring the hymn to *next* year’s Hymninar.  If
they require only minor changes, I will make them, send the hymn out for yet
another round of reviews from everybody I think might possibly supply one, and
then, if there are no more changes I wish to make, I will declare the hymn
final.  Start to finish, this ordeal can
take years, and I haven’t even mentioned the hours of study and prayer that
must accompany it.  The hymn of mine that
is closest today to being taken final is one I began in 2011, and it may be
months or even years away from completion.

Clearly, the way that I handle sermons and the way that I
handle hymns could not be more different. 
This difference exists because the standards for a successful sermon
versus a successful hymn are also very different.  The goal of a successful sermon is to
“declare the whole counsel of God” in a clear and truthful manner.  It requires nothing more from its audience (at
least during the assembly) than 45 minutes of their time.  It can be a successful sermon even if nobody
in attendance is so excited about it that he wants to go out and preach it
himself.

The same is emphatically not true of a successful hymn.  I believe that a hymn has succeeded only if
it has become part of the repertoire of the brotherhood, and that is
extraordinarily difficult to accomplish. 
A successful sermon needs only an audience; a successful hymn requires
widespread and enthusiastic participation.

It’s hard for brethren who don’t read music to learn to sing
a new hymn.  It’s hard for a song leader
to be the first to lead that hymn on Sunday morning.  It’s hard for the fifth-string song leader to
introduce it on Wednesday night, even if he doesn’t have the first clue about
how to blow pitch or beat time.  All of
those people start off opposed to any new hymn, and they must be persuaded to
support mine.

I can’t go from church to church and lobby brethren to sing
my hymns, and I wouldn’t even if I could. 
Those hymns have to speak for themselves, and they must speak in such
compelling tones that every Christian who encounters them is excited about
using them in worship.  When we already
know and sing hundreds of hymns, even the tiniest flaw can keep a new hymn from
being widely accepted.  Because I want my
hymns to be accepted and used, it follows that they must be, so far as is possible
with me, flawless.

I don’t care what people do with my sermons.  Those so inclined can find hundreds of them
on jolietchurch.org with no copyright restrictions whatsoever.  I haven’t invested much time in each one, and
by the time they make it online, they have accomplished their purpose
anyway.  If some brother wants to take
the idea and “make it his own”, fine.  I
have problems with other full-time preachers preaching my sermons verbatim
(barring extraordinary circumstances, of course), but generally speaking, I
figure that’s between them and the Master to whom they must give an account.

Hymns are different. 
I live in mortal terror of some ham-fisted reviser demolishing my
painstaking work and erecting on the rubble a crude structure of his own
devising.  I consider it a personal
violation for someone else to alter my self-expression without my consent.

What is more, such an editor may well defeat my
purpose.  In every hymn I have finished
in the past five years, I have scrutinized literally every word and every
note.  Those in the final have been
carefully chosen (whether by me or by my composer) for maximum effect.  Casually rearranging such a hymn is about
like hitting a Swiss watch with a sledgehammer. 
The result is unlikely to be an improvement.

This process can and will destroy a hymn.  There are hymns that I do not lead and will
not teach because they suffer from too many competing versions.  In the case of some contemporary praise
songs, it’s impossible for even a hymnal editor to discern what the original
was.  It has been buried beneath an
avalanche of (most likely unauthorized) revisions.  That’s a terrible fate for any hymn to
encounter, particularly if the original possesses quality that its illegitimate
descendants do not.

In the face of these abuses, hymnists have no other choice
than to seek the protection of copyright law. 
It is true that a copyright notice at the bottom of a hymn provides
uncertain protection, but in the eyes of many, any hymn that even appears to be
in the public domain is fair game for rewriting.  Admittedly, the restrictions of copyright do
impose a slight additional burden on song leaders and supplement
compilers.  However, when hymnists have
expended hundreds of hours on a hymn, is it really too much for them to ask for
fifteen minutes from those who want to use it?
