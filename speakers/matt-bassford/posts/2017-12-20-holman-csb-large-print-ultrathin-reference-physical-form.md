**OVERVIEW.** Man, this is a pretty Bible!  Man, it’s got stiff hinges!

I got this Bible through the good offices of Paul Stevenson,
who interceded for me with Holman’s PR department and got them to send me one
of the goatskin advance copies.  I myself
made no promises to Holman whatsoever, but pretty much any Bible that passes
through my hands is a Bible I’m going to review.  So there.

**VITAL STATISTICS.**

**Translation:**  Christian Standard Bible (2017)

**Cover:**  Goatskin

**Binding:**  Sewn

**Trim Size:**  6 ⅛”
x  9 ⅛”

**Text Block Width:**  ⅞”

**Weight:**  1 pound, 12 ounces

**Column:**  Double

**Layout:**  Paragraphed

**Print Size:**  9.5 Bible Serif

**Words of Christ:**  Black

**Cross-References:**  Yes

**Ribbon Markers:**  2

**SIZE.** This Bible has the classic large-print
thinline dimensions:  tall, wide, and
skinny.  As a result, I can’t quite get
it to fit in the glove box of my Corolla, though it came closer than you would
expect from a Bible that’s more than six inches wide.  It would do very well in the side pocket of a
briefcase or a laptop bag, though.

**COVER.** Holman has produced some high-end
mass-market Bibles in the past, but this is the first Holman product I’ve seen
that qualifies as a true premium edition. 
The outer cover is goatskin, the Bible is edge-lined, and the lining
appears to be calfskin.

What goatskin, too! 
I’ve never owned an Allan Bible, but I own a couple of Jongbloed-bound
goatskin Bibles and an NASB thinline rebound by Leonard’s in goatskin.  To be honest, those Bibles have left me
wondering what all the goatskin fanciers are raving about.  Their covers are certainly supple and durable,
but I wouldn’t describe them as either luxurious or lovable.

The cover of the LPUR is different.  When I first picked it up, it was so soft
that I had trouble believing it was goatskin. 
However, closer inspection revealed that it had a very fine natural
grain, and when I opened it, I could feel the cover bending along all those
grain lines, so that it felt almost like tiny fish scales under my
fingertips.  It’s as floppy and flexible
as one could ever hope for in a Bible cover. 
The leather also smells delightful, though it doesn’t smell like Bible
leather normally does.  It smells more
like a high-end shoe store, if that makes sense.

Like the other edge-lined goatskin Bibles I’ve reviewed, the
LPUR is perimeter-stitched.  However, in
what I think is a pretty cool feature, and not one I can recall having seen
before, the stitching involves two different colors.  On the exterior side, it’s black; on the
lining side, it’s red (to go with the overall red-black-silver color scheme of
the Bible).  My wife assures me that this
is something you can set up a sewing machine to do.  I have no idea.  I just think it’s cool.

One thing that annoys me about the LPUR, though, is the way
that the edges of the goatskin catch on the text block after the Bible has been
opened widely.  Over time, this might
lead to one or both of them wearing away. 
I chalk this up to Holman being a recent player in the premium-Bible
market (at least, this is the first Holman edge-lined Bible I’ve seen), and I
anticipate that later Bibles will correct the problem.

The spine of the LPUR has three hubs.  All it contains are the words/phrases “Holy
Bible”, “Christian Standard Version”, and “Holman”, stamped in silver.  No logos. 
I like the look, but I think I would have liked it better if Holman had
used a serif typeface instead of a sans-serif typeface for the text.  It’s maybe just a little too plain-Jane for
my taste.

This is certainly a beautiful Bible, but I think it would
prove durable as well.  The goatskin is
so soft that I expect it to scuff up quite a bit, but it shouldn’t tear or fall
apart.  It also helps that the LPUR is
edge-lined.  Edge-lining results in a
more flexible cover than paste-down binding does, which means that the hinges
in an edge-lined Bible face much less stress and are much less likely to
tear.  Also, the tabs (the flaps of the
lining that attach the cover to the text block) in the LPUR are extremely
stiff.  That creates some problems for
opening the Bible (it will not lie open, much less flat, until you get to
Exodus), but they do make splits at the hinge even more unlikely.  Holman’s promo literature insists that
recipients will be able to use this Bible for many years to come, and I think
that’s accurate.

**TEXT BLOCK.** It’s sewn, of course.  Holman has been known to try to get away with
glued text blocks in lower-end Bibles, and even in some that aren’t lower-end,
but nobody pulls such shenanigans with an edge-lined Bible.  If you drop $150 on a Bible and the pages
start falling out within a year, you’re going to be, shall we say, irate.

Interestingly, this is a text block with edge silvering
rather than edge gilding.  Equally
interestingly, unlike all other edge-lined goatskin Bibles of my acquaintance,
the LPUR doesn’t have art gilding.  The
copyright page tells me that the text block is printed in China, so I wonder if
the premium LPUR’s have the same text block as the mass-market LPUR’s.  Normally, publishers who attempt to do this
get into trouble, but the text block in my LPUR is well-executed enough that it
doesn’t seem out of place in premium.  In
particular, Holman’s decision to make all the LPUR’s black-letter means that it
doesn’t suffer from the various disasters that befall mass-market red-letter
text blocks.

**VALUE.** Though it has a different ISBN, my LPUR
is apparently the same thing as the premium-leather LPUR that Holman is
currently retailing for $130.  If you’re
smart, you won’t pay retail for it, instead grabbing it on christianbook.com or
similar for a little less than $90.  By
contrast, the Foundation edge-lined calfskin Large-Print UltraThin retails for
$150, but it’s not a goatskin Bible.  The
Crossway Omega Thinline, another large-print thinline reference Bible, retails
for $250, but it’s Jongbloed-print and has art gilt.  In terms of quality, the Holman LPUR probably
falls somewhere between the two, which means that it’s a phenomenal value.



**CONCLUSION.** I really like it.  People who like to read their Bibles by
laying them flat on a desk won’t like it, but I literally never do that.  This Bible is lovable enough that I would be
willing to adopt it as my primary.  However,
it does get dings for the larger size, the cover catching, and the stiff tabs, so
I award it an 8.5/10.
