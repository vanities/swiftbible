Over the past few years, I’ve preached from nine different
translations, from the interlinear-like ASV to the sometimes bizarrely loose
NLT.  However, throughout this time, I’ve
always returned to the NASB.  First of
all, the translation is a sentimental favorite of mine.  My mother was an NASB user; my dad still
is.  It’s the translation I grew up
using.  The apostle Paul preached out of
the NASB (kidding).



Also, the translation philosophy of the NASB makes it useful
to me as a study translation.  Every
translation out there has to strike a balance between precision and
smoothness.  On the one hand, there are
translations that emphasize readability, but they achieve their goal at the
cost of textual nuance.

On the other hand, you have the NASB.  A descendant of the old ASV, it sometimes
strikes readers as being more a transliteration than a translation.  It retains many Hebrew and Greek idioms and sometimes
even sentence structure.  In instances
where the translators must supply words to make a passage sensible English, the
supplied words are italicized.  It has
something of the air of a reference work.

This doesn’t bother me any when I’m reading it (I’m used to
it), and it’s an asset when I am studying. 
I don’t read Hebrew or Greek, but I think the NASB takes me closer to
them than any other English translation, with fewer interpretive layers imposed
by the translators.  This allows me to
consider the semi-raw material and come to my own conclusions.

However, recently I’ve been considering switching from the
NASB to the ESV.  Outside of the NASB,
the ESV is the translation I know best. 
I preached out of it for five years. 
It’s a solid formal-equivalent translation that is weighted toward the
“precision” end of the spectrum.

At the end of that time, though, I decided to return to the
NASB.  There were a couple of places in
particular where I thought the ESV hid the ball.  Unlike the NASB, the ESV doesn’t italicize
supplied words, and at times, those hidden supplied words put a different spin
on the text.  I would rather have those
additions clearly distinguished so that I don’t have to rely on the
translators’ judgments in such places. 
Also, even though the ESV is smoother than the NASB, I found that the
smoothness didn’t help me get any closer to the meaning of the text.

Recently, though, a couple of factors have led me to revisit
my decision.  The first is that I’m not
simply an ordinary Christian who is quietly reading my Bible to myself in the
pew or at my desk.  I’m a preacher, so
what matters in my use of the Bible is not only my ability to comprehend, but
my hearers’ ability to comprehend.

Objectively, the ESV is easier to understand.  Over the past few weeks, I’ve invested a lot
of time and thought in ways to make my preaching more comprehensible.  I’ve started running my sermons through the
readability checker on Microsoft Word, and based on word and sentence length
(not an ideal measure, but one which has some utility), I preach at a
Flesch-Kinkaid grade level of about 7.4. 
The NASB, by contrast, has a Flesch-Kinkaid grade level of 8.8, and the
ESV one of 7.8.

This lines up with my subjective sense that the ESV is
easier to understand.  When I’m buying a
Bible for a young student or a new Christian, I invariably end up buying an
ESV, not an NASB.  I don’t think they
need the precision that the latter provides (the babe in Christ does not need
to come to a thoughtful, reasoned conclusion about 1 Thessalonians 4:4), and its
added complexity might deter them from reading.

Also, last Saturday, I met with a group of brethren for a
study, and it so happened that I brought an ESV, while they were all using an
NASB.  As a result, I was listening to
the NASB while reading my ESV.  I could
tell that what was on the page in front of me was simpler and clearer than what
I was hearing.

If I were a proof-texty preacher, this wouldn’t be a big
deal.  One verse written at a ninth-grade
level isn’t a big deal, compared to one verse written at an eighth-grade
level.

However, I have a great fondness for expository sermons,
particularly ones covering several chapters. 
If I’m reading 60 verses in context over the course of a sermon, that’s
plenty of time for confusion to accumulate. 
Dropping a grade level in my customary translation might make a big
difference for the comprehension of my hearers.

Second, there’s a Bible design issue here.  Currently, I do my expository preaching out
of a Cambridge NASB Wide-Margin reference. 
It’s a beautifully laid-out Bible, but I have two problems with it.  First, it’s a double-column Bible, which
means that half the time when I’m writing in the margins, I’m writing in the
gutter.  That’s a pain.  Second, it’s a reference Bible.  I don’t need the 95,000 NASB cross-references
in the pulpit, but the Wide-Margin has them anyway, which makes it larger and
heavier than I need it to be.

However, Crossway (which does a marvelous job of offering
the ESV in a wide variety of formats) publishes an edition called the
Single-Column Journaling Bible.  It’s
single-column (durr), which means that I would only have to write in the
accessible outside margins.  Second,
rather than a full cross-reference suite, it only contains the minimal
references that are the ones I most use. 
As a result, it’s about three-quarters of a pound lighter than the
Wide-Margin Reference.

Basically, the Single-Column Journaling Bible is my dream
pulpit Bible, but nothing like it is available in the NASB.  If I want to use it, I have to switch to the
ESV.

It’s not at all certain that I’m going to make the switch,
but I figure that I might as well crowdsource first.  Anybody else have any thoughts about a move
from the NASB to the ESV?
