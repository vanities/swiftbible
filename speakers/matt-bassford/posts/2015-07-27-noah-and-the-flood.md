These days, it’s only natural for Christians to feel some
concern about the moral direction that our society seems to be taking.  I’m not very old yet, but even I can remember
times when things that are now openly endorsed were never even discussed in
polite company.  However, it’s important
for us to keep our perspective on these things. 
The times are not as good as they once were, but the moral climate is
still a whole lot better than it was in many periods of Bible history.

Of the periods of corruption that mankind has seen, none
have been worse than the time immediately before the great flood.  After all, at that point, the wickedness of
man became so great that God saw no alternative to destroying the world with
water.  However, even in that dark time,
we still have an example of how we can continue to serve God, no matter how
wicked the people around us may become. 
Let’s look, then, at the story of Noah and the flood.



**Before the Flood**

The first section of this story concerns itself with events
that take place before the flood.  The
author of Genesis begins by describing **THE
CORRUPTION OF MANKIND.** Here, let’s
read from Genesis 6:5-12.  The text
doesn’t go into great detail about what this wickedness consisted of, but it
was so great that God became sorry that He had created man.

Now, there are those who want to seize onto this statement
and use it as proof that the Bible does not present God as omniscient, or that
the Bible contradicts itself on the subject. 
However, I think there’s a way to read the text that doesn’t force a
contradiction.  Let’s put it like
this.  Several months ago, I decided that
I was turning into a slug, and that I needed to start working out three times a
week.  There are parts of my workout
routine that I do not enjoy; indeed, I find them acutely miserable.  I go into each and every workout knowing that
they are going to be acutely miserable this time too, but I do it anyway
because I don’t want to turn into a slug. 
However, when I am suffering in the middle of that workout, do I regret
that decision?  You bet I do!

In the same way, God knew exactly what He was getting into
even before He created man.  However, His
love for us, even before we existed, was so great that He was willing to endure
the pain our sin causes Him.  However,
when mankind almost universally rejected Him, the experience of that rejection
was so painful that it made Him regret setting the whole thing in motion.

This should remind us, friends, of an essential truth about
God and sin.  He cannot bear it, and
sooner or later, He will judge it.  Most
people around us today would prefer to deny this.  They’d rather focus on God’s mercy and
love.  However, God’s holiness and
justice are just as much a part of His character as His mercy and love are, and
when we ignore them, we invite disaster.

However, even though this disaster is coming, there is still
one spiritual bright spot.  It is the
righteous man Noah, and God offers him the chance to escape by **BUILDING THE ARK.** Here, let’s read from Genesis
6:13-22.  This is quite a specific list
of instructions!  The details don’t
really matter to us because we will never be called upon to build arks
ourselves, but the bottom line is critically important.  The text tells us that everything that God
commanded, Noah did.

In context, this makes perfect sense.  When God told Noah that all of mankind was
going to be destroyed, Noah believed Him. 
When God told Noah that he could escape if he did what God said, Noah
did what God said.  Noah obeyed like his
life depended on it, because his life did.

Today, brethren, we need to obey like our lives depend on
it.  This does not mean that we should
think that we can rescue ourselves from hell through works of merit, any more
than the story of Noah is about Noah rescuing himself from the flood.  Noah was righteous, but he wasn’t perfect.  Instead, the story of Noah is about God in
His grace giving Noah an opportunity to be saved, and Noah taking advantage of
that opportunity through obedience.

Likewise, God in His grace has given us an opportunity to be
saved from His wrath.  Our perfection is
not a condition of our salvation, any more than perfection was a condition of
Noah’s salvation.  Like Noah, though, we
must take advantage of God’s grace through obedience.  Like Noah, we have to do our level best to
put God’s commandments into practice. 
God tells us to be immersed in water for the forgiveness of our sins, so
if we want to be saved, we had better obey Him. 
God tells us that we need to sing in our assemblies, so we’d better
sing.

Problems don’t arise here when we do our best to obey and
fall short.  Problems arise when we
consider the commandments of God and decide for one reason or another that
we’re not going to obey that one.  That
would be like Noah deciding that he wasn’t going to build the ark out of gopher
wood after all because there was a nice stand of oak trees right next to the
building site.  Noah knew the situation
was too serious for that kind of foolishness! 
When we start dismissing parts of the word of God, we only show that we
don’t appreciate the gravity of our situation.

**The Flood**

Next, Genesis takes us to the story of the flood itself.  This begins with Noah and his family **ENTERING THE ARK.** Consider Genesis 7:1-17.  Noah was 500 years old when his children were
born, and he was 600 when the flood began. 
He may well have spent most or all of that intervening 100 years either
building the ark or trying to convince his neighbors to repent before it was
too late.  A whole lot of nothing
happened for decades, and then everything happened at once.  If Noah hadn’t spent that time preparing for
no obvious reason, he would have been doomed too.

We also need to recognize the importance of preparedness in
our own spiritual lives.  I’ve heard it
said that one of the most significant distinctions we can make is the
distinction between what is important and what is urgent.  Urgent things are those that demand our
attention right now.  They are the
production deadline at work or the big paper that’s due in English class
tomorrow.  However, they are not
necessarily very important.  I spent lots
of time writing lots of papers when I was in college, but not a soul remembers
what those papers said, including me!

The problem is that all of those urgent but not important
things have a way of crowding out the things that are important but not
urgent.  If we’re in such a rush to get
to work one morning that we skip our usual Bible reading, the roof will not
fall in on our heads.  If we’re too busy
to take our kids to Bible class one Wednesday evening, the ground will not open
up and swallow us.  Those things are not
important because of the urgent problem they are solving right now.  They are important because of the
life-and-death problem they will solve years or decades from now.

Whether we remember it or not, judgment is coming.  Like Noah, we need to be getting ready for
it.  Day by day, we need to be preparing
our spiritual arks, even at the cost of ignoring the things that are urgent but
not important.  We need to be
assembling.  We need to be studying.  We need to be praying.  We need to be training our children in the
discipline and instruction of the Lord. 
If we don’t, it won’t produce problems now, but it will produce
catastrophe later.  As with the flood, by
the time our need for spiritual preparation is obvious, it will be too late to
prepare.

Immediately after Noah, his family, and the animals enter
the ark, **THE FLOOD** destroys
everything else.  We see this dreadful
tale in Genesis 7:17-24.  The flood has
been a favorite target of skeptics for hundreds if not thousands of years.  However, our belief in the flood must be of a
piece with our belief in the Bible as a whole. 
In the New Testament, the Hebrews writer, the apostle Peter, and the
Lord Jesus Himself all talk about the flood as a historical event.  Dreadful and incredible though it may seem, a
deluge did indeed annihilate almost all life on earth.

Of all the human race, the only survivors are Noah and his
family, eight people out of who knows how many. 
They are saved because of their obedient faith in God and His
promises.  In 1 Peter 3, Peter compares
this event to baptism.  They were brought
safely through the water, and when we are baptized, we are saved through the
resurrection of Jesus Christ.

Before the Lord’s Supper, then, let’s consider what a
difference salvation makes.  On the one
hand, you have Noah and his family: 
probably wet, probably terrified, probably sick of being cooped up with
a bunch of animals.  On the other hand,
you have everybody else—dead.

When the Day of Judgment comes, the difference will be even starker.  On the one hand, you will have the few who
are ushered into eternal life by the grace of Jesus.  On the other hand, you will have the many—dead,
and worse than dead.  As terrifying as
the spectacle of God destroying the world by water would have been, the
spectacle of God destroying the whole universe by fire will be even more
dreadful.  We could not hope to stand in
that day without Jesus, and it’s then, I think, that we will fully grasp what
His death has done for us.  Let’s remember
Him as we partake.

**After the Flood**

The last part of this story concerns, naturally, what
happens after the flood.  This begins
when **THE WATERS RECEDE**.  This part of the story is recounted in
Genesis 8:1-22.  It’s critical to our
understanding of God because it reveals His mercy in the midst of a story about
His justice.  Now that only eight remain
from the whole human race, it would have been an easy thing for God to complete
the destruction by killing Noah and his family too.  None of them were sinless.  They all deserved it just as all of us
deserve it.  However, God remembered them
for good, not for evil, and resolved that He would never again destroy the
world.  This is not a blessing that
mankind had earned; indeed, God knew they would again choose to be wicked.  It’s a commitment that God made not because
of us, but because of Him, because His nature is merciful and good.

This too is something that we need to consider in our walk
with Him today.  I’ve known Christians
who took God’s warnings too lightly, who weren’t at all concerned about their
souls when they should have been very concerned.  However, I’ve also known Christians who
didn’t think enough about God’s mercy.  So
far as I can tell, they think of Him as some kind of mean old lawyer up in
heaven, somebody who is scrutinizing our lives with a magnifying glass, looking
for any excuse to withdraw His grace from us and condemn us to hell.

In reality, that understanding of God is exactly wrong.  He’s not our enemy.  He’s our friend!  More than anything else, He wants us to spend
eternity with Him in heaven.  This goal
is so important to Him that He sacrificed His own Son to make it possible.  He will give us every chance.  He will give us all the help we ask for and
more.  Sadly, it is possible for us to
reject Him so thoroughly that we leave Him with no choice but to reject us, but
that’s not His fault.  It’s ours.

Finally, let’s turn our attention to **GOD’S COVENANT** with Noah.  Consider
Genesis 9:1-19.  One of the sad ironies
of our society is that the rainbow has been turned into a symbol of sin when in
reality it is a symbol of the faithfulness of God.  From the people and animals on the ark, the
whole world was repopulated, and to this day, all of us survive because of
God’s faithfulness.

Tragically, most people use God’s forbearance in the face of
evil as an opportunity for more evil. 
For us, though, it should prove something else.  It proves that God keeps His promises.  Think of it like this.  Everything in God’s nature is revolted by
sin.  It is certainly in His power to send
another flood to cleanse the earth of the wicked.  However, He doesn’t do that because He
promised that He wouldn’t.  If God will
keep that promise, surely He will keep His promises to us!

The implications of this conclusion are profound.  If God keeps His promises, every promise in
the New Testament is a promise that we can rely on.  Just like God made a covenant with Noah, so
God makes a covenant with everyone who obeys the gospel.  When we choose to be baptized, God forgives
us and remembers our sins no more.  Like
the flood cleansed the earth of wickedness, so the waters of baptism cleanse
our souls of wickedness.  We can’t see
this, but we know it’s true, because it’s founded on the promises of God, and
God keeps His promises.

Similarly, we can rely on God’s promise of life after
death.  Death is one of the great
mysteries of human existence.  Nobody has
returned from the grave in 2000 years, and even those who were raised from the
dead in the time of the New Testament didn’t have a whole lot to say about
their experiences on the other side.  To
our human minds that are so used to a finite, limited world, it seems
incredible that we can have eternal life in a realm that we can’t even see.  However, the hope that we have in that
eternal life isn’t illusory or vain. 
It’s a certain hope, because it is founded on the promises of God, and
God keeps His promises.
