My post on the 10-minute rule for learning new hymns
generated many comments, both positive and negative.  One of the more interesting of these came
from my friend and brother Eric Sparks, who wrote,

“Ok, I understand what you are saying and the reasoning
behind it. What I don’t get is why we only appeal to the lowest common
denominator. The Ordinary Christian, the Untrained Singer, etc. When I read the
New Testament I find that all Christians are to be priests, and when I look at
the teacher that brings us to Christ I find that the priests were expected to
not only know what to do in worship, but how to do it well.

“Secular studies would tell you that it takes 10,000 hours
to become an expert at anything. I have been alive for well over 10,000 days
and not one of them goes by without me singing something...why are we not all
professionals? Why are we not all priests rather than ‘Ordinary Christians?’”

In other words, why is it too much to ask of Christians that
they be musically competent?  Why
shouldn’t we expect the ordinary pew-sitter to learn to read music so that more
difficult music (like the music of “Oceans”) doesn’t pose such a challenge?



PERSONAL OR CONGREGATIONAL?

In answer to Eric’s question, I think it’s important to
distinguish between urging Christians to learn to read music on a personal,
voluntary level and pushing a whole congregation to learn to read music, such
that they transition from a majority non-sight-reader group to a majority sight-reader
group.  I’m enthusiastically in favor of
the first.  I learned to read music as an
adult, and if any brother came up to me and asked whether he should do the
same, I would answer, “Absolutely!”  In
fact, I’d volunteer to help him.

However, that kind of thing is only ever going to happen on
a small scale.  People making that
individual decision might add another member or two to your sight-reading core,
but it won’t really change the ability of the congregation to sing more
difficult hymns.

Instead (speaking as a preacher, somebody who tries to get
the congregation to do things on a professional basis), if you want to make a
significant difference in a congregation’s hymn-learning capacity, you’re going
to have to make a big campaign out of it. 
You’re going to have to spend a year emphasizing it, like you would
spend a year emphasizing daily Bible reading or evangelism.  You’re going to have to have classes on sight
reading, a number of sermons on the subject, and so on.

THIN SCRIPTURAL JUSTIFICATION

I see several problems with doing so.  The first is the thinness of the Scriptural
justification for it.  If you want to
persuade people that they ought to be reading the Bible more, you can start
with Psalm 119 and go from there.  If you
want to emphasize evangelism, you start with the Great Commission and Acts
8:4.

However, there is no comparably clear call for improvement
of technical singing ability.  Eric makes
what is probably the best available argument for that—reference to the
Old-Testament priesthood and our responsibility to offer our best—but getting
from there to “Learn to read music” requires several steps.  As a result, if I’m going to lead a charge in
something, I’m going to lead it in Bible reading or evangelism, not in music.

Second, a lot of brethren are going to question the linkage
between singing well and pleasing God. 
Even if a brother can’t carry a tune in a bucket and is musically
illiterate, who’s to say that he isn’t offering a better sacrifice than the
opera singer one pew over?  I will argue
loud and long that people ought to improve their worship skills, but that
generally has much more to do with mindfulness than with melody.

PUSHBACK FROM THE CONGREGATION

Additionally, though the mileage of others may vary, my
sense is that in most of the congregations where I’ve been a member, a focus on
musical mechanics is a good way to generate pushback.  Even Christians who don’t intend to start
reading the Bible daily aren’t going to make much of a fuss when you tell them
they should.  They acknowledge the
justice of the argument.

The same isn’t true when it comes to learning to read music,
probably because of the thinness of Scriptural support.  Non-singers don’t appreciate being made to
feel like second-class spiritual citizens unless they convert themselves to
singers.  Most will passively resist;
some will be noisy about it.

LOW LIKELIHOOD OF SUCCESS

This takes us to the final problem with a sight-reading
campaign:  it probably won’t work.  The heart of the king may be like a stream of
water in the hand of the Lord, but it is not so with the congregation and the
church leadership.  The congregation
emphatically does not turn wherever the leadership wishes!  Any kind of effortful change is
extraordinarily difficult.

I am under no illusions, for instance, that a majority of
the members at Jackson Heights follows our daily Bible-reading plan, even
though Shawn and I feature it at the bottom of every slide for every
sermon.  If I knew that an extra 20
percent read their Bibles because of it, I would be extremely happy.

I think that the number of Christians who would learn to
read music because the leadership pushed it would be much smaller.  Maybe 5 percent would put in the hours of
work required over a span of years (that’s how long it took for me, at least),
10 percent if they’re particularly receptive.

However, even a full 20-percent increase wouldn’t be
helpful.  Let’s say a congregation goes
from 15 percent sight-readers to 35 percent sight-readers.  Probably, a song leader will be able to lead
more complicated songs without them falling flat.  However, you’ve still got a bewildered 65
percent sitting mutely in the pews, almost certainly not worshiping.  The musical quality may have improved, but
from a spiritual perspective, the experiment is still not a success.

CONCLUSION

Rather than improving the singers, the best solution to
song-difficulty problems is to improve the selections.  Non-singers with good hearts (a numerous
tribe, despite the suspicions of some singers) will be willing to attempt
unfamiliar hymns, provided that they aren’t too demanding.  They can pick up simple tunes easily enough;
it’s the complicated stuff that throws them for a loop.  I’m not interested in insisting that people
like that need to change.  I’m interested
in handing them music that they can easily use to worship.
