As I mentioned a few months back, at the end of last year, I
burned my Christmas-gift money on a hardback copy of the Crossway Reader’s Bible,
Six-Volume Set (henceforth the Six-Volume Set). 
It has recently returned from a sojourn at my photographer’s, but when
it came back, it brought a friend with it. 
My photographer was one of the original Bibliotheca subscribers, so he
acquired the plus-Apocrypha version of Bibliotheca several months back.  He graciously allowed me to borrow it so that
I could compare the two.

**A HISTORICAL
OVERVIEW.** Historically speaking, I
think both Bibliotheca and the Six-Volume Set are brainchildren of Adam Lewis
Greene, the Bibliotheca mastermind.  Back
in 2014, Greene hypothesized that some niche section of the Bible-buying market
might be interested in a multi-volume Bible with no cross-references, verse
numbers, or even chapter numbers.

He launched a Kickstarter to fund his project and was
quickly proven wrong.  He didn’t raise
$37,000 from backers.  He raised $1.4
million.  In a culture that seems intent
on reverting to a more analog way of life, a multi-volume Bible designed purely
for the tactile experience of reading had tremendous appeal.

Because the project had swelled to such gargantuan
proportions, Greene had many more challenges getting Bibliotheca to production
than he had originally anticipated.  The
appearance of Bibliotheca was delayed for years.  This opened the door for Crossway.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEhF17sddDtpnEpLepdtdTkRYVvOIOyH0UVB15V_SN3c_q-hP3aA6UAPT0u6YqAwhwjz2Qr5yfK2xQmFEI-o0lqjtkPPlM2taUwEnUZXu1DJJH3uEEuwgkiRvGiU65ZRFAVldpkktPFQWg/s640/BEG+Xway+narrative.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEhF17sddDtpnEpLepdtdTkRYVvOIOyH0UVB15V_SN3c_q-hP3aA6UAPT0u6YqAwhwjz2Qr5yfK2xQmFEI-o0lqjtkPPlM2taUwEnUZXu1DJJH3uEEuwgkiRvGiU65ZRFAVldpkktPFQWg/s1600/BEG+Xway+narrative.jpg)



Crossway is, in my opinion, the most innovative Bible
publisher out there.  For the past ten
years or so, they’ve been driving the changes in the Bible market, most notably
with their lineup of journaling Bibles. 
They’re used to making others play catch-up, so I would imagine that it
was with some chagrin that they realized that Greene had seen and exploited a
market niche that they hadn’t.  They
decided that they too needed to be selling a multi-volume Bible, and because
they’re repeat players, they were able to get their set to market at about the
same time as Bibliotheca, even though they started later.

**A BASIC
SIMILARITY.** Because both Bibliotheca
and the Six-Volume Set arose from more or less the same source, they’re much
more alike than they are different.  Both
are the result of questioning the basic assumption that all 66 books of the
Bible should be contained in one volume. 
Once you accept that assumption, it forces all kinds of design
compromises on you, in terms of paper opacity, print size, leading, text column
width, and a host of other factors. 
One-volume Bibles have the form they do because they have to.  Once you abolish the one-volume constraint,
you have freedom to set the text in the most accessible, readable way possible.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEitgg7htzXnCmkqjS-OzXJKYg2CFbAKqyyy2wBQ1ePIJgIi2yMmJT6x-j1DVvywh-EY2if99wT8Mltkzck108HTcp1Gj-yyT78sPl8WjlypGjwaVH70SgbHwTo8rkoYSeoDvp6-6u-wwg/s640/BEG+Biblio+narrative.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEitgg7htzXnCmkqjS-OzXJKYg2CFbAKqyyy2wBQ1ePIJgIi2yMmJT6x-j1DVvywh-EY2if99wT8Mltkzck108HTcp1Gj-yyT78sPl8WjlypGjwaVH70SgbHwTo8rkoYSeoDvp6-6u-wwg/s1600/BEG+Biblio+narrative.jpg)



Also, as observed above, both Bibliotheca and the Six-Volume
Set get rid of the reference apparatus that appears in nearly every
single-volume Bible.  These are no longer
reference books.  They are books to be
read.

This seems like a small difference, but it is anything
but!  During my comparison period (during which I read Joshua and Psalms 1-11 in Bibliotheca and Judges, Ruth, and Psalms 12-23 in the Six-Volume Set), I was
also doing a considerable amount of reading out of the brand-new Holman CSB
Large-Print UltraThin Reference (I’m reading through the whole New Testament in
it to see what I think of the CSB as a translation).  The LPUT is an extremely well designed
reference Bible.  In fact, it’s one of
the best-designed single-volume Bibles I’ve ever used.  However, there’s simply no comparison between
reading the LPUT and Bibliotheca or the Six-Volume Set.

The problem is that all of the chapter numbers, verse
numbers, reference notes, and in-text headings are in the text but are not part
of the text.  As a result, they cannot
help but distract you from the text. 
They’re like static on the radio. 
They make the meaning more difficult or even impossible to grasp.

At one point during my reading of the LPUT, I was lying on
the couch in my living room.  However,
some of the other people in my house started talking—more static.  I couldn’t concentrate enough to follow the
meaning of the text, so I gave up on the LPUT and picked up the Six-Volume Set
instead.  I could follow it even though I
couldn’t follow the LPUT because its setting inherently had less static in it
(and the same would have been true for Bibliotheca).

**CONCLUSION.** The bottom line is this.  If you’re interested in reading the Bible,
and you have the cash to spare, you should buy and read from a multi-volume
Bible.  Trust me on this; during my
Bible-reviewing career, I’ve done daily readings from several dozen
single-volume Bibles, and they’ve generally been good, solidly designed
editions.  However, I don’t think that
any of those Bibles can compete with a multi-volume Bible for ease of
reading.  No matter how well designed,
single-volume constraints and design static drag them all down.  Even the single-volume ESV Reader's Bible, which
doesn’t have the reference apparatus, has lines that are too long and print
that is too small to be ideal.

This makes me wonder, in fact, if the famous inability of
Christians to read the Bible regularly has as much to do with bad Bible design
as it has to do with anything else.  I’ve
been a voracious reader all my life, and I’ve been preaching the gospel for 13
years, but I’d never been able to make a Bible-reading plan stick until a few
years ago, when I put my foot down and said, “I Will Do This.”  I think I had to exercise that level of
willpower because Bibles are harder to read than anything else, not because the
text is harder, but because their design makes reading harder.  It’s a massive spiritual stumbling block that
I never saw until I was presented with a better alternative.

Multi-volume Bibles don’t come cheaply.  So far as I can tell, Bibliotheca is only
available from the Bibliotheca site, bibliotheca.co.  There, the complete clothbound set (including
Apocrypha) sells for $179.  On Crossway’s
site (crossway.org), the Six-Volume Set “retails” for
$200.  However, this is the usual
Bible-publisher markup.  In real life,
you can find the set at evbible.com for $105 (the best price I could find
online at the time of writing, and Evangelical Bible offers free domestic
shipping on orders over $75 too).

For me, at least, that’s a speed bump.  I don’t have even $105 sitting around that I
can casually drop on a Bible.  However,
there are few things available for sale about which I can honestly say, “If you
buy this, it could make an eternal difference in your life.”  I can say that about a multi-volume
Bible.  Plan and purchase accordingly.
