**OVERVIEW.** The Pitt Minion and the Wide-Margin are
very different in size but the same in quality. 
They don’t have the goatskin-and-art-gilt bling of a high-end Cambridge Bible,
but they’re both sturdily and pleasantly constructed.  Though not as expensive as their goatskin
siblings, these are still fairly pricey Bibles. 
Nonetheless, they offer respectable value for money.



\*             \*             \*

These two Bibles came into my hands along very different
paths.  The Pitt Minion was a Christmas
gift from my children; the Wide-Margin Reference was a review copy sent to me
by Cambridge.  Both of them, though, are
examples of Cambridge’s calf-split editions.

**VITAL STATISTICS** (Wide-Margin
characteristics in parentheses where they differ)

**Translation:**  New American Standard Bible, Updated (1995)

**Cover:**  Calf-Split

**Binding:**  Sewn

**Trim Size:**  4 ¾“ x 6 ⅞“ (7 ¼“ x  9“)

**Text Block Width:**  ¾ “ (1 ¼“)

**Weight:**  15 ounces (2 pounds, 10 ounces)

**Column:**  Double

**Layout:**  Paragraphed

**Print Size:**  6.75/7 (7.9/8.2)

**Words of Christ:**  Red

**Cross-References:**  Yes

**Ribbon Marker:**  1 (2)

**SIZE.** Comparing the Pitt Minion to the
Wide-Margin is about like comparing a Chihuahua to a Saint Bernard.  Yes, they’re both dogs, but that similarity
fails to capture how different they are. 
The Pitt Minion is a carry Bible par excellence.  Assuming you can read it, there is no better
Bible for grabbing and going.  If you
can’t fit a Pitt Minion in your glove box, you probably need to clean out your
glove box.

The Wide-Margin, on the other hand, is a great sprawling
space hog of a Bible.  It is easily the
widest Bible I own, passing up even the 2002 wide-margin Foundation Side-Column
Reference by three-quarters of an inch.  Unlike
the Pitt, the Wide-Margin has “HOLY BIBLE” stamped on the front cover.  I suspect that this is so that people won’t
mistake it for a high-end photo album. 
It has that kind of proportion to it. 
Note, though, that it’s not as thick as most other large Bibles, which
means that it actually weighs the same as a Crossway Legacy.

Do not be deceived, though; this is a serious hunk of paper
and calfskin.  I didn’t even bother
trying to fit the Wide-Margin into my glove box.  I might as well try to climb in there
myself.  Unless you’re really committed
to using One Bible For Everything, there is no reason to use the Wide-Margin as
a carry Bible.  For its size, its print
is relatively small, so people who can’t read the Pitt Minion very well might
not be able to manage the Wide-Margin either. 
This is a Bible that exists for one purpose:  staying in one place and accumulating vast
quantities of study notes.

**COVER.** Though they differ vastly in dimension,
the covers of my Pitt Minion and Wide-Margin are the same in substance.  Both of them are constructed of split
calfskin (the bottom layer of a calf’s hide), stamped with a pebble grain and
expertly fashioned into cover form by Jongbloed.

When it comes to Bibles, I tend to prefer editions with
covers somewhere in the genuine-leather/cowhide/calf-split range.  They may not offer the decadence of a
goatskin or top-grain calfskin cover, but they offer something more important to
me:  durability.  When you get right down to it, I’m a
practical man.  I wear a Carhartt winter
coat not because it is the most stylish, but because it is nearly
indestructible.  I drive a Toyota Corolla
not because it is particularly fast or beautiful, but because one day, I hope
to teach my daughter to drive in it.  At
the moment, she’s four.  I like things
that will last.

Experience has proven, though, that covers made of
polyurethane or bonded leather aren’t going to last.  Under hard, prolonged use, they fall
apart.  A calf-split Cambridge Bible,
though, is going to endure.  I see
nothing in the cover of either the Pitt Minion or the Wide-Margin to suggest
that it will fail before the text block it encloses does.

Both of these Bibles have paste-down rather than edge-lined
bindings.  This means that the cover is
held to the text block by endpapers, not by a leather tab.  As one would expect from Cambridge, these
endpapers are made of leatherette, which appears to be sturdy stuff.  It may split down the seam in the middle one
day, but I don’t see that happening anytime soon.

Of course, durability is not the sole measure of an object’s
worth.  Nobody writes paeans to the Toyota
Corolla, and no matter how durable a Bible may be, you won’t use it unless you
love it.  I think that both my Pitt
Minion and my Wide-Margin are lovable.

As every reviewer of a calf-split Cambridge Bible ever has
noted, it is certainly true that Cambridge calf split starts out stiff.  For many Bible geeks, this may conjure up visions
of some of the dreadful plasticized “genuine leather” covers out there, which
start out stiff and remain stiff perpetually. 
Personally, I think that Bible covers like that ought to be reserved for
patching potholes instead.

I am, however, convinced of better things concerning my Pitt
and my Wide-Margin.  For one thing,
though they are stiff, they start off with considerably more softness and
suppleness than Cambridge’s French Morocco Bibles.  Second, Cambridge calf split breaks in
beautifully.  After only a few weeks of
daily use, it starts to lose its rigidity and flexes as you bend the cover
instead.  I anticipate that after
prolonged use, the covers of my two Bibles will become downright yielding.

**TEXT BLOCK.** For both of these Bibles, the text block
is so good it’s almost boring to write about. 
Yes, the text block is sewn.  To
my knowledge, every Cambridge text block is sewn.  Also, both the Pitt Minion and the
Wide-Margin are printed with the grain, so they are cockling-free.  Both of them will have page corners flare up
a little bit with temperature and humidity changes, but it’s hardly a big deal.

**VALUE.** To me, “value” means something different
in the context of a Cambridge Bible than it does in the context of a Crossway
Value Thinline.  Over the past year, I’ve
come to understand that not all text blocks are created equal.  Some settings are better than others, and
even good settings can have their quality compromised by indifferent printing
on indifferent paper.  On the other hand,
you can also have a good setting printed by Jongbloed, in which case the
execution will be superb.

I know that John 3:16 reads the same in the meanest
mass-market poly Bible as it does in a Jongbloed text block, but I’ve looked at
enough Bibles now that I notice the difference in quality.  The print is crisper.  The paper is that perfect shade of
off-white.  The spine of the text block
doesn’t have scoliosis.

Let’s be real here. 
If you like Jongbloed text blocks, and I do, you’re going to have to pay
to get one.  I know from experience that
I trash hardback Bibles.  Over a slightly
longer time frame, I trash poly Bibles. 
The next tier up for Cambridge is calf-split.  A calf-split NASB Wide-Margin Reference
retails for $210.  Even a calf-split NASB
Pitt Minion retails for $115.  Of course,
you can do substantially better than retail by shopping online, but it still
adds up to serious money.  However, if
you want a Jongbloed text block of the NASB in a durable cover, these two
Bibles (along with the mid-sized NASB Clarion) are the cheapest way to
accomplish it.

**CONCLUSION.** Both the Pitt Minion and the Wide-Margin
are extremely well constructed Bibles, with a price to match.  Especially if you’re looking to buy them
together, $325 retail is enough to make most of us think twice.  However, buying them together and using them
together cancels out the massive size disadvantage of the Wide-Margin.  Even with the sticker shock, I think 8/10 is
a fair rating for the set.
