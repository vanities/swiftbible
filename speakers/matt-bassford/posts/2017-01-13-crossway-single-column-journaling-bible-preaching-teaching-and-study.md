**OVERVIEW.** With its combination of light weight,
good paper, and plenty of room to write, the SCJB is a versatile tool for the
preacher.  The long lines cause a little
bit of trouble for verse citation, but even there, Crossway’s design works to
minimize the problem.

As I normally do, I used the SCJB for two weeks’ worth of
work.  During that time, I preached three
sermons and taught four Bible classes.  I
didn’t really get to use it in personal studies because the people I was
studying with during the trial period were unfamiliar with the Bible.  As a result, they and I used matching Bibles
so I could guide them to passages using page numbers.  Regardless, the SCJB got a robust workout.  Here’s what I thought.



**PREACHING.** I seem to be getting into a habit I don’t
like here, of not writing in Bibles that are designed to be written in.  Last time, it was because writing in
Crossway’s Interleaved Journaling Bible seemed like a waste of time.  You have a Whole Page to write on.  If you can’t fit your notes in that, you’re
hopeless.

This time, I elected not to write in the SCJB for a
different reason.  If I switch to the
ESV, this one is going to be my primary Bible. 
Should I make that choice, I will want to start with a pristine Bible,
rather than having the book of 2 Corinthians cluttered up with a sermon from
three years ago.  Thus, I elected to
preach out of my sermon notebook, even for the aforementioned expository sermon
on 2 Corinthians 1-3.

However, I have a pretty good sense of how I would have
liked the SCJB had I written in it.  For
one thing, the SCJB is a single-column rather than a double-column wide-margin
Bible.  This is why Crossway called it
the “Single-Column Journaling Bible.” 
Astonishing, I know.

That’s very important. 
Double-column wide-margin Bibles inevitably go wrong in one of two main
ways.  Some of them don’t have any
writing room in the inner margin at all. 
If you just want to draw pretty pictures in your Bible, I guess that’s
OK, but if you want to write notes next to a particular verse (like I do), that
layout means you’re going to be frustrated half the time.

Even double-column wide-margin Bibles with room in the inner
margin aren’t as good.  Because the paper
curves down into the gutter when the Bible is open, writing in that inner
margin is awkward.  It’s doable; I do it
in my Cambridge NASB Wide-Margin Reference all the time.  However, I’m much happier when I can write in
the outer margin.

A single-column wide-margin Bible, by contrast, has nothing
but outer margin.  You can write next to
every verse without ever writing in the gutter. 
The SCJB allows me to do this, which makes me happy.

I’m similarly happy that the SCJB has lines in that outer
margin.  Without lines, my handwriting
straggles all over the place.  I’d
probably be annoyed by the lines if I wanted to draw or color or paint or
whatever (kind of like drawing on notebook paper in elementary school), but I
don’t, so I am wholly delighted to have them there.

Finally, the SCJB has thick, opaque paper.  It’s not as good as the 38-gsm Tervakowski
ThinOpaque in my Cambridge Wide-Margin, but it appears to be thick enough to
limit bleed-through when I’m writing in it.

However, because I wasn’t writing in the SCJB, I had my
trusty sermon notebook with me all the time. 
This created some awkwardness because the pulpit from which I usually
preach isn’t large enough to have more than my sermon notebook and a Pitt
Minion open side-by-side.  If I’m going
to have the problem with any Bible larger than a Pitt, I’m going to have it
with pretty much every Bible I review. 
As always, the SCJB went up on the corner of the monitor, and everything
was fine.

In times past, I’ve complained about trouble locating verse
numbers in single-column paragraphed Bibles. 
It’s easier to get lost and overlook a verse number in a longer line,
and Crossway Bibles were among the worst offenders here.  I think many of my problems had to do with
the alignment of the verses.  In older
Crossway layouts like the Legacy and the Personal Size Reference, the tops of
the verse numbers were level with the tops of the ascenders.  As a result, they blended in with the text
and looked pretty much like quotation marks. 
I missed them regularly.

However, Crossway has really upped its game here of
late.  In the SCJB, the verse numbers are
true superscript number.  Now the whole
top half of the number protrudes above the height of the ascenders.  Not only does this make the text more readable
(more on that later), it also makes the verse numbers stand out more.  I don’t think I missed a citation in any of
the sermons I preached.

Because I didn’t have anything written in the SCJB, I never
took it out of the pulpit.  However, its
light weight means that I wouldn’t have had any difficulty had I wanted to do
so.

**TEACHING.** Normally, I’m not a big fan of
wide-margin Bibles when I’m teaching an auditorium class.  Any notes that I’m working from are in my
lesson book rather than my Bible, so all the white space in the margins is
meaningless to me, and it adds weight that I don’t want.

However, I thought the SCJB performed well here too.  I don’t take Bibles away from the pulpit when
I’m reading from them, but I will take them from the lectern.  I’m particularly prone to this when I’ve
invited someone else to read a lengthy passage. 
During the reading, I like to take my Bible in hand and drift toward the
reader for some reason.  It may also be
that I’m working through a question about the text with a student and want to
move closer to them as we’re talking.

Regardless, Bible size is important to me when teaching, and
the size of the SCJ doesn’t offend me.  I
carried it around without thinking about it, and that’s exactly what I want
from a Bible.

I don’t remember any times during the class when I was
called on to hunt a passage from cross-references, but the SCJB is a typical
Crossway tweener Bible.  It has
references at the bottom of the page for Old-Testament prophecies cited in the
New Testament and parallel accounts in the Synoptic Gospels.  However, the class I was teaching was on Ezra
and Nehemiah, so neither of those things would have been particularly
useful!  There are times when
cross-references can be helpful, but generally, in a wide-margin Bible, I’d
rather have the saved weight than the references.  If you really care about references, you can
write your own in anyway.

I do seem to recall, though, that I missed one verse
citation in a Bible class.  The moral of
the story may be, then, that even the best-designed single-column paragraphed
Bible doesn’t lend itself to citation as easily as a double-column Bible
will.

**STUDY.** In my office, a different dimension of
Bibles becomes relevant—their height.  I
want to be able to hold a Bible in my hand, or even in my lap, between the
keyboard of my computer and me.  Some
Bibles are so tall that they push me back farther than I want to go.  Not so with the SCJB.  It’s such a stubby thing that it fits right
into the space I naturally leave between the desk and me.

Also, when I have my glasses on, 7.5 Lexicon No. 1 is plenty
big enough for me to read.  The format
wasn’t an issue here either.  I don’t
believe I missed a citation in any of the material that I prepared.

**CONCLUSION.** If you’re a preacher, and you need
reading glasses, the SCJB probably isn’t for you.  Even Lexicon No. 1 can cause issues for the
farsighted if it’s not any bigger than 7.5. 
Other than that, though, the SCJB is a strong candidate for a
ministerial workhorse.  It’s small enough
to be handy, it has lots of room to write, and its clever design minimizes the
citation issues that accompany single-column paragraphed Bibles.  I think the SCJB merits a 9/10 for preaching,
teaching, and study.
