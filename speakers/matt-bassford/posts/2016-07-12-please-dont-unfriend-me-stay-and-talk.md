The online world has gotten increasingly ugly these days,
but one of the ugliest trends of all has been the issuing of belief-based
ultimatums:  “I believe X.  If you can’t support that, unfriend me.”  “I’m going to say Y, and a lot of people are
going to unfollow me.”  “If you won’t
support Candidate Z, I can’t support you.” 
Every time I read one of those messages (or their innumerable
variations), my heart sinks.

We live in a divided nation and a divided world.  Paradoxically, the rise of the Internet,
which was supposed to be a unifying force for the human race, has led to
fragmentation instead.  Instead of
watching the TV shows that everyone watches and reading the newspapers that
everyone reads, I can construct my own virtual world filled with only the
subjects that interest me and the opinions I already agree with.



Similar fragmentation has taken place in more traditional
media too.  Just consider the difference
between people who watched Walter Cronkite’s CBS Evening News decades ago
versus those who watch Fox or MS-NBC (depending on preference) today.  Even the environment on most college
campuses, which are supposed to be strongholds of intellectual inquiry, has
become much more like an ideological echo chamber.

Hearing only things with which we agree is indeed agreeable,
but it has two significant negative consequences.  First, it deprives us of the opportunity for
intellectual growth.  We learn most not
when we are confirmed, but when we are challenged, and those challenges can
only be mounted by those who sincerely disagree with us.  Tearing down a strawman with a group of the
like-minded is nothing like a conversation with someone who truly sees the
world differently.

I learned a long time ago that I can never be as honest
about my writing as an outside editor. 
My own desire for my work to be good gets in the way of my seeing where
it isn’t good.  The same thing is true of
my beliefs and opinions.  I want to be
right, so I believe that I am right. 
It’s much easier for me to see the speck in somebody else’s eye than the
beam in my own.  True intellectual
honesty must involve not merely examination of myself, but an invitation of
critique from others.

The second problem with living in an echo chamber is that it
leads us to react with hostility to precisely the differing viewpoint that we
most need to hear.  We’re much more
annoyed by the sound of a motorcycle at 3 in the morning than we are during the
5 P.M. rush hour.  It becomes more
offensive because of the absence of other sounds like it.

Similarly, if we’re used to an environment of robust,
collegial give-and-take, a differing opinion isn’t a threat.  It’s part of the status quo.  However, if we’ve walled ourselves off from
those who aren’t like us, but a differing opinion sneaks through an unguarded
gate, what should be unremarkable is instead intolerable:  “What an idiot this guy is!  I can’t believe he would say such a thing!”
(Even though millions of others believe as he does.)  “I’m sick of him, and I’m going to block
him!”  Thus we seek to seal up every
crack in our echo chambers, until finally we become as perfect in our peace as
we are in our ignorance.

I think that’s a disaster. 
I want no part of it.  I
desperately need to hear from people who disagree with me.  Even if they don’t change my thinking, they
almost always clarify it.  Beyond that, I
need them to remind me of the basic humanity that we all share, no matter what
our perspectives may be.  It is the
unknown other who gets dehumanized, with predictably terrible consequences.

I have many opinions. 
Some I hold lightly (generally anything having to do with politics);
some are the foundation of my life (generally anything to do with my
faith).  There is probably at least one
person on the planet who disagrees with every single one of my
convictions.

If that’s you, don’t unfriend me.  Stay and talk.  Come to me in good faith, looking for a
fellow sharer of the human condition, not an audience.  Tell me how you see things; explain where you
think I’m wrong.  Assume that I’m doing
my best to be honest; I will strive to grant you that courtesy, whether you
grant it to me or not.

Persuade; don’t rant. 
Talk; don’t shout.  Even if you do
shout, I will try to listen for the truth beneath the anger (though I’ve got a
lot of progress to make in this!).  Maybe
I won’t change my mind, but I will understand you better, and that’s a worthy
goal by itself.

Similarly, if you say something I don’t agree with, and I
engage you, please see that for what I want it to be:  persuasion rather than diatribe, search for
truth rather than defensiveness.  I’m a
pretty good writer, but on the Internet, it’s easy for even good writers to
strike a wrong note.  Be understanding
with mine.

Above all, don’t give up on me.  Even if we can’t come to a meeting of the
minds, that does not keep us from a meeting of the hearts.  An adversary need not be an enemy, and
friendship need not be based on agreement. 
Or, if we must agree, let us agree on this:  that every human being is entitled to a
voice, and the way in which he uses that voice does not make him less human or
less worthy of our warmest affections.

Come; let us reason together.  Let us speak the truth in love.
