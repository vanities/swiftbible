A few weeks ago, a blog post entitled "Dear Church, Here's Why People Are Leaving You" started making the rounds on Facebook.  It pointed to the dwindling number of American churchgoers and identified a number of reasons why.  Essentially these focused on two areas:  emphasis of style over religious substance, and a judgmental, self-righteous attitude toward sinners.  For those who are curious, you can find the article [here](http://www.churchleaders.com/outreach-missions/outreach-missions-articles/244545-dear-church-heres-people-really-leaving.html).

Last Friday, a Facebook friend of mine posted it on my wall and asked for my reactions.  Here they are:

> I'd seen this a couple weeks ago, Shelly, but it's definitely a thought-provoking read! Some of it simply doesn't apply to the Joliet church--our worship services have never been a production, we don't have a super-nice building filled with cool stuff, and we don't get involved in political/cultural debates.

> The idea of being filled with the love of Jesus, though. . . that's a tricky one. Part of the problem is confusion about who Jesus is. People have all kinds of opinions about what Jesus stands for, when they haven't read through Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John even once. Those opinions are likely to say less about Jesus and more about the people holding them!

> In reality, Jesus is a bundle of paradoxes: merciful yet demanding, loving yet angry, simple yet subtle. He invited everyone to heaven, yet had more to say about hell (and in more graphic terms) than any other preacher in the entire Bible. Just trying to figure out Jesus is a lifetime's work, let alone trying to be like Him!

> As for how that plays out in the Joliet church, that's tough. I've seen members do things that were stunningly loving and Christlike, but I've also seen members do things that were stunningly unloving and un-Christlike. Sometimes, these two extremes have come from the same person. People in churches, even the best of them, still do plenty of inconsiderate and downright evil things. That doesn't make us hypocrites. It just makes us people.

> I'm not a Christian because I think I'm better than everybody else. I'm a Christian because I know I'm not. When I compare myself to Jesus, I see all kinds of warts and ugliness. I am driven to Him by my desperate need for forgiveness and grace.

> Here, I think, is where I locate the real problem that many Americans have with churches. They don't seek Jesus because they don't feel the need for grace. They don't come to Him crushed, dejected, humbled, willing to say and do anything in exchange for forgiveness. Instead, they come with their own worldviews and agendas and sacred cows, desiring a relationship with God only if it takes place on their terms. They are not the prodigal son of the gospels. Instead, they are the rich young ruler, driven away when Jesus demands a price higher than they are willing to pay.

> As I've said, I'm a country mile away from perfect, but I do try not to be a barrier between anybody and God. I've baptized strippers, studied with alcoholics, prayed with drug addicts. Sometimes that's worked out well; other times it hasn't. I've never seen, though, someone who was truly seeking who didn't find. The problem has always been with searches that were less than genuine.
