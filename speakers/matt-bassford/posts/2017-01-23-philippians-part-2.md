In
the sermon two weeks ago, I began a whole-book examination of Paul’s letter to
the Philippians.  I didn’t continue it
last week because I didn’t want to bring half a sermon series to Lockport that
evening, but this morning, I’m going to wrap things up.

In
addition to helping us understand the Scripture better, I think sermons like
this are important because they make the lesson bigger than the preacher.  When I write and preach a topical sermon, the
underlying intellect there is mine.  I’m
the one who decides what passages to use and what order I want to present them
in.  As a result, such sermons can never
go deeper than I am.

The
same is not true for expository studies. 
There, the underlying logic isn’t mine. 
It’s the Holy Spirit’s.  Studying
the text makes me see things that I otherwise wouldn’t, which is kind of the
point of Bible study, and even if I miss an important nuance, simply reading
the text out loud gives the congregation the opportunity to spot it.  Sermons like this, then, partake of the
infinite depth of the mind of God, and when I’m preaching, I can feel the
difference.  This morning, then, let’s
take another journey into the depths of revelation as we study the second part
of Philippians.



**Knowing Christ**

Taken
broadly, the first section of our text this morning is about knowing
Christ.  However, this begins with a
discussion of Paul’s **FELLOW
WORKERS.** This appears in Philippians
2:19-30.  This section of the text has
bothered commentators for centuries because it doesn’t seem to fit.  Right before it, you’ve got Paul urging the
Philippians to obey and rejoice.  After
it begins Paul’s discussion of the basis of his righteousness.  In the middle, though, you’ve got Timothy and
Epaphroditus popping up and saying hi.

However,
thematically this makes a great deal of sense. 
Paul doesn’t hope to send these two men to Philippi because they need a
vacation.  Instead, he expects them to be
useful in various ways to the brethren there. 
Paul expects Timothy to be a leader. 
Even though he is surrounded by Christians who have trouble with
self-seeking, he knows that he can count on Timothy to put the Philippians’
good before his own.

Epaphroditus,
though, seems less like a leader and more like a companion.  Apparently, he was originally a member of the
Philippian church himself.  However, he
got terribly sick after traveling to Rome to bring Pual support from the
Philippians, but now he has recovered, and Paul is sending him back to Philippi
so that the Christians there can be encouraged by his presence.

Perhaps
the moral of the story here is that any church needs both kinds of people.  Every congregation needs leaders, people who
can be trusted to look out for the congregation and its members and help them
along.  However, every church needs
companions too, those who draw their brethren closer together through the
relationships they have.  We don’t all
have to be useful in the same way, but every one of us can find some way to be
useful.

As
part of the help that Paul himself is offering to the Philippians, he next
urges them to beware of **CONFIDENCE IN
THE FLESH.** Consider Philippians
3:1-6.  This reading begins with the
already-familiar exhortation to rejoice in the Lord, but this time, the
emphasis seems to be on the Lord part rather than the rejoicing part.  Paul continues to warn the brethren against
those who would lead them to rejoice in themselves, especially the “mutilation”,
by which Paul means Judaizing false teachers. 
They, Paul implies, put their confidence in themselves, which is
contrary to the spirit of Christianity. 
He says this even though he has as much right to be spiritually puffed
up as they do.  In his former life, Paul
had a full collection of Jewish merit badges.

Today,
we aren’t too concerned with Jewish merit badges, but it’s easy for us to get
lost in admiration of our own collection of merit badges.  Just to use myself as an example, here’s my fleshly
resume:  I was raised in the church by
two Christian parents.  On my mother’s
side, I’ve got ancestors who were Christians stretching back to the
1820’s.  All my life, I’ve faithfully
attended services.  I’m a preacher of the
gospel who trained under Max Dawson and David Banning.  I have a wife, two kids, and a good
reputation.

Know
how much all that stuff counts for? 
Nothing!  All those things could
be true of me, and yet I could be bound for hell by the fastest road.  Despite all those things, without the grace
of Jesus in my life, I would be bound for hell by the fastest road!  There is nothing about the flesh that I or
any of the rest of us should be putting confidence in.

Instead,
the true source of our salvation is not ourselves, but our **FAITH IN CHRIST.** Paul
explains in Philippians 3:7-11.  Once
Paul figured out who Jesus was and what he had to do about it, he promptly
round-filed everything about his life that he had previously treasured.  In fact, many of our English translations
don’t capture the strength of Paul’s rejection. 
In v. 8, the ESV says that Paul counted these things “but rubbish”,
which sounds to me like something a snippy English lady would say when she’s
mad.  The actual Greek word there,
though, means something more like “dog feces”. 
This is stuff you would scoop up with a plastic bag and throw away as quickly
as you could because of the depths of your disgust for it!

Why?  Because knowing Jesus and being found in
Jesus is that much better than anything else. 
Only if we are found in Jesus can we have the righteousness that comes
from Him through faith.  Only if we are
found in Jesus can we come to know Him, and for Paul, knowing Jesus can only
come from experiencing what Jesus experienced. 
Paul wants to be united with the suffering and death of Christ because
he knows that the resurrection of Christ waits on the other side.

This,
friends, is one of the most radical representations of what it means to be a
Christian in the entire Bible.  It’s
no-holds-barred, giving up everything that one might value for the sake of
Christ.  What’s more, Paul is holding himself
up as an example for the Philippians to follow.

Momentarily,
we’re going to be partaking of the Lord’s Supper.  As we do so, let’s remember the no-holds-barred
nature of the cross.  If Jesus had it, He
gave it, and He gave it for us.  Let’s
ask too, though, if we’re living for Jesus the way He died for us.  What’s Christianity to us?  Is it a nice religious accessory, so that we
come here on Sundays to get God off our backs for the rest of the week?  Or instead, is it the central, consuming
passion of our lives?  It certainly was
for Paul.  Let’s ponder this as we
partake.

**The Upward Call**

In
the second main section of our reading this morning, Paul considers the upward
call of Christ and what it looks like in practice.  This starts with the importance of **PRESSING ON.** Paul elaborates in Philippians
3:12-16.  In some ways, this is a
depressing section of Scripture.  The
subject here is the climax of the last reading: 
the resurrection of the dead. 
Paul has already told us about all the things he gave up to gain the
resurrection.  He’s told us about all
he’s suffered.

However,
Paul says, despite everything he’s done for Christ, he knows that he has not
attained to the resurrection yet, so eternal life for him is by no means
assured.  It’s not enough that he’s gone
all-out for the Lord.  He is going to
have to continue going all-out.  The
whole rest of his life is going to have to be about seeking the prize of the
upward call of God in Christ Jesus. 
What’s more, he tells all of us that this is the way we have to think
and live going forward too!

This
is a warning, brethren, that every one of us needs to listen to.  The good news is that in Christ, we have no
history.  The bad news is that in Christ,
we have no history.  We start off every
morning with a blank score card, and the only thing that matters is what we do
next.  We can’t coast on who our friends
and relatives are, and we can’t even coast on what our past spiritual behavior
has been.  If we used to be pillars of
the church, but now we’ve become more like pillows, that’s a problem!  Each one of us must constantly ask, “How am I
growing in service to God today?”

If
we don’t constantly ask this, we’ll end up like the **ENEMIES OF THE CROSS.** Here,
let’s read from Philippians 3:17-4:1. 
There are two groups of people Paul wants us to watch.  First, he wants us to look at him and those
who are walking according to the pattern he has set out.  Second, though, he wants us to watch those
who used to be part of us but now are following their lusts right down the road
to destruction.  Paul’s not OK with
this.  He tells us that he’s weeping
because these people have chosen to be enemies of Christ, and I know just how
he feels.

In
the time since I’ve been here, there have been a lot of Christians who have
fallen away.  A lot.  This is not pleasant for us to think about,
but we have to think about it.  If we
don’t, we only ensure that we are going to end up just like them.  I baptized many of those Christians
myself.  I know that when they obeyed the
gospel, not one of them thought they were going to abandon the faith.  However, the devil picked his spots, and he
got all of them.

TV
preachers like to tell us that God has a plan for us.  I believe that’s true, though I don’t mean it
in the way they do.  What’s just as
certain, though, is that the devil has a plan for us.  He knows where our weak spots are, just like
he knew for all of those Christians he got. 
Unless we are vigilant, unless we are determined to seek Christ with
everything we have, he will get us too. 
We need to remember that our citizenship is in heaven, not here, and we
need to live like it.

At
the close of this section, we see Paul handing out some **MORAL INSTRUCTION.** Look at
Philippians 4:2-9.  These are things that
all of us have to work on if we want to gain the prize.  The first of them is harmony with our
brethren.  Euodia and Syntyche have
become famous as examples of Christians who couldn’t get along, but there have
been plenty of others who have run into the same problem.  Our relationships with one another are
supposed to be a foretaste of heaven, not a harbinger of hell!  If we give free rein to strife, bitterness,
and resentment in our hearts, those things will surely consume our souls.

Second,
we’ve got to seek not only outward peace, but inward peace.  In our society, all of us have far more
material security than did people in Paul’s day, and yet anxiety is no less a
problem for us than it was for them.  I
think this comes from the conviction that we’re the ones who are in control of
everything, so we need to do everything right in order for our lives to work
out right.  In reality, the most
important thing we can do is pray.  God
has never forsaken any of His people, and He’s not going to forsake us
either.  If we put the future in His
hands, everything will be all right.

Finally,
Paul encourages us to keep our mind set on godly things.  This is a particular challenge for us when so
many of the entertainment options today are so wicked.  We need to be honest with ourselves about
what we’re doing.  Can we say of our
favorite TV shows that they are honorable and right and pure and lovely?  Are our most-frequented Internet sites filled
with things that are excellent and morally praiseworthy?  How about the movies we watch or the books we
read?  If we can’t answer those questions
yes, brethren, we are building a highway in our hearts for the devil.  There are so many good and righteous things
we could dwell on.  Let’s think about
those.

**Sharing**

Finally,
though, let’s leave Philippians with a discussion of one of the first topics
the book introduces:  the topic of
sharing.  To understand what Paul says
about that, though, we first have to look at **PAUL’S ATTITUDE** toward the support that he gets, whether from the
Philippians or from anybody.  He explains
in Philippians 4:10-14.  The first thing
we see here is that Paul is rejoicing because the Philippians have reached the
point where they’re able to start sending him money again.  However, he hastens to explain, that’s not
because the money has made some massive difference in his life.  He reveals that no matter what his financial
situation may be, whether he’s prospering or he literally doesn’t have enough
money to buy food to eat, his contentment doesn’t change.  Why? 
Because the Lord strengthens him so that he can endure any deprivation.

In
this passage, it’s certainly true that the most familiar verse to us is
4:13.  All of us have seen “I can do all things
through Christ who strengthens me” written in flowing script along the bottoms
of inspirational calendars and photos of sunsets.  Back when Lauren and I were in childbirth
class, the instructor hinted that this would be a good verse for women in labor
to repeat.

However,
as tempting as all those other applications are, I think this is one that we
need to confront in its original context. 
Paul is telling us that in addition to all the other things that he has
said don’t matter, the amount of money that we have in our lives doesn’t
matter.  This runs head-on into the
worldly wisdom of our materialistic culture, and I’m afraid that a lot of
Christians buy the lie and make their lives about money and stuff instead of
Jesus.  In reality, money, whether we’ve
got it or not, can never be more than a distraction.  What matters is the strength we get from
Christ.

Despite
this, Paul goes on to explain why money matters when it comes to **GIVING AND RECEIVING.** Consider Philippians 4:15-23.  Paul is glad that the Corinthians are sending
him support, but he’s mainly glad for them, not him.  It is the Philippians, not Paul, who are
going to benefit the most from their gift. 
It’s true that Paul now has the money he needs to provide for himself,
but the Philippians have laid up treasure in heaven, offered an acceptable
sacrifice to God, and ensured that God will supply their needs as they have
supplied Paul’s.

I
don’t think about it so much these days, but back when I was younger and trying
to figure out how to handle money, I was very interested in personal
finance.  I spent a lot of time weighing
the pros and cons of various investments and deciding how to plan for the
future.  What Paul is telling us here,
though, is that the very best investment we can make, the very best use for our
money and stuff, is to use it for the Lord’s work.  Like the unrighteous steward of Luke 16, our
possessions are only under our control for a limited time, and the wisest thing
we can do with them is to make sure that we gain some lasting benefit from
them.

That
automatically rules out any earthly investment. 
There are a lot of smart guys in the finance industry, but not a one of
them has figured out how to store up our money so that we can get it after we
pass on.  Paradoxically, the only
treasure we keep is the treasure we give away. 
If that’s not the first investment we’re making, we’re investing
foolishly.
