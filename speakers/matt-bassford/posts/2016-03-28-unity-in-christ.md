There are a lot of ways in which the first-century church had a different experience than the church today does.  In everything from persecution to the Internet to indoor plumbing, we inhabit a different world than they did.  However, I think it’s fair to say that the biggest problem that churches had in the first century is still the biggest problem that they have now:  the problem of strife among brethren.  There’s more written about contentiousness in the epistles than nearly any other spiritual topic, and the gloomy record of church splits reveals that we struggle with the issue.

However, not all early churches had unity problems.  In particular, even though the first church of all, the church in Jerusalem, had all sorts of internal and external difficulties, they fought through those things together.  Let’s see what we can learn from them about unity in Christ.

**Caring for the Needy**

Our text this morning will come from the fourth and fifth chapters of Acts, and its first main subdivision concerns caring for the needy.  Before the story takes a darker turn, it begins by emphasizing the way that the church was UNITED IN GENEROSITY.  Let’s read here from Acts 4:32-35.  Unity is all over this text.  These were brethren who were of one heart and soul.  They had their possessions in common.  All the homeowners and landowners sold their real estate to support the needy.  All the needy were sustained in this way.  Finally, they all shared in God’s grace.

This great outpouring of generosity from everybody was necessary because of the unusual situation of the Jerusalem church.  Remember that the church was established on the day of Pentecost, a time when Jews from all over the known world had returned to Jerusalem.  Some of those Jews obeyed the gospel and stayed in Jerusalem, not returning to their homes and jobs.  Naturally, those Christians quickly ran through their money, and many of them began to need help even with money for food.  It’s in this crisis situation that the others took such dramatic measures.

From this, I think we can learn that unity rises to the occasion.  Today, all of us belong to a church near where we live and work.  As a result, we don’t have a massive population of Christians who need financial help from their brethren.  It wouldn’t make sense for us to have all things in common because there’s no need for us to do that.  However, if the need did arise, if we are truly of one heart and soul, we should be willing to go to the same extremes as the Jerusalem church did.

However, the story of ANANIAS reveals that not everyone had bought in completely.  Let’s keep reading, in Acts 4:36-5:6.  I’ve heard it said that hypocrisy is the tribute that vice pays to virtue, and that certainly seems to be the case here.  It seems like Ananias and Sapphira are inspired by the example of Barnabas, who sold some property, turned around, and laid the entire value of the property at the apostles’ feet.  However, Barnabas’ example puts Ananias and Sapphira in a bind.  On the one hand, they like the recognition that Barnabas got for his selflessness.  On the other hand, though, they like money too, and can’t bear to part with the whole value of their property.

As a result, here’s the scheme they come up with.  They sell their property, but they only bring part of the value of the property to the apostles.  That’s OK.  As Peter says, it’s their money.  They have the right to contribute all of it, part of it, or none of it.  What’s not OK, though, is that Ananias insists that the portion that he brought was the whole value of the property.  He lies about it.  As Peter observes, he’s really lying not to men, but to God, and God strikes him down for it.

Even today, lying to God never works.  That’s the problem with hypocrisy.  As I’m looking out at the congregation this morning, I don’t look at anybody and say, “Yeah; I know he’s up to no good.”  I don’t have a fleet of drones following everybody around and nosing into their business.  You can fool me.  However, none of us can fool God.  He knows every secret that every one of us has.  Our hypocrisy might win the respect of men here, but it can only win condemnation on the Day of Judgment.

This senseless tragedy is repeated with the arrival of Ananias’s wife SAPPHIRA.  The story continues in Acts 5:7-11.  She’s colluded with Ananias, so several hours later, she comes trotting in, unaware that her husband is dead, and tells the same lie.  Sadly for her, the same lie leads to the same result.  When it comes to striking down hypocrites, God is no respecter of persons.

It seems to me, though, that the instigator here is not Sapphira, but Ananias.  She knew what he was up to, and she even supported him in his scheme, but it was his idea in the first place.  Nonetheless, even though she wasn’t the ringleader, she still paid the same price.

The moral of the story is clear:  don’t follow family into evil.  I hear people say all the time, “Family first.”  Well, no.  God first.  Family second.  I love my earthly family, but I care a whole lot more about the approval of my Creator than I do about their approval.  I respect my earthly father, but the purpose of my existence is to honor my heavenly Father.  Following your family into a church that doesn’t teach the truth is a disastrous mistake.  Allowing a family member to lead you into sin will doom them and you both.  Refusing to obey the gospel because some loved one hasn’t will only make sure that you share their fate.  Sadly, untold millions of people care more about their families than they do about God, and one day, they will pay the price.  Let’s make sure we don’t.

**The Council, Again**

The next section of the narrative is about the apostles appearing again before the Jewish high council.  However, this begins with a very public display of GOD’S POWER.  Here, let’s read from Acts 5:12-16.  As I read this text, I can’t help but remember the Sesame Street song about how one of these things is not like the others.  There’s an awful lot of out-in-the-open stuff going on in this passage.  The apostles are working all sorts of signs and wonders.  The church is meeting on the temple grounds.  The sick are lining the streets in the hopes that Peter’s power will heal them.  The word about the apostles is traveling throughout the whole region around Jerusalem.

However, the text tells us that even though the Jewish people have a favorable opinion of these early disciples, they don’t dare to associate with them.  This is our one thing that is not like the others.  All these Christians are out in public glorifying God, but everybody else is afraid even to be seen with them.  In other words, the whole Jewish nation knows that the church is on the chief priests’ bad list, but rather than going to ground, the church continues to be defiantly open.

The point, I think, is that the gospel is not for the shadows.  Our brethren 2000 years ago made no bones about what they believed, even when they were under the threat of looming persecution.  Today, even though our situation is much less dangerous, we’re often tempted to tiptoe around when it comes to matters of faith.  Can’t let anybody else find out I’m a Christian; something bad might happen!  To be honest, I don’t think the apostles would have been terribly impressed with that attitude.  We serve a God who is worth glorifying.  Let’s glorify Him daily.

Next, we see THE APOSTLES ARRESTED.  The story continues in Acts 5:17-26.  Here, just like the Jewish people were expecting, the other shoe drops.  The chief priests simply can’t tolerate any competitors for religious dominance, so they grab the apostles and toss them in the slammer.  An angel releases the apostles, so they go right back to preaching.  Much to their alarm, the bad guys discover that the apostles have escaped, so they go ask them, nicely this time, to come to the meeting of the Sanhedrin.  As with Jesus, a public arrest might have caused a riot.

I can’t help but be impressed with the dedication of the apostles here.  Even though they knew that they had just been arrested for preaching Christ, once the angel releases them to go back to preaching, that’s exactly what they do.  They take what God had given them and use it for Him.

Although none of us are likely to have an angel release us from prison, this is still a principle we need to keep in mind.  Whenever God gives us a gift, we should look to use that gift for Him.  If we’re good at teaching, we should be using that ability to teach others about the Lord.

The same is true of a whole bunch of other talents that we might possess.  If our job or our business is going well and we’re prospering financially, we need to make sure that we’re giving as generously to the Lord as He has given to us.  If we’ve reached the point in our lives where we’re able to retire so that we have lots of free time on our hands, great!  Let’s look for a way to use all that free time to glorify God.  Just like the angel put the apostles outside the prison for a reason, God has blessed us for a reason too.  We need to put those blessings to work for Him.

Finally in this section, we see the apostles UNITED IN SPEAKING OUT.  This appears in Acts 5:27-32.  They appear before the Sanhedrin, which predictably points out that even though they’ve been warned not to preach Jesus anymore, they’ve continued to do so.  Last time, the Council didn’t have a rule that the apostles were breaking.  This time, they do.

However, with one voice the apostles declare that if they’re going to obey anybody, they’re going to obey God.  It is still true that the Jesus whom the chief priests crucified is the Christ, the Son of God.  It is still true that He rose from the dead.  It is still true that forgiveness is only available through Him.  If anybody doubts the apostles, they need to consider the apostles’ miracles.

I think this text is an illustration of the iron-sharpening effect of good people on good people.  It’s hard to speak out for Christ when you feel alone when you’re doing it.  It’s a whole lot easier to speak out when you know some brethren are going to have your back.

Obviously, most of us don’t spend our weeks around groups of Christians.  However, it’s still true that we need to have one another’s backs.  We need to live godly lives ourselves, so that our bad reputation in the community doesn’t undermine our brother’s efforts to reach the lost.  We need to welcome visitors, particularly when another Christian has made the effort to bring them here.  If we serve in the assembly, we need to make sure that our efforts are as good as we can make them.  When an outsider obeys the gospel, we need to work to bring them into our lives too, so that the one who led them to the Lord isn’t the only one trying to keep them from falling away.

In just a few moments here, we’re going to be partaking of the Lord’s Supper, but before we do, let’s refocus our attention on Peter’s thumbnail sketch of Jesus. Even though He was innocent, He died because we were guilty.  Today, He has ascended to the right hand of the Father in heaven, where He always intercedes for us.  To put things in the terms that we’ve been using, Jesus has our back, period.  He has given us everything we need for this life and the life to come.  We can count on Jesus.  Can He count on us?  Let’s remember Him and examine ourselves as we partake.

**The Trial’s Aftermath**

Finally this morning, let’s consider the trial’s aftermath.  This begins with the ADVICE  given by Gamaliel.  Here, let’s read from Acts 5:33-39.  The first thing that this text establishes is that the apostles took a very real risk by speaking up.  They didn’t get applause and flowers from the chief priests for their boldness.  Instead, the Jewish leaders intended to have them killed.

However, Gamaliel, one of the most respected members of the Council, stops the lynch mob before it really gets started.  He makes a simple argument.  Either the proclamation of Jesus as the Christ is from men, or it is from God.  History has shown that if it is from men, it’s not going to last.  If Jesus is just another nutcase revolutionary, His followers will soon be dispersed.  However, if Jesus truly is the Son of God, and the Sanhedrin opposes Him anyway, that’s not going to go well.  It’s best for the Council to wait and let God decide what to do with the apostles.

Even though 2000 years have passed since Gamaliel spoke these words, his point remains.  God’s will is always going to triumph.  This is still true today.  Like many Christians, I can’t say that I’m pleased with the direction that our country is headed.  It looks like the forces of evil have the strong hand now.  Doesn’t matter.  God is still going to accomplish His purpose.

Maybe there will be a dramatic revival of faith in this place 100 years from now.  Maybe God will destroy our nation as He has destroyed so many wicked nations before it.  Even that will not mean a defeat for Him.  The gospel is bigger than the United States, and the gospel is not going to be overthrown.  Only those who set themselves against it will be.

The last thing that we see in this context is that despite their narrow escape, the apostles remain UNITED IN DILIGENCE.  This appears in Acts 5:40-42.  At first glance, this looks like a good news/bad news situation for the apostles.  The good news is that they don’t get murdered.  The bad news is that they get publicly whipped.

However, from the apostles’ perspective, even the bad news is good news.  The way that Luke puts this is particularly interesting.  He says that the apostles were rejoicing because they had been considered worthy to suffer shame for His name.  In other words, what looked in earthly terms like public humiliation was actually a divine endorsement in spiritual terms.  Only somebody who was following closely in the footsteps of Christ would get that kind of pushback from the enemies of Christ.  The suffering of Jesus was the result of His righteousness.  So too for the apostles.

We see, then, that rather than being discouraged by such clear evidence of governmental opposition, the apostles are heartened.  Together, they shrug off the scourging and get back to preaching and teaching, every bit as openly as they had before.

This shows the importance of other Christians in our lives when the spiritual going gets tough.  All of us are going to face setbacks and opposition in our walk with Christ.  If we don’t, we’re not actually walking with Him.  Even though He is always with us, it sure helps us to keep going when we’ve got brothers and sisters who also are going through it with us.

For a counter-example, consider the prophet Elijah.  He had buckets of faith and willpower, but when he felt like he was alone after his stunning victory on Mt. Carmel, he gave up and asked God to kill him.  In reassuring him, God pointed out that there were still 7,000 left in Israel who hadn’t bowed the knee to Baal, whether Elijah knew it or not.  Even the strongest Christian needs help from others.  We need to be willing to accept that help, and we also need to be willing to offer it.  That way, both we and those we rely on can continue to serve God together.
