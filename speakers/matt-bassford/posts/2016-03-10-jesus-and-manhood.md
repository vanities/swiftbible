There are many things about modern American society that we
take for granted, but that would baffle people from many other cultures.  One such thing is the American presentation
of masculinity and manhood.  It seems to
be derived from beer commercials:  to be
a man is to party hard, chase girls, and fight to prove your honor.  A man who does these things is a Real
Man.



Even though physical strength is quite evident in this
portrait, moral strength is not. 
Self-discipline and self-control are increasingly considered to be
feminine virtues, not masculine ones. 
Increasingly, it is women who go to college, not men.  It is women who are stereotypically
religious, not men (and men who are religious are milquetoast Ned Flanders
types).  It is Sitcom Mom who is the
anchor for her family, not Sitcom Dad.  Sitcom
Dad is an unreliable, immature lightweight. 
Sitcom Mom has character.

Not surprisingly, American manhood has become an institution
in crisis.  Too many men are interested
in sex but not fatherhood, pornography rather than building their relationships
with their wives, the cheap triumphs of the video-game world instead of the
hard work of reality, and spiritual neglect rather than spiritual
leadership.  They’re man enough to go out
of control but not man enough to be in control.

Unless we want to perpetuate this poisonous viewpoint, it’s
past time for us to start taking our vision of masculinity from someplace other
than an increasingly dysfunctional culture. 
Jesus would be a good place to start. 
Because so many Americans view religious devotion as a feminine trait,
the popular conception of Jesus has also become feminized.  Most people think of Jesus as a languid
hippie in a bathrobe, someone who was always peaceful and loving and nice and
kind and sweet.  It’s a wonder somebody
like that had enough testosterone to grow a beard!

By contrast, even though the Biblical portrait of Jesus
doesn’t have much in common with beer commercials, it is unmistakably,
compellingly masculine.  Jesus wasn’t
merely the child of God.  He was the SON
of God, and He acted like it.

The Jesus of Scripture is fearless, tough, determined,
demanding, and awesomely self-disciplined. 
He spent His whole ministry with His face turned toward the cross, and
He never flinched once.   Jesus was
nothing like the directionless, self-indulgent adult males so prevalent
today.  He knew what He wanted, and He
gave His life to get it.

Of course, any portrait of Jesus must include His love and
compassion too, but the Bible presents even those things to us in a masculine
context.  Jesus didn’t express His love
through soothing words of affirmation. 
He showed it by surrendering His body to a violent, bloody, painful
death.  The love of Christ has a high steel
content.

More than anything else, this is what our society and our
churches need:  men who are willing to
step up and honor the Biblical vision of manhood.  The Lord’s church has a continuing leadership
crisis because far too few Christian men have the determination necessary to
forge themselves into leaders.  They
aren’t willing to sacrifice worldly distraction on the altar of the cross.  They aren’t willing to go to war against
secret sin.  They aren’t willing to speak
truth to outsiders who might not want to hear it.  They aren’t willing to imitate the
uncompromising example of Jesus and His first disciples.

The Lord’s church in America has survived many things, and
it has the potential to survive many more. 
As the first century showed, no external threat can destroy the temple
that Christ built, 2000 years ago or today. 
However, the church cannot survive the failure of its men to rise up and
be men.  Where there are no disciples,
there can be no church, and where there is no self-control, there can be no
disciples.
