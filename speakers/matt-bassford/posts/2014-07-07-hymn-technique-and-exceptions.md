Every technical error in the writing of a hymn can be
justified by reference to a good or even a great hymn.  Let me repeat:  every technical error in the writing of a
hymn can be justified by reference to a good or even great hymn.

Try it and see.



Broken meter?  “Holy,
Holy Holy”

Weak rhyme?  “When I
Survey the Wondrous Cross”

Inconsistent rhythm? 
“I’m Not Ashamed to Own My Lord”

Clichéd expression? 
“Victory in Jesus”

Poor phrasing?  “My
Lord, My Truth, My Way”

Slangy tone?  “This
World Is Not My Home”

Awkward sentence structure? 
“The Spacious Firmament”

And so on.  Indeed,
several more examples could be supplied for each of the above traits.  From this, there are two conclusions that we
could draw.  The first is that technique
doesn’t matter.  We might as well write
using broken meter, weak rhyme, inconsistent rhythm, clichéd expression, poor
phrasing, slangy tone, and awkward sentence structure, because they make no
difference to the utility and even use of a hymn.

Experientially, though, this position cannot be
supported.  We are familiar with, say, a
hundred hymns from the eighteenth century. 
However, those hundred hymns don’t make up even one percent of the body
of hymns written in the eighteenth century. 
They barely make up one percent of the hymns written by Charles
Wesley!

Of Wesley’s prodigious input, thousands and thousands of
hymns, we sing perhaps a dozen, and there is a difference between the dozen we
sing and the thousands we do not.  It
becomes apparent upon inspection of the hundred-odd Wesley hymns on the Cyber
Hymnal.  It becomes extremely apparent
upon perusal of the seven or eight hundred hymns in a volume of Wesley’s
collected works.

In technical terms, the dozen Wesley hymns we sing are good
to excellent.  Likewise, in technical
terms, the thousands of Wesley hymns we do not sing are mediocre to frankly
bad.  Content isn’t the problem.  Wesley always has something to say.  However, his expression of those ideas is
usually poor and frequently unreadable.

As a rule, Wesley did not write with good technique.  We don’t sing his hymns that conform to this
rule.  We sing the exceptions.  Once every few years, Wesley happened to
write a hymn that coincidentally followed the norms of technique, and those were
his hymns that survive.  Of course, even
those hymns do not have flawless technique. 
No hymn does.  However, their
technique is strong enough that they do not suffer by comparison to other
enduring hymns.

If hymnists today do not emphasize good technique, the very
best they can hope for is success at the level of Wesley.  If they write 8000 hymns, 10 of them will
endure, a hit rate of .125 percent, which generates one successful hymn for
every 800 attempts.  At the rate of
writing one new hymn every week (which is an extremely high level of output),
it would take more than 15 years of constant writing to produce one good hymn,
a hymn which would almost certainly mirror the norms of good technique anyway.

Maybe you want to spend 15 years writing 799 failed hymns to
produce one success.  I don’t.

Alternatively, rather than citing exceptions to good
technique to justify our refusal to “follow the rules”, we can recognize those
exceptions for what they are: 
exceptions.  Yes, “When I Survey”
does rhyme “God” or “Lord” (depending on version) with “blood”.  It’s a weak rhyme.  However, every other rhyme in the four
commonly used verses of “When I Survey” is a strong, perfect rhyme.

We don’t sing “When I Survey” for the weak rhyme.  We sing it despite the weak rhyme, because
the technique throughout the rest of the hymn is so good.  Other hymns from that era that use primarily
weak rhymes are not sung.

The same is true of older hymns with primarily broken meter,
or primarily inconsistent rhythm, and so forth. 
We don’t sing them either.  The
norms of technique are norms precisely because they are normal in good
hymns.  After about a hundred years, only
the good hymns survive, so all of the older hymns we sing are technically
sound.

Of course, the same is not true in newer hymns.  Technical problems abound in contemporary
and, to a lesser extent, radio-era hymns. 
However, we don’t sing those hymns because they are necessarily good.  We sing them because they are culturally
resonant.  As our culture progresses,
modern hymns without strong technique will drop out of the repertoire.

This is already happening with the Stamps-Baxter hymns of
almost a hundred years ago.  It is no
longer true, as it was in the 1950’s, that a hymn with hokey lyrics will be
sung because of a rhythmically interesting chorus.  We’ve moved past that (into a whole new set
of lyrical and musical excesses). 
Because we don’t identify with the radio era any more, hymns that appear
to have been assembled, like Mr. Potato Head, from a bucket of clichés
(“crystal sea”, “home o’er the foam”, “He saved my soul/He made me whole”,
etc.) have been exposed as the mediocrities they always were.

We easily see the mediocre in hymns that are no longer
modern.  Few indeed are the Christians
today who will rise up to defend “He Bore It All” (and pretty much all of them
are drawing Social Security checks).  However,
our cultural blinders conceal the fact that most trendy hymns are mediocre too.

It is impossible to predict what style of hymn will be in
favor a hundred years from now, or even if there will still be sizable numbers
of Christians worshiping in the English language a hundred years from now.  Attention to technique does not guarantee
that a hymn will endure.  However, if the
past is any guide, inattention to technique may well guarantee that a hymn will
not.
