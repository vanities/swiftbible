If you pay attention to the news at all, and maybe even if
you don’t, you’ve heard the recent hubbub about President Trump’s decision to
restrict immigration from several largely Muslim countries.  This issue has caused a lot of online
controversy, even among brethren, and the exchanges between different points of
view have not always been gracious and loving

Personally, I find the issue troubling, particularly because
there isn’t a straightforward Biblical principle to apply to it.  Yes, Christians are supposed to be charitable
and kind, but our country isn’t even a person, let alone a disciple of
Jesus.  Taking texts directed to
individuals and applying them to nations strikes me as a perilous exercise.  That’s not what the Bible’s for.

However, even if we are uncertain about what God’s word says
about national policy, we should be in no doubt about what it says about
individual action.  The Scripture speaks
with one voice about how we should treat our brother, our neighbor, strangers,
and even enemies.  Given the current
emotional climate in our country right now, I think this is an excellent time
to revisit these ancient truths.  Let’s
consider what the Bible says about the second commandment.

**Defining It**

Before we can talk about how this commandment calls us to
behave, we must first define it.  This
definition has to begin with **JESUS’ TAKE**on it.  This appears most famously in
Matthew 22:34-40.  When I refer to the
second commandment, of course, I’m not talking about the second of the Ten
Commandments in Exodus.  I’m talking
about this second commandment:  the
commandment to love our neighbor as ourselves, which stands below only the
commandment to love God.  According to
Jesus, this one is more important than any of the Ten Commandments, and by this
statement, he puts it at the heart of Christianity’s moral code too.  If our lives are not defined by our love for
our neighbor, we need to seriously re-evaluate our discipleship.

In addition, Jesus tells us that the Law and the Prophets
depend on these commandments.  I really
like the old King James rendering here, where it tells us that the Law and the
Prophets hang on these things.  That’s
actually there in the word “depend”, which originally meant that something hung
down like a pendant, but the connection is more subtle in modern English.

The image here, then, is that the commandment to love is
like a peg, and all the other commandments are like a bunch of purses hung on
that peg.  If you remove the peg,
everything else is going to fall to the ground. 
That’s how central loving our neighbor is to our faith.

We learn more about this relationship in **PAUL’S EXPLANATION** of it.  We find that in Romans 13:8-10.  All the other commandments, at least the ones
that govern our conduct toward one another, exist because they are expressions
of the commandment to love.  They are how
you put “Love your neighbor as yourself” into practice.  If we do not understand this, we literally do
not understand how to be a Christian. 
Without love of our neighbor as the motivating force, the entire system
of Christian ethics collapses and becomes meaningless.

We have a marvelous illustration of how this plays out in
practice in the Pharisees.  They knew the
Law of Moses, but they had forgotten that the Law too was supposed to be an
expression of love.  In their hands, it
became an instrument of hypocrisy rather than righteousness.  They established their religious standing
through public acts of piety and made a big deal about the strictness of the
standard they followed, but in practice, they eroded that standard with so many
loopholes that they could do whatever they wanted.  The same people who so carefully gave God 10
percent of their garden herbs saw nothing wrong with throwing a widow out of
her home.

Brethren, this could be us. 
If we forget that love of others is at the heart of Christianity, we
will soon turn it into a vehicle for love of self.  We too will become hypocritical and arrogant,
priding ourselves on our religion while in reality behaving as wickedly as the
worst sinner out there.

However, if we do adopt love of our neighbor as our guiding
light, it will have certain **EFFECTS** on
the way we behave.  Consider here John’s
words in 1 John 4:16-18.  Normally, we think
of love as opposed to hatred, and there are passages in the Bible that make
that comparison.  However, in this
passage, John argues that at least an opposite of love is fear.

I know it’s kind of a duh moment when a preacher says, “I
think the word of God is insightful,” but friends, this really is
insightful.  The older I get, the more I
appreciate how often fear leads to sin. 
More times than not, people don’t do evil to other people because they
hate them.  Instead, they do evil because
they’re afraid.  The embezzler steals
from their employer because they’re afraid of not making a mortgage payment and
losing their house.  The liar lies
because they’re afraid their friend will be angry if they tell the truth.  For that matter, Christians are too often
silent about the gospel because they’re afraid of the conversation and its
consequences.  Once we start acting out
of fear, we will just about always end up failing to love.

This means, then, that whenever we begin to feel afraid, we
have got to start paying close attention to our thoughts and our words and our
actions.  The devil is going to try to
use that fear to keep us from being guided by love.  If our fear guides our conduct, the devil
wins.

Next, let’s consider what James says about **THE IMPORTANCE** of loving others.  Look at James 2:8-13.  For some reason, we have a tendency to rank
sins, and to act like some of those sins are less dangerous than the
others.  Murder, for instance, is a Real
Bad Sin.  Adultery is a Real Bad
Sin.  However, being partial to ourselves
and failing to love our neighbor, well, everybody does that!  It’s not a Real Bad Sin, right?

James begs to differ. 
He urges us to understand that it’s all part of the same law.  The same God who said “Don’t murder,” and
“Don’t commit adultery,” also said “Love your neighbor as yourself.”  Refusing to do any of those things doesn’t
make you a breaker of that commandment only. 
It makes you a breaker of the law, period.

At this point, I think all of us start shrinking down a
little bit.  None of us want the awesome
majesty of the justice of God coming down on our heads, and so we start crying
out, “Be merciful to me, the sinner!”  At
that point, God answers, “Mercy?  You
mean like the mercy you didn’t show the neighbor you didn’t love?”  Brethren, there are whole parables that make
the point that we don’t want God asking us that question!  If we get anything right, we have to get love
right.

**Practicing It**

Now that we have a good theoretical understanding of the
second commandment, let’s take a look at practicing it.  This starts with **DOING GOOD TO ALL.** Paul
raises this subject in Galatians 6:9-10. 
Let’s frame our discussion of this text by talking about what it doesn’t
say.  First of all, this passage does not
justify spending church funds to supply the needs of the world’s poor.  I have two problems with that reading.  This is a text that is addressed to
individuals, not churches, so it concerns individual action, not church
action.  Also, I think even making that
argument is an attempt to avoid individual responsibility for doing good by
pinning that responsibility on the church. 
If the church does it, than I don’t have to, right?  Brethren, we should be very wary of attempts
to solve our spiritual problems by making them the church’s problem instead!

Second, this passage does not read, “Do good to the
household of faith.”  It says, “Do good
to all.”  Yes, that especially applies to
the household of faith, to Christians whom we know and love who are fine
upstanding people but need some money, or maybe meals for a week or help
loading a moving van.  We should
especially help those people, but we should also help all the other people who
are not Christians, the people in the world with the messy lives that people in
the world have.  If we aren’t willing to
help those people, we’re leaving half of this commandment undone.

Our care for everyone needs to start with **MERCY FOR THE AFFLICTED**.  Consider here James 1:27.  This is another text that is pointed at
individuals, not churches, so it doesn’t let us duck caring for others
either.  Furthermore, we should read our
responsibilities here expansively.  James
cites orphans and widows here because in his society, those two groups were the
poorest of the poor, those who were completely incapable of supporting
themselves through any honest means. 
However, I am quite certain that he does not mean for us to use orphans
and widows as a limitation on those whom we are willing to help.  We don’t get to say, “Nope!  Not an orphan!  Not a widow! 
Won’t help!”  That’s simply not an
interpretation that comes from love.

Instead, we should make a point of helping those who are
dealing with any affliction.  The help we
offer could be money, time, expertise, or even encouragement.  In all these things, the common element is
that we see the affliction and are willing to step up to alleviate it.

Of course, we have to make sure that our help actually is
help.  Giving the drug addict $20 in cash
isn’t helping him.  It’s enabling
him.  At the same time, though, we don’t
have the right to demand that the people we help live up to our moral
standards.  That’s not looking to
help.  That’s looking for a reason not to
help.  We need wisdom to do this right,
but we need love even more.

Similarly, every Christian should practice **HOSPITALITY.** Here, give some thought to Hebrews 13:2.  This text is not about having our best
friends over for a meal every now and again. 
The Greek word here is *xenophilia*,
which literally means “love of strangers.” 
It refers to the Middle Eastern custom of offering meals and even
lodging to foreigners who wander into town. 
Unless we’re inviting people we do not know into our homes, we’re not
doing this right.

This too is something that can certainly start with
Christians.  If some brethren have just
moved into the area, sharing Sunday dinner with them is a great way to get to
know them better and a righteous act besides. 
For that matter, it doesn’t have to be newcomers.  If there’s somebody we’ve worshiped with for
years whom we don’t know well at all, hospitality can solve the problem.

However, if only baptized believers and their families ever
pass the doors of our home, that’s a problem too.  It’s not in the Bible, but there’s a lot of
truth to the old saying, “People don’t care how much you know until they know
how much you care.”  Inviting somebody
else to eat at our table says pretty clearly how much we care about them, even if,
and especially if, we don’t know them yet. 
Meals like that can open doors for the gospel, but even if they don’t,
even if we never see those people again, God is still pleased by our generosity
toward them.

Finally, loving our neighbor means **LOVING OUR ENEMIES.** This one
is straight from the lips of the Lord, in Luke 6:27-31.  Of all the texts in Scripture, this is one of
the most challenging.  None of us start
out naturally loving our enemies.  It’s
something we have to learn to do through study, prayer, and practice.

This instruction embraces those who are our enemies for any
reason, and even those whom we think might be our enemies.  Once again, I’m not going to offer any
opinion about how the United States should treat Muslims, but at the very least,
this text defines how individual Christians must treat Muslims.  Yes, I certainly believe that Mohammed was a
false prophet and those who follow him are deceived, but it is still our
responsibility to treat them with respect and love.  That means not posting nasty slurs about
Muslims on Facebook.  It means not saying
nasty things about them in person.  It
means not interfering with the right of Muslims to worship.  It means that if Muslims move into our
neighborhood, we welcome them warmly, and possibly even take advantage of the
opportunity to show hospitality to them. 
Basically, every nice thing we can think of to do to and for the Muslims
in our lives, we should do, because that’s what Jesus expects us to do.

Of course, the application of this text is not limited to
Muslims.  It applies to any person or
group that we think may hate us.  For
instance, this is the way that African-American Christians should treat white
supremacists.  In a world where there is
so much fear and mistrust, we must not return evil for evil.  Instead, we should seek to overcome evil with
good.

**The Love of Christ**

If all of the above leaves us with any doubts about how
disciples of Christ should behave, let’s consider the love that Christ showed
toward His neighbor.  This love is first
evident **IN HEALING.** Among many other possible examples, let’s
examine Matthew 9:35-36.  To me, the most
evocative phrase in this passage has always been that the people were “like
sheep without a shepherd”.  However, in
my Bible reading the other day, I noticed something very interesting.  In Numbers 26, when God tells Moses that he’s
about to die because of his sin, Moses asks God to appoint a successor for him,
so that the people won’t be like sheep without a shepherd.

By Jesus’ time, of course, both Moses and Joshua are long
gone, so that the people have returned to their shepherdless state.  However, Jesus doesn’t prove that He’s a
fitting successor to Moses by leading the people into battle.  Instead, He asserts His leadership by feeling
compassion for the harassed and helpless and by offering them His healing.  Jesus didn’t help people out of political
calculation.  Instead, He helped them
because He loved them and cared about them.

Likewise, the love of Jesus appears **IN HIS TEACHING.** Look, for
instance, at the invitation He offers in Matthew 11:28-30.  Even though there are a couple chapters
between this and Matthew 9, it’s obvious that Jesus is still talking to the
same helpless, harassed people.

To people like this, He promises rest twice in three verses,
even though He simultaneously appeals to them to take His yoke upon them.  The point here, of course, is that there are
no people who are truly free, no people who truly belong to themselves.  Everybody ends up serving a master.  Most choose to serve the devil, who, although
He promises freedom, in reality only gives heavy burdens and spiritual
slavery.  Everywhere around us are people
who still experience this, even 2000 years later.  Sin is not a gracious master!  Instead, it brings fear, heartbreak, and
despair.  If we ever get to thinking that
somehow sin will make our lives better, we are desperately deluded.

By contrast, Jesus doesn’t tell us any lies about
freedom.  Being a Christian doesn’t mean
that we get to do whatever we want.  We
do have to serve Him, but in contrast to the tyranny of sin, Jesus is gentle
and lowly in heart.  The yoke He lays on
us is for our benefit, not His, and it’s all because He loves us and earnestly
desires our good.  The cemeteries are
full of people who died regretting a life of sin, but nobody ever regrets a
life spent in the service of our mild Master. 
The more we learn from Him, the more we discover the joys of the rest He
promises.

Finally, of course, Jesus reveals His love **IN DYING**.  Here, let’s read together from 1 John
3:16-18.  It is strange to think that the
most significant act of human history, an act that changed the world and the
human race forever, was a death.  After
all, death is universal.  Everybody
dies.  Back in Bible times, if you were
raised from the dead by Jesus, you got to die twice.

And yet, Jesus turned this thing that everybody does into a
unique act with a universal effect.  None
of us have any choice about dying.  We’re
born into it.  Jesus didn’t have to die,
but He chose to be born, not only aware that He would die, but intending to do
so.  He didn’t gain anything by His
death.  In fact, it was the final step of
His willing surrender of everything He had.

However, in Jesus’ death, all the rest of us gain two things
that are of incomparable worth.  The first
is the possibility of our salvation and the forgiveness of our sins.  Because Jesus died, those who believe in Him
and obey His gospel can inherit eternal life.

Second, though, Jesus’ death on the cross will forever be
the defining example of what love is.  If
we are ever in doubt about what the loving thing to do is, all we have to do is
look to the cross, and we will see our way clear.  This is not necessarily easy.  After all, Jesus expressed His love through
suffering and self-sacrifice.  However, it
is a definition of love that is so gracious and powerful that we can build our
lives around it. Let’s remember this dying love as we partake.
