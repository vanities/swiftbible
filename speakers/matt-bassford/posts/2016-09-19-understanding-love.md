I’ve heard it said that the problems in life don’t come from
what we don’t know.  Instead, they come
from “what we know that just ain’t so”. 
In the religious world especially, there’s a lot of truth to that.  If somebody is ignorant about the Bible but
wants to learn, you can bring them up to speed fairly quickly.  However, if somebody thinks they know all the
answers already but haven’t been getting those answers from Scripture, that’s
harder to deal with than ignorance.

Nowhere is this more obvious than with the Biblical concept
of love.  Everybody thinks they already
know what love is.  Isn’t a word we’ve
all been using since we were toddlers? 
The problem is, though, that people will take their own homegrown
conception of love, stick that into a Bible passage with no sense of its
context, and use it to defend any kind of questionable or straight-up sinful
behavior that they choose.  Of course, we
can be as guilty of bad Bible arguments as anybody.  To prevent this, then, let’s turn to the word
to understand love.



**Objects of Love**

Let’s begin our study this morning by considering the
Biblical objects of love, in other words, those to whom love is properly
directed.  This starts, of course, with
our love for **GOD.** Look at what Jesus says about this in
Mark 12:28-30.  This is a familiar
statement, but it presents us with a problem. 
How is it that we can possibly love a being whom we have never seen?

Even though our relationship with God is very different from
our relationship with other people, I think the answer starts there.  Stories of love at first sight to the
contrary, we rarely if ever love strangers. 
Instead, our love is primarily directed toward those we know, and the
better we get to know them, the more we love them.  This is true not only for Christians but even
for sinners.

If familiarity is at the heart of love, then, our love for
God has to begin with our familiarity with Him. 
Even though we can’t see Him, there are lots of ways we can come to know
Him if we keep our eyes open.  His
goodness is reflected in the physical creation and the myriad of ways He
blesses us.  We come to a deeper
understanding of His nature through study of His word.  Most of all, though, we come to know God and
so to love Him as we strive to imitate His character.  The more we practice His will, the better we
will understand Him and the more we will love Him.

In descending order, the next Scriptural object for our love
is **OUR BROTHER.** Here, consider 1 John 4:20-21.  Like a lot of passages in John’s writing,
there’s a warm tone to this, but it’s got a bite to it as well.  If we say we love God, but we don’t love our
brother, we’re wrong about the first.  If
we can’t love our brother whom we have seen, odds are that the reason that we
think we love God is because we haven’t seen Him yet!  For all of us who find real people harder to
love than an abstract God—which is all of us—this comes down on our toes with
considerable force.

The key here is to consider one another from a God-informed
perspective.  Our failure to love our
brethren is proof that we don’t know God, because if we did, He would have
taught us better.  He knows our failings
better than anybody, yet loves us more than everybody!  No matter how badly we mess up, He is still
able to see the soul created in His image.

We can’t see souls, but our love for one another has to
start with the recognition of the spiritual value that every Christian
possesses.  We may not feel that a
particular Christian matters very much to us right now, but they sure matter to
Jesus!  In fact, they matter enough that
He shed His lifeblood for them.  When we
start incorporating that priceless worth into the way that we assess one
another, we will find it much easier to love.

Next in line comes **OUR
NEIGHBOR.** This is the second half of
Jesus’ identification of the greatest commandment, as we see in Mark
12:31.  Who is our neighbor?  According to the parable of the good
Samaritan, our neighbor is anybody we choose to love, even if it’s an anonymous
robbery victim lying bleeding by the side of the road.  Indeed, it is our responsibility to go
through our lives identifying everyone we meet as our neighbors and treating
them accordingly.

We can struggle with that. 
How can we learn to love people we don’t like, much less complete
strangers?  However, I’m reminded here of
the words of a wise friend of mine.  She
said, “Find something beautiful in every person you encounter.”  Maybe that’s a physical beauty.  More likely, it is a beauty of character or
soul.  There is something about them that
is lovely and praiseworthy, something that reveals their true, God-created
self.

Maybe our co-worker has a mouth like a sailor, but we can
tell from talking to him that he really loves his family.  Maybe that guy down the street is socially
awkward, but when he takes his kids to the park, he’ll push other people’s kids
on the swing too.  Maybe our classmate
isn’t the most moral person out there, but she has an innate honesty about her
that we respect.  The devil tries to ruin
all of us, but in just about everybody, there is some part that he hasn’t
ruined yet, that still reflects the glory of the Creator.  Our job is to find that part and love it.

Finally, of course, is our responsibility to love **OUR ENEMIES.** Here, consider Luke 6:27-28.  Of all of the affirmative commands in the
Bible, this is one of the most difficult. 
These people are our enemies! 
They do mean stuff to us!  How can
we possibly love them?

I think in order to do this, we need to understand both our
enemies and the nature of enmity itself. 
Although I suppose hatred at first sight is possible too, that’s not
usually where problems between two people come from.  Instead, enmity is often the result of a
death spiral.

Here’s how it works. 
There’s an initial event, one where Person A does something Person B
doesn’t like.  Person B, from their own
perspective, reacts angrily to it, and Person A, from their own perspective,
reacts angrily to the angry reaction. 
Soon, they’re at each other’s throats.

In those circumstances, the best and most important thing we
can do is try to understand.  It’s
usually not the case that the other guy sees things the way we do and is determined
to be evil.  Instead, they’re acting from
a different perspective and are doing what they think is best, even if what
they think is best is getting mad and believing that we are determined to do
evil!  For that matter, it may even be
true that we’ve got tunnel vision and are misreading the whole situation.

Once we learn to see things through the eyes of the other,
we can acknowledge their value as a human being and find something in them to
love.  Maybe they’ve gotten all twisted
up by the situation, but once we understand, we can love and offer forgiveness
and grace.

**Jesus and Love**

Now that we’ve explored the dimensions of love, let’s
consider love as perfectly practiced by our Lord, Jesus.  His life reveals ample expression of His love
**FOR GOD.**One of many such expressions appears in John 8:28-29.  For Jesus to go around saying, “I love God”
all the time would be like Usain Bolt going around all the time saying, “I’m
fast.”  There’s no need for it.

In particular, in this text, we see Jesus demonstrating His
love for the Father in two ways.  The
first is by speaking the things that the Father has taught Him.  The second is by always doing the things that
are pleasing to Him.  We prove our love
not by asserting it, but by showing it.

Jesus’ practice here reveals the hypocrisy of much of the
religious world.  The planet is full of
people who happily proclaim their love for God, at least in the right
setting.  However, they spend their time
repeating their own think-so’s instead of His word and doing what they want to
do rather than what He wants them to do. 
Brethren, that’s not love.  It’s
selfishness.

Of course, we can easily fall under the same
condemnation.  As James warns, it’s easy
for us to fall into the trap of blessing God with our lips while also cursing
those who have been made in the image of God. 
Similarly, every sin problem in our lives is a love problem.  We sin because we don’t love enough, and he
more we learn the love of Christ, the more those problems vanish.

Similarly, Jesus’ actions proved His love **FOR US.**Let’s ponder His famous statement in John 15:12-14.  Jesus loves me, this I know, not just because
the Bible tells me so, but because of what the Bible tells me about what He
did.  He suffered and died for my sake
before I even existed.

This is one of the places in Scripture where I think we’re
tempted to compartmentalize out what Jesus did. 
He died for me, so if I’m ever called upon to die for the sins of
mankind, I guess I’d better do that too. 
In the meantime, I’m going to do what I want.

Of course, that’s not the implication of Jesus’ words at
all.  The meaning might emerge more
clearly if we employ an idiom and say that Jesus gave His life for us.  His life was about others.  Even if we are never called on to die for our
friends, and most of us won’t be, it is nonetheless true that our lives need to
be about others too.

This starts with our families.  Like charity, service and self-sacrifice
begin at home.  We need to live daily in
such a way that our spouses and children see Christ in us.  Our selfless love should permeate all of our
friendships too.  However, it shouldn’t
end there.  As Jesus observes, even
godless sinners love their families and friends.  Our love should motivate us to treat everyone
we encounter with kindness, grace, and respect. 
That’s when the light of Christ shines in us.

Jesus was so good at this that He showed His love even **FOR HIS ENEMIES.** Consider the events of Luke 23:33-34.  I think that if we’re honest, every one of us
will admit that on some level, this bothers us. 
Sure, we understand our obligation to forgive others when they repent,
but the chief priests and scribes around the foot of the cross were about a
million miles from repentance.  They were
standing around hurling insults at Jesus even as He suffered and died.  And yet, Jesus not only forgives them
Himself, He appeals to God to forgive them.

Walking in the footsteps of Christ, then, not only means
loving our enemies and forgiving them when they repent, but loving and forgiving
them even when they don’t.  We really
don’t like to do that.  We want those
people to come crawling to us and acknowledge their sin. Until and unless they
do, we are going to stand right here on our rights and not budge an inch!

However, we must learn to forgive anyway, not even for the
sake of those who wrong us, but for our own. 
There are many problems with storing anger and resentment in our hearts,
but one of the biggest of those problems is that such emotions are
corrosive.  If we allow them to linger
inside of us, they will eat away at our souls like battery acid at a plastic
cup.  The only way that we can prevent
catastrophic spiritual damage is by forgiving fully, freely, and sincerely.

Before the Lord’s Supper, let’s reflect on these
things.  First of all, let’s consider our
Lord Jesus.  He revealed awesome power
when He worked His miracles, but to me, the most awesome thing about Him is His
love.  Imagine saying “Forgive them!” about
the very people who have successfully carried out a vicious plot to have you
crucified!  Let’s remember Jesus, His
undying love, and our responsibility to follow in His footsteps as we partake.

**Love in Practice**

For the final part of our study this morning, let’s turn to
a fairly tight look at 1 Corinthians 13 and love in practice.  The first main section of this text concerns
love and **CONFLICT.** Here, let’s read 1 Corinthians
13:4-5.  To be honest, brethren, I think
that if this passage doesn’t step on all of our toes, it’s probably because
we’re not paying enough attention!  That
certainly goes for me.  Years ago, I put
this passage on the door of my church-building office because it’s so
difficult.

Interestingly, this description of love is mostly negative.  On the positive side, Paul does report that
love is patient and kind.  However, we’re
familiar enough with those ideas that they don’t have much of an impact.  It’s the negatives that really start coming
down hard on our feet.

First of all, love is not jealous.  It doesn’t look at the gifts and
accomplishments of its friends and get mad about all of them rather than being
happy for the friends.  Love does not
brag.  In our day, Paul might have said,
“Love does not use Facebook to craft and present a careful image of the perfect
life, in which even our complaints are actually boasting, so that the whole
Internet can know how wonderful we are.” 
Love is not arrogant.  It does not
claim competence that it does not actually possess.  It doesn’t try to run other people’s lives
for them.

Love does not act unbecomingly.  Other translations here say that love does
not act improperly or is not rude.  In
other words, love is so mindful of others that it always remembers to treat
them in a respectful way.  Love does not
seek its own.  It doesn’t believe it’s
the most important person in the room. 
It doesn’t insist on its way or the highway.

Love is not provoked. 
It doesn’t go around with a chip on its shoulder, just waiting for the
next inevitable provocation.  Finally,
love doesn’t take into account a wrong suffered.  The sense here is of somebody who is a
spiritual accountant, carefully noting in the debit column in red ink every
time somebody else sins against them or treats them badly.  Love chooses to forget instead.

That, brethren, is an intimidating list!  The fleshly man, of course, is very good at
doing all of those do-nots.  All of us
are probably better at them than we care to admit.  We need to be honest, friends, about what we
see here in the mirror of the word and reform our lives accordingly.

Next, Paul discusses love and **JOY**.  Here, let’s read 1
Corinthians 13:6.  In English, at least,
I find “joy” to be a frustrating word. 
It mainly rhymes with the not-terribly-uplifting “destroy”, so if you
want to end a line with it, you’re going to have to do some gymnastics to get
there.

However, as much as I don’t like the rhyming sound of the
word, I appreciate the thought.  We all
know what joy is, and we all take joy in different things.  Here, it’s particularly striking what love
does and does not find joy in.  Love does
not rejoice in unrighteousness, but rejoices with the truth.  That’s unusual because “unrighteousness” and
“truth” aren’t usually opposites.  Righteousness
is the opposite of unrighteousness; falsehood is the opposite of truth.  I think Paul wants us to notice that and try
to figure out why he did it.

Like all the other attributes we’ve looked at so far, I think
these two are relational too, so that this verse is about unrighteousness in
others and the presentation of truth to others. 
We might be tempted to overlook sin in a brother or a friend because it
doesn’t feel loving to call them on it and create a confrontation.  Paul wants us to understand that precisely
the opposite is true.  Nobody needs a
lying doctor, and nobody needs a lying friend. 
When we fail to raise an important spiritual issue, we’re lying by
omission.  We’re allowing somebody to
think they’re better than they are.

Instead, love rejoices with the truth.  Love is honest, even when honesty is
painful.  All of us need brethren who are
close enough to us that they’re willing to serve us as spiritual
unrighteousness detectors.  They love us
enough and trust us enough to tell us the truth we need to hear.  What’s more, we need to be unrighteousness
detectors for the people who are close to us. 
We need the moral courage and concern for their souls to plainly and
openly point out their problems.

Finally, Paul describes the relationship between love and **ENDURANCE.**Consider 1 Corinthians 13:7.  First
of all, we learn that love bears all things. 
I can remember from back when I was a kid a lady at church who had a
cross-stitched Bible cover with this on it, along with a cute cross-stitched
teddy bear.  The reality, though, is
serious business.  The Greek here can be
translated as “love covers all things”. 
In other words, love continues to forgive, no matter the sin.

Similarly, love believes all things.  It consistently chooses to put the best spin
on somebody else’s behavior rather than the worst.  It looks for explanations and justifications
rather than an excuse to become angry. 
It only sees evil when it is given no other choice.

Third, love hopes all things.  Love doesn’t look at others with world-weary
cynicism.  It doesn’t give up on
people.  Love thinks that this time is
going to be different.  It gives second
chances until it becomes brutally apparent that doing so is pointless.

Finally, love endures all things.  No evil can outlast it.  It does not approve of bad behavior, but it
continues to love.  No provocation is
enough to turn love to apathy or hatred. 
All of these things are the ideals, and a big part of our Christian walk
is learning to put them into practice.
