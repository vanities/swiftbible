In English, the word “sacrifice” has a couple of main
senses.  In a Biblical context, we are
used to speaking of sacrifices as something that has been offered to God, such
as the animal sacrifices of the Law of Moses. 
Outside the church building, though, when we talk about sacrifices, we
usually aren’t talking about goats and altars and all the rest.  Instead, we typically use “sacrifice” to talk
about giving something up that we value for a higher purpose.  For instance, parents often sacrifice quite a
bit to care for their children.

Here in the assembly, we will often discuss the sacrifice of
Christ.  Generally, when we do so, we use
Meaning #1.  Jesus was an offering for
sin like the bulls and calves in the Old Testament.  However, it’s equally legitimate to speak not
merely of the sacrifice of Christ, but of the sacrifices of Christ.  Because of His love for us, He surrendered
everything.  Let’s look this morning,
then, at what Jesus gave up.



The first of these sacrifices that Jesus made was His
sacrifice of **HIS POSITION.** Here, let’s consider the words of Paul in
Philippians 2:5-7.  The contrast here
with usual human behavior could not be more striking.  Even though we’re still more than a year and
a half away from the next presidential elections, it is sadly apparent that
election season is already upon us.  All
kinds of politicians from left and right are declaring their intention of
running for president.  They are willing
to spend every waking moment for the next eighteen months, along with millions
of dollars, in order to win that office for themselves.

Now admittedly, being president of the United States is a
pretty important thing to be in earthly terms. 
However, being president is nothing next to being the eternal King of
the universe.  No matter how badly we
might want to, none of us will ever sit on the throne of God in heaven.  And yet, it is precisely that position of supreme
authority that Jesus surrendered in order to come to earth and live like one of
us.  Rather than grasping at equality
with God, He let it all go for our sakes.

Similarly, Jesus surrendered **HIS RICHES.** Paul has an
interesting take on this in 2 Corinthians 8:9. 
We don’t often think of God as being rich or poor, but when we think
about what Jesus had, we cannot describe Him as other than rich.  After all, money isn’t important for its own
sake, unless we have so much of it that we swim in it like Scrooge McDuck.  Instead, money is important for what it can
buy:  the necessities of life, things and
experiences that we can enjoy, and even protection from future disasters.  When Jesus was in heaven, He was perfectly
happy, perfectly safe, and able to have whatever He wanted.

On earth, though, it was a different story for the
Lord.  He started His ministry with a
house, but at some point, He lost it, so that He could say to a would-be
disciple that the Son of Man had nowhere to lay His head.  He wasn’t able to work to provide for Himself
while He was preaching, so He was dependent on the generosity of others.  Finally, rather than being free from fear,
protected from future disaster, Jesus spent His whole life in the shadow of the
cross.  By any standard, Jesus on earth
was poor, not because He had to be, but because He chose to be, because only
through His poverty could He make us rich.

Third, Jesus sacrificed **HIS
HONOR** because we needed Him to.  This
also was something He was well aware of even before it happened.  Here, consider Mark 9:12.  When Jesus went on that final journey to
Jerusalem, He knew that He would suffer not only pain, but also shame.  This is no small thing!  As I think most of you know, I spend a lot of
time preparing and rehearsing before every sermon that I preach.  There are a number of reasons why I do that,
but one of them is certainly fear of embarrassment.  I can’t stand the thought of getting up here
and looking unprepared and foolish, even though I know that all of you are my
friends and would no more judge me harshly than I would judge any of you.

Imagine, then, what Jesus faced—not some mild social
awkwardness, but the most withering shame that a human being can endure.  He wasn’t embarrassed before His friends, but
before the worst enemies He had.  They
spat on Him, they beat Him, they mocked Him, and they crucified Him.  The cross, of course, wasn’t merely designed
to hurt and kill.  It was designed to
humiliate, to degrade, to strip away every shred of dignity and self-worth that
the victim might have.  That’s what the
chief priests wanted to do to Jesus.  He
knew going in that He was going to face that, and He went anyway.

Finally, of course, Jesus sacrificed **HIS LIFE.** Once again, let’s
consider His own words about this in Matthew 26:26-28.  We have it from no less authority on human
nature than the devil that our lives are what is most precious to us.  We will step away from a high position, spend
every dime we own, and even endure indignity and shame in order to continue to
live.  The human animal within us will
cling fiercely to life as long as even a shred of hope remains.  What we cling to, however, is exactly what
Jesus laid down.  He knew that in order
to save us, He would have to forfeit both His body and His lifeblood.

Here we come full circle to the other conception of sacrifice.  Jesus had to sacrifice Himself because His
sinless life was the only sacrifice that would avert the wrath of God.  Of all the people on earth, Jesus was the
only one who was safe.  He had nothing to
fear on the Day of Judgment, but all the rest of us did.  To rephrase the mocking words of the chief
priests, if He wanted to save others, He couldn’t save Himself.

Before the Lord’s Supper, then, let us remember not merely
the sacrifice of Christ, but the sacrifices of Christ.  Everything that we hold dear, He gave up,
because the great goal before Him was our salvation.  That’s what Jesus did for us.  What are we willing to give up for Him?  We can never give so much, simply because we
don’t have so much to give.  Like the
widow with the two mites, we can only give what we have.  Do we surrender what we have to Him, or do we
hoard it instead?  Let’s consider this as
we partake.

One of my bad habits as a preacher is my tendency to assume
that the Joliet church is made up of mind-readers.  I naturally think that if I sketch out a
broad outline of what the Bible says Christians ought to be doing, all of you
will immediately pick up on the ways that you ought to put my preaching into
practice.  However, none of you actually
are mind-readers, so I’ve found that I need to do a more careful job of
spelling out the applications of what I’m saying.

Even though it’s not an explicitly Scriptural principle,
I’ve realized that I’ve made something of the same mistake with my recent
emphasis on note-taking sermons.  I’ve changed
up my sermon format, but I’ve never really spelled out what I would like you to
try.  Here’s the deal.  What you’re seeing on the screen is
essentially a duplicate of what I have written in my Bible to preach the sermon.  I outline the main points and subpoints of
the text, underline key phrases, and write conclusions and applications in the
margin.

There are a number of different things you can do with this
information.  First, you can copy down
what I’m putting up pretty much verbatim in a wide-margin Bible or a
notebook.  In a wide-margin Bible, you
can underline verses and write next to them, just like you see on the
screen.  In a notebook, you’d have to
write down parenthetical verse ranges and “Key verse:  v. 19” or something like that.  If you don’t have a wide-margin Bible and
would like to try writing in one, you can find well-constructed hardbacks in a
variety of translations online for $30 or less. 
If you’re curious, I’d be happy to point you in the right
direction.  I suspect something similar
to this could be done with a Bible app on a tablet or phone, but I’ve never
really used a Bible app, so I’m afraid you’ll have to figure out how that works
on your own.

Even if you don’t have a wide-margin Bible but want to stick
with the Bible you’ve got, there are still things that you can do.  Don’t copy down my topics and subtopics; just
write a Roman numeral “I”, letter “A” or what have you in your narrow margin where
I’ve got the whole phrase on the screen. 
That will still show you the structure of the text, and that’s the
important thing.  You can still underline
the key phrases, and if you think an application is really, *really* important, you can write it in
sideways.

Of course, none of this is required.  You don’t suddenly become a bad Christian
because you don’t want to take notes. 
However, I’ve found that I enjoy it, and you may find it both enjoyable
and useful.

\*     \*     \*

Imagine, for a moment, that each of us were to compile a
list of the five most important books of the New Testament.  This would not be a terribly useful
exercise—after all, all 27 of them are inspired—but it is at least
thinkable.  I daresay that if each one us
were to write down our list, none of them would contain the epistle to Philemon.  Of all the letters of Paul that survive
today, it is the shortest, and rather than dealing with some doctrinal issue,
it concerns a personal matter between him and Philemon.

For these reasons, we might be tempted to overlook it, but
that would be a terrible mistake.  After
all, Philemon was preserved for us for a reason, and if we never study it, we
will never hear the whole counsel of God. 
Even though Paul doesn’t set out some great theological argument, he
gives us one of the best examples in Scripture of how Christians should resolve
disputes among themselves, an example that will help us in our friendships, our
marriages, and our relationships with brethren. 
Let’s look, then, at Philemon.

**Matters Past and
Present**

If we were to chop the book of Philemon in two, roughly the
first half would concern the past and the present.  As is true of many of the Pauline epistles,
we have a great deal to learn even from Paul’s opening **GREETING.** Look here at
Philemon 1-3.  This apparently
standard-issue greeting gets a lot more interesting once we start to compare it
with the fourth chapter of Colossians. 
The seventeenth verse of that chapter is the only other place in the New
Testament where we meet Archippus.  This
suggests a connection between the church in Colossae and the congregation of
which Philemon is a member.  It could be
that Philemon is a member of the church in Laodicea, which appears in
Colossians 4:16.  It could even be that
Philemon belongs to the church in Colossae itself.  Regardless, because of this and some other
reasons we’ll explore later, it is nearly certain that Philemon is at least
located near the city of Colossae, and that Paul is writing this letter to him
during his first imprisonment, likely sometime around 60 AD.

This makes Paul’s address of Philemon as a “beloved brother”
particularly interesting.  Here and
throughout the letter, it’s obvious that Paul and Philemon already have a
relationship, but according to Colossians 2:1, Paul has never been to Colossae.  It appears, then, that like Aquila and
Priscilla, Philemon is one of those Christians who moved around a bunch.  He met Paul, was apparently converted by him,
but then moved to the vicinity of Colossae, which is where this letter finds
him.

After this, we come to a fairly typical section that
describes Paul’s **PRAYERS** about and
for Philemon and those who worship with him. 
Read with me from Philemon 4-7. 
As is often the case, Paul notes that he has heard from others of
Philemon’s faith and love.  However, the
way that he describes the impact of that love is unusual.  He says that the hearts of the saints have
been refreshed through Philemon.

We often talk about the importance of encouraging one
another, but rarely have I heard teaching on the importance of refreshing other
Christians.  “Refreshment” is a word that
advertisers love because it’s so evocative and pleasant.  Refreshment is a dip in the pool on a hot
July day.  Refreshment is a tall glass of
ice-cold lemonade.  When we’re worn down
and hot and sweaty, refreshment renews and reinvigorates us.

Apparently, Philemon was the spiritual equivalent of a glass
of lemonade.  He had the knack of
refreshing the hearts of weary Christians. 
As we’ll see in a bit, Philemon was most likely a man given to showing
his brethren hospitality.  Likewise, he
seems to have had the gift of rising above low expectations.  As we know, people in the world are often
mean-spirited and selfish.  When we’ve
had a day that’s been filled with mean-spiritedness and selfishness, sometimes
it’s positively shocking to spend time around Christians instead, not because
they’re doing some amazing thing, but simply because they’re decent,
compassionate, respectful people and act accordingly.  Sometimes it’s just weird to have a Christian
ask us how we are and know that unlike everybody else who has asked us that
today, they actually want to know the answer. 
Let’s remember, friends, how good it feels when other Christians refresh
us.  Let’s resolve to be refreshing
Christians ourselves, to ease the burdens on others by expressing our care and
concern for them.

All of this, of course, is only a prelude to **PAUL’S APPEAL** to Philemon.  We find it in Philemon 8-14.  Here, we learn a great deal more about the
circumstances surrounding the epistle. 
Its main subject is a man named Onesimus, who was a slave belonging to
Philemon.  In Greek, his name means
“useful”, which is rather ironic. 
Onesimus had so little interest in being useful to Philemon that he ran
away, fleeing all the way to Rome itself. 
While in Rome, he encountered Paul somehow, perhaps because he had met
Paul while a slave, and also in Rome, he obeyed the gospel and became a
Christian.  From that point on, he
started living up to his name, making himself useful to Paul in all kinds of
ways.  However, this put Paul in a tough
spot; regardless of the state of his soul, Onesimus still legally belonged to
Philemon.

In that situation, most people would have been inclined to
unleash the awesome authority of an apostle, to make Philemon do the right
thing, at least not punishing Onesimus for running away, at most setting him
free.  However, that’s not Paul’s
approach.  Instead of commanding, he
appeals.  He sends Onesimus back to his
master, but armed with this letter from Paul, in which Paul spells out what he
would like Philemon to do but emphasizes that the choice lies with Philemon and
not with Paul.

Here, we see a valuable lesson for all Christians, whether
in positions of authority or not.  The
point is this:  don’t try to force people
to do what you want them to do!  Sadly,
our natural inclination is exactly the opposite.  My children already love to bark orders at
me, as though they were the parents and I the child.  However, as we get older, we start to learn
the lesson that Paul has already internalized. 
There really is no authority outside of persuasion.  A boss who tries to browbeat his employees or
an elder who tries to browbeat the congregation is never going to get the
willing compliance that he wants.  It’s
even less effective when we aren’t in positions of authority.  Ironically, the harder we push, the less likely
we are to win our point.  Only if we win
over the other by persuading him to our way of thinking do we succeed.

This exchange also gives us our second point of contact with
Colossae.  Colossians 4:7-9 is the only
other place in the Bible we find Onesimus, and there, along with Tychicus, we
see him identified as one of the Christians who is carrying Colossians to the
Colossians.  There, Paul emphasizes that
Onesimus is “one of your own”, implying that the last time Onesimus was in
Colossae, he wasn’t.  The simplest
explanation here is that Paul wrote Colossians and Philemon at the same time,
and Onesimus was tasked with carrying both letters simultaneously to Asia.  We’ll consider more evidence for this as we
continue.

**The Future**

In the last part of Philemon, Paul turns his attention to
what he wants the future to look like. 
He begins this by describing what he wants **THE DESIRED RESULT** to be. 
Here, let’s consider Philemon 15-16. 
Paul here expresses the hope that Onesimus’s decision to run away will
turn out to be a good thing in the long run. 
In some ways, it already has. 
Now, Paul’s own relationship with Onesimus is such that he can call him
his beloved brother.  If Philemon does
the right thing and frees Onesimus, it’s true that he will be down a slave, but
he will be up a brother in Christ and, Paul anticipates, a close earthly
associate too.

This too is a powerful rhetorical strategy.  Freeing Onesimus would have some costs for
Philemon, but down the road, there are going to be some important benefits that
arise from it.  Godliness is often this
way.  We pay up front, but the future is
going to be better because we made the right decision now.  We need to recognize this for ourselves when
we are wrestling with whether we are going to be godly or not, but equally
importantly, we need to point it out to others. 
Sure, it’s going to be hard for the alcoholic to give up drinking, but
if he does, he’s going to make his whole life better.  Sure, it’s going to be hard for that family
to start coming on Wednesday nights, but if they do, it’s going to help them
build a closer relationship with their brethren and with God.  We need to acknowledge the current costs but
emphasize the future benefits.

Next, Paul raises the subject of **REPAYMENT.** Look at Philemon
17-21.  Paul here brings up the
possibility that in leaving, Onesimus did some kind of harm to Philemon.  If that’s the case, though, Paul asks for
those debts to be transferred from Onesimus’s account to Paul’s.  Paul will pay up if that’s what Philemon
wants.  Of course, before he shows up
with his hand out, Philemon needs to remember that he owes Paul his own salvation.  What Philemon has the right to ask and what
he should ask are two different things.

Paul closes this discussion by appealing to Philemon’s
better nature.  As he’s already noted,
Philemon loves to refresh the hearts of the saints.  Now is the time for him to refresh Paul’s
heart too.  Paul makes this appeal
because he knows that Philemon will receive it with the right frame of
mind.  Because Philemon is a decent guy
who wants to do right, Paul is confident that he will do even more than Paul
has asked.

In the same way, friends, especially in our appeals to other
Christians, let’s remember to treat them as people of good will.  If a leader in the church or in the family
approaches those he is leading as though they are not trustworthy, as though
they need to be coerced or browbeaten into doing the right thing, that will
come across, poison the relationship, and be utterly ineffective.  Instead, we need to recognize our brethren as
people who deep down inside want to do good but perhaps don’t see the good
right now that they ought to be doing. 
Only if we trust them and make that clear to them will we be able to
lead them effectively.

Paul wraps up the letter with a few **CLOSING COMMENTS.** They
appear in Philemon 22-25.  Here, Paul
both expresses his hope that he will be able to visit Philemon and passes on
greetings from some other brethren who are with him in Rome.  Both of these things are important.  The first shows that Paul is concerned about
the long term in his relationship with Philemon too.  He doesn’t want to get his way if he’ll make
Philemon angry by doing so.  This too is
an attitude that we should learn from, especially for those, like me, who are
prone to get caught up in the heat of the moment during an argument.  We must always be careful not to say anything
now that we’re going to regret later. 
Unless we’re dealing with false doctrine or something like that, there
are few arguments that are worth winning at the cost of a friend.

Second, here is where we see the third strand in the cord
linking Philemon and Colossians.  Here,
we meet five men:  Epaphras, Mark, Aristarchus,
Demas, and Luke.  All of these men appear
somewhere else in Scripture at least once, but the only place we find them
grouped together is Colossians 4:10-14. 
From all this, we can conclude that Colossians and Philemon were written
together and carried together.
