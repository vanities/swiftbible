Over the past two-and-a-half years, I’ve reviewed 29
different Bibles, putting them all through a two-week trial period.  They’ve come in all kinds of different shapes
and sizes.  Some of them, I’ve loved;
some of them, I’ve, well, loved less.  I
think it’s fair to say, though, that by now, I’ve become a repeat player in the
Bible-review game.

As I consider the reviews that I’ve written recently, one
thing in particular stands out to me.  I
care much more about layout and design for reading Bibles than for pulpit
Bibles.  Indeed, I would almost go so far
as to say that layout and design matter more for reading than for
preaching.

 When it comes to preaching Bibles, there are really five
questions that I care about:

1. Does the Bible have margins wide enough to put a sermon
   outline in?
2. If so, is the paper opaque enough to write on without
   suffering from bleed-through?  In
   wide-margin Bibles of any manufacture, the answer here is almost always “Yes”.
3. If the Bible does not have wide margins, is it small enough
   to share the pulpit top with my sermon notebook?
4. Is the Bible light enough that I don’t mind holding it for
   45 minutes?
5. Are the verse numbers clear enough that I will cite them
   correctly?  Again, the answer to this
   question is almost always “Yes”.



Basically, as long as a Bible manufacturer can assemble a
wide-margin Bible with reasonably opaque paper, that isn’t a brick, and that
doesn’t have verse numbers that look like quotation marks, I’ve got everything
I want.  In years to come, I may insist
on a wide-margin Bible with larger print, but I’m not there yet.

By contrast, in a reading Bible, I want:

1. A Bible as small as possible.
2. A characters-per-line count of 40-60 (65 in exceptional
   cases).
3. A generous gutter margin.
4. Generous leading, particularly in single-column Bibles.
5. Black lettering (no words of Christ in pink!).
6. Line-matching.
7. At least 8-point text (I preach with my glasses on but read
   with them off, so preaching with a small-print Bible is easier for me than
   reading).
8. Dark, crisp, consistent print.
9. Opaque paper.



I don’t want much, do I? 
The CPL-count requirements alone are very restrictive.  For Bibles of traditional dimension (as
opposed to, say, the squarish Pitt Minion), this means that I will prefer to
read from double-column Bibles without a reference column and single-column
Bibles with a reference column. 
Center-column reference Bibles generally have a CPL count that’s too
low, and single-column text Bibles generally have a CPL count that’s too
high.

Also, several of the requirements are deal-breakers.  If a preaching Bible doesn’t have wide
margins, I can still use it fairly happily. 
However, I simply will not read out of a Bible with an inadequate
gutter.  The skimpy gutter in the Cambridge
Standard Text REB has kept me from using it as a daily reader, even though the
REB intrigues me as a translation.

Finally, reader selection is where my Bible snobbery
emerges.  A mass-market China-print Bible
with a poly cover is fine for preaching, but nothing compares to reading from a
goatskin Bible with a Jongbloed text block, with paper that’s just the right
shade of cream and laser-sharp print.

This has several implications.  First, I suspect that it shows that reading
the Bible is a more effortful activity than using it in the pulpit or at the
computer.  I must devote more sustained
attention to make it through a daily Bible reading than to read a passage out
loud, even a long passage.  Even when I’m
writing a sermon, once I’ve skimmed it and developed an outline, I only
consider the text one bite-sized chunk at a time.  Because it’s harder to read the Bible than it
is to do anything else with it, minor annoyances become more significant while
reading.

Second, it shows that designing a Bible for readability is
what is most important.  I’ve never used
a wide-margin Bible that didn’t work as a wide-margin.  “Gives me a place to write stuff” is not a
high bar!  However, I’ve reviewed quite a
few Bibles that I didn’t care for as readers. 
I don’t read from the same Bibles I write in (it’s too distracting), but
I know there are plenty who do things differently.  For their sakes, even designers of
wide-margin Bibles should look to make the layout readable first and then
encase it in a wide margin.  I think both
the various Cambridge Wide-Margins and the wide-margin Foundation SCR both
excel as readers.

Third, if you want to buy a single premium Bible, but funds
are tight, buy a good reader.  That’s
where the premium will matter most.  The
Lord knows that reading through the Bible in a year is difficult enough to
begin with!  A Bible with a subpar design
or subpar manufacture will only pile additional obstacles in your way.  In fact, I wonder how many would-be Bible
readers have failed because they didn’t start with a good reader.

I think this is true even for a preacher.  I’ve been known to argue that it makes sense
for a preacher to invest in a high-quality Bible, just like a professional
carpenter buys professional-grade tools. 
However, I don’t recall noticing any correlation between the cost of a
Bible and how much I liked using it in the pulpit.  I gave the Foundation Wide-Margin In Touch a
9.5/10 as a preaching Bible, and my copy is a hardback!

If you like the idea of having several premium Bibles, you
might as well bling it up in the pulpit too, but the place where a premium
makes the biggest difference is on the end table next to your recliner.  Everyone will benefit from having a good
daily reader, but the preacher will benefit even more than most.  The more you know, the more effectively you
will preach.  For the preacher who isn’t
able to stay consistently in the word, finding the best available reading Bible
for him is worth whatever he spends on it.
