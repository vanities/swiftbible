I spend wayyy too much time on Bible publishers’ websites, constantly sniffing around, hoping to find an interesting Bible that I’d overlooked or to learn about a new Bible that soon will be appearing.  One day, I’d wandered over to Crossway’s site when I noticed that their “Bibles” section gave me an option to sort by bestselling.

I hypothesized briefly about what result I would get (the Thinline?  The Personal Reference Bible?), clicked the button, and saw to my surprise that the first three results on the list were various study Bibles.  Indeed, if we exclude institutional purchases (pew Bibles and giveaway Bibles), five study Bibles appear before the first non-study Bible, which is an ESV Value Thinline.  Apparently, believers really, really like to buy study Bibles.


I must admit that this surprises and even dismays me.  Don’t get me wrong; I’m happy when anybody buys any Bible, though using one is even better.  I also prefer paper Bibles to e-Bibles.  I do not think it is sinful or wrong to use a study Bible.  I do not want to see a study-Bible bonfire in the street outside my house tomorrow evening.

However, I do think there are better choices out there.  Let me explain.  By inclination and by upbringing, I am passionately committed to an individualistic view of the Christian’s walk with God.  My salvation, and indeed the salvation of everyone, is too precious to entrust to another human being, be he ever so wise.  My constant, perhaps wearisome, refrain from the pulpit is, “But don’t take my word for it!  Read for yourself!  Study for yourself!”

I do my best to take my own advice.  Though from time to time I may engage in word study or historical research, by and large, I am the mythical one-book preacher.  My preferred study tools are a Bible, my brain, and prayer.  It is true that I make mistakes sometimes, but if my understanding falters when I read the inspired text, how much more will I fail to grasp the meaning of the words of mere men!  Besides, the style in which most commentaries are written can make a bus schedule appear diverting and delightful by comparison.

I recognize that my views and practice here are extreme.  Many, perhaps most, students of the word find great value in others’ interpretations of the text.  However, even the most useful interpretive tool can become a crutch if used wrongly.  For all of us, our study of the Bible itself must come first.  Our use of various helps must come second.

The problem with study Bibles is that they elevate the help to the same level as the text.  There they are, between the same two covers, right next to each other!  I think most Christians who use study Bibles would say the right things about using them properly.  If I asked them, they would have no trouble distinguishing between the word of God and the words of man, how the latter can be mistaken, and so forth.

In practice, though, is that really the way that things work?  When a Christian is reading from a study Bible and he encounters a difficult text, how does he respond?  Does he read the text again?  Does he pray for help in understanding it?  Does he turn to other passages?  Does he close the Bible and walk away to give himself time to meditate on it?  Or, instead, does he turn to the seductively convenient explanation in the next column over, accept it, and continue reading?

When a study Bible is wrong (and they often contradict each other, so some of them are), the problems with this practice are obvious.  Even when the study Bible is right, though, problems are still present.  The canned answer may be convenient, but it won't have the same impact on our spiritual growth as will honest grappling with the text.  We aren’t studying for a test; we’re seeking to renew our minds, and only the answers for which we struggle ourselves are the answers that will truly change us.
