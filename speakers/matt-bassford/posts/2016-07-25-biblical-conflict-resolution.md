Conflict is a reality of life on earth.  It is probably fair to say that every single
one of us will, in the course of a day, find ourselves disagreeing with
somebody.  It happens in families, in friendships,
in workplaces, and in churches.  There’s
nothing we can do to prevent it.

Precisely because conflict is so prevalent, one of the most
important skills that we can master is how to resolve it.  In the long term, simply avoiding conflict
and papering over disagreement will hurt us, not help us.  Of course, the other extreme isn’t any good
for anybody either.  We’ve all had to
deal with people who went through life with a chip on their shoulders, and that
too is a pretty good way to wreck every relationship we’ve got.

Wisdom, then, calls us to a middle path.  We have to learn how to disagree while
simultaneously not becoming disagreeable. 
This is certainly a godly art, and both the Old and New Testaments have
a great deal to say on the subject.  If
we can take this inspired counsel and put it into practice, we’ll have lives
that are both happier and more pleasing to God. 
We need to listen to what the Bible is teaching us about conflict
resolution.



**Things to Do**

The first section of Scriptures that we’re going to look at
this morning concerns what we ought to do when we find ourselves sideways with
somebody.  At the top of the list, our
overriding goal must be to **SEEK
PEACE.** Paul emphasizes this in
Romans 12:18.  Probably some of you right
now are thinking that this is a duh point. 
“What else would I be trying to do in a conflict besides make
peace?”  Then, there are the rest of you,
who know perfectly well what else one might be trying to do in a conflict
besides make peace.  You want to
win.  To be perfectly honest, I’m right
there with you.  I’m a competitive,
high-achievement guy.  I want to win at
everything, and as Lauren would be the first to tell you, when I get in an
argument, my inclination is to keep bludgeoning away until the other side gives
up from sheer exhaustion!

The point here is not that it’s bad to be competitive and
high-achievement.  The point is that
relationships are a bad place to be competitive and high-achievement in.  I’m here to tell you:  no matter what the relationship is, if you
insist on winning every argument, that’s going to come back to bite you in the
end.  For all of us, the goal needs not
to be the win.  It needs to be the
relationship.  Maybe I don’t get the same
adrenaline rush from pulling back and not insisting on my way, but that makes
for peace, harmony, and all the other quiet joys that enrich our lives.

Second, we need to learn to **LISTEN.** Proverbs 18:15 makes
this point pretty clearly.  We must be
careful, though, not to confuse adversarial listening with friendly
listening.  Adversarial listening is the
kind that you do in the middle of a debate. 
Come what may, you’re not there to be persuaded by the other guy.  You really don’t care about the good points
he’s making.  Instead, you’re listening
for the weak spots in his argument, for the things that you can attack.  I spent three years of my life learning how
to listen this way, how to find the flaws in the other guy’s position and nail
him to the wall.  However, it is almost
never a good idea to practice this skill in real life.

Instead, we need to listen with open minds and hearts.  This involves first of all loving truth more
than being right.  Maybe the other guy
has a point, and if we don’t listen to him and try to figure out what he’s
saying, we’ll never understand it, and we’ll be the poorer for it.  Similarly, we need to work not just to
understand the point, but understand the person.  A lot of the time, disagreements arise as a
result of different perspectives, and when we’re able to figure out where
somebody’s coming from, that will often be key to resolving the disagreement.

Third, we must be careful to **THINK BEFORE SPEAKING.** Consider
the stern warning of Proverbs 29:20. 
Impulsive speech is such a problem because of how thoughtless it is.  I haven’t thought this through.  I haven’t considered how it’s going to be
received.  However, I really want to say
it, so here I go!  At that point, you’re
basically just rolling the dice.  Maybe
it’s going to work out well.  Maybe
you’re not going to needlessly offend somebody. 
Maybe you’re not going to look like an idiot.  However, sooner or later, impulsive speech
will certainly get us in trouble.

The only cure for the disease is thought.  We must first of all recognize that there are
few things that absolutely must be said right now.  Silence is always an option, and until we’ve
got things worked out in our minds, it may well be the best option.  Even if somebody is pressing us for an
answer, we can always say, “I’d like a few minutes to think about that.  Can I get back to you in a little bit?”  In fact, most people will take that as a
compliment because it shows that we’re seriously considering what they’ve said.  That will give us the opportunity not only to
figure out what we should say but also how we should word it.  Considered speech is always more effective.

Finally, we all need to work on **BEING PATIENT.** Consider the
encouragement of Colossians 3:12.  There
are a lot of virtues discussed in this one verse, but patience is far from the
least of them.  There are many times in
our lives when patience is important, and it’s especially important when we’re
working to resolve a disagreement.  As
long as we’re making sure that our goal is winning the person, not winning the
argument, patience will always help us toward it.

It may be that we need to be patient with what they’re
saying.  I can think of people I know who
argue like an answering machine.  You
push the button, and they will say exactly the same thing.  It doesn’t matter that you just shot down the
argument five minutes ago; they’re still going to return to the same talking
point.  I realized a while back that
people weren’t doing this just to annoy me. 
The problem is that their brain hasn’t caught up with their mouth
yet.  Give them time, and eventually
they’ll work through to a more reasoned position.

Similarly, some people just need a while for other arguments
to sink in.  I’m like this.  I don’t like to change my mind quickly.  I can do it if I have to, but I’d much rather
have a day or two to reflect.  As a
result, Lauren has learned that sometimes, it’s best to make a case and then
drop the subject.  Continued argument is
actually counterproductive.  Not
everybody is like this, but many people are, and we need to understand them well
enough to know when less is actually more.

**Things to Think**

In addition to learning to behave in godly ways during a
dispute, it’s also important for us to learn to think in godly ways.  Of these godly thought patterns, perhaps the
most important is learning to **KEEP OUR
COOL.** Look here at James
1:19-20.  In addition to reminding us of
a bunch of things we’ve already considered, James warns us against the dangers
of anger.  Generally speaking, whenever
some disagreement arises, the biggest downside risk is that somebody is going
to get angry.  When everybody is calm, we
can work toward a solution.  However,
once tempers start heating up, the new goal is to avoid doing damage to relationships.  It’s certainly possible for us to say unwise
things even when we haven’t lost our tempers, but the odds go way up when we’re
mad.

The key here is to remember that somebody can disagree with
us without necessarily being a bad person. 
They can even press their point pretty strongly without becoming a bad
person.  The angrier we get, though, the
more and more trouble we have seeing this. Once we give into the temptation and
start treating others like bad people, we almost guarantee that we’re not going
to be able to come to a resolution. 
We’re not going to conduct ourselves in a way that glorifies God,
either.  We need to know the signs of
anger in ourselves and stamp it out when it appears.

As part of this, we must learn to **BELIEVE THE BEST** about others. 
Paul reminds us of this aspect of love in 1 Corinthians 13:7.  I, at least, really struggle with this one
when disagreements start becoming more intense. 
At that point, I start telling myself stories about why they don’t agree
with me, and the stories are never flattering. 
This person disagrees with me not because they simply see things
differently, but because they’ve conceived some kind of vendetta against me,
and the argument is the means they’ve chosen to express their hostility.  This person disagrees with me because they’re
a bad person with an evil heart.  This
person disagrees with me because they are arrogant and never listen.  And so on.

I’m convinced that whenever those stories start popping up
in my head, they are the work of the devil, who is trying to sow trouble in my
life.  If I listen to them and believe
them, I will almost certainly ratchet up the tension in the argument and start
to behave with less consideration.  Be
evil, will they?  Well, I’ll show them!  Once I start acting that way, I always end up
regretting it.

Instead, rather than assuming the worst about others, we’re
much better off assuming the best.  Yes,
this person disagrees with me, but they are sincere and acting from good
motives, so if I work with them to solve the problem, we’ll be able to arrive
at a good solution.  Interestingly
enough, just as assuming the worst about somebody drags them down to be the
worst, assuming the best about them lifts them up to be the best.  If we are gracious, we’ll generally receive
graciousness in return.  This godly habit
of thought has the potential to change the whole direction of a conflict.

Similarly, we must all **BEWARE
CONTEMPT.** Our Lord warns us of the
dangers of contempt in Matthew 5:21-22. 
When I was growing up, my mother never allowed me to use the word “fool”
because of this passage, but in all honesty, I think that misses the
point.  This passage isn’t really about
the particular words we use.  It’s about
the heart that causes us to use those words. 
Whether we say “fool” or not, if we speak contemptuously, we are
endangering our souls.

It seems like contempt is a particular problem in online
conversation.  Maybe because we aren’t
sitting down across a table from somebody, it’s easier to forget that somebody
we disagree with is a human being who has been created in the image of
God.  It’s easier to lump them in with a
whole category of Them that is inherently less worthy of love and respect
because they don’t agree with Us.  We
reduce them to a caricature of their convictions and then sneer at the
caricature we have created.  That may be
very satisfying, but it only turns disagreement into personal animosity.

Instead, friends, we must remember that we have a duty to
love and honor everyone we come in contact with, from our enemies on up.  Even if somebody has shown by their actions
that they are worthy of contempt, that contempt can never come from us.  We don’t show them respect for their
sake.  We show them respect for the
Lord’s sake, and that can never change.

Sometimes, though, we must learn to **RECOGNIZE THE IMPOSSIBLE.** Jesus
points this out in Matthew 7:6.  We ought
to listen to others, but sometimes people don’t listen to us.  We ought to believe the best about everyone,
but it’s still true that there are people out there who are acting from impure
motives.  We must treat others with
respect, but there are plenty of people who won’t show us the same
courtesy.  We must to be able to spot
this bad behavior and learn when we need to walk away.  Maybe it means that we concede the point
because the point isn’t worth the argument. 
Maybe it means we end the relationship because nothing good will come
from it.  Regardless, all of us need to
have a sense of when the time has come to cut our losses.

If we don’t learn to do this, if we insist on continuing to
beat our heads against a brick wall, nothing good will come of it.  We will probably only harden the other
person’s opposition to us, and we may well be exposing ourselves to all sorts
of personal abuse too.  Once a
disagreement has reached this point, there is no more upside, only downside.  We should act accordingly.

**Jesus and Peace**

Finally this morning, let’s consider the greatest
conflict-resolution expert of all time and what He did to bring peace to our
lives.  I refer, of course, to Jesus, and
we can only appreciate what He did when we consider **OUR SITUATION.** Here, let’s
read from Ephesians 2:11-12.  There are
passages in Scripture where you simply do not want to find yourself, and this
is one of them.  Just listen to the
words:  Separate.  Excluded. 
Strangers.  Hopeless.  Godless. 
Basically, anything we might think of that we would want to be, we
weren’t.  Before Jesus, only Jews and
people who were willing to become Jews in everything but DNA were part of the
people of God.  It was the best club on the
planet, and Gentiles weren’t invited.

However, that problem pales in comparison to the problem of
being without God.  God is everything to
human existence.  It’s simply not
possible to live a meaningful life apart from Him.  Once we had separated ourselves from Him by
our sins, we were estranged from our only hope of eternal life, and there was
nothing we could do to bridge the gap.

This dismal situation, though, changed utterly because of **THE WORK OF CHRIST.** Consider Ephesians 2:13-18.  In this text, Paul outlines the solution to
our problem, but he begins by pointing out what the solution cost.  Our reconciliation came at the price of the
blood of Jesus.  Anything less than the
death of the Son of God would not have been able to bring us near.

However, through that death, Jesus accomplished for us what
we ourselves could not.  First, in His
death, He blew up the Law of Moses.  It
was the Law that kept Jews and Gentiles separate and kept the latter from
belonging to God’s people.  This might
seem abstract to us, but back in the day, it was a source of considerable
racial tension and even hatred.  Jews and
Gentiles had very little to do with each other, and Jews were quick to treat
even God-fearing Gentiles as second-class citizens.  Christ took these warring factions and on the
cross, He joined them into one.

His peacemaking didn’t end with making peace between Jew and
Gentile.  Instead, His sacrifice was so
powerful that it even made peace between man and God.  Even the Jews, though they didn’t know it,
needed someone to pay the price for their sins. 
Otherwise, even those who attempted to keep the Law of Moses were still
enemies of God.  Jesus put to death that
enmity, and He put to death the enmity between the Gentiles and God too.  He graciously took on Himself the guilt of
our sins so that all of us could come together to our Father.

We see **THE RESULT** of
His work in Ephesians 2:19-22.  Notice
the way that Paul begins this context with a series of opposites.  We used to be aliens, but now we are citizens
of heaven.  Note, by the way, that
citizenship or not was a big deal 2000 years ago too!  Similarly, we used to be strangers, but now
we belong to God’s household.  Before, we
couldn’t even have gotten through the front door, but now the Father accepts us
in as His adopted sons and daughters. 
Why?  Not because of anything that
we did right, but because of what Jesus did right.

However, Jesus’ unifying work continues on past that
point.  He doesn’t only give us
relationships.  He gives us a
purpose.  We don’t get to decide what
that purpose is.  That’s the point of us
being built on the foundation of the apostles, prophets, and Jesus.  A building has to stay true to its
foundation, and we have to stay true to them.

If we do, we have the opportunity to do something
wonderful.  We will be built into an
edifice where God dwells and that will give Him glory.  Before, we were like a bunch of rocks
scattered around in a field someplace. 
In Christ, we have been united into a holy temple.

Before the Lord’s Supper, let’s reflect on this.  Without Jesus, literally everything about our
spiritual lives that could be wrong was wrong because of our own foolishness
and sin.  We were at odds with everybody,
including God.  However, the mediation of
Christ brought us near to one another and to Him.  Let’s remember this great act of peacemaking
as we partake.
