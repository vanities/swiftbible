**OVERVIEW.** The Giant-Print has almost all of the
traits I look for in a reader.  Only its
inevitably small characters-per-line count presents a problem.

I did two weeks’ worth of daily readings out of the GP,
though I didn’t do them on a daily basis. 
Here’s what I thought:

**SIZE.** I think I’m about to prove that this
category doesn’t matter much to me.  As
I’ve said throughout this review, the Bible is a brick.  Didn’t bother me when I was reading from it.  I noticed, but it wasn’t a significant
distraction.

**LAYOUT.** Here I am again, beating up on
giant-print Bibles.  I generally insist
that a double-column Bible needs to have about 40 characters per line to be a
truly top-notch reader.  It’s difficult
for a giant-print Bible to meet this standard, even when, as with the GP, the
Bible in question is a text Bible.  You
can only fit so many great big letters onto a single line.

In the case of the GP, it has a cpl count of about 37.  That’s not terrible, but it’s not optimal either.  The problems show up in the poetry of the Old
Testament, where, due to Crossway’s traditional practice of generous indenting,
the text has a number of widows and orphans.  
I’ve griped about the Crossway style sheet before, but other than that,
there’s nothing that the designers could have done to get the cpl count up and
still print the Bible in 14-point Lexicon. 
Unless you want to exceed the traditional 6 ½” width limit, which is *not* a good idea in a Bible that’s
already large and heavy, you’re stuck with the low count.  It is what it is.

Line length is a minor problem, but gutter width isn’t.  As I noted in my “Preaching, Teaching, and
Study” comments on the GP, this is a Bible with a half-inch gutter margin.  That’s plenty, even for a Bible as portly as
the GP is.  Even in Ecclesiastes and
Isaiah, the text doesn’t fall into the abyss.

Similarly, I have no complaints about Crossway’s decision to
make this a black-letter Bible.  I have
lots of complaints about red-letter Bibles (there’s usually something wrong
with them, no matter the publisher or printer), but putting the words of Jesus
in black allows for sharp execution. 
Good call by the designers here.

Better still, the GP is almost entirely line-matched (all
the prose is, but I guess that line-matching was impossible in some parts of
the poetic sections of the Old Testament). 
The older I get, the less tolerant I become of Bibles that aren’t
line-matched, unless the paper in question is extremely opaque (for Bible
paper, at least).  The GP has good paper,
but it’s line-matched too.  I did most of
my reading of the GP in my office at the church building, in which the
fluorescent light appears to be about ready to give up the ghost.  Despite the greenish flickering, no ghosts
emerged from facing pages to trouble me.

Finally, we come to the font size.  So far as I can tell, the GP is set in 14/15
Lexicon No. 1.  Lexicon is a bold, heavy
typeface to begin with, and at sizes that would be large for any book, let
alone a Bible, it reads like a billboard. 
If anything, the font was too large for me (which I never thought I
would say about a Bible!).  Because my
eye traveled considerably greater distances to go through a given chapter, I
felt like I was reading more, and doing more work, than I actually was.  However, it’s ludicrous to cite this as a
disadvantage.

**PRODUCTION
QUALITY.** To all the printers in
China I say, “You’ve come a long way, baby!” 
China-print Bibles from a decade ago were atrocious, but my 2014-print
GP offers no insult to the Scripture. 
Quite the contrary; with my naked eye, I’d be hard-pressed to discern
the difference between the GP and a Jongbloed-printed Bible, which is the gold
standard for Bible publishing.  The print
is dark and even throughout, and even the finest lines appear clear and
sharp.  A specialist might be able to
tell the GP from a Jongbloed Bible, but I can’t, and I usually can without
trying.

The paper is equally good. 
Here we find the culprit behind the Bible’s noticeable heft.  It’s got 2000 pages of excellent paper in
it—off the top of my head, I’d guess 36 gsm. 
Even the non-line-matched pages don’t have significant ghosting.  The paper is bright white paper rather than
cream-tinted, but that’s a minor issue to me when I like everything else about
it so well.

**CONCLUSION.** Here is where the GP excels.  I can see somebody with bad eyes struggling
to find a string of verse numbers in the GP, but if you’re basically looking
for chapter headings and reading from there, this may well be the best reading
Bible on the market for someone whose vision isn’t what it used to be.

For that matter, if you simply want to do readings from a
Bible with billboard-like print, you should at least seriously consider the
GP.  You’re never going to get a better
cpl count on a giant-print Bible, and the text block does everything else
right.  At the moment, Crossway doesn’t
make this Bible available in a trim above genuine leather, but the text block
is good enough to be an excellent rebind candidate.

I think it deserves a 9.5/10 as a reader.  I’m impressed.  Overall, the Bible rates a 23/30.  That’s a little below average, but remember
that my rating system is biased against giant-print Bibles (because I don’t
need one).  I’d recommend this Bible.
