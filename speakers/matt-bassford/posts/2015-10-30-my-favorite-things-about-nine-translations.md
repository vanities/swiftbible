One of the unintended consequences of my reviewing of Bibles
has been that I’ve been exposed to a wide variety of translations; wider, in
fact, then I would ever have considered otherwise.  Even though my translation home is (and
probably always will be) the NASB, I’ve found that even translations that are
on the opposite end of the spectrum from it are spiritually useful too.  Here are some of my favorite things about all
the translations I’ve used.



**ASV:  Second-Person Plural Pronouns.**  Even though it was published in 1901, the ASV
continues to employ the archaic pronouns of the KJV:  “thou/thee” and “ye/you”.  At first, I thought this was pointless
anachronism.  However, as I continued to
use the translation, I learned that the different pronouns communicate an
aspect of the Greek that modern English translations do not:  second-person plural pronouns.

Formal English uses “you” for both second-person singular
and second-person plural.  English
dialects may have second-person plural pronouns, for instance, “y’all” or “you
guys”.  This is useful for distinguishing
whether the speaker is addressing one member of a group or the entire group.

Likewise, in the ASV, the “thou/thee” pair is second-person
singular and the “ye/you” pair is second-person plural.  This has helped me to clarify my
understanding of several places in Scripture, most notably, the transition
between John 13 and John 14.  In John
13:38, Jesus is addressing Peter only; in John 14:1, He returns to addressing
all the disciples present.  Obviously,
the KJV also shares this feature, but I first noticed and appreciated it in the
ASV.

**NASB:  Supplied Words in Italics.** Every translation contains words that are
not present in the Greek.  However, the
NASB italicizes those words to show that they have been supplied by the
translator.  This allows me to study the
text with greater precision because it makes the translator’s work more
apparent.  I can see the translator’s
thought process in adding the supplied word and even ignore it if I don’t think
it clarifies the meaning of the text.

**ESV:  Wide Utility.**Of all the translations out there, I think the ESV has the best
claim to be the strongest all-rounder. 
It’s not as precise as the NASB is, but it reads more smoothly.  It’s not as smooth as the NIV, but it’s more
precise.  For those who aren’t already
familiar with the highly idiomatic nature of the NASB and want to adopt a
single translation, the ESV is probably the best choice.  As a result, whenever I buy a Bible for one
of my students in the downstairs Bible classes at Joliet, I buy them an ESV.

**KJV:  Public Reading.** Even though the KJV is a
formal-equivalent, word-for-word translation, in many ways, it functions more
like a dynamic-equivalent translation today. 
The unfamiliar vocabulary and word usage often obscures the precise
meaning of the text, much like a dynamic-equivalent translation will.  As a result, the KJV will often be the source
of frustration if used for close study.

However, in the public reading of large sections of Scripture,
when precise understanding of the text isn’t really the point, the KJV
shines.  It inarguably has the most
beautiful English of any translation, and when it is read out loud, ideally a
chapter or more at a time, the cadenced grandeur of its language invests the
Scripture with the dignity it deserves. 
Never does the Bible sound more like the inspired word of God than when it
is read from the KJV.

**NKJV:  Broken-Out Dialogue.**Many translations put passages of dialogue in Scripture into one big paragraph.  The NKJV, by contrast, follows the practice common in novels and begins a new paragraph whenever the speaker changes.  This nifty bit of formatting helps clarify who is talking when.

**HCSB:  Use of “Yahweh”.** Most English translations follow the
traditional Jewish practice of not translating the Tetragrammaton (“YHWH”) when
it appears in the Old Testament, generally using instead a small-caps “Lord”. 
I’m not a fan.  The problem with “Lord” is that it reads like a title, not
a name.  This results in a subtle
depersonalization of the Old Testament. 
God comes across more as an office-holder than an actual being.

The HCSB, by contrast, uses “Yahweh” hundreds of times in
the Old Testament.  I find that this adds
a welcome individual dimension to my reading of the OT. No more does the sin of
Israel come across as rule-breaking. 
Instead, it is a personal offense offered to God by His covenant
people.  I think all of us would benefit
if we thought of our own relationships with God in similarly personal terms.

**NIV:  Restrained Interpretation.** With the NIV (and, to a lesser extent,
the HCSB), we’ve shifted across the spectrum from word-for-word
formal-equivalent translations to thought-for-thought dynamic-equivalent
translations.  Translators working
according to dynamic-equivalent principles will use their own words to
translate a verse if they believe the result more clearly indicates the
author’s original intent.

This leads to a translation that requires much less mental
effort to figure out (it reads like normal English instead of like Greek and
Hebrew rewritten using English words) but isn’t as precise.  When I’m using the freer dynamic-equivalent
translations, the NLT especially, I disagree with the interpretation the
translator places on the text with some regularity.

From what I’ve observed, the NIV doesn’t take that kind of liberties nearly as much.  Some
of the old problems with “sinful nature” in the NIV84 are retained in the
NIV11, but outside of that, I feel like the thought-for-thought renderings in
the translation do a pretty good job of capturing the thought of the
authors.  The result is a
dynamic-equivalent Bible that is more precise than most of its
dynamic-equivalent brethren.

**REB:  Fresh Renderings**.  The REB is kind of a quirky translation.  It doesn’t see much use on this side of the
Atlantic, but it takes a much more literary approach to the text than many more
familiar translations do.  It’s an
unabashedly dynamic-equivalent translation, but rather than just presenting a
straightforward interpretations, the translators obviously took pains to render
the Scripture in vivid and memorable language. 
For instance, where stodgier translations will say of Jesus and the
Pharisees that He was “grieved by their hardness of heart,” the REB will say,
“grieved by their obstinate stupidity.”  I
can hardly dispute the characterization of the Pharisees as obstinately
stupid!  As a result, an REB makes a
great second-look Bible, containing many delights for readers who love words.

**NLT:  The Old Testament.** The NLT makes no bones about its
translation philosophy.  Unless a strict
translation of the original is as clear as a cloudless sky, the NLT will reword
it until the rewording is as clear as a cloudless sky.  Needless to say, this results in the loss of
a lot of subtlety and nuance.

However, I feel that the NLT’s clarity-first approach really
works well in the Old Testament.  With
few exceptions, I’m not much concerned with precise word-by-word study of the
Hebrew Scriptures.  Generally, when I’m
there, I just want the gist of it.  This
is what the NLT gives me in a couple of different ways.  First, I think the NLT translators’ notes are
exceptionally helpful and good.  Second,
because the NLT frees me from having to puzzle through awkward phrasing, I come
away from the text with a much better handle on the emotions and motivations of
the characters.  When I read Jeremiah in
the NLT, for instance, I clearly see the depth of God’s anger and grief at the
sin of His people.  This makes the text
come alive in a way that word-for-word translations often struggle to manage.

As always, I remain convinced that there are no perfect
translations, but there are many valuable ones. 
I insist with the translators of the KJV that “the very meanest
translation of the Bible in English. . . containeth the word of God, nay, is
the word of God.”  No good-faith
translation will excel at everything, but every one of them is useful for
something.
