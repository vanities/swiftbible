**OVERVIEW.** The Large-Print Thinline Reference may
well have been designed from the ground up as a preacher’s Bible.  Its double-column, verse-by-verse reference
format is admirably suited for preaching and study, and its moderate size makes
it fairly easy to hold for prolonged periods of time.

As always, I used the LPUT for two weeks in my work as a
preacher.  Pretty much everything I did
with a Bible, I did with it:  a sermon, a
funeral, four auditorium Bible classes, four small-group studies, and two
weeks’ worth of study, preparation, and generic poking around in the word of
God.  Here’s what I thought:

**PREACHING.** For most of my Bible-blogging career,
I’ve preached from a sheet of copier paper tucked inside the cover of my
Bible.  I’m not doing that anymore, so it
wasn’t terribly important to me how big the LPUT was.  However, had I wanted to conceal a sermon
outline inside it, it would certainly have been up to the task.

Once I started preaching, the LPUT continued to perform
well.  The first mark in its favor is its
double-column, verse-by-verse format. 
Say what you will about how verse-by-verse Bibles aren’t as easy to read
(and honestly, I’ve found this complaint to be a little overblown), but they
excel in the pulpit.  I’m fine with using
a double-column paragraphed Bible to preach, but a double-column verse-by-verse
Bible is even more effective than that.

As I said above, I only preached one sermon using the LPUT,
but in that one sermon, I put the Bible through a workout!  The sermon was topical in nature and required
me to quickly locate ten different Scriptures. 
I didn’t miss a citation, and I don’t remember having any trouble
finding one either.  If you’re a preacher
and aren’t looking to write your sermon outlines in the margins of your Bible,
an LPUT would be an excellent choice.

In fact, in a pinch, the LPUT might actually be able to
serve as a wide-margin Bible.  Like many
larger Bibles, its margins are more generous than those of the thinlines toward
which I gravitate.  Instead of
quarter-inch inner and outer margins, the margins on the LPUT are about ⅝”.

That doesn’t sound like much, and indeed I didn’t consider
trying to write a sermon outline in its margins while I was using it, but once
I started writing the review, I got to thinking.  True, ⅝” isn’t much space, but the LPUT is
verse-by-verse and relatively large-print, so you wouldn’t *have* to put many words in a given ⅝” span.

Then, I got to ciphering. 
In the Cambridge NASB Wide-Margin, commonly accounted the gold standard
of wide-margin Bibles, John 21 comes with about 13 square inches of margin
space.  A wide-margin Foundation
Side-Column Reference offers 12 square inches of margin space in John 21,
though of course that margin space is more valuable because it’s all on the
outside.

The LPUT?  About 11.4 square
inches of margin space through the chapter. 
That ain’t half bad!  Admittedly,
the inner margins would be super-tight, but the setting and trim size of the
LPUT would allow it to be a workable, if not an ideal, wide-margin Bible.

My biggest concern about using a currently-produced LPUT as
a wide-margin Bible is the paper.  It
simply isn’t very opaque and might cause considerable ghosting and
bleed-through.  I advise the would-be
Bible writer to test his writing instrument of choice someplace inconspicuous
before marking up John 3 or something. 
Of course, the LPUT’s printed in 2003 and 2007 don’t have this
problem.  If you’re determined to write
in an LPUT, you might want to consider seeing what you can find on the
Bible-auction market.

**TEACHING.**Normally, I insist on using smaller,
lighter Bibles for teaching Bible classes. 
I like to roam around a lot with my Bible in my hand, and over the span
of 45 minutes, a large, heavy Bible can get annoying.  However, I wasn’t annoyed by the LPUT.  It weighs a touch over two pounds, and
apparently that’s below my Bible-weight-obnoxiousness threshold.  It’s also possible that the edge-lined
binding made the Bible more pliable and easier to carry.  Whatever the reason, I never left it on the
lectern.  Instead, I carried it with me
quite happily throughout each class.

The LPUT is a reference Bible.  According to the box, it has all 95,000-plus
NASB references.  I didn’t go through and
count, so we’ll just have to take the publisher’s word for that.

I like that Foundation obviously put some thought into the
design of the reference column.  Unlike
the reference column in the earlier SCR, for instance, the reference column in
the LPUT is in a contrasting sans-serif font, probably Arial or something like
that.  That adds visual interest to the
page, and it also makes the reference column more distinct from the text
column.

So far, so good. 
However, the layout of the LPUT doesn’t do much to address the basic
problem of center-column reference Bibles, which is that the references and
verses don’t line up with each other.  In
a single-column Bible like the SCR, they do. 
Glance over to the outside of a given verse, and there are its cross-references.  In the LPUT, by contrast, the references for
the verse at the top of the right-hand column are probably going to be a little
more than halfway down the page.  That
makes them hard to find.

Other center-column reference Bibles, most notably the Pitt
Minion line, use a couple of different layout tricks to ameliorate these
problems.  They start the references for
the right-hand column at the bottom and fill up, which leaves a gap in the
middle between the references for the left and right columns.  Also, Pitts left-align the references that go
with the left column and right-align the references that go with the right
column.  As a result, even though it’s
not easy to find the right reference in a Pitt, at least it’s easier.

The LPUT, on the other hand, does neither of these things,
and finding the right reference in the column is fairly difficult as a
result.  There were several points in my
teaching and study when I wanted to find a reference, and I couldn’t
intuitively glance at the center column and pick out the reference I
wanted.  Instead, I had to think about
where to find it.  As always, design that
forces you to think is bad.

**STUDY.** The print in the LPUT is much larger than
the print in the Pitt Minions I’m used to. 
Except for those whose eyes are truly terrible, the Bible really
couldn’t be much easier to read. 
Likewise, even though the references are clunky, they are certainly
there.  Reference format is less
important in an office Bible than in a Bible-class Bible because office study
is rarely time-pressured.  If I have to
spend a few seconds figuring out where a reference is, it’s hardly a big deal.

If anything, the only problem I noticed with the LPUT is
that it’s a little too big to sit comfortably in my lap when I’m sitting at my
computer.  It keeps bumping into either
the desk or my belly.  It may be that I
should solve the problem by losing a few pounds, but I prefer to blame it on
the Bible.

**CONCLUSION.** The LPUT makes a fine preacher’s
Bible.  It’s a design well suited for
reference and close study, and it’s in the translation that (in my opinion) is
the best suited for close study.  I’m
going to ding it a little bit for an unwieldy reference column and being a hair
on the big side, but overall, it’s entitled to a 9/10 for preaching, teaching,
and study.
