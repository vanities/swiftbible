I’ve heard it said that one of the great achievements of
American religion has been the conversion of the church into a
corporation.  Even though the impact of
this has been somewhat limited in churches of Christ because of our strong
insistence on congregational autonomy, there are nonetheless a number of
brethren who believe that the church ought to take its cues from the business
world.

Though I certainly agree that Christians and churches alike
ought to excel in everything they do for the Lord, I’m not at all certain that
the modern corporation ought to be our benchmark for excellence.  In fact, there are several ways in which the
spirit of the corporate world seems antithetical to the spirit of Christ.



First, corporate America seeks achievement that can be
quantified.  Profits and share prices
must continually increase, or else changes must be made.  This makes sense for an organization that
exists to make money (which is a quantity), but the paradigm doesn’t translate
well to other areas.

Consider, for instance, the recent testing epidemic in
American schools.  It is the product of a
corporate mindset.  The success or
failure of a teacher is reduced to a student’s ability to score well on a
standardized test.  Schools with high
average scores are rewarded; schools with low average scores are punished.

The problems that this has produced are many and
profound.  It fails to account for the
fact that not all students or groups of students come from similar backgrounds
with similar opportunities to learn.  A
teacher may have great success in helping a student manage their behavior
problems, yet not see that success reflected in test scores.  Budgets for art and music programs are slashed
because the contributions of those things to child welfare can’t be measured by
testing.  Finally, and without anyone
realizing it, the lives of American children now focus on the soul-crushing
drudgery of learning to take standardized tests.  I fear that the results will be very
different from what the architects of these programs intended.

So too for the church. 
If church becomes about numbers like attendance and weekly contribution,
that focus will lead to shortfalls in areas not so easily quantified.  Let’s start with attendance.  If the number of people in attendance on
Sunday morning is what matters most, those people have become a commodity, and
church leaders have every incentive to treat them like a commodity.  It no longer matters what happens to a
particular disciple so long as the overall number of disciples is
increasing.

Indeed, in *The
Purpose-Driven Church* (which is something of a Bible for megachurches),
Rick Warren urges church leaders not to waste time on weak Christians who are
struggling and in danger of falling away. 
Instead, they should focus on evangelism and bringing in new converts, because
it’s more efficient to teach a dozen people than to spend months persuading one
weak brother to repent.

Rarely have I read a statement that is both so correct and
so wrong.  Yes; I’m sure it’s less
efficient to spend all kinds of time working with my spiritually ill brother,
BUT HE’S MY BROTHER!  I don’t have the right
to write off anybody for whom Jesus died because it’s too much trouble to
rescue them.  My brother is not a
number.  He is a soul in need of help.

Also, a focus on numerical growth is likely to lead to a
diminished focus on Scripture.  As the cliché
goes, in the business world, the customer is always right.  In the megachurch world, the “customer” is
the unchurched.  They’re the ones who are
always right, so whatever we can do to cater to them is something that we
should do.

This mindset inevitably leads to apostasy.  The past 20 years have seen corporate-minded churches
of Christ abandon the teachings of Scripture on instrumental music and even
baptism for the sake of being more accessible to outsiders.  These churches have changed dramatically so
that worldly people who visit them will have to change as little as possible,
which is precisely the opposite of the pattern of the New Testament.  As we see in John 6, Jesus stuck by the truth
even if it meant that His disciples would desert Him in droves.  In the church, it must be the Lord, not the
customer, who is always right.

Underneath it all, I think the corporate mindset reveals the
conviction that it is human wisdom, not God, who gives the increase.  They envision the church as kind of a
spiritual factory, where the unchurched enter as raw materials, are subjected
to a number of precisely controlled processes, and emerge from the other end as
finished disciples.  Everything is predictable.  Nothing is left to chance.

Factories are a reflection of the mind of man, but they are
not a reflection of the mind of God.  The
created world around us is not neat and orderly.  Rivers do not flow in straight lines; plants
do not grow only where they are supposed to. 
I spend all summer pulling weeds out of my garden and flowerbeds (or
making my children do it).

And yet, all this apparent chaos is ordered at a level of complexity
far beyond our understanding.  Even the
most powerful computers in existence cannot accurately model the weather, but
the sun still shines, the rain still falls, and the seasons follow in orderly
progression.  An entire ecosystem may
depend on some tiny little insect we’d never even notice.  We don’t know what’s going to happen, but God
does.  We don’t understand why there are
seeds in the soil of the desert until the rain comes and the barren landscape
is overwhelmed with flowers.

I think that’s the way churches should be.  Our job is not to control the future.  It is to be the best disciples that we can
be, diligently keeping every commandment of the Lord, even when, and especially
when, we think we see a shortcut.  The
working out of God’s will is not a smooth acceleration like a car on a freeway
on-ramp.  It’s more like the course of a
stream in the mountains:  a waterfall
here, an apparent reversal there, with never a smooth stretch to be seen.  However, as the water in that stream will
inevitably find its way to the ocean, so the will of God will inevitably achieve
its end too.  We don’t need to improve on
His plan.  We only need to trust Him and
follow it.
