**OVERVIEW.** The Giant-Print is a large, heavy Bible
without room for serious marginal notes. 
In my evaluation scheme, those things tend to be Strikes One and
Two.  However, the Bible was perfectly serviceable
for all occupation-related uses.

I wielded the GP in the pulpit for two sermons and used it
to teach three conference-table Bible classes. 
I’m also teaching the preschool class this quarter, but I didn’t find
much call to use it there.  Here’s what I
thought overall:



**PREACHING.** This thing’s a load, man!  That is my predominant impression of the GP
while using it to preach.  In my
subjective experience, somewhere between three and three-and-a-half pounds, a
Bible shifts from being heavy, but bearable, to being heavier than I really
want to use.  The GP is on the wrong side
of that line.

Stylistically, I like to spend a lot of time out of the
pulpit these days.  I think it makes my
presentation less formal, which mitigates one of my weaknesses as a
preacher.  However, the GP is heavy
enough that I wouldn’t want to spend 45 minutes holding it, even though I’m a
37-year-old man in decent shape.  I could
do it, but it would be uncomfortable enough to become a distraction.

As a result, the GP ended up tethering me to the pulpit more
than I generally prefer.  Our wireless
mic wasn’t working during the review period, so I was tethered to the pulpit
anyway, but the GP cinched things.  In
fact, during my first sermon, I found the Bible so awkward that I set it down
on the pulpit to turn to passages, which I’ve never noticed myself doing
before.  It was a combination, I think,
of weight and large, awkward pages.

All of that bulk, however, does not accommodate my most
desired feature in a preaching Bible: 
wide margins.  The margins in the
GP are about ⅜” on the outside and ½” on the inside.  That’s enough to set the text off from the
edge of the paper, and also to keep it from falling into the gutter, but it’s
not enough to scrawl a sermon outline in, which is what I really want to do.

On the plus side, I had no trouble locating and correctly
citing passages in the GP.  In a Bible
with 14-point font, even the superscript verse numbers are going to be 6-point
bold, which, in a double-column Bible, is more than enough for me to
notice.  The paper in these Bibles is
also high-gsm, the setting is line-matched, and the print is dark.  I could have set the Bible on the pulpit and
read it even without my glasses on!

If you’re a preacher in search of a Bible you can still use
in the pulpit, this may be what you’re looking for.  It depends. 
As my wife’s Uncle Chuck observes, part of the problem with growing
older is that your hands grow weak along with your eyes, so that those who most
need large-print Bibles are least able to hold them.  If you want a giant-print Bible that’s easy
to hold in your hand for a long time, the GP isn’t it.  On the other hand, if you want a Bible you
can put down on the pulpit and then pick up periodically, the GP is the
clearest-print Bible I’ve ever used.

**TEACHING.** I never used the GP to teach any
auditorium classes, so size was never an issue. 
In all the classes I taught, I rested it on the table all the time.  However, I’m sure that if I had been teaching
an auditorium class, the GP would have stayed on the lectern.  Generally, I like Bible-class Bibles to be
light enough to carry around and wave like a flyswatter.

Despite its massive size, the GP doesn’t have most
cross-references.  It has references to
Old-Testament quotations in the New Testament and parallel synoptic passages,
but nothing more.  I missed the
references that weren’t there.  This was
most notable during the last class of last quarter, when I was teaching
Revelation.  There were also a couple of
points during informal studies when I wanted to run something down with
references, and couldn’t.  In my
experience, a cross-reference suite is like a can of Fix-A-Flat.  When you have it, you don’t need it, but when
you don’t have it, then you do.

Also, the references that are present are in 7- or 8-point
italic font at the bottom of the page.  I
can’t help wondering whether someone who needs a giant-print Bible could make
out the references.

**STUDY.** Of the three areas I cover in this stage
of the evaluation, the GP performed best here. 
It’s a classic lap Bible.  I could
easily glance down and read it without picking it up, though as is often the
case, it moved me farther away from my keyboard than I wanted to go.  The maps in the back are prettily executed,
though I’m not sure why they have a reference grid when the Bible does not
appear to have a gazetteer.  So that you
can write down the grid coordinates for Shiloh once you find it, maybe?

As I would expect, throughout the two weeks in which I used
the GP, I never noticed myself making a citation mistake.  This is a Bible that lends itself to
error-free work, which is important.

**CONCLUSION.** Once again, the GP is a Bible constructed
to provide something that isn’t all that important to me—extreme
readability.  In order to achieve that
goal, it sacrifices several things that are more important:  wide margins, cross-references, and smaller
size.  As a consequence, it can’t fare
well in my subjective rating process.

Before I get to the number, though, it’s worth noting that
those who do care about extreme readability in a paper Bible would do well to
consider this one.  For preachers in that
position, my only concern is that verse numbers, translators’ notes, and references
are in a much smaller font than the ultra-readable main text.



For me, though, and for what I want to use a Bible for, this
one merits a 6.5/10 in preaching, teaching, and study.
