**OVERVIEW.**  This Bible was designed from the ground up
for preachers and teachers, and it shows. 
The only complaint I have about it is that I wish there were room to
write in the inner margins.



As I always do, I used the Verse-by-Verse as my primary
preaching Bible for two weeks.  During
its two-week tour of duty, I used it to preach three sermons, teach six Bible
classes, and prep for all of the above. 
Here’s what I thought:

**PREACHING.** As I mentioned during the first part of
the review, the VbV is a big, handsome Bible. 
Back in the old days when I preached off of a half-sheet outline, this
might have been a benefit.  Now, however,
when I’m preaching topical sermons, I write my outline in a little
notebook.  As a result, the VbV’s size
became something of a liability.  Because
the pulpit in the Joliet church building is a small pulpit, I couldn’t have
both the notebook and the Bible open right next to each other.  Instead, I ended up placing the notebook
halfway under the Bible, so that only the side of the page that I was preaching
from was visible.  This wasn’t a big
deal, but it was a worthwhile reminder that depending on how much room you
have, a big Bible might take some maneuvering.

As big as it was, the VbV wasn’t quite big enough to allow
me to do something that I wanted to do: 
write my sermon outlines in the margins. 
Because the VbV is a double-column Bible, both the inner and outer
margins need to be able to hold an expository outline.  At ⅞”,
the outer margins are large enough to be usable, particularly because a
verse-by-verse layout offers plenty of crannies to tuck more words in.  However, the inner margins are maybe ½”, and
that’s simply not enough room to include more than very brief comments.

However, for those who aren’t looking to put very much in
the margins, the paper looks to me like it would be opaque enough to bear
writing on.  I haven’t tested this
because I plan to pass the Bible on, but I suspect that the usual Bible-writing
implements (005 Pigma Microns, etc.) would work fine here.

Note-taking issues aside, the VbV excels at the primary work
of a pulpit Bible:  enabling quick and
accurate location of the passages cited in the sermon.  In a perfect world, the VbV would maybe be a
little bit smaller.  As always, when I’m
turning pages larger than 6” x 9”, they’re a little more unwieldy than pages in
a smaller Bible would be.

However, this didn’t particularly bother me, and once I was
on the right page, I had zero trouble locating the verse that I wanted.  As with any verse-by-verse Bible, verse
numbers in the VbV appear along the left side of each column.  They’re even boldfaced.  All of the sermons I preached using the VbV
were topical sermons that used 11-12 different verses each.  Never did I have trouble locating the verse
that I wanted; never did I read the wrong verse or stop reading before I meant
to.  Like a good offensive line, the VbV
did its job without attracting attention.

**TEACHING**.  I didn’t teach our auditorium Bible class the
entire time I was reviewing the VbV, so I was never put in a position where I
would have to wander around holding the thing for 45 minutes.  However, because the VBV is a good five
ounces lighter than my Cambridge Wide-Margin standby, which I have used for that
purpose, I suspect it wouldn’t have given me any trouble.

The VbV contains the usual 80,000-reference ESV reference
suite, which should be plenty of references for anyone.  It also organizes those references in a
distinctively Crosswegian way.  Rather
than putting them in a column down the center like most double-column reference
Bibles, the VbV keeps its cross-references in a block in the lower right-hand
corner.  Translators’ notes and alternate
readings appear at the bottom of the page.

I think this is a vast improvement over the usual center
reference column.  The problem with the
latter is that in a center-column reference Bible, the references are rarely
next to the verse from which they come. 
Also, the center reference column takes up valuable horizontal real
estate, which generally makes center-column reference Bibles less useful for
reading than they should be.

By contrast, it’s no harder to locate a reference in the VbV
than it is in a center-column reference Bible. 
In fact, I think it’s easier because there are no misleading visual
cues.  I don’t expect the reference to be
next to the verse, so I don’t look for it there.  Also, putting the references in a block
rather than in a column means that the VbV doesn’t sacrifice anything as a
reader.  All in all, this is a great
design choice, and Crossway is to be commended for adopting it.

**STUDY**.  In my office, the size of the VbV was both a
blessing and a curse.  The print in it
was large enough that I could read comfortably without eyestrain.  However, it wasn’t entirely comfortable for
me to set the VbV in my lap while I was at my computer and type over it.  I have vague memories of using the VbV’s
cross-references to run down something during my study, but the very fuzziness
of those memories indicates that the Bible did its job without making me think
about it.

When it came time to correctly cite passages, however, the
VbV performed like a champ.  I did have a
rather conspicuous citation error in one of my sermons.  I meant to cite Hebrews 13:2 and cited
Hebrews 12:2 instead.  However, that’s
not an error that the Bible led me to make. 
That one came from my own dumb brain. 
There is no Bible format in existence that will keep you from citing the
wrong chapter!



**CONCLUSION.** Unless you’re a minister who writes as
heavily in the margins as I do, the VbV might be the best preaching Bible on
the market.  The cross-referencing system
has a superior design, and you just can’t beat a verse-by-verse Bible for ease
of verse location.  Even though the Bible
keeps me from doing what I really want to do with a preaching Bible, I still
feel compelled to give it an 8.5/10 for Preaching, Teaching, and Study.
