It’s a tad unusual for the U2 frontman to have opinions on
anything I care much about, so when [this article](http://www.huffingtonpost.com/entry/bono-wants-christian-music-to-get-more-honest_us_5720f787e4b0f309baef690d) popped up on my newsfeed, I read it.  Confession:  I wasn’t actually brave enough to watch the
20-minute video.   I gather, though, that
Bono and Eugene Peterson (best known as the creator of *The Message*, a hip-for-2002 Bible paraphrase) see quite a
difference between the Psalms, which are often hard to read but honest, and
modern Christian music, which is easy to listen to but dishonest.



**Dark Psalms**

I think there’s quite a bit of truth to both halves of the
critique.  It may not be apparent from
call-to-worship Scripture readings on Sunday morning, but many of the Psalms
are quite dark.  The author of Psalm 137
is so upset about Edomite behavior during the sack of Jerusalem that he
expresses the hope that someone will take the Edomites’ small children and dash
them against rocks.  Psalm 88 does not
have a single optimistic line in it.  The
author starts the psalm miserable and finishes it miserable.  In Psalm 51, we see David groping desperately
for forgiveness after his sin with Bathsheba.

There are many other psalms that express sentiments as
shocking as these.  It is, indeed, life
in the raw.  None of the authors of the
Psalms appear to be concerned with following some cultural standard of decorum
in their expressions.

**One-Sided Worship**

By contrast, the repertoire in most modern worship services,
traditional or contemporary, has little in it that might shock or offend.  When we map their emotional content against
the emotional content of the book of Psalms, the darker half of the latter
doesn’t really have a modern counterpart. 
There are no howls of anger. 
There are no anguished confessions of sin.  There are no bitter questions aimed at God.

Even the music is relentlessly upbeat.  For about the past 150 years, minor-key hymns
have been vanishingly rare in the worship repertoire of most American
churches.  This is quite different from
the body of work of the Lutheran hymnists of the sixteenth through eighteenth
centuries (which a friend of mine who was raised Lutheran calls “Lutheran
funeral music”).  It also marks a dramatic
shift from the pre-Civil War Sacred Harp hymn tunes, many of which were
minor.  Most people find minor-key music
to be peculiarly haunting, plaintive, or even harsh, and those moods have been
largely absent from our hymn tunes.

The result isn’t so much life in the raw as it is life in
the sanitized.  Most of our song worship
occupies a relatively narrow emotional range. 
You have joyful hymns of praise. 
You have more didactic hymns.  You
even have “hymns of consolation”, which concern themselves with grief that is
decorous and pretty.

They’re the kinds of hymns that you sing when you’re at the
funeral of a beloved church member who has been a shining example of godliness
for the past 70 years.  They’re not the
kinds of hymns you sing when you’ve just driven your life off a cliff, or
somebody else has done it for you.  They’re
not about ugly grief.

I think the current repertoire is honest, so far as it
goes.  However, I don’t think it goes far
enough.  Bono suggests here that writers
of spiritual songs need to write about their failed marriages and their anger
at the government.

I can’t quite sign on to that.  In fact, I think it highlights the difference
between being a singer-songwriter and writing songs you will perform yourself,
and being a hymnist who writes songs for the church.  If you’re writing as a self-expression,
fine.  Be as specific as you want.  However, the details of your dysfunctional
relationship with your ex or with Uncle Sam will probably not resonate with a
worshiper who doesn’t share your situation.

**For Writers**

That quibble aside, I think we need, if not more honesty, at
least more openness in our worship.  This
has to start with writers who are willing to write about the ugliness of their
existence and tie it back to God.  The
specifics can and should stay below the surface, but themes of guilt, loss, anger,
and confusion will resonate with nearly everyone.  Life is often like that, whether we own it or
not.

I think I need to work on this.  Back in the day, I would write hymns about
being bitter and bewildered, about being crushed by my own stupidity, and even
though I was far from being a polished writer, people would pick those up and
sing them.  I also didn’t have a filter
for my mouth, which turned out to be a rough way to go through life.

As time passed, I developed the filter, but I dropped the
candor.  In the past 10 years, I’ve lost
my mother and a daughter, but I didn’t write about either one of those
things.  There was plenty of dark
underbelly to both events (I still struggle with being mad at my mom for
dying), and I’m sure that if I’d written about it, many Christians would have
identified with it.  However, I never
even tried to set pen to paper.  Maybe
what I felt didn’t line up with the sweetly pious resignation that I thought
was the only appropriate expression.  Writers,
then, do need to get out there and be real. 
Where we hurt, others will hurt too, and they need a voice.

**For Worshipers**

However, I don’t think writers are the only group that needs
to get a better handle on candor.  I
suspect that many worshipers are so used to sweetness and light in worship that
they would struggle with sacred song with a darker tinge.

A couple months ago, I tried out a hymn entitled “O Christ,
Our Hope in Grief” in a hymn-evaluation session.  It is a hymn about ugly grief.  I wrote it after a conversation I had with a
dear friend about his horrific experiences with being sexually abused as a
child.  His revelations left me
emotionally overwhelmed, gut-level certain that this was a problem that had to
go to Jesus, because nobody else could possibly handle it.

The hymn came from a dark place, and it’s a dark hymn.  It also proved to be a polarizing hymn.  I asked the singers in the evaluation session
to score all the hymns that were sung on a 1-10 scale according to how much
they would like to use the hymn in worship. 
The other five hymns had a bell curve. 
“O Christ, Our Hope in Grief” had a V curve.  Some people really wanted to sing it.  Others really did NOT.

I get that.  I
understand the desire to make the Sunday-morning assembly into a refuge, an
oasis from the awfulness of our sin-defiled world.  The monsters are out there, but only angels
are in here.

The problem is, though, that denying evil in a church is
like denying illness in a hospital.  When
you ignore the enemy, you don’t defeat him. 
You give him power.  As long as
churches have actual flesh-and-blood people in them, sanitized worship can
never be more than an elaborate game of let’s-pretend.  Maybe some worshipers haven’t been out there
fighting with the monsters recently. 
However, some have, and a corporate refusal to acknowledge their
struggle leaves them feeling like they don’t belong.  Sacred song can’t only be about what ought to
be.  It has to be about what is too.

**Discomfort Is Good**

As a writer, I don’t think I’ll ever be comfortable with
that, unless all my filters get ripped off by some unimaginable disaster.  Really, I’d prefer that God not put me
there.  However, I think I need to work
through the discomfort to say the things that all of us need to have said, at
least sometimes.

So too for worshipers. 
Maybe you aren’t in a place where only Jesus is holding you together,
but it’s nearly certain that you’re worshiping with somebody who is.  If you want them to help you with your
expression, you need to help them with theirs. 
Be willing not only to rejoice with those who rejoice, but to weep with
those who weep.

Bono would expect no less.
