The other day, I learned from this [this article](http://theweek.com/articles/710374/game-thrones-bad%E2%80%94bad) that Season 7 of the hit HBO miniseries *A
Game of Thrones* is almost upon us. 
As with previous iterations of the series, this inspires mixed emotions
in me.  Honestly, a big part of me would
love to watch it.  I am a diehard fantasy
geek, and just about anything with swords and knights and dragons will get my
pulse racing.



**My Experience with *A Game of Thrones***

However, I’m not going to watch a single episode.  *A Game
of Thrones* is exactly the sort of thing I can’t let into my mind and my
heart if I want to go to heaven. 
Admittedly, I’ve never seen the show, but 10 years ago, I was a big fan
of the books, so I know this from experience. 
I read the first four books of the series (which is actually called *A Song of Ice and Fire*), probably
multiple times.

Why not?  The
storylines were compelling.  The
characters were cool.  The setting was
enthralling.  Sure, it’s obvious even in
the books (and my understanding is that the miniseries is much worse) that
there’s all kinds of gratuitous sex, but I can always skip over those parts,
right (whether I actually did or not)?

Side note:  if there’s
a VidAngel version of *A Game of Thrones*,
it would probably end up being about 15 minutes long.

There I was, blind to the compromises I was making with my
faith, until two people jolted me out of my illusions.  The first was my sister, who reads as much
fantasy as I do, but got about halfway through the first book of the series and
declared to me that she was not, no way, going to read any more of that
filth.  The second was a brother to whom
I recommended the series.  He came back
to me and said that he couldn’t read books like that in good conscience.

Both of those things together got me to thinking, and I soon
realized that I had been deceiving myself about the spiritual effect of my
choices.

**The Sex Problem**

The first problem with *A
Game of Thrones* is, obviously, all the nudity and explicit sex.  You have incest, homosexuality, pedophilia,
and probably other perversions I don’t remember spread out before you to savor,
in addition to garden-variety adultery, fornication, etc.  The bedrooms have no doors.  Even my brother, a fantasy geek who is not a
Christian (though he is a decent man), couldn’t make it through a whole episode
of the fleshscape.

In theory, we can always flip a few pages forward or hold
down the Seek button until less skin is visible.  In practice, because we are all sexual
creatures, most of us are going to find the goings-on too alluring to
resist.  I suspect that most people who
are wise enough to reliably ward off such temptation are also wise enough not
to subject themselves to it in the first place.

**The Worldview Problem**

Let’s suppose, though, that we could close all the bedroom
doors.  I still don’t think that a
de-sexed *A Game of Thrones* would be
good for Christians.  The problem is its
overall perspective on the world.  The
books come from a sub-genre of fantasy called dark fantasy.  Dark fantasy tries to subvert the traditional
fantasy tropes of Good Guys and Bad Guys (see *Lord of the Rings* for details), resulting in novels that are
theoretically grittier and more realistic.

I don’t have anything against dark fantasy.  I think it’s interesting to read books
populated with flawed, human-feeling characters instead of cardboard heroes
(though cardboard heroes can be fun too!). 
However, since about the turn of the century, a lot of dark fantasy has
gotten so dark and so hard-edged that it depicts a world that is worse than the
actual planet Earth.

I think this is where *A
Game of Thrones* is.  The author,
George R. R. Martin, obviously modeled the series on the Wars of the Roses, a
series of late-medieval dynastic conflicts in England.  Many of the plot events of the series (the
Red Wedding, for instance) have historical antecedents.

However, I read as much (or more) history as I do
fantasy.  I know the actual Wars of the
Roses pretty well, and *A Game of Thrones*
is worse.  People in fifteenth-century
England had moral standards; people in Westeros generally do not.  When Richard III wanted to marry his
12-year-old niece, the shocked outcry was so great that he had to give over his
plans.  Sure, most of the players in the
Wars of the Roses were no angels. 
However, almost all of them believed in absolute good and evil, and some
of them lived pretty faithfully according to their ethical codes.  A make-believe world that doesn't include people like that will lead us to become cynical about our own.

**Evil and Consequences**

Second, the problems with evil do not emerge in Westeros the
same way they do in real life.  Compare,
for instance, the Bible.  There are
plenty of villains in Scripture, but because the Bible is actual history, the
story of those villains reveals how their vices bring them down.  In real life, you reap what you sow.

So too it was during the Wars of the Roses.  Edward IV was a skilled commander and a good
king, but in the later years of his life, he gave himself over to gluttony and
adultery.  As a result of his excesses,
he died relatively young, while his sons were still minors, and his house did
not endure.

In fact, after his death, his sons were murdered by his
brother, who became Richard III.  However,
Richard III died in battle because after his murder of his nephews, the
nobility wouldn’t tolerate him.  Both
kings failed because of their moral flaws.

In real life, virtue works and vice doesn’t (though of
course there are exceptions), so that even secular history comes replete with
moral lessons.  I read history in part
because I think it makes me wiser.  In *A Game of Thrones*, though, vice is not a
flaw.  It’s a fashion accessory.  Characters rise or fall because of chance and
cunning, not because of the consequences of good or evil.

We can’t help but learn from what we watch or read, and I
think that’s an extremely dangerous lesson. 
It’s one that is increasingly popular in our society (“Do what you
want—no consequences!”), but that makes it no less dangerous.  In real life, sin does not lead to
happily-ever-after, but once we begin to believe that it does, we become more
likely to sin.

**Conclusion**

Ultimately, of course, people are going to do what they
want.  I’m sure that some Christians are
going to read this and say, “Those books/shows don’t affect me like that!”  I suppose that may be true.  However, I fear that if we react that way, it
is not because the subject matter is innocuous.  It is because our consciences have already been seared by what is not.
