In the larger online worship-sphere, there’s been a lot of
discussion over the past several years about why most congregants in most
churches don’t sing anymore.  Typically,
one of the arguments advanced is that the body of contemporary praise music
doesn’t invite them to sing.

Among the churches of Christ, this typically isn’t a huge
issue.  After all, if your worship
tradition is a-cappella and nobody sings, instead of a band playing, you’ve got
silence.  However, a few weeks back, my
brother and friend Shane Scott [blogged](http://thinkingthroughfaith.com/2017/03/09/the-dangers-of-sophistication-in-barbershop-music-and-worship-music/) about his concern that a-cappella worship is endangered by a recent increase in
the sophistication of new worship music. 
He worries that too much sophistication isn’t congregational and will
lead the congregation to drop out.

He also invited me to weigh in on the topic, and who am I to
refuse such an invitation?



First of all, Shane’s right. 
He’s not a hymnist (though I have hopes of luring him in one of these
days!), but he’s musically astute enough to sense something that most
Christians don’t think about.  A hymn or
praise song does not become congregational because it is introduced to the
congregation.  Instead, in order for a
hymn to work in the congregation, it has to be built from the ground up with
the congregation in mind.  Here, then,
are the musical attributes that I think a congregational hymn must possess:

**Simplicity.** Your typical praise band is at least semi-professional.  Its members get together every week to
rehearse, and they do what they do because they consider themselves
musicians.  The congregation, by
contrast, only assembles to worship, and it almost certainly includes people
who only sing because God tells them to.

Guess which one of these groups is going to be able to
handle more musical difficulty?

As a result, anything the congregation can handle, the
praise band can too, but the converse is emphatically not true.  Much of the praise-band repertoire is too
difficult to translate well, and the a-cappella congregation can only learn it
by investing massive amounts of effort (and I’m here to tell you that trying to
learn some praise songs in a congregational setting is about like being beaten
with a stick) or shifting to a praise-team style of worship.

This is particularly true when the arrangement of the song
is instrumentally dependent.  As Steve
Wolfgang likes to point out, when a measure starts with a quarter-note rest, generally,
that’s where the guitar came in on the downbeat.  If there’s no guitar, there’s nothing
happening on the downbeat, and the congregation is trying to syncopate with
silence.

If you want a hymn to work congregationally, how much
simplicity is enough?  After 20 years of
trying to write congregational hymns, I think the answer is, “As much as you
can get away with.”  I still have yet to
see a congregational hymn fail because the harmony was too simple.

**Limited Range.** Classically speaking, the range of a
soprano line is supposed to be C4 to F5. 
In real life, you can expect a range of about an octave out of the
congregation, D4 to D5 starting out on Sunday morning, warming up to Eb4 to
Eb5.  If you double soprano and alto, you
can dip below that a little bit and still have it sound OK.  Go up above E5, and you start getting shrill
and squeaky.  Also, the men who are
singing soprano an octave down will nearly always drop out.  “Shrill and squeaky” is not anybody’s idea of
manhood.  Not even a tenor’s.

Of course, just as praise bands are generally more
proficient than the congregation, so too are praise-band singers.  Many of them are quite talented and have an extensive
range.  However, if you try to take that
praise song with the two-octave melodic range and export it to the
congregation, things won’t go well.

**Common-Practice
Harmony.** This is the style of
harmony that composers in the Western world employed from about 1650 to
1900.  It proposes a set of rules for
musical composition, and by writing in accordance with these rules, the
composer creates an arrangement that is (comparatively) easy to sing and
aesthetically pleasing.

However, as far as most praise bands are concerned,
common-practice harmony is what happens when everybody shows up for the
rehearsal.  Because they don’t follow the
rules, they write music that is a lot harder than it ought to be.  I think this is what Shane was getting at
when he expressed a concern that harmony was growing more sophisticated.  Really, though, it’s not that the music is
intrinsically more complex; it’s that simple music is made confusing by writers
who don’t write common-practice harmony.  Of course,
this is not true in the case of praise songs rearranged by Keith Lancaster,
D.J. Bulls, and other brethren with technical skill, but it still tends to be more
the rule than the exception.

Another friend of mine, Randy Daw, is on the music-editing
committee of *Timeless*, a modern-day
psalter.  Whenever a new volume of *Timeless* is produced, the editors
arrange for some of the paraphrases to be recorded by a group of professional
singers, the we-sing-for-Disney-movies kind of professionals.

At times, the music committee has accepted tunes that depart
from the rules of common practice.  Randy
observes, though, that every time a tune like that is handed to these
world-class singers, even they struggle with it.  Singers sing what makes sense to them, and
harmony that doesn’t follow the rules is the musical equivalent of word
salad.  Sure, there are words/notes
there, but they don’t go together.  This
kind of sloppy writing can only burden the church.

**Consistent
Rhythm.** The congregation in song
loves patterns and hates unpredictability. 
Its members aren’t there to show off their crazy sight-reading skills
and mad musical proficiency; they’re there to worship.  In their perfect world, they get to put the
music on autopilot and focus their minds on God, and a hymn that maintains a
consistent rhythm throughout allows them to do that.  Of course, some variation in rhythm is
interesting and useful (like the altered rhythm in the third phrase of a
rounded-bar hymn tune—see “I Sing the Mighty Power of God” for an example), but
even the variation should be something the congregation can anticipate.

By contrast, the rhythm in contemporary songs is often
utterly unpredictable.  The sheet music
commonly looks like somebody puked up a bunch of dotted sixteenth notes.  There are slurs and ties in odd places, and
much like the latest cinematic thriller, absolutely nothing that has happened
already allows you to anticipate what will happen next.

This works OK in the praise-band milieu; nobody’s singing
with the lead singer anyway, so the singer gets to make up the rhythm as they
go along.  However, if you hand music
with a bizarro praise-song rhythm to people who are a) not very good singers,
but b) trying to sing together anyway, they are more likely to win the lottery
simultaneously than they are to sing the music as notated.  And these are people who think playing the
lottery is a sin!

However, the tune will succeed marvelously at creating
distraction and confusion, so it’s got that going for it.

**Conclusion.** In sum, even though God is mindful of our
frame, the writers of contemporary Christian music usually are not.  They have no reason to be; they’re not
writing for the congregation, so whether the congregation can sing what they’ve
written is of no concern to them.  They
just want to sell CD’s in the church bookstore.

However, the congregation and those who lead it in worship
should be very concerned about the attributes of the new songs they
consider.  It doesn’t really matter how
good the song sounds on the CD; what matters is what the congregation will be
able to do with it.  I can’t sing like
Chris Tomlin, and neither can you.  Why,
then, should we behave as though what Chris Tomlin sings is any kind of
recommendation for what we should sing together?
