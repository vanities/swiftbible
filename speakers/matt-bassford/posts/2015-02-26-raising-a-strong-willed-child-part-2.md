**Lead by example.** It may well be true for most children,
but it is certainly true for me that my parents had the greatest influence on
me not by what they tried to make me do, but by what they did.  I don’t ever remember my dad saying to me,
“You have to read the Bible,” but in my mind’s eye, I will always see him
sitting in his blue recliner downstairs, a knee-high stack of Bible helps
beside him, studying for a Bible class he was due to teach, or simply studying
because he loved the word of God.



I don’t remember my mother ever saying to me, “You have to
love people and teach them about Jesus,” but I do remember her taking me as a
little kid to the rattiest trailer park you can imagine, where she met with a
group of single mothers so poorly educated that they were illiterate.  First, she taught them to read.  Then, she taught them the Bible.  All her life, she saw to it that the poor and
the downtrodden and the despised and the friendless had at least one friend,
and her memory shames me even as it inspires me.

You want to get inside your strong-willed kid’s head?  Don’t try to hammer an alien set of standards
into his thick skull.  My experience
suggests that the effectiveness of so doing will be limited at best.

Instead, live the way you want him to live.  Be at church every time the doors are
open.  Be a seven-days-a-week disciple,
not a Sunday-and-Wednesday-night disciple. 
Do good.  Under your own roof, make
sure that you serve your family, not yourself. 
Don’t be nicey-nicey with others in public and skewer them in
private.  Be hard-working.  Be conscientious.  Keep your promises.  Love the Lord your God with all your heart,
and with all your soul, and with all your mind, and with all your
strength.  Love your neighbor as
yourself.

Whether you see the process or not, your strong-willed child
is spending his formative years developing the internal standards that will
guide him all his days, and your behavior is the single biggest influence on
those standards.  For good or ill, what
he becomes is going to look an awful lot like what you are.

**Play the long
game.** Strong-willed children usually
have strong-willed parents, parents who are very used to being in control of
themselves and their environment.  I’m
one of those parents, and I struggle with having these two small people in my
life who defiantly will *not* be controlled.  If my life is a Mozart concerto, they’re
banging trash-can lids together in the middle of it.

Temperamentally, I tend toward the direct approach to
problem-solving.  I apply the massive
force of my will to the problem until it goes away.  This works well with, for instance, learning
to become a better hymnist.  Time and
effort will solve that one.  Time and
effort will not keep my three-year-old son from messing with the Blu-Ray player
in an attempt to figure out how it works. 
It could not be more clear to me that no punishment I am willing to dish
out is sufficient to keep him from further experimentation.

At some point, I need to become OK with the fact that God
did not see fit to send me docile, compliant children who are going to color inside
the lines all the time.  I can hardly
complain; after all, nobody ever accused me of being docile and compliant
either, certainly not when I was a child! 
From time to time (Regularly? 
Continually?), they’re going to act up, and when they do, I’m going to
hear about it, particularly from relatives and blue-haired church dragons.  That’s just the way that life is going to
be.

However, it does not mean that I am failing as a parent, nor
that I will fail as a parent.  We’ve all
known perfect little church angels who, once they turned 18 and were out from
under the parental thumb, threw themselves joyfully into every kind of
debauchery imaginable.  Nobody ever
accused me of being a perfect little church angel.  Even when I was a teenager, I used to wear a
T-shirt to Wednesday-night services that said “I wish you were dead” on the
back, much to the dismay of color-inside-the-lines types.

However, from the day I left home, I have never once
considered missing services because I didn’t want to go.  I wanted to be a Christian.  I wanted to serve God.  So I did. 
Just let anybody try and stop me!

Will that be Zoë’s story, or Mark’s?  I don’t know the answer there yet, only what
I want the answer to be.  I do know,
though, that not every bump in the road means the car is falling off a
cliff.  I have to be OK with chaos.  I have to be OK with coloring outside the
lines.  I have to be OK with violations
of good taste, good sense, and sanity. 
My role is to address those things but not be more distressed about them
than they deserve.

No parent chooses who his child will be.  Perhaps parents of strong-willed children
choose even less than most.  All I can
do, all we can do, is patiently continue to teach the right thing, patiently
continue to do the right thing, and trust that they will make the right choice.
