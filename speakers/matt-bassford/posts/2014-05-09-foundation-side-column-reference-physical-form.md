I started using this Bible in
early-to-middle 2013.  My TruTone
Crossway PSR had started looking shabby, so I decided it was time to switch it
out for a Bible that would provide a neater appearance in the pulpit.  Since that time, it has been my primary
church-building Bible, which means that it has borne the brunt of my preaching,
teaching, and study.  In preparation for
this review, I began taking it home from the church building and using it for
reading and travel purposes as well.

After I wrote this review,
though, I discovered two things about the SCR. 
First of all, in late 2013, Foundation discontinued production of the
narrow-margin SCR (like mine) and replaced it with a wide-margin SCR like the
one they used to publish 10 years ago. 
Second, at some point during the production of the narrow-margin SCR,
they switched to a text block that is considerably slimmer than the one I had
been using.  I’ll be talking about both
newer editions in bonus-coverage sections after my primary review has
concluded.



**VITAL STATISTICS [brackets indicate differences in different editions]**

Translation:  New American Standard Bible, Updated (1995)

Cover:  Genuine Leather

Binding:  Sewn

Trim Size:  6” x 9” [6 ½” x 9 ¼” for wide-margin]

Text Block Width:  1 ¾” [1 ⅜” for newer narrow-margin and
wide-margin]

Weight:  2 pounds, 13 ounces [2 pounds, 7 ounces for
newer narrow-margin]

Column:  Single

Layout:  Verse-by-Verse

Font Size:  11

Words of Christ:  Black

Cross-References:  Yes

Ribbon Marker:  1

There is a
fair amount of snobbery in the Bible-aficionado world directed at Bibles with
genuine-leather covers.  In some cases,
this snobbery is justified.  In the case
of the Foundation SCR, it isn’t.  My
narrow-margin SCR is currently out of print, but you can find its slightly
larger wide-margin brother online for about $40.  That’s a fraction of the cost of a Bible with
a calfskin or goatskin cover, but the genuine-leather cover will perform nearly
as well.

Let me
explain.  When the SCR arrived, yes, the
genuine-leather cover was stiff. 
However, it didn’t have the same shellac-like hardness that
characterizes many other genuine-leather covers.  The leather is both thicker and softer.  As a result, with use and breaking in, it
loosened up in a way that shellac covers won’t.

Now that I’ve
used it for six months, the cover of my SCR is as limp as I could want it to
be. It doesn’t droop all over my hand like a goatskin Bible might, but it has
enough flex to it that it feels alive in my hand.  It has a nice softness, too, that makes it
pleasant to hold.  If all genuine-leather
covers had the quality of this one, I would never feel the need to upgrade to a
premium-leather Bible.

I haven’t put
the SCR through five years of daily use yet, so I can’t speak to its
durability, but I do have some experience with the durability of
genuine-leather SCR covers generally.  My
father bought a genuine-leather wide-margin SCR in 2002 and used it *hard*, as in hours and hours every day
kind of hard, for 11 years.  The cover is
about 90 percent of the way to falling apart, but the text block is right there
with it.  The page corners are curling,
the pages themselves are gray with finger oil, the notes in ballpoint are
smearing and fading, etc.  It’s the most
disgusting tribute to a heart for God that I’ve ever seen.

To me, that’s
all the durability that you need out of a Bible cover.  If the cover will last as long as a
high-quality sewn text block (and the 2002 wide-margin SCR is a legendarily
good text block), it’s done its job.  The
cover on my SCR is of similar if not better quality, so I would expect it to
perform equally well.  All the genuine-leather
SCR’s that I’ve seen have leatherette endpapers instead of thick paper ones, so
that increases their durability too.

Like its 2002
wide-margin predecessor, the text block for my SCR is sewn.  If it were not, I would probably have put a
crack in it by now, in only six months of use. 
It’s easy to tell that the Bible is sewn because it has a moderate degree of
cockling.  The crackling noise when I
open the Bible isn’t my favorite, but it’s not a big deal.

The SCR came
with normal edge gilding.  The gilding is
already pretty banged up.  I don’t
care.  As far as I know, the only way to
keep a Bible pretty and nice is not to use it, and a Christian who doesn’t use
his Bible so that it stays looking pretty and nice has missed the point.

Overall, I
really like the physical form of the SCR. 
Only the cockling bothers me, but I still give the Bible a 4.5/5 here.
