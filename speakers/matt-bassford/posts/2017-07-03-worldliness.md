In one of the most famous passages of the Sermon on the
Mount, the Lord tells us that we need to be salt and light for the world around
us.  In other words, we’re not supposed
to move to Montana, seal ourselves off from the world, and become our own
little godly commune of monks.  Instead,
we are here because Jesus wants us to be an influence on the people around us
and lead them to Him.  We’re here to
engage with the world, not shun it.

However, influence is a two-way street.  Even as we are hopefully trying to bring the
worldly into the kingdom of God, those same people are working, albeit
unwittingly, to remove us from the kingdom of God and bring us into the world.  Every day, from a multitude of sources, we
all face pressure to abandon the teachings of Christ.  Needless to say, if we give into this
pressure, the result can only be disaster for us.  Let’s consider, then, the problem of worldliness.

**Characteristics of
Worldliness**

Our study here must begin with an understanding of the
characteristics of worldliness.  What
does a Christian who has become worldly do, say, and think?  In my estimation, the process of becoming
worldly starts with **FRIENDSHIP WITH THE
WORLD.** James delivers an ominous
warning on the subject in James 4:4.  The
point here is not that we can’t have friends who are not faithful
Christians.  Otherwise, as Paul says in 1
Corinthians 5, we would have to go out of the world.  Instead, James is telling us that the people
of the world can’t be our people.  They
can’t be the ones with whom we most identify. 
They can’t be the ones in whom we confide, from whom we consistently
seek advice, with whom we most commonly associate.  If they assume those roles in our lives,
instead of the Christians who ought to be there, then they will have more
influence on us than we have on them. 
The more time we spend with them, the more we become like them.

This is always true, regardless of the particular strain of
worldliness they practice.  Worldly
people live everywhere from trailer parks to gated communities, and no matter
how respectable they may be in worldly terms, there are two things that are
true of all of them.  They are not going
to heaven, and they are not going to help us go to heaven either.  We must keep that in mind in all of our
dealings with the worldly.  If we don’t,
their friendship will lead us into enmity with God.

Second, we must watch out for **THE WISDOM OF THE WORLD.** Paul
explains in 1 Corinthians 1:20-25. 
Basically, there are two kinds of wisdom to which we can listen.  There is the wisdom of the Spirit revealed in
the word of God, and there is the wisdom of the world revealed in the words of
the worldly.  To the wisdom of the world,
the wisdom of the Spirit is inaccessible and incomprehensible.  Those who aren’t disciples of Christ simply
don’t understand why disciples do the things they do.  To them, dedication to the gospel is
foolishness.

We see the wisdom of the world at work whenever someone or
something influences us to give less than our all to Jesus.  For instance, let’s say it’s been a hard year
financially.  Both our kids broke their
arms, the deductible on our health insurance went up to about a million dollars
at the start of the year, and the last storm through blew a whole bunch of
shingles off the roof.  Basically, we’ve
got a whole lot more outgo than we do income.

What do we do about that? 
Do we cancel the vacation we’d been planning and looking forward to all
year?  Do we not get the AC on the van
fixed like we’d been meaning to?  Do we
quit eating out three times a week?  Or,
instead, do we cut back on our giving to the Lord’s work?

To worldly wisdom, that’s a slam dunk.  It hurts to give up on Disneyworld and Big
Macs.  It doesn’t hurt to put less in the
plate than we would spend on Sunday dinner. 
None of us are going to get mean calls from the church treasurer because
we’ve been slacking on the contribution. 
And yet, Jesus warns us that where our treasure is, there our hearts
will be also.  When we consistently put
the Lord second with our money or our time, it’s a sure indication that without
realizing it, we have put our hearts in the wrong place.

If we get this wrong, it naturally leads to **THE LOVE OF THE WORLD.** John addresses this problem in 1 John
2:15-16.  This is a familiar text.  I probably bring it up in a sermon every
couple of years or so.  However, despite
its familiarity, countless Christians have ended up forfeiting their souls to
the lust of the flesh, the lust of the eye, and the pride of life anyway.

Sometimes, the devil’s attacks are obvious.  Take, for instance, our society’s emphasis on
sexual autonomy.  It’s pretty bluntly a
teaching that satisfying the lust of the flesh is the most important thing
about our lives.  However, despite its
obviousness, it’s still cutting a swath through our young people.

At other times, the appeal is less obvious, though no less
deadly.  In our society, one of the most
pervasive forms that the pride of life takes is our idolization of our
children.  There are millions of
Americans, probably tens of millions, who seek to validate themselves by
producing these stellar, amazing, wonderful children.  The guiding impulse of their lives is to make
sure that little Johnny and Jane live in the best school districts, are enrolled
in a million different sports and extracurriculars, and are protected from
every mishap that might damage their wonderfulness.

Now, don’t get me wrong. 
The Bible does call us to care for our children.  However, the Bible emphatically does not call
us to build our lives around our children. 
God says, “You shall have no other gods before me,” and when we treat
our children like the most important thing in our lives, we have actually made
them into our gods.  That’s a terrible
tragedy for all concerned.  Our children
cannot bear up under the weight of our idolatry.  They need to see and understand that even
though we value and love them, God comes first and they come second.

Sadly, if we follow the course of the world, we will meet **THE FATE OF THE WORLD.** John describes it just one verse down, in
1 John 2:17.  Those who do the will of
God abide forever; those who pursue the lusts of the world do not.  This verse can very easily be about us,
brethren.  It can very easily be about us
even if we show up faithfully here every Sunday morning.  It’s true that God wants our worship in the
assembly, but it’s equally true that He wants our lives and our hearts.  Indeed, He wants a relationship with us that
is exclusive.  He is a jealous God.  If we’re only willing to give Him half of
ourselves, that’s about on a par with a husband telling his wife that he only
plans to cheat on her three days a week. 
We are not called to be lukewarm, Laodicean Christians, friends, and if
that’s what worldliness makes us into, the devil has succeeded in his work.

**Jesus on the World**

Next, let’s consider what Jesus has to say about the
world.  The text we’re going to be
looking at appears in the 15th chapter of John, and it begins with a
description of **HEAVENLY FRUIT.** Read with me from John 15:12-17.  When you get right down to it, worldliness is
ultimately about love for the self, but Christianity is ultimately about love
for the other.  Jesus, of course, is the
paradigmatic example of this love.  He
showed love that literally cannot be surpassed in His willingness to lay down
His life for all those who are obedient to Him. 
We can’t choose God.  If Christ
had not been willing to die for our sins, there is nothing we could have done
to bridge that gap on our own.  Instead,
it had to be Jesus reaching out that hand of reconciliation.

However, Jesus didn’t do this so that we could sit there and
be saved and do nothing.  Instead, He
expects us to bear fruit, and contextually, there’s not much doubt about what
this fruit looks like.  It’s love, first
for God, second for our brother, and third for our neighbor.  We don’t obey the commandments of the Bible
because we are servants mechanically marking off items on a checklist.  Instead, we obey because we are friends.  We understand the meaning of His life, and we
obey Him because we recognize that keeping His commandments is an expression of
His love.

However, Jesus promises that this apparently blameless way
of life will stir up **THE HATRED OF THE
WORLD.** We see the explanation in
John 15:18-20.  No matter how respectable
it may appear to us, the world is evil—all of it.  As a result, it will always react negatively
to good.  Jesus, of course, did nothing
wrong.  He came only to help, heal, and
speak the truth in love.  However,
because His actions didn’t line up with the worldly prejudices of the Pharisees
and other outwardly religious Jews, they hated Him, opposed Him, and ultimately
killed Him.  I’m convinced that exactly
the same thing would have happened to Jesus if He had come to the 21st-century
United States.  Somebody who couldn’t
stand His message would kill Him to shut Him up.

It is inevitable, then, that if we follow Jesus, we will
encounter opposition from the world.  In
fact, we will encounter opposition and even hatred from the world in direct
proportion to our success in following Jesus. 
If we don’t experience this, paradoxically, it’s a sign that we’re doing
something wrong.  The only way to avoid the
hatred of the world is to avoid the footsteps of Jesus.  If we walk in them, only the people who love
Him will love us.

After this observation, Jesus continues to outline **THE MOTIVATIONS OF THE WORLD.** His thoughts appear in John
15:21-25.  Here, He identifies three
problems in particular.  The first of
them is that His enemies don’t know the Father. 
This is especially interesting because almost no one in Jewish society
would have agreed with Jesus about this. 
The high priests, the scribes, the Pharisees, and the Sadducees were the
religious elite.  At least outwardly,
they appeared to know the Father better than anybody.  So too, there are plenty of conspicuously
religious people in our day, but we must remember that those who know the
Father seek to obey Him.

Second, Jesus claims that the people of the world hate him
because He has spoken to them about their sin. 
He presented the truth to them, gave them the opportunity to act on it,
and they made the wrong decision.  Here
too, I think we find an ugly secret about the lack of enthusiasm for evangelism
among Christians.  We know that if we
tell the people around us the truth about their sin, they’re not going to take
it any better than the people around Jesus did, so we remain silent.  That’s certainly an easy and popular choice,
but it does nothing to make us more like the Lord.

Finally, Jesus points to His miraculous works as a source of
hatred.  All the miracles that He did
boxed His enemies into a corner.  They
couldn’t deny what He had done, but they didn’t want to accept Him either, so
they hated Him instead.

We can’t work miracles today, but we can hold the evidence
of a miracle in our hands.  God’s word
speaks as eloquently about God’s Son as the wonders He displayed.  If we spend enough time talking Bible with
the lost, we will certainly encounter those who can’t deny the truth of what we
say but don’t want to obey the gospel either. 
They’re not being honest, and on some level, they know they’re not being
honest, so their reaction will also be anger and possibly even hatred.

All of this leads us into some serious reflections before
the Lord’s Supper.  As Jesus Himself
said, they hated Him without a cause.  In
fact, they hated Him so much that they killed Him.  In death, He is both our atonement and our
example.  He rescues us from our sins so
that we can walk as He walked.  Let’s
remember this and remember Him as we share in the Lord’s Supper.

**Defeating Worldliness**

We see, then, that we have a choice to make.  Either we line up with the world, or we line
up with Jesus.  We can’t do both.  If we want to go to heaven, then, we have to
defeat worldliness.  This starts with **LEARNING SPIRITUAL WISDOM.** Paul describes the importance of this
kind of wisdom in 1 Corinthians 2:12-13. 
There is only one place to get this kind of wisdom.  It comes from God and His word.  Sometimes, people fall into the trap of
believing that they can figure it out on their own, that spiritual wisdom is
the exact opposite of worldly wisdom in every way.

So, for instance, if worldly wisdom tells us to do what
feels good, spiritual wisdom must tell us to do what makes us miserable.  This is hardly true.  God doesn’t want us to live in sensuality,
but He does give us richly all things to enjoy. 
In reality, even on earth, the joys that come with godliness get richer
the more we savor them.  It’s sin that
oppresses us and makes us get sick of it.

Instead, the way that Christ sets out for us is a way that
we can only learn from Him.  He teaches
us how to order our lives so that we can live the good life now and spend
eternity with God later.  Many aspects of
this spiritual wisdom make no sense to the world.  How can things like service and
self-sacrifice leave us better off rather than worse off?  And yet, every mature Christian will testify
that this is true.  The more time we
spend with the word, in assemblies, in Bible classes, and by ourselves, the
more we will imbibe spiritual wisdom, and the better off we will be.

Second, we must **SANCTIFY
OUR MEMBERS.** Paul explains what this
entails in Romans 6:19-22.  A little
earlier, we talked about our society’s emphasis on sexual autonomy, the idea
that our bodies are our own and nobody has the right to tell us what to do with
them.  This text says exactly the
opposite.  If we are Christians, we are not
our own.  Not, not, not!  We have been bought and paid for with the
blood of Christ.  We are His slaves.  I know this is strong language, but it
emphasizes Christ’s absolute right to control our behavior in everything.

When we sin with our members, then, we are not just
sinning.  We are stealing.  We are taking the body that belongs to Christ
and using it for our purposes instead of His. 
It’s like hotwiring somebody’s bulldozer and using it to demolish their
construction site.

Instead, we are supposed to sanctify our members by
presenting them to Christ for His glory. 
These aren’t my hands.  They’re
God’s hands, and I reveal their true ownership when I use them for God’s
work.  To the worldly, this sounds
awful.  They don’t want anybody else
telling them what they have to do, in all areas of their lives from the public
to the intimate.  God does tell us, and
only by dedicating ourselves to Him can we hope to inherit eternal life.

Finally, we defeat worldliness by **FIXING OUR EYES ON JESUS.** Consider
the Hebrews writer’s take on this in Hebrews 12:1-3.  Home ownership will teach you a lot of
things, and one of the things that it has taught me is that I am incapable of
mowing in a straight line.  If I’m mowing
the boundary between my yard and my neighbor’s yard and I’m just staring at the
grass in front of the mower, I’m likely to end up in Kendall County before I’m
done.  What I have to do, then, is fix my
eyes on some object, something like a fence post or even a crack in the sidewalk,
and if I mow with my eyes set on my destination, then I will mow in a straight
line.

Jesus serves the same purpose for us as we seek to determine
the direction of our lives.  Most people
lead lives that look about like the swath of lawn I mow when I’m not paying
attention.  They’re all over the
place.  There is no purpose.  There is no order.  There is no plan.  But, when we fix our eyes on Jesus and move
steadily closer to Him, not paying attention to anything else, we will
inevitably find ourselves on the shortest path that leads to heaven.  Now, I probably wouldn’t be able to see that
sidewalk crack in the distance without my glasses on.  Unless we put in the time in study of the
word, we won’t be able to see Jesus. 
Once we develop that understanding of the Lord, though, and once we
develop the determination to seek Him, worldliness will lose its power over
us.
