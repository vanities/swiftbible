It seems like I’ve been getting a lot of requests for
sermons recently, which from my perspective is great, and that’s the case again
this morning.  A couple of weeks back,
after a ladies’ Bible study, Lauren mentioned to me that some of the sisters in
attendance had been thinking about evangelism and wanted to know what to say to
somebody to start a spiritual conversation.

I sat around and chewed on that for a while, and eventually
I concluded that even though the question is a sensible one, it’s still not the
question that we ought to be asking.  I
think all of us, myself included, would like there to be some quick, easy
external change that we could make that would make us more effective in
personal work.  We want it to be like one
of those clickbait links on the Internet: 
“Thousands were saved using this one weird trick!”

Sadly, though, I don’t think this is an area where there are
any shortcuts, which is why we find evangelism so difficult.  There’s no way to become better at personal
work other than becoming better people. 
This morning, then, let’s consider the inward change that is
required.  Let’s consider what the Bible
has to say about the connection between evangelism and compassion.



**Jonah and the Plant**

Let’s begin our study this morning by looking at my favorite
story from the book of Jonah, the one that does not involve any sea
creatures.  I’m talking, of course, about
the story of Jonah and the plant.  We see
**THE PROBLEM** emerging in Jonah
3:10-4:4.  For many of us, there’s a lot
of irony in the story.  We aren’t very
good at saving people even though we want to be, but Jonah, who has convinced
the entire city of Nineveh to repent, is upset precisely because he has
succeeded.

In order to understand why Jonah might feel this way, we
need to consider the political climate of the time.  Jonah lived in the northern kingdom of Israel
during the reign of Jeroboam II, which ran from around 790 to 750 BC.  Even then, the empire of Assyria, which has
its capital in Nineveh, is already looming over the Middle East.  In fact, fewer than 30 years after Jeroboam’s
death, the Assyrians are going to conquer Israel and carry its people away
captive.

However, even though the Assyrians are Public Enemy No. 1,
God tells Jonah to go to Nineveh and convince its people to repent.  This is the last thing in the world that
Jonah wants to do!  He’d much rather see
God destroy them all, so rather than going to Nineveh, he tries to flee to the
other side of the world, which leads to the famous story of Jonah and the great
fish.  Reluctantly, Jonah obeys God, the
Assyrians repent, and Jonah gets mad because they have been spared.

At this point, if we are in God’s place, we might be tempted
to squish the hateful prophet, but instead, God teaches Jonah using an
ingenious illustration involving **A
PLANT.** The story continues in Jonah
4:5-8.  Even after he complains to God,
Jonah continues to harbor resentment, so he camps outside the walls of Nineveh,
hoping that God is going to see things Jonah’s way and destroy the city.  God graciously provides him with a plant to
shade him while he waits.The next day, though, God sends a worm to kill the
plant and then spends the rest of the day broiling Jonah, to the point where he
pleads for death.  Now that God has
killed his plant, why go on living?

This reveals a fascinating disconnect in Jonah’s
thinking.  Here, he’s mad because God
destroyed his plant, but earlier, he was mad because God didn’t destroy the
Ninevites.  The plant belonged to him,
but the people didn’t, so he was more concerned about the plant than the
people.

Even while we see the problem in Jonah’s thinking, we have
to be careful not to duplicate it in our own. 
It’s all too easy for our compassion to decrease with distance.  We have some little problem, or our loved
ones have some little problem, and we’re really upset about that.  However, someone we don’t know very well has
the great big problem of not being right with God, but that does very little to
stir our compassion because it’s not our problem.  This is certainly the way that people in the
world think, but it’s not the way that we can think if we want to glorify God.

God next exposes the flaw in Jonah’s thinking by discussing
the situation from **HIS PERSPECTIVE.** This appears in Jonah 4:9-11.  He first of all points out how childish Jonah
is being in mourning for his poor widdle pwant. 
Jonah didn’t plant the thing, he didn’t water the thing, he didn’t do
anything but benefit from it, but he’s unhappy that God took his gift back.

Consider, by contrast, God’s investment in the people of
Nineveh.  It’s true that Israel is God’s
chosen people, but God is still the Creator of all mankind.  He loves the Ninevites as infinitely as He
loves the Israelites, and their sin grieves Him no less.  God is a just God, and His justice requires
Him to punish the wicked, but God would always prefer to be merciful.  He would far rather send Jonah to Nineveh to
rescue the people there than simply destroy them.  When Jonah does his best to refuse the
mission, he shows how little he understands the compassion of God.

In the same way, friends, we must recognize that God isn’t
just our God.  He’s the God of everybody
around us too, even though they live in wickedness.  Some few of them are wicked because they have
rejected the gospel, but most of them have never even heard the truth.  In the words of 4:11, when it comes to the
things of the spirit, they don’t know the difference between their right hands
and their left.  From the depths of His
compassion, God calls us to go to them. 
If we share that compassion, nothing in our lives will be more
important.

**The Good Samaritan**

As we continue our study of what compassion looks like, we
next come to the parable of the good Samaritan. 
It begins with a conversation between **JESUS AND A LAWYER.** This
conversation appears in Luke 10:25-29. 
This starts out, like so many of the stories about Jesus do, with a man
who is trying to score points on the Messiah. 
This particular challenger, a lawyer, wants Jesus’ take on the greatest
commandment.  Jesus deftly turns his
question around on him and then agrees with his answer.  Even though Jesus doesn’t say it out loud,
it’s obvious that He’s thinking pretty loudly, “If you knew the answer, why did
you ask Me the question?

At this point, the lawyer’s pride is obviously stung.  Jesus made him look like an idiot, and, not
coincidentally, has pointed out a spiritual failing.  It sure looks like the lawyer knows he hasn’t
been loving his neighbor as himself.  In
his defense, he asks for a definition. 
Who is my neighbor, exactly? 
However, the aim of the question is not really to define who his neighbor
is so that he can help them.  It’s to
define who his neighbor isn’t so that he has an excuse for not helping them.

Here, we see a good example of what compassion does not
do.  Compassion does not look for excuses
to justify its inaction.  Let’s be honest
here, friends.  If we in our hearts do
not want to win the lost for Christ, we will always be able to find a reason
not to.  However, the problem there isn’t
really the situation.  It’s the heart.

As Jesus often does, He answers the lawyer’s question
obliquely by beginning a story about **A
MAN IN NEED.** We find it in Luke
10:30-32.  Even though Jesus supplies particulars
relevant to His audience, this story is universal.  It could as easily be about a man who is
mugged, shot, and left for dead on a street in Gary or Ford Heights.  However, the robbery victim isn’t really the
focus of Jesus’ story.  Instead, it’s the
passersby.  One of them is a priest, someone
who is presumably an expert in the Law, including the commandment to love your
neighbor as yourself.  However, rather
than stopping and helping, he goes by on the other side of the road.  So too does a Levite.

Jesus doesn’t dwell on their reasons for not helping, but we
can easily come up with several possibilities. 
Maybe they were worried about the robbers using the injured man as bait
to catch other travelers.  Maybe they
were concerned about their own ritual cleanliness.  Maybe they were in a hurry and didn’t have
time to stop.  Whatever the reason,
though, they didn’t help.

Here, though, we find a universal truth about
compassion.  Compassion is never
convenient.  We are never going to find
the perfect situation to be compassionate, with the gospel or with anything
else.  Being compassionate might put us
at risk in some way.  It might get us
involved with someone who isn’t up to our moral or social standards.  It will certainly take time, money and effort
that we could spend on something else. 
If we aren’t willing to pay the price, we won’t be merciful.

In contrast to the behavior of the priest and the Levite, we
see the actions of **THE SAMARITAN.** Jesus finishes up His story in Luke
10:33-35.  This character differs from
the religious all-stars of the previous section in a number of ways.  First, he’s a Samaritan, somebody who isn’t
Jewish at all.  The Jews of Jesus’ day
held Samaritans in contempt.

That’s not the most important difference, though.  The important difference is that when the
Samaritan sees the robbery victim, about whom we know nothing, he sees a man in
need and feels compassion.  The priest
and the Levite may have worried about the risk; the Samaritan stops to help in
the middle of bandit country.  The priest
and the Levite may have been concerned about their purity; the Samaritan cleans
and bandages the man’s wounds with his own hands.  The priest and the Levite didn’t want to get
involved; the Samaritan spends his own time and money on a stranger.  It’s clear to everybody who hears the story
who the real religious all-star is.

Likewise, friends, we need to remember what best reveals our
faith to the people around us.  People in
the world aren’t going to be terribly concerned about whether we have a piano
in our auditorium.  They’re not going to
be impressed by the number of Bible verses we can quote from memory.  What’s going to matter to them is whether we treat
them with compassion, and whether they see us treating others with compassion.  That is what will reveal Christ to them.

Finally, we come to **THE
APPLICATION** of the story.  Jesus
winds things up in Luke 10:36-37.  Here,
notice the way the Lord subtly turns around the original question He
received.  The lawyer wanted to know whom
he had to help.  Jesus, though, is asking
whom helping proves us to be.  The answer
is obvious.  The priest and the Levite
didn’t act like the victim’s neighbor. 
The Samaritan did.  However, the
lawyer can’t even bring himself to utter the loathsome S-word, so he says, “The
one who showed mercy.”  That, Jesus tells
him, is what you need to do.

Words cannot express how brilliant this answer is.  It’s very nearly a proof of the divinity of
Jesus by itself because nobody but God is that smart.  It’s simple, yet it turns previous
expectations inside-out.  Our neighbor
isn’t someone who lives close to us, or who is the same race as us, or who
shares our religious beliefs.  Our
neighbor is not defined by anything they are or do.  Instead, we define our neighbors for
ourselves by whom we help.  We obey the
second-greatest commandment only to the extent that we show compassion by
taking advantage of our opportunities to help others.

We don’t get good at evangelism by parroting a snappy pickup
line.  We don’t succeed at personal work
by having a primrose path of passages that we lead people down to teach them
the truth.  Instead, we reach the lost by
showing them through our love for them that regardless of how they feel about
us, we are their neighbors.  If we love
them as ourselves, we will lead them to Christ.

Before the Lord’s Supper, let’s reflect on this, one of the
greatest teachings of the Lord.  Jesus
didn’t only say this.  He lived it.  By His willingness to surrender His life for
us on the cross, He proved to us not only that we are His neighbors, but that
He wants us for His brothers and friends. 
Even now, His life, His death, and His resurrection shine with His
love.  Do our lives shine like that with
our love for those around us?  Let’s
consider this as we partake.

**Learning Compassion**

Let’s round this sermon off by looking at three ways that
each one of us can learn to be more compassionate.  The first of these is to **NOTICE OTHERS.** I can think
of no better passage to illustrate this principle than one of the most
heartwarming texts in the Bible, Matthew 10:29-31.  Even though it doesn’t use the word
“compassion”, I think it does a wonderful job of illustrating the compassion of
the Father.  God cares about the fate of
little birds that are practically worthless in human terms.  Even when we’re talking about Christians who
are insignificant to anybody else, God knows the number of hairs on their
heads.  There are many ways in which the
future is uncertain, but one thing that we can be certain about is that God
will remember us.

Obviously, none of us are God, but I think we have much to
learn from His concern for others.  All
around us, there are people who are insignificant in the world’s eyes.  Very rarely do we encounter somebody who
matters to millions of different people. 
All too often, we encounter those who don’t matter to anybody except
God.

That should give rise to two questions.  First, do they matter to us?  Second, do we make sure that they know
it?  In every conversation we have during
the day, do we take that extra 15 or 20 seconds to show the person we’re
talking to that they matter to us?  It
can’t be faked.  People will know whether
we care about them or not.  Let’s make
sure we do.

Second, let’s work to **BE
OPENHEARTED.** Let’s learn here from
the example of Paul in 2 Corinthians 6:11-13. 
When we consider the life of Paul, we see that it was filled with all
kinds of hardships, not least the emotional suffering he endured because of the
bad behavior of others.  At different times
in his life, both Christians and non-Christians wounded Paul deeply.  This happened because of what we see
here.  Despite the pain, Paul was willing
to make himself vulnerable to others because he knew he had to in order to win
souls.

In the same way, friends, we also must be openhearted.  In order to be successful in reaching the
lost, we have to let others into our lives, to the point where they have the
power to hurt us if they want to.  I think
many of us find that very difficult. 
We’ve been hurt by others before, to the point where we prefer to open
up only to our families and close friends, people we think we can count on not
to hurt us again.  However, when we keep
our interactions with people in the world on a surface level, we make sure that
we are never going to have the relationship with them that we need to affect
their lives.  A closed heart will always
meet with a closed heart.  Being
vulnerable is a frightening proposition, but it brings with it an honesty that
is essential for saving souls.

Finally, we must **CARE
ENOUGH TO SPEAK.** Look at Paul’s
self-description in 1 Thessalonians 2:5-8. 
That’s a fascinating insight into the mind of one of the greatest
evangelists of all time, isn’t it?  Paul
taught the Thessalonians, and indeed everybody else he taught, because he loved
them, because they were very dear to him. 
That love led him to teach them the truth.

This reminds me of one of the most frequent questions I hear
about evangelism:  “What do I
say???”  We’ll likely be talking about
that in a week or two—anything I can do to help, I will—but I really think the
key is not what we say, but who we are. 
Let’s take a quick survey this morning. 
There are several dozen Christians in the auditorium this morning.  How many of you were *not* brought to Christ by somebody who loved you?  Let’s see a show of hands.

The point is pretty clear, isn’t it?  As it was 2000 years ago, the most effective
evangelistic strategy in existence is to love people and be emotionally
committed to their well-being.  Good
intentions are not the same thing as love. 
We have to genuinely care.  It has
to be true that the people we are trying to teach are very dear to us.  If we speak with the tongues of men and of
angels but do not have love, we have become noisy gongs and clanging
cymbals.  On the other hand, if we’re
uncertain about our book, chapter, and verse but do have love, what we say will
still be right.
