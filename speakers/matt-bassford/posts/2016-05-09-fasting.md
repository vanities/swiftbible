As I think most of you are aware, it’s important to me to
answer the spiritual questions I get from the members here.  There’s nothing I’d rather do than tell
people what they need to know to inherit eternal life.  There’s no shame in not knowing, but not
asking is a missed opportunity!

A few weeks ago, I received such a question from Josh
Collier.  He was wondering what the Bible
had to say about fasting, so he asked me if I would mind preaching on the
subject.  I have no objection to
preaching on any Scriptural topic, so this morning, let’s consider fasting.



**How to Fast**

According to the dictionary, to fast is to abstain from all
food.  It’s certainly a subject that
shows up in the Bible a fair amount, so let’s begin by seeing what the
Scripture says about how to fast.  It is
often the case in the Old Testament that fasting is **A SIGN OF MOURNING.** This is
obvious in Judges 20:24-26.  The story
here is simple.  The Israelites attack
the people of Benjamin, the people of Benjamin stomp the Israelites, and so the
Israelites weep and fast.

Interestingly, this is the very first time that fasting is
mentioned in the Bible.  Before I started
studying for this lesson, I had thought that fasting was commanded under the
Law of Moses.  However, once I started
looking for such a commandment, I couldn’t find it.  The closest you get is the instruction to “afflict yourselves” on the Day of Atonement in Leviticus 16:29, which is hardly explicit.  Fasting, when done correctly, is something
that is acceptable to God, but it’s never been part of either covenant.

Instead, it’s a natural human reaction to grief.  This is something that I think all of us have
experienced before.  We get so upset
about some problem or tragedy that we don’t particularly feel like eating, and
if the problem has a spiritual dimension, our thoughts also turn to God.

However, God only looks with favor on fasting if it is **HEARTFELT.**Consider the words of God in Joel 2:12-14.  Joel is the only book of the Bible in which a
command to fast appears, and it appears to be related to the particular problem
in Joel:  the people have been sinful, so
God has sent a massive plague of locusts against the land.  Here, fasting seems especially appropriate.  If the people don’t stop eating because they
are mourning for their sins, God is going to make sure that they stop eating
and start mourning.  He’ll destroy all
the food and cause a famine.

Even in this fast-or-else setting, though, God is quite
clear that the Jews’ fasting can’t be a merely external observance.  He doesn’t so much want them rending their
garments as rending their hearts.  God
has never been interested in check-the-box religion, and that’s particularly
true with something like fasting.  A show
of sorrow for sin without actual sorrow is meaningless.

Today, friends, it’s still true that when we fast, it should
be something that we do because we feel like we need to do it.  For various reasons, I don’t fast, and I
think that’s acceptable to God.  I think
it would be equally acceptable for me to fast if I felt the need.  We’re suspicious of “Do what you feel like
doing”, as a standard for conduct, but I think that here, it’s the right
answer.

In the New Testament, Jesus instructs us to fast only **WHEN APPROPRIATE.** Let’s read here from Luke 5:33-39.  There are several things to note in this
reading.  First, once again, note that
fasting is not commanded here.  Jesus’
disciples don’t fast during His ministry, and though He says that they will
fast when He isn’t around anymore, He never instructs them to fast.

Second, we see Jesus critiquing the just-because fasting of
the Pharisees.  If the parable of the
Pharisee and the tax collector is any guide, the Pharisees would go on fasts
throughout the week because it seemed like a righteous thing to do.  Jesus’ parable of the new wine in old
wineskins is about the inapplicability of this kind of Pharisaical tradition to
His disciples.  Just-because fasting as a
ritual observance doesn’t make sense for those who are following a higher
calling.

Today, if we up and decide, “I’m going to fast on Tuesdays
and Thursdays,” that doesn’t appear to have anything to do with Biblical
fasting.  There’s no reason behind
it.  There’s no there there.  Instead, we’re making ourselves miserable
because it makes us feel holy, and that’s not any kind of way to actually draw
closer to God.

Finally, in the era of the church, we see fasting done **FOR CONSECRATION.** The best example here appears in Acts
13:2-3.  The church leaders in Antioch
are worshiping and fasting—why, we don’t know—when God tells them to dedicate
Paul and Barnabas to a special work. 
They fast some more, lay hands on them, and send them off.

This too is more of a could than a must.  We see, for instance, fasting accompanying
the appointment of elders in Acts 14, but when Matthias is appointed to replace
Judas as the twelfth apostle in Acts 1, there’s plenty of prayer, but no
fasting.  In my judgment, it would be
wrong to appoint a church leader without prayer, and I don’t know why any
Christian would want to do that anyway. 
However, the case for fasting before appointment isn’t nearly as
strong.  If some brethren find that
spiritually useful, great!  If not,
that’s certainly fine too.

**How Not to Fast**

Now that we’ve seen what the Scripture says about fasting,
let’s consider how we shouldn’t do it. 
First, we see that we shouldn’t fast **AS A TRADITION.** Here, look
with me at Zechariah 7:2-6.  This story,
of course, comes to us from right after the Jews have returned from exile.  The people who have returned to Bethel want
to know if they should continue the tradition they followed through 70 years of
exile, a tradition of fasting on the fifth and seventh month.

Even before we get to God’s answer, we can already see that
this doesn’t look like what we’ve seen in the examples of people doing fasting
right.  In all of those cases, fasting
was a response to a particular situation. 
Here, fasting is a response to a particular month.  It’s not like the exiled Jews were more upset
about being in exile in the seventh month than they were in the eighth
month.  It’s simply that those were the
two months they had picked to fast in.

In fact, God does point out the problem with this
behavior.  In fasting according to their
traditions, they weren’t fasting for Him. 
They were fasting for themselves, just as through the rest of the year,
they ate for themselves.  This wasn’t
worthwhile.  It was meaningless.

Similarly, I think we have every reason to be suspicious of
the traditional practice of Lent.  Lent
used to be much stricter than it is now, but as it has become more lax, it has
also become more popular.  Nowadays, I
see signs in front of all kinds of denominational churches promoting Lent.

Biblically speaking, this doesn’t make much sense.  We’re not supposed to remember the death of
our Lord one week a year, or six weeks a year. 
We’re supposed to remember it 52 weeks a year.  If at some point we are so overwhelmed by His
sacrifice that we can’t eat, that’s an appropriate occasion for a fast.  However, a scheduled display of piety isn’t
appropriate.

Likewise, we shouldn’t fast **TO IMPRESS OTHERS.** Consider
the words of the Lord in Matthew 6:16-17. 
Here, we start getting some insight into why the Pharisees liked to fast
twice a week.  It gave them an
opportunity to grandstand.  They wouldn’t
wash their faces and anoint their heads, so that everybody could tell just how
much they were suffering—suffering!—for God. 
Jesus contemptuously dismisses them as hypocrites.  If you want to fast, He says, keep it
private.

There are times when I wonder whether the denominational
world uses the same Bible I do, and this is one of those times.  Jesus says, “When you fast, don’t disfigure
your faces so that everybody can see you’re fasting.”  And yet, what do so many do on Ash Wednesday?  They disfigure their faces so that everybody
can see them fasting.  It’s almost like
they took the Bible and decided to do the opposite.  I don’t have any idea why they would do this,
but I have a pretty good idea what the Lord thinks of the practice.

Finally, we shouldn’t fast **AS A SUBSTITUTE FOR RIGHTEOUSNESS.**This idea appears in our longest reading of the day, Isaiah
58:1-12.  This is another manifestation
of that classic problem:  people trying
to serve God their way instead of His way. 
At this time, the people of Judah were dedicated to fasting.  They were champion fasters!  They bowed their heads down to mourn as
easily as a reed bows its head before the wind.

Sadly, this easy display of piety had about as much effect
as the wind does on a reed.  Rather than
actually repenting and becoming righteous, the people of Jerusalem continued
down the same wicked path, and then they had the nerve to be astonished when
God didn’t bless them.  They expected to
gain His favor by something He had not commanded while ignoring what was.

In reply, God tells them that if they want Him to accept
them, rather than giving up food, they need to give up wickedness.  The fast that God wants to see is a fast from
oppression and hard-heartedness.  If His
people will embrace charity and mercy, He will deliver them.

The same thing is true for us today.  From somewhere, mankind has gotten the idea
that if it hurts, it’s holy.  Like the
monks in *Monty Python and the Holy Grail*,
it’s not enough to worship God.  You have
to hit yourself over the head with a board while you’re doing so.  Then, after we’ve tormented ourselves enough,
God is somehow going to be happy with us.

Brethren, God doesn’t want our pain.  He wants our obedience.  He doesn’t take pleasure in our misery.  He takes pleasure in our righteousness.  Sure, sometimes being righteous does involve
suffering, but that suffering is the means to the end, not the end.  We should never think that if we hurt
ourselves enough, we will somehow get Him to overlook our sin.  That’s a price that Jesus has already paid
for us, and if He hadn’t, there’s nothing we could do to pay it.  Given a choice between fasting and good
works, God will pick the good works every time. 
So too should we.
