A couple weeks back, I ran a blog post entitled “1 Peter
3:21 and Gotquestions.org”.  It was a
response to an article on a website that argued that 1 Peter 3:21 does not say
that we need to be baptized to be saved. 
As my blog posts go, this one was fairly successful.  Since I posted it Tuesday a couple of weeks
ago, it’s attracted almost 1200 unique page views.

One of my readers, however, was my mentor back in Texas, Max
Dawson.  He thought enough of it to drop
me a note about it.  In the note, he
complimented the post, but he also observed, “Preachers ought to be preaching
more sermons like this.”  I’m not the
most subtle guy out there, but I know a hint when I see one, and when somebody
I respect as much as I respect Max tells me that I need to turn something into
a sermon, it becomes a sermon.  This
morning, then, I’m going to expand on the arguments in that post to help us
understand 1 Peter 3:21.



**The Passage Itself**

However, before we delve into all the arguments and
counter-arguments, let’s spend some time considering the passage itself and its
intended meaning.  It begins with a discussion
of **SUFFERING.** Let’s read here from 1 Peter
3:17-18.  Throughout 1 Peter, suffering
is one of its major themes.  Here, Peter
is encouraging his audience to be willing to suffer for the sake of
righteousness.  He reveals that in doing
this, they’re following the example of Christ, who suffered despite having done
nothing wrong, just as these Christians would be suffering despite having done
nothing wrong.

However, this suffering isn’t meant to be pointless.  Instead, it’s for a purpose.  In being willing to suffer for God’s sake,
Christians glorify Him and lead others to Him. 
Similarly, Jesus suffered to lead us to God.  We sinned, but He paid the price.  In the end, though, Jesus was rewarded for
His suffering when God brought Him back to life again.  So too, Peter implies, will Christians who
are willing to suffer for Christ be rewarded.

This takes us to one of the strangest passages in 1 Peter
and indeed the New Testament, Peter’s brief discussion of **THE SPIRITS IN PRISON.** It
appears in 1 Peter 3:19-20.  If this text
baffles you upon first reading it, you are not alone!  However, I think that if we sort through it
patiently, we can figure out what’s going on. 
First, Peter says of these spirits that they are now—the “now” of 2000
years ago—in prison.  Second, they were
once disobedient, which presumably is why they are now in prison.  Third, their disobedience took place during
the days of Noah.

When we put all this together, it becomes apparent—to me, at
least—that Peter is talking about the generation that was alive at the time of
the flood, a collection of people whose hearts were entirely set on doing
evil.  They were as bad as the human race
has ever been.  God couldn’t do anything
with them, so He sent the flood and exterminated them.  Today, as was true 2000 years ago, their
spirits remain in torment, awaiting the Day of Judgment.

I don’t think the text means that Jesus literally went and
preached to them at some point, though I suppose it’s possible.  Instead, He preached to them by His example.  In contrast to their continual practice of
evil, Jesus lived a sinless life.  He
showed them how they should have lived.

Peter points out, though, that not everyone alive at the
time of the flood perished in it. 
Instead, eight people—Noah, his wife, his three sons, and their
wives—were brought safely through the water in the ark.  They weren’t sinless, but they were
righteous, so God rescued them.

This brings us to Peter’s comparison about **BAPTISM.**Look at 1 Peter 3:21-22.  Notice
that it starts out by pointing out the correspondence between what happened to
Noah’s family and what happens to us. 
God brought them on the ark through the water to safety, and when we are
baptized, He brings us through the water to safety too.  Of course, this salvation isn’t physical in
nature.  God isn’t protecting us from
dirt by giving our flesh a bath. 
Instead, it’s a spiritual matter. 
Our consciences are stained by the guilt of our sins, so in baptism, we
appeal to God to make our consciences good again.  In Acts 2, Peter describes this as “calling
on the name of the Lord”, and he may even have been thinking of the prophecy of
Joel 2 as he was writing 1 Peter 3.

After this, Peter reveals what makes baptism spiritually
effective:  it saves us through the resurrection
of Jesus Christ.  There are a couple of
senses in which this is true.  First, the
resurrection declared Jesus the Son of God in power.  After He was raised from the dead, God highly
exalted Jesus and set Him over everything and everyone.  This is why we baptize in the name of
Jesus.  As it often does in Scripture,
“name” means “authority”.  It is by the
authority of Jesus that we baptize and also by that authority that the baptizee
is saved from their sins.

Second, baptism unites us with the experience of
resurrection.  In baptism, we die with
Christ to our old selves.  We are buried
under the water as He was buried in the earth. 
Then, when we come up out of the water as He rose from the ground, we
are united with His resurrection.

Momentarily, we will share in the Lord’s Supper.  Before we do, let’s think about these
things.  Let’s think about Jesus’
willingness to suffer for our sakes. 
Let’s remember the death He died and the great act of power that brought
Him up from the dead.  Let’s remember
that this same power has worked upon our lives so that through baptism, we have
been raised from the dead.  We’ve been
reborn to serve Him.  Are we?  Let’s examine ourselves as we partake.

**Is Baptism a Work?**

Now, though, we need to turn our attention to the
gotquestions.org article.  Let’s begin
with the question of whether baptism is a work or not.  In the opening paragraph of the article, the
author opines, “In the case of baptism and salvation, the Bible is clear that
salvation is by grace through faith in Jesus Christ, not by works of any kind,
including baptism (Ephesians 2:8-9). So, any interpretation which comes to the
conclusion that baptism, or any other act, is necessary for salvation, is a
faulty interpretation.”

There are things here that I agree with.  I’ll affirm as loudly as anybody that we’re
saved by grace through faith, not by works. 
However, I find that completely in line with my conviction that baptism
saves, and those two ideas go together because baptism isn’t actually a work.

First, baptism isn’t a work in the Ephesians 2:8-9
sense.  Paul’s point here is not that we
don’t have to do anything in connection with our salvation.  Baptism aside, this is the same man who at
various points tells us that we have to believe, repent, and confess Christ in
order to be saved.  Paul certainly
believes that there are things we have to do to lay hold of salvation.

Actually, he’s using “works” in Ephesians 2 as shorthand for
what he spells out in Galatians 2:15-16. 
Here, it’s clear that “works” stands for the works of the Law of Moses
that justify no one because no one keeps them perfectly.  Throughout all of Paul’s writing, every
single faith-versus-works passage is about this distinction:  earning salvation through perfect Law-keeping
versus being justified by faith in Christ.

People who are baptized for the forgiveness of sins aren’t
trying to justify themselves by perfect Law-keeping.  They’ve already failed, and they know
it.  In fact, their imperfection is the
very reason why they want to be baptized! 
It’s ludicrous to argue that the man who wrote the discussion of baptism
in Romans 6, the longest discussion of the saving effect of baptism in the
whole Bible, didn’t believe baptism was necessary for salvation.

This specific Biblical usage aside, baptism isn’t even a
work in the ordinary English sense of the word. 
Gotquestions.org calls it an act, but it isn’t.  It’s entirely passive.  During a baptism, the baptizer is
working.  The baptizee isn’t.  They’re just standing there and letting
somebody else baptize them.  If being
baptized is a work, so is being hit by a bus.

**Are Miraculous
Spiritual Gifts a Sign of Salvation?**

Second, let’s consider whether spiritual gifts are a sign of
salvation.  Our author writes, “A good
example of someone who was saved before being baptized is Cornelius and his
household in Acts 10. We know that they were saved before being baptized
because they had received the Holy Spirit, which is the evidence of salvation
(Romans 8:9; Ephesians 1:13; 1 John 3:24).”

In particular, the passage that the author is referring to is
Acts 10:44-48.  Here, the Holy Spirit
falls upon Cornelius and his household prior to their baptism, and they all
start to speak in tongues.  God has given
them a miraculous spiritual gift.

However, there are people in Scripture who have and use a
miraculous spiritual gift yet clearly aren’t righteous.  Most notably, look at John 11:49-53.  Here, the evil high priest Caiaphas utters a
true prophecy while he is in the middle of plotting the death of Jesus.  Inspired by the Holy Spirit through his
office as high priest?  Definitely.  Saved? 
Definitely not.

As a result, we simply can’t conclude from the display of
tongues in Acts 10 that Cornelius and his household were saved at that
moment.  Instead, the tongues showed
Peter and the other Jewish believers present that the time had come for the
Gentiles to be saved through baptism too.

**Is Receiving the Holy
Spirit a Sign of Salvation?**

In addition, it’s worth noting that the three passages that
the author cited do not demonstrate that receiving the Holy Spirit is the
evidence of our salvation.  Let’s begin
here by looking at Romans 8:9.  The first
thing to realize about this text is that it isn’t about our initial salvation
at all, so I’m honestly not sure why the author is bringing it up.  Instead, it’s about our walk in Christ
afterward.  During this walk, we are
indwelt by the Spirit of God.  This
concept is parallel to the indwelling of sin that Paul discusses in Romans
7.  In neither case does it mean that we
have a little sin demon or a little piece of God literally living inside us and
pulling our puppet strings.  Instead, indwelling
here is a matter of influence and control. 
When we walk according to the flesh, we are indwelt by sin, and when we
walk according to the Spirit, we are indwelt by God.  This is good and important to know, but it
has no bearing on how we should read 1 Peter 3:21.

Next, let’s consider Ephesians 1:13.  Here, we learn that we are sealed by the
Spirit. According to 2 Timothy 2, the seal of God reads, “The Lord knows who
are His.”  When we obey the gospel, we
are spiritually marked as belonging to Jesus. 
That’s very important, of course, but it’s hardly controversial, and it
does nothing to prove that baptism does not save.

Finally, let’s look at 1 John 3:24.  There are two main parts to this verse.  First, it tells us something similar to what
we saw in Romans 8.  When we keep God’s
commandments, God abides in us, and we abide in Him.  We’re doing what He wants, so He’s the one in
control.

Second, the text tells us that we know that God abides in us
through the Spirit whom He has given us. 
This doesn’t mean that we know God abides in us because we feel that He
does.  That’s way too subjective.  I guarantee you that there are people right
now who feel that God abides in them even though they’re in the middle of
cheating on their spouses.  Feelings
justify anything!

Instead, because the Spirit is the truth, I know that God
abides in me from consulting the truth. 
I read the word and learn that I’m keeping God’s commandments, which was
our sign for abiding in God in the first place. 
The point here is that rather than trusting our feelings, we should
trust the word.  Once again, this is not
some alternative for salvation that excludes baptism.

**What About the Thief
on the Cross?**

The article author also returns to that favorite chestnut of
the enemies of Bible baptism, the story of the thief on the cross.  They say, “Was Peter really saying that the
act of being baptized is what saves us? If he were, he would be contradicting
many other passages of Scripture that clearly show people being saved (as
evidenced by their receiving the Holy Spirit) prior to being baptized or
without being baptized at all (like the thief on the cross in Luke 23:39-43).”  Let’s go to that passage now.  In it, Jesus says to the thief that he will
be with Him that night in paradise, with no mention of baptism.

However, this does nothing to prove that we can be saved
today without being baptized.  Consider what
Jesus says in Mark 2:10-11.  The point
here is plain:  the Son of Man has authority
on earth to forgive sins.  Jesus could
say to somebody, “Your sins are forgiven,” and they would be.  He told the thief on the cross that his sins
were forgiven, and so they were.

That’s great as far as it goes, but 2000 years after Jesus’
foot last trod the soil of this planet, it doesn’t go very far.  If Jesus, literally in the flesh, says to you
that your sins are forgiven, I’ll agree that they have been.  However, if He hasn’t, and you want to be
forgiven, you’d better be baptized.

**An Appeal to God for
a Good Conscience**

The final part of the author’s argument that I want to
discuss concerns his reading of 1 Peter 3:21 itself.   “Thankfully,
though, we don’t have to guess at what Peter means in this verse because he
clarifies that for us with the phrase ‘not the removal of dirt from the flesh,
but an appeal to God for a good conscience.’ While Peter is connecting baptism
with salvation, it is not the act of being baptized that he is referring to
(not the removal of dirt from the flesh). Being immersed in water does nothing
but wash away dirt. What Peter is referring to is what baptism represents,
which is what saves us (an appeal to God for a good conscience through the
resurrection of Jesus Christ). In other words, Peter is simply connecting
baptism with belief. It is not the getting-wet part that saves but is the ‘appeal
to God for a clean conscience’ which is signified by baptism, that saves us.”

To be quite honest, I find the logic here baffling.  Somehow, the author is able to take the
phrase, “Baptism now saves you” and get it to mean, “Baptism does not now save
you.”  They accomplish this
transformation via sentences like, “While Peter is connecting baptism with
salvation, it is not the act of being baptized that he is referring to.”  Of course, in the normal operation of
language, baptism is the act of being baptized, so Peter is connecting the act
of baptism to salvation.

The author is also fascinated by the phrase “not the removal
of dirt from the flesh”.  They assert,
“Being immersed in water does nothing but wash away dirt.”  This is completely wrong, and we see that it’s
wrong from Acts 22:16.  Here, we learn
from the words of Ananias that in addition to washing away dirt, immersion in
water can also wash away sins.  Of
course, it’s the latter usage that Peter is talking about.  He wants us to understand that we don’t get
baptized to take a bath.  When I think
back to my baptism, I don’t recall it having made me any cleaner outwardly.

Instead, grammatically speaking, baptism and Peter’s “appeal
for a good conscience” are exactly the same thing.  I want a good conscience, so I submit to
baptism, and in so doing, call upon the Lord to save me.  I am emphatically not saving myself.  I am placing my faith in the promises He has
made to all who are baptized.  Then,
through the resurrection of Jesus Christ, I too rise from the (spiritually)
dead to live as one of God’s redeemed people.

Baptism now saves you. 
It now saves me.  It now saves
everybody who needs to be saved.  That’s
the clear testimony of Scripture.  If
somebody wants to gamble their soul on rejecting this clear testimony, I guess
they’re free to do so (though I wish they wouldn’t!).  However, they won’t be able to argue on the
Day of Judgment that God never told them what they needed to know.
