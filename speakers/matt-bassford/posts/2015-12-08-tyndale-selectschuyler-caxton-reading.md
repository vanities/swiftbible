**OVERVIEW.** I’m too much the lawyer to be comfortable
with dramatic, absolute statements. 
Having said that, the Select (along with its book block-mate the Caxton)
is the best reading Bible I’ve ever used. 
It might be the best reading Bible currently available, period.

I put the Select through the usual 2-week testing period as
my daily reader.  I’ve been using the
Caxton as my daily reader ever since I got it several weeks ago.  Here are my impressions:



**SIZE.** When compared to the other Bibles I’ve
used, the Select and Caxton are almost perfectly middle-of-the-road.  They’re not thinlines.  They’re not hand-size Bibles like the
Cambridge Clarion.  They’re not full-size
Bibles like the Crossway Legacy, let alone behemoths like the Foundation
Side-Column Reference.  They’re right
smack-dab in the middle.

As a result, I read from either one like I do a mid-size
Bible.  I don’t hold them with my thumb
in the gutter like I would a paperback, nor do I cradle them against my
chest.  Instead, I hold them in my hand
away from my chest, and neither one gives me a problem in that position.  If I could ignore the laws of physics and
have a Bible of precisely the dimensions I prefer, I might want a Bible a
little smaller than the Select or the Caxton. 
In real life, though, a Bible with all the features of the Select can’t
be any smaller, and I’d rather have the features than the size.

**LAYOUT.** From nearly the beginning of my
Bible-blogging career, I’ve been an advocate of double-column reading
Bibles.  I know single-column Bibles like
the aforementioned Legacy perform well for others, but they don’t for me.  Even in cases, such as the aforementioned
Clarion, where I could see the skill that had gone into the design, something about
single-column Bibles simply didn’t click.

The Select and the Caxton demolish the rule.  Their characters-per-line count is about 65,
which is low for a single-column Bible, and that doesn’t hurt.  The extraordinarily generous leading probably
also helps, as do other features that will appear in later paragraphs.  It may even have to do with the NLT itself,
which is certainly a less demanding translation than those on the
formal-equivalent side of things. 
Regardless, despite being single-column, the Select and Caxton alike are
magical readers.

I’m not alone in this. 
I’ve talked to other double-column Bible geeks online who feel the same
way about the Select setting that I do. 
If this is a layout that excels for us, it’s a layout that will excel for
everybody (with the lamentable exception of those who can’t handle 8.75-point
text).

This is particularly important in the poetic sections of
Scripture, like Psalms and many portions of the prophets.  It’s simply not possible for a double-column
Bible to handle versified passages as well as a single-column Bible does.  Even the best double-column reader is going
to be full of one-word “widows” exiled from previous lines.  There are a couple of widows in the Select
and the Caxton, but by and large, the versified poetry appears on the page as
the translators intended, which makes a big difference in reading speed and
comprehension.  If a single-column Bible
can handle prose well (which the Select setting does), its handling of poetry
will always make it an outstanding reading Bible.

Naturally, this is a setting that checks all of my other
ideal-reader boxes.  It’s the only Bible
I’ve ever used that has a wider inner than outer margin.  This keeps the text well away from the
gutter.  No squishing the spine and
squinting with the Select or Caxton, either one!

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEi7Q0B8qD7OpVYm62jKQ17p0nuclTTqQYNdDd1a7QkEJ4412AFPsS11WnxIrasCzm-ctmWz9Sn3MJPejQP4gm8t29mrnPS3D1GQRurA0r92ukQiboO2D3T4XqxEeoOFhOPS4JW-5hVong/s1600/Reading+Gutter.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEi7Q0B8qD7OpVYm62jKQ17p0nuclTTqQYNdDd1a7QkEJ4412AFPsS11WnxIrasCzm-ctmWz9Sn3MJPejQP4gm8t29mrnPS3D1GQRurA0r92ukQiboO2D3T4XqxEeoOFhOPS4JW-5hVong/s1600/Reading+Gutter.jpg)

Similarly, the Tyndale designers had the courage to go
black-letter.  Printing the words of
Christ in red, whatever emotional appeal it might have, is awfully hard to
execute well in practice.  Even Jongbloed
won’t get red-letter Bibles exactly right all the time.  There are so many pitfalls down that road
that the best choice is to avoid them all and print the Bible in one color of
ink.  Tyndale did.  It’s great.

Next, the Select setting is line-matched.  This is extremely important in the Select
because, as a Bible with more than 2000 pages (single-column Bibles with
generous leading are always going to have a high page count), the only way the
designers could get it down to a manageable width was by using thinner
paper.  28-gsm Indopaque isn’t terrible,
but nobody’s going to be confusing it with the paper in a 2002 SCR,
either.  You can certainly see ghosting
on any page of the Select, but because it confines itself to grayish bands
behind the text you’re reading, it doesn’t cause any reading-comprehension
trouble.

Finally, 8.75 Lexicon No. 1 with 11.5-point leading is
certainly text that’s big enough for me to read easily, especially when it’s
printed by. . .

**PRODUCTION
QUALITY.** Jongbloed.  Bible geeks know that Bibles produced today
fall into two categories:  those printed
by Jongbloed, and those that aren’t.  China-print
Bibles are much better than they used to be 10 years ago, but Chinese printers
still have a lot to learn from the Dutch masters!  When I open a Jongbloed Bible, I expect
super-crisp print and perfect execution, and neither the Select nor the Caxton
disappoints in this respect.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgbBqj5LTdxSjH0gzF-Pl94C2PEv-R1rPAnUJg-2iyYtNVSFFh06yypNtRzqbCYJ2kqAsfI440wKHdBsLNELc4zbzdalZnAja0WyjjLj1CygTJ244EEcjS_Ejw5RSFcRGa-yO_apapcXA/s1600/Jongbloed+print.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgbBqj5LTdxSjH0gzF-Pl94C2PEv-R1rPAnUJg-2iyYtNVSFFh06yypNtRzqbCYJ2kqAsfI440wKHdBsLNELc4zbzdalZnAja0WyjjLj1CygTJ244EEcjS_Ejw5RSFcRGa-yO_apapcXA/s1600/Jongbloed+print.jpg)

For instance, even though I’ve used a number of Bibles
printed in Lexicon, this is the first time that I’ve noticed that the
exclamation point in the typeface, unlike the exclamation point in (for
instance) Times New Roman, isn’t symmetrical. 
Instead, the Lexicon exclamation point has a little penstroke flare on
the upper left-hand corner.  Jongbloed
printing, especially when line-matched, is clear enough that I see details like
that even when I’m not looking for them.

It makes a difference in comprehension too.  One of the light bulbs over my desk at home
has gone out, but because the Caxton reads so clearly even in the dimmed light,
I haven’t bothered to change it.  Yes;
I’m lazy.  I know.

As I said above, 28-gsm paper isn’t great.  However, I’m sure that the Indopaque was the
most opaque paper available at that weight, and the Select design wouldn’t work
with heavier paper.  If you want a
single-column Bible that works for everybody, this is the kind of paper you
have to have.

**CONCLUSION.** This is an amazing setting, and it
contains a translation that I find to be one of the smoothest-reading, if not
the smoothest-reading, of them all.  I
have trouble imagining how a reading Bible could be better than this.  If you’ve never been able to complete a
Bible-reading plan, you’d like to try again, and you’re willing to drop at
least $120 (the cost of the still-premium brown calfskin Select) on a Bible,
this might be your silver bullet.  The
Select and the Caxton both are clearly 10/10 readers.



Overall, they score an extremely strong 26.5/30, despite the
anti-premium bias built into my rating system. 
Bibles don’t come much better than this.
