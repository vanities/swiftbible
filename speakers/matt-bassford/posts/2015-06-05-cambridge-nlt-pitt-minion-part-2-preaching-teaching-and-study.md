As is my wont, I used the Pitt Minion for two weeks as my
primary church-building Bible.  During
that time, I used it for two sermons, a 45-minute public reading (of Romans
1-8), and three Bible classes.  I also
used it to write two sermons.  It’s fair
to say then, that the Pitt got more of a workout than my typical review
Bible.  Here’s how I thought it did.

**PREACHING.** I know, I know.  All the way through these reviews, I’ve been
insisting that a Bible needs to be at least 5 ½” x 8 ½” so that I can slide a
half-sheet sermon outline inside the cover and not have the edges stick out
like an untucked shirttail.  Really,
though, that statement should have an asterisk after it, and the asterisk should
read, “Unless it’s a small Bible that I really, really like.”

The Pitt Minion is a small Bible that I really, really like,
so rather than folding my sermon outline in half, I folded it in quarters
instead.  It would probably be hard on
the binding to carry a quarter-folded sheet of paper around in there all the
time, but I don’t think doing so once a week is likely to cause any lasting
harm.  There were no white edges, and I
got to preach from a small Bible, so I was happy.

In terms of layout, the Pitt Minion isn’t right in the
middle of my preaching preferences, but it’s close enough.  My favorite preaching Bibles are
verse-by-verse, but through the course of 2014, I learned that double-column
paragraphed Bibles work pretty well too. 
The Pitt is double-column paragraphed, and a well-executed exemplar of
the type at that.  With its unusually
crisp print, I didn’t miss a citation throughout the time I used it.

**TEACHING.** Regardless of how some of my other
preferences have changed over the past year, I still prefer small Bibles for
teaching Bible classes.  This is not such
a big deal this particular quarter, because I’m doing all of my teaching
sitting down, but a small Bible such as the Pitt is still handier.  If I were marching around the front of an auditorium
teaching, by contrast, the small size of the Pitt would be invaluable.  I could wave the thing around like a
conductor’s baton if I wanted to.

Of course, part of the genius of the Pitt layout is that
despite being a small Bible, it’s also a reference Bible.  It is not a “reference Bible” in the sense of
the Holman Large Print Personal Size Reference NKJV, which is really a text
Bible with occasional references stuck in the white space at the ends of
paragraphs.  Instead, Pitts contain the
full suite of references available in any given translation.

At the moment, I’m teaching a Bible class on Revelation, so
believe you me, I’m using those cross-references!  “Where is the Old-Testament prophecy about
locusts again?  Oh, that’s right—Joel
2!”  Of course, no reference Bible is
going to have every possible cross-reference. 
For instance, the NLT does not take me to Daniel 12:7 to explain the 42
months and 1260 days of Revelation 11:2-3. 
However, it’s still better to have what help the references provide, and
more help is better.

**STUDY.** Size in a study Bible doesn’t matter
much, so even the Pitt’s size didn’t matter. 
However, readability in a study Bible is very important, and
surprisingly, the Pitt did well even here. 
In recent months, I have begun to learn that I inherited not only my
mother’s nearsightedness, but also her weak eyes.  After reading for several hours, I start
losing the ability to focus on text (oh, for my teenage years, when I could
knock out a whole book in one twelve-hour sitting!).

However, the Pitt still didn’t cause me any trouble
here.  I never found myself rubbing my
eyes when I focused on it for prolonged periods of time, as when I was
rehearsing my reading of Romans 1-8.  The
combination of Lexicon No. 1, with its exaggerated serifs, and Jongbloed
printing makes the layout work for me.

Equally importantly, the Pitt worked as a reference
volume.  As I mentioned, I wrote two
sermons during the Pitt’s field test and preached one of the sermons twice (for
two different congregations, of course). 
In all of that, I never noticed myself missing a citation.  In my experience, that’s about how any
double-column paragraphed Bible will perform, and when the Pitt is smaller than
just about any other such Bible, its success is a mark in its favor.

**CONCLUSION.** During its trial period, the Pitt Minion
just didn’t give me a whole lot to object to. 
Normally, preaching, teaching, and study is the area in which large
Bibles excel.  However, the Pitt came
into the land of the giants and competed. 
I enjoyed using a Pitt as my office Bible and will likely do so again in
future.  In this category, it earns
a 10/10.
