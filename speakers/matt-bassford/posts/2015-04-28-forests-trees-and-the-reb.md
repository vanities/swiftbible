I’m not a translation guru, and
different translations are not the focus of my Bible blogging.  Because I typically confine my reviews to
translations like the NASB, ESV, NKJV, and NIV, all of which are familiar to a conservative/evangelical
audience, I rarely find nuances of translation worth discussing.  However, my next review concerns an edition
of the Revised English Bible, or REB, of which American evangelicals have
little knowledge.  As a result, a brief
discussion of the REB and translations generally is in order.

The REB has had little impact in evangelical
circles for two reasons.  First, it is a
dynamic-equivalence rather than formal-equivalence translation.  It seeks to translate the Hebrew and Koine
Greek of the original Bible manuscripts on a thought-for-thought rather than a
word-for-word level.  My religious
tradition is extremely conservative, and its adherents have a particular
reverence for the very words of Scripture. 
In consequence, they (I?) often regard dynamic-equivalent translations
(even the NIV) as “men changing the words of the Bible” and reject them
accordingly.  For similar reasons, we are
also suspicious of gender-neutral translations, of which the REB is one.

Second, the REB is oriented toward
members of mainline denominations rather than evangelicals, and its treatment
of Old-Testament prophecy reflects this. 
The evangelical student who reads Paul’s argument about “seed” and
“seeds” in Galatians 3:16 and seeks support for that argument in Genesis 22:18
in the REB is not going to find it. 
Likewise, if he looks for “mighty God” in Isaiah 9:6, he’s not going to
find that either.

Gallons of ink and bushels of
pixels have already been expended in print and online arguments about translation
philosophy in these locations.  I do not
intend to add to the commotion.  I lack
the original-language expertise necessary to make a substantive contribution on
the subject, so rather than sharing my biased opinion about which camp of
translators is correct, I merely note that a difference exists.

So, then, the REB does not appear
to be a Bible “for me”, but I have chosen to use and review it anyway.  Even though its principles of translation
make it unsuitable for me for some uses, those same features may well make it
more useful for other purposes than the translations on which I customarily
rely.

Ever since my parents first found
(and quickly purchased) the New Testament of the original NASB in Katz’s
Drugstore in Kansas City in 1963, we have been an NASB family.  If I am exiled to Patmos for the rest of my
life and am allowed to bring only one Bible with me, I’m taking an NASB.  I am willing to tolerate, and indeed have grown
used to, its sometimes awkward phrasing because of its great utility in verse-by-verse
study.

However, as much as I love the
NASB, it isn’t a perfect translation (if such a thing can even exist).  I believe that meaning exists in the Bible on
a verse-by-verse and even a word-by-word level. 
In that kind of close textual analysis, the NASB shines.  However, I also believe that meaning exists
in the Bible at the level of context (which is not necessarily captured by our
chapter-numbering system) and whole book. 
The Bible has trees in it, but it is a forest too.

The very attributes that make the
NASB useful for tree-by-tree study make it less useful for surveying the
forest.  Even the NASB is not a true
word-for-word translation like the English text in an interlinear would be, but
it comes closer to that extreme than does any other modern translation.  As a result, there are many places where the
NASB emphasizes precision of language over clarity of thought.

For instance, in 1 Thessalonians
4:4, the NASB does not supply the reader with the meaning of the Greek idiom
“his own vessel”.  It simply translates
the idiom into English and leaves the reader to figure things out on his
own.  The translation is precise, but it
may well be unclear.

In slow-paced verse-by-verse
study, that lack of clarity isn’t a significant problem.  After all, the object of close study is to
discern every nuance of the text.  If the
student has to spend a few minutes puzzling over the meaning of “his own
vessel”, so much the better.

However, if our goal is to read
the entirety of 1 Thessalonians and develop a general idea of what Paul is
talking about, “his own vessel” is a hindrance to understanding, not an
asset.  No matter how we read “vessel”
(whether it be as “wife”, “body”, or even “member”), the meaning of 1
Thessalonians 4:1-8 does not change.  It’s
still a context about sexual purity.  If
all we want to draw from the context is Paul’s main point (“Be sexually pure”)
then a translation that forces us to dwell on three-word Greek idioms is
keeping us from our objective.

Of late, I have come to realize
that my primary difficulties with understanding the Scripture do not appear at
the verse-by-verse level.  Instead, they
present themselves at the level of context and book.  I’ve never had trouble grasping the basic
meaning of the two parables of the kingdom that Jesus relates in Luke
13:18-20.  However, I’ve only recently
come to see that they should be understood in the context of the events of Luke
13:10-17.  I’m good with trees, but I’m
less good with the forest.

It strikes me, then, that I ought
to spending less time with tree-y translations and more time with forest-y ones.  Of the latter, I’ve decided that the REB is
among the most intriguing.  It isn’t so
gender-neutral that the gender-neutrality obscures some of the larger arguments
of Scriptures, it is reputed to be among the most literary of the modern
translations, and I find some of its renderings delightful.  For instance, Mark 3:5 in the REB reads,

And looking
round at them with anger and sorrow at their obstinate stupidity, he said to
the man, “Stretch out your arm.”  He
stretched it out and his arm was restored.

Even the strictest NASB purist
must acknowledge that as descriptions of the scribes and Pharisees go,
“obstinately stupid” is a pretty good one!

Ultimately, the purpose of Bible
reading and study is not to be amused by the wit of the translators.  It is to help us understand God and His will
for us.  In pursuit of such a noble goal,
no tool that offers usefulness, even if it is a translation we would not
ordinarily use, should be cast aside.
