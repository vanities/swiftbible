One of my great frustrations with politics is the silliness
of the promises that politicians so often make. 
They’ve concluded, probably correctly, that Americans will vote for the
candidate who promises the most goodies for everybody at the least cost.  The voters don’t want to hear about tradeoffs
and tough choices—you know, the things that actually exist in the real
world.  Instead, they want to hear about
butterflies and rainbows, so that’s what the politicians promise.

As eye-rolling as these promises can get, there are extremes
to which even politicians will not go. 
They might promise an end to war and poverty (fat chance on either one,
honestly), but even the most brazen office-seeker will not promise an end to
death, even though death in its various flavors is the biggest problem that the
human race has.

If we want that kind of promise, we have to look beyond
those who are merely human to the Lord Jesus Himself.  What’s more, unlike the empty promises of so
many, we know that Jesus will make good on His promise of life, because He’s
already done it.  We see the evidence of
His divine power in John 11, in the story of Jesus, the resurrection and the
life.  As we go through this story, let’s
pay special attention to the way that Jesus is able to use even tragedy to
glorify God.



**Traveling to Bethany**

The story begins with John describing how Jesus traveled to
Bethany.  Strangely, though, this trip
begins with **JESUS DELAYING.** John tells the tale in John 11:1-6.  As John is at pains to point out, even though
Mary, Martha, and Lazarus are minor characters in the story of Jesus’ ministry,
they’re important minor characters. 
Jesus has stayed with them before, and indeed is going to stay with them
throughout the week before His crucifixion. 
These are good people whom He knows. 
Indeed, these are good people whom He loves.

Mary and Martha have sent to Him asking Him to come help
Lazarus, who is clearly very sick. 
Normally, when Jesus receives a request like this, He hops up and goes
a-healing.  We would expect Him to do the
same for His dear friends, but He doesn’t. 
Instead, He stays where He is for two more days.  What in the world is going on here?

I think there are two answers to the question.  The first is that even if Jesus left
immediately, He would still arrive too late to save Lazarus’ life.  Do the math. 
We see here in v. 6 that Jesus waits for two days, but by the time he
arrives in Bethany, Lazarus has already been dead for four days.  From the very beginning, any lesser miracle
than resurrection is off the table.

That, I think, explains Jesus’ delay.  He knows what He is going to have to do to
restore Lazarus, so He waits in order to make the miracle as God-glorifying as
possible.  Back in some forgotten science
class, I learned what’s called “the rule of three” about human survival.  On average, a human can survive for 30 days
without food, three days without water, or three minutes without air.  According to the burial customs of the day,
Lazarus would have been buried as soon as possible after his death, certainly
within 24 hours.  Jesus waits two days so
that Lazarus will have been in the tomb more than three days, by which point he
would be dead even if he had been entombed alive.  Jesus waits to make the miracle of Lazarus’
resurrection undeniable.

Next, we see a discussion of **JESUS TRAVELING.** Read on
through John 11:7-16.  When Jesus informs
the disciples that He’s leaving for Judea now, all of a sudden, a whole bunch
of resistance develops.  They point out
that the last time He was in the vicinity of Jerusalem, the Jews tried to stone
Him.  One gets the feeling here that
they’re just as worried about their own skins here as they are about
Jesus.  Jesus says He has to go wake up
sleeping Lazarus, and the disciples take this as literally and craftily as
possible.  If Lazarus is just sleeping,
then he’ll get better on his own, so there’s really no need for Jesus and the
disciples to travel into danger, right?

In reply to this convenient failure to understand, Jesus
explains what is actually going on. 
Lazarus isn’t merely sleeping. 
He’s dead.  He also explains why
this is all going on—it’s for the sake of the faith of the disciples.

This is central to understanding the story.  If you haven’t heard it before, I apologize
for the spoiler, but most of us are aware that Jesus raises Lazarus from the
dead.  Why do that, though?  If Jesus has the power to raise Lazarus from
the dead, He also has the power to heal Lazarus’ sickness or even keep Lazarus
from getting sick in the first place.

Why, then, does Jesus choose the dead-and-resurrected
route?  There are lots of reasons for Him
not to.  Mary, Martha, the other
mourners, and even Jesus Himself suffered greatly because Lazarus died.  For that matter, I doubt Lazarus had a lot of
fun dying.  And yet, Jesus chose a course
that took Him and a number of people whom He loved through a great deal of
suffering.  Why?

The key, I think, lies in the effect that the miracle has on
others.  If Jesus prevents Lazarus from
getting sick in the first place, no one else is even aware that something has
happened.  Who knows how many disasters
God protects His people from all the time? 
If Jesus shows up in time to heal sick Lazarus—which He could
have—that’s just another garden-variety healing.  If, however, Jesus heals a man who has been
dead four days, that’s going to change some hearts.  Jesus allows them to suffer, and indeed
allows us to suffer, because great good can come even from suffering.

**Jesus in Bethany**

All this takes us, then, to the things that occur when Jesus
reaches Bethany.  First, He meets **MARTHA**. 
The story unfolds in John 11:17-27. 
One of the things I love about the Bible is how authentically human the
characters in its stories are.  So here
with Martha.  She goes to meet Jesus, and
the first thing she does is pin the blame on Him for her brother’s death.  Then, probably shocked by what just came out
of her mouth, she crawfishes with a “But I still believe in You.”  Jesus assures her that Lazarus is going to
rise again, but rather than connecting that with what she has just said, she
treats it as one of those platitudes that you hear at funerals.  Apparently, she does not think that God will
give Jesus whatever He asks, at least not with respect to her dead brother.

In response, Jesus tells her something that she does not
understand, but which is the heart of the story.  The resurrection is not a date.  The resurrection is a man, and that man is
Jesus.  He has such life-giving power
that those who believe in Him and die will live again, and also that those who
live and believe in Him will never die.

We understand that Jesus here is talking about two different
things.  The first is the physical
resurrection from the dead; the second is the protection that Jesus offers us
from the spiritual death of sin.  I’m
pretty sure, though, that Martha doesn’t understand either one.  She isn’t expecting Jesus to raise Lazarus,
and she has no reason to grasp that Jesus’ death will offer salvation from sin.  I read her final words to Jesus, then, not as
a ringing affirmation of faith, but as spiritual boilerplate.

From Martha, we learn an important lesson about how much
slack God gives His people sometimes, and it’s a lot more than we often
think.  When we’re dealing with some
trial or tragedy, it’s okay for us not to get it.  Even more provocatively, it’s okay for us to
be mad at God.  Martha is definitely mad
at God, and Jesus never slaps her down for it. 
What’s not okay, though, is for us to give up on God, and even in her
anger and confusion, Martha never stops believing.

Next, we meet **MARY
AND THE CROWDS.** Consider John
11:28-37.  Martha precipitates this scene
by returning home and telling Mary that Jesus has asked for her.  So far as we can tell from John, Jesus has
done no such thing, so Martha’s motives for saying so are unclear.  Maybe she’s big-sistering and trying to get
Mary out of the house because it would be good for her.  Mary comes weeping to Jesus, followed by a
crowd of mourners, also weeping.  She
falls at his feet and repeats a more dramatic version of Martha’s
accusation.  Jesus is to blame for their
suffering.

As much as I love the humanity of the other figures in this
story, still more do I love the humanity that is revealed in Jesus.  If we were in His sandals and two friends of ours
accused us of being responsible for a third friend’s death, that would rock
us.  It rocks Jesus, in part because He
knows that accusation has some truth to it. 
Jesus could have kept Lazarus from dying if He had chosen to do so.  When Mary invites Jesus to Lazarus’s tomb, to
confront the concrete evidence of what His decision has cost, He loses it.  In what is famously the shortest verse in the
Bible yet simultaneously one of the most powerful, we see Jesus weeping.

Again, this is a verse from which we can take tremendous
comfort.  Sometimes, I think we have this
vision of God as some kind of heavenly chess grand master, moving pieces around
the board to accomplish His will without having much concern for them.  I don’t think it was like that before the
cross, and I know it’s certainly not like that after the cross.  Because Jesus is emotionally invested in us,
when we hurt, He hurts too.  This doesn’t
mean that He will necessarily spare us pain, but He does understand the price
we pay, and He pays it right along with us.

The climax of the story, though, comes when **LAZARUS** is raised from the dead.  John explains in John 11:38-44.  A couple of things stand out about this
account.  The first is that Martha continues
to reveal her confusion and lack of faith. 
Yeah, yeah, she thinks Jesus is the Son of God and all that, but even
when Jesus commands that the stone be rolled away from the mouth of Lazarus’s
tomb, she fails to grasp that Jesus is going to raise him from the dead right
now.  Rather than being excited about the
miracle, she’s concerned about the stench.

There’s a lesson here for us.  Even though we don’t have Jesus roaming
around today raising folks from the dead, we can still be more concerned with
the stench of spiritual failure than excited about the power of God.  For instance, we can be so sure that nobody
would be interested in the gospel that we never tell anybody about it.  We can be so convinced that nobody at church
likes us that we never reach out to them. 
We can be so certain that we wouldn’t be any good at serving God in the
assembly or in our Bible classes that we never even make the effort.  Friends, in none of those cases is it really
about us.  It’s about God, and no matter
whether we repeat a boilerplate confession of faith like Martha, our failure in
those areas shows that we don’t believe when it counts.

Jesus, of course, is not deterred by Martha’s lack of
faith.  Instead, He praises God for
answering Him, and a man who has been dead for four days rises from the
dead.  This is unique.  Generally in the gospels, Jesus only raises
from the dead those who have only recently died.  I suspect the Pharisees did their best to
explain those away.  However, there’s no
explaining this one away.  It is a
public, spectacular, incontestable miracle.

Before we partake of the Lord’s Supper, then, let’s draw the
obvious line from Lazarus’s resurrection to Jesus’ resurrection to our
resurrections.  We don’t have to wonder
whether Jesus can defeat death, because the Bible shows us that He already has.  Because Jesus died for us, we have the sure
and certain hope of eternal life.  Let’s
ponder this as we share in the bread and cup.

**The Leaders’ Reaction**

The final section of the John 11 account concerns the
reactions of the Jewish leaders to this miracle.  This begins when **THE SANHEDRIN CONFERS** about what to do.  Let’s read here from John 11:45-52.  At this point, we see Jesus’ ministry enter
its final phase.  Up until now, Jesus has
been a religious irritant in a country that is full of religious irritants.  He’s been doing most of His preaching and
performing most of His miracles off in the backwater of Galilee.

Now, however, He’s raised Lazarus from the dead in a suburb
of Jerusalem with a whole bunch of people from Jerusalem watching him.  Understandably, many of them believe in
Him.  Jesus has hit the big time.  In the eyes of the Jewish leaders, He has
moved from being a religious irritant to being a threat to national
security.  If He keeps on doing what He’s
been doing, He’s going to bring the Romans down on everybody, and the Jewish
elites are going to lose everything.

To the Sanhedrin, this is an intolerable threat.  Apparently, it never crosses their mind that
if Jesus is truly from God—which the miracles prove He is—then not even the
Romans can stop Him.  They decide they
have to stop Him themselves, and the high priest Caiaphas voices the ugly truth
that everyone is thinking.  Miracle
worker or no, they’re going to have to kill Jesus.  The innocent man is going to have to die so
that the whole nation can live.

In saying this, Caiaphas is more right than he knows.  In fact, because he is high priest, he is
prophesying—predicting that Jesus is going to die for the holy nation of all
God’s people.  This is important because
of what it shows us about the connection between spiritual gifts and
salvation.  Caiaphas is an evil man
plotting to kill the Son of God, yet he can still be inspired to speak a true
prophecy.  Thus, when people today insist
that speaking in tongues or some other display of so-called spiritual gifts
shows that they have been saved, they could not be more wrong.

Finally, we come to **THE
FALLOUT** from all of this.  Look at
John 11:53-57.  First, the Jewish leaders
start looking for opportunities to kill Jesus. 
They give orders that He be reported and arrested on sight.  It’s a sure thing that once they get their
hands on Jesus, they don’t intend to let Him go again.  Of course, as the week before the crucifixion
proves, it ends up not being very easy to arrest a man whom the multitudes
consider a prophet, but the leaders don’t know that yet.

Because the Jewish leadership is after Him, Jesus decides
that it’s time to lie low for a while. 
He goes to a town named Ephraim, which was probably about 15 miles due
north of Jerusalem.  However, His concern
here is not for His safety.  Instead,
it’s for the timing of His death.  If
Jesus had wanted to, He clearly could have gotten the chief priests to have Him
killed right then and there.  However, He
wanted to wait until the Passover, so that in His death, He would be the
antitype of the Passover lamb, killed so that the angel of death would pass
over His people.

The third ingredient here is the people.  As Jews from all over start drifting into
Jerusalem in preparation for the Passover, they quickly find out about this
prophet who has raised a man from the dead. 
Naturally, they want to seek Him out, but nobody knows where He is.  Many even assume that fear for His life is
going to keep Him from putting in an appearance at the Passover.  Obviously, this is a tense situation, and it
sets the stage for everything that happens on the week of Jesus’ crucifixion.
