*Be devoted to
one another in brotherly love; give preference to one another in honor; ...
contributing to the needs of the saints, practicing hospitality.*

-- Romans 12:10, 13 (NASB)

This text, and several others besides, stresses the
importance of hospitality to God’s people. 
However, many brethren are hospitable rarely, if at all.  I don't think any program can solve the
problem (I tend to be suspicious of the power of programs to solve any
problems). Instead, the solutions have to lie in the hearts of individual
Christians.



First of all, we have to be determined to enlarge what
my mother called "the circle of care". A lot of Christians I know
seem to care about their families and maybe one best friend outside those
families. Other than that, they will be polite and say hi after services, but
they don't want to let anybody else in. I don't think they're being willfully
evil. They're just wrapped up in the lives they already have and not thinking
of others. Instead, we have to make a concerted effort to care about more and
more people all the time, especially the marginalized and excluded and
overlooked. Be mindful, and then be active!

Second, we have to be determined not to over-schedule.
As far as I can tell, most Christians are extremely busy. However, we rarely
pause to ask ourselves what we are accomplishing with all the busy-ness, nor
what we are losing by not leaving quiet spaces in our lives. If there isn't
room for anything else in our schedules, there isn't going to be room for
anyone else either. I think Christian women particularly have to learn to say
"No", even if what they are saying "No" to is good in and
of itself. A life with too many good things in it will crowd out people too.

Finally, we need to be less concerned with our images.
If most people's homes are anything like mine, they're chronically messy.
Ironically, I think this is another ill effect of materialism. We have so much
stuff it can't help but make a mess. When my dad was growing up, he had one toy
car. One. My son has dozens. Whose mess was easier to clean up? For that
matter, which boy would be less likely to leave a toy car lying around where it
could be broken?

So we've got houses full of stuff that is spilling
everywhere (and children are not the only, nor even the worst, offenders here),
and we're ashamed to let people see our mess. The simple solution, rather than
giving up or giving away our abundance of possessions, is never to invite
anyone over. We have houses that are for stuff, but not for people. Personally,
I suspect that if we all flat-out trashed half of everything we owned, we'd be
better off for it.  Failing that, if
concern for the mess keeps us from being hospitable, we need to welcome others
to our homes in spite of it.  After all,
their houses are unlikely to look any better than ours do!
