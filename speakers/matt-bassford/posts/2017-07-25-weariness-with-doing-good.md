Last week, a Facebook friend posted [an article](http://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/desert-fathers-sins-acedia-sloth) that informed me that wayyy back in the day, instead of the familiar Seven
Deadly Sins (though I’m not too sure about the basis of the ranking), there
were eight.  The eighth was called acedia
(and “acedia” is an actual English word. 
Who knew?)

Acedia is a precursor to the idea of sloth, but according to
the author, “It was more like dejection that made it difficult to be spiritual,
avoiding ascetic practices, boredom that led to falling asleep while reading,
and frustration with life in a monastery.” 
Dejection.  Boredom.  Frustration. 
I don’t know too much about the spiritual struggles of monks, but that
sounds a whole lot to me like the very modern problem of spiritual
burnout.  Or, to borrow from Galatians
6:9, it is weariness with doing good.

I think anybody who has been a Christian for longer than six
weeks has experienced this, and no matter what our spiritual maturity level may
be, the devil will do everything he can to make sure that our situation wearies
us.  This weariness arises from several
sources:  the feeling that we’ve fallen
into a spiritual rut, doubt that our efforts are accomplishing anything useful,
impatience with the failings of other Christians, a life that makes heavy
demands on scarce resources of money, time, and energy, and the enticement of
secular activities (whether sinful or not) that appear more rewarding.



**The Effects of
Weariness**

If we give into weariness, we will become lesser disciples
than we were.  Christians in the front
ranks of spiritual combat will drift toward the rear echelons.  Compassionate types who used to focus on
others will draw inward and focus on their own problems.  Faithful attendees will assemble less faithfully.  Bibles will collect dust.  Prayers will go unspoken.  Any time God’s word calls us to action, we
will answer the trumpet with a shrug. 
Been there.  Done that.  What’s the use?  We’re not lazy, exactly.  We’re tired of following Christ.

I think this problem is common in churches all across the
country, and it strikes at Christians in all sorts of different situations,
from relatively new converts to preachers and elders.  However, its familiarity should not deaden us
to its danger.  If left to flourish,
weariness with doing good will inevitably keep us from bearing fruit for God,
and it will open our lives to sin.

Note that in many cases, the sin will coexist with an
outward appearance of righteousness. 
This is the deacon’s wife with the porn habit (porn, alas, stopped being
an exclusively male problem a while back). 
This is the preacher who starts an affair.  Their motivation isn’t love of sin, exactly;
it’s being sick of being righteous all the time.  The evil is inviting because it calls them to
feel in a way that the routine of godliness no longer does.  The routine will continue, but it will be
slowly devoured from the inside by the sin.

**Weariness and Growth**

The disease is deadly, but the cure is less apparent, and
the best way to deal with the problem is to work on avoiding it.  Weariness with doing good usually doesn’t
coincide with spiritual growth.  After
all, we might not be enjoying our struggle to defeat a sin or the challenges of
taking on some new work, but even if those things aren’t particularly pleasant
on their own, at least they’re not the same old same old!

Instead, weariness tends to come with spiritual
plateaus.  When we’re no longer trying to
conquer the evil in our lives, but we’re just kind of skirmishing with it,
that’s when we get sick of the process. 
When we’ve been doing exactly the same thing for the Lord for about the
past 500 years, yeah, we’re likely to get tired of it.  However, the problem there isn’t really that
we’re being godly.  It’s that we aren’t.  Following the upward call of God in Christ
Jesus does not mean filling out the same old checklist!  If we aren’t growing, if we aren’t changing
because we aren’t growing, we’re setting ourselves up for a world of problems.

**Weariness and Joy**

Similarly, we can handle weariness with doing good by
resolutely finding joy.  It’s striking
that Paul wrote Philippians, the great epistle of joy, in circumstances that
would have tempted most to weariness.  He
was in prison, for one thing.  Outside,
Christians in Philippi and elsewhere were using their freedom to bicker with
one another.  Some of them were even
turning the gospel itself into an instrument of self-promotion.

Paul answers all of these things with joy.  “Rejoice with me!” he cries to his
audience.  This is not the syrupy slogan
of a Pollyanna who has never encountered hardship.  These are the words of a man who, rather than
allowing himself to become oppressed by all that is unsatisfactory and evil,
instead fixes his eyes on the good.  The
Philippians have their problems, but they’re generally glorifying God.  Even the self-serving preachers can’t dent
Paul’s joy.  Even if they are looking to
exalt themselves by preaching the gospel, they are still preaching the gospel!

So too, we can learn to look for the good.  Maybe a brother said something that
discouraged us, but it is almost certainly true that somewhere else in the
congregation, a Christian is growing into a new role.  Maybe we’ve sung this song a hundred times
before, but there’s probably an elderly sister in the pews who is clasping that
worn-out hymn to her heart as she prepares to make her departure.  Wherever the word of Christ and the disciples
of Christ are, there will also be cause for joy.  If Paul always had reason to rejoice, we do
too.

**Weariness and Heaven**

In this, we must most of all fix our eyes on the greatest
good.  In Galatians 6:9, Paul tells us
that we must not become weary because if we don’t, we will reap in due
season.  Perhaps even more famously, he
exhorts us at the end of 1 Corinthians 15 to be steadfast and immovable,
because our labor is not in vain in the Lord. 
What’s the use?  There is always a
use in serving God.  Always, always,
always.

Biblically speaking, we don’t do the right thing because of
the earthly consequences at all.  We do
the right thing because it is the right thing, because even if it doesn’t seem
to matter to anyone else, it matters to God. 
We don’t get our reward from life always going our way and people
noticing our godliness and saying nice things to us.  We get our reward from eternal life, from
Jesus noticing our godliness and saying, “Well done, good and faithful
servant!”

We don’t gain that accolade by our perfection.  We gain it by our faithfulness, by refusing
to become weary even when life is wearisome. 
All of us hit rough patches now and again.  Our job, with the Lord’s help, is to press on
through them.  For the sake of the prize
set before us, there is no weariness that is too great to be endured.
