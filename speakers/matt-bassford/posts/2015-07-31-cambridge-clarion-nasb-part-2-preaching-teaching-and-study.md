**OVERVIEW.** Despite its small size, the Clarion
performed capably in just about everything I needed it to do as a preacher.  However, determined Bible-writers would do
well to look elsewhere.

As always, the Clarion was the only Bible I used for pulpit
and office work for two weeks.  However,
due to some last-minute changes, it didn’t see as much time in the pulpit as my
review Bibles normally do.  I only used
it to preach one sermon, and that sermon had fewer Scriptures than I normally
cite.  However, I did employ it to teach
four Bible classes and two less formal Bible studies.  I also used it to write sermons and blog
posts.  Here’s what I thought:



**PREACHING.** The size of the Clarion was fine in the
pulpit.  As it happens, the Clarion has
just about the same trim size as the notebook in which I write my notes for
topical sermons these days, though a brown-calf Clarion would have been a
better stylistic match.

This small size proved to be important.  A year or two back, my church replaced the
old pulpit with a much smaller model. 
The surface of the pulpit is only about 18 inches wide, so I can’t have
a larger Bible and an open sermon notebook on it at the same time.  However, the closed Clarion fit in neatly
beside the notebook, so that I didn’t have to put it on top of the computer
monitor or anything else awkward.

The Clarion, then, is a good choice for the way I preach
topical sermons.  For expository sermons,
it would be a less good choice.  Since my
conversion to the adherents of wide-margin Bibles earlier this year, if I’m
going to preach an expository sermon, I’m going to want to preach it out of a
Bible like that.  I don’t want a sermon
notebook.  I want all the notes I need
written in my Bible.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEjnSps6JvhmU3dSBfuaFWhEWVygV84k0RjL2GvJD7xCEvqxz86YoMYeRT85Jms_TPRXxmWgN1GCxLU84eRHmrwhlJeSGEUnI7fyEyL4vmRjyGHCpihIjz-oTKLFQ8Z_OuZt9O0X3U0iwg/s640/PTS+margins.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEjnSps6JvhmU3dSBfuaFWhEWVygV84k0RjL2GvJD7xCEvqxz86YoMYeRT85Jms_TPRXxmWgN1GCxLU84eRHmrwhlJeSGEUnI7fyEyL4vmRjyGHCpihIjz-oTKLFQ8Z_OuZt9O0X3U0iwg/s1600/PTS+margins.jpg)



Quite frankly, the Clarion is not a Bible that is intended
to be written in very much, and it wouldn’t be a good idea to try.  It has quarter-inch left and right margins,
which is enough space to keep the text from falling into the gutter, but not
enough space to write much in.

Also, that super-thin paper that keeps the thickness of the
Clarion down wouldn’t do much to keep ink from bleeding through.  I did not test my theory in an edge-lined
goatskin Bible that does not belong to me, but I’m pretty sure that even a
Pigma Micron 005 would do terrible things to a Clarion.  Those who want to try writing in such a Bible
would be best advised to stick to pencil.

Though the Clarion doesn’t even make a poor substitute for a
wide-margin Bible, at least it didn’t give me much trouble in finding
citations.  As I said, I only preached
one sermon with it, and during said sermon, I only read eight different
passages.  However, none of them were
part of the same context, so I had to turn and locate passages eight different
times.

During the passage-location process, I found myself bothered
a little bit by the extremely thin paper in the Clarion.  It was hard to turn precisely.  I often found myself turning four or five
pages at a time when I only intended to turn one.  I doubt that anyone in the congregation
noticed any delays, but it might have taken me half a second longer to find
each passage than I’ve been used to with Bibles with more robust paper.

However, in those eight passages, I didn’t miss a single citation.  This is particularly interesting because the
Bibles that gave me fits in the past, the Crossway Personal-Size Reference and
the Crossway Legacy, are both also single-column paragraphed Bibles.  Indeed, the Legacy is even set in the same
Lexicon No. 1 as the Clarion.  I was
expecting to have trouble, but I didn’t.

I attribute this difference to two main features of the
Clarion.  First, the Clarion has a
shorter line length than the Legacy or the PSR. 
The former has a characters per line count of around 65, while the
latter two come in at 73 and 78 respectively. 
That might not sound like a big deal, but it’s the difference between a
line of manageable length and a line that’s long enough to get lost in.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEjol0yhIjgm2008rrkJtu0_oBQ6Iab5nf_E3_4YS9bscjfIFMo7BtVX4n4N_3uV4NsAMglp1XR7dSn1xZ7emuktFTKqWITpCbx6H8yuISYWNxmAcm8SKSH_Aw88MrRnT4QkY_q1QrKGrg/s640/PTS+line+length.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEjol0yhIjgm2008rrkJtu0_oBQ6Iab5nf_E3_4YS9bscjfIFMo7BtVX4n4N_3uV4NsAMglp1XR7dSn1xZ7emuktFTKqWITpCbx6H8yuISYWNxmAcm8SKSH_Aw88MrRnT4QkY_q1QrKGrg/s1600/PTS+line+length.jpg)



Second, the Clarion
has fairly dramatic leading:  10.5-point
leading with 8.75-point text.  Unlike
Foundation, which tends to under-lead its Bible layouts, Cambridge generally
tends to add a little bit of extra leading, but even for Cambridge, nearly two
points’ worth of leading is unusual.

Personally, I suspect that the amount of leading is a design
consequence, rather than a design choice. 
I think Cambridge found that with Lexicon No. 1, which is a pretty
squatty typeface, a page with 65 characters per line, 500 words per page, and a
reference column is going to be pretty close to a square page.  Cambridge’s solution to the unlovely page
dimensions was to bump up the leading until it looked right.  However, the leading does make each line in
the text more distinct, which makes finding a particular verse number easier.

**TEACHING.** Experience has taught me that generally
speaking, I’m willing to wander around with a Bible that weighs two pounds or
less, but that I will set a heavier Bible on the lectern and not have it with
me.  The Clarion is four ounces shy of
two pounds, so predictably, I was comfortable carrying it with me.  This is a good thing.

As for cross-references, despite its relatively small size,
the Clarion packs what looks like the full NASB reference suite.  That’s enough cross-references for
anybody!  I didn’t use them during the
times-of-services Bible classes, but I did during some smaller study
sessions.  In particular, I remember
locating the blasphemy-of-the-Holy-Spirit passage in Matthew 12 by following
the cross-reference from the similar text in Mark 3.  As always, cross-references don’t seem like a
big deal until you need them, but once you do need them, you’re glad to have
them.

**STUDY.** The Clarion was a kind office Bible.  It had all the references I could have wanted
(though as it turned out, I didn’t want them). 
I could put it in my lap and type without having to strain my back to
reach the keyboard.  I didn’t have to
strain my eyes, either.  8.75/10.5
Lexicon No. 1 when printed by Jongbloed is extremely readable!  Also, so far as I have yet discovered, I
didn’t miss any citations either.

**CONCLUSION.** As a preaching Bible, the Clarion has one
problem.  It doesn’t have wide margins
that you can write in.  Sadly, for me
these days, that is the one characteristic that a preaching Bible must have.  However, it pretty well managed to do
everything else right.  I’ll give it a
7.5/10 for preaching, teaching, and study.
