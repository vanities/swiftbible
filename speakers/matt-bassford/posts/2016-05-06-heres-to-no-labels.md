A couple weeks back, *The New York Times* ran [this op-ed.](http://www.nytimes.com/2016/04/24/opinion/sunday/findinglove-again-this-time-with-a-man.html?_r=1)  It was written by Harris Wofford, a former U.S. senator from Pennsylvania, and concerns his upcoming marriage.  Normally, this is not the sort of thing that
intrigues *The Times*, but Wofford was
about to marry a man.

The story gets stranger. 
This union, since celebrated on April 30th, is Wofford’s
second marriage.  His first was to a
woman.  It was a happy marriage that
spanned nearly 50 years and produced three children.  A few years after his wife’s death,
75-year-old Wofford encountered 25-year-old Matthew Charlton at the beach.  After the passing of time and appropriate
legislation, they were wed.



This is not the sort of incident in which I rejoice.  However, I thought there was one particularly
interesting paragraph in Wofford’s editorial. 
He wrote, “Too often, our society seeks to label people by pinning them
on the wall — straight, gay or in between. I don’t categorize myself based on
the gender of those I love. I had a half-century of marriage with a wonderful
woman, and now am lucky for a second time to have found happiness.”

I may not agree with the man about much, but I am with him
100 percent when it comes to labels.  One
of the weirdest things about our society, and we’re so used to it that we can’t
even see how weird it is, is our habit of characterizing people according to
their sexual attractions.

**The Origin of Labeling**

This kind of labeling doesn’t stand up either practically or
Scripturally.  Sometimes, I hear
conservatives make fun of the alphabet soup of LGBTQ, etc.  However, the problem is not that the ever-growing
acronym is too complicated.  It is that
it is too simple.  People are
complicated, and they are particularly complicated when it comes to sex.  The only way to have a nice neat label that
precisely captures the sexuality of every human being is to have 7 billion
different labels.

Nor did the labeling game start with the Bible.  Certainly 1 Corinthians 6:9-10 speaks of
effeminate and homosexuals, but it speaks also of the covetous and drunkards
and revilers.  Those are not global
descriptions.  Those are not statements
of personal identity.  Nobody, when
asked, “Tell me about yourself,” says, “I’m a reviler.”  Instead, each of those words means nothing
more than that the person in question had committed a particular sin in the
past (and in context, was no longer committing it).

Instead, all the labeling started with the rise of
psychology in the late 19th century. 
“Homosexual” as an identity can only exist against its counterpart
“heterosexual”, a word which was only coined in 1892.  From that sprang the gay-straight dichotomy that
has caused our society no end of trouble.

Now, most Americans believe and accept that one of the most
important things about you is whom you want to have sex with.

**The Problem with Labeling**

I reject that.  I
don’t think it’s true for most, and I know it’s not true for me.  I guess you could slap the label “straight”
on me if you wanted to, but that is way, way down the list of things about me
that I consider important.  I’m a
Christian.  I’m a preacher.  I’m a hymnist.  I’m a husband.  I’m a father. 
Those are all things that matter deeply to me.  The satisfaction of bodily appetite does
not.

I have the same problem with being described as a
heterosexual that I would with being described as a pizza-gustator.  Both are terribly reductionistic.  They might be what I do sometimes, but they
are not who I am.

One of the greatest problems with the culture wars of the
past few decades is that they have elevated behavior to the status of
identity.  Whether the label fits or not,
we are forced to pick one, wear it, and define ourselves by it and others by
theirs.  As a Bible-believing
conservative (speaking of really inadequate labels!), I am not allowed the
rhetorical room to object only to somebody else’s behavior.

Instead, the cultural narrative goes, either I love them or
I hate them.  If I love them, I must
accept everything they do.  If I don’t, I
must hate them.  Here as elsewhere, the
labels leave no room for complexity and nuance.

**Labeling and Relationships**

In my experience, this simply isn’t how these issues play
out in real life.  There are people out
there who know that I don’t approve of their behavior yet love them with all my
heart.  To me, they are more than their
label, and I refuse to reduce them to it. 
They have sinned, true, but in that, they are neither better nor worse
than I.  They are not faceless members of
some generic group.  They are unique
human beings, dear to me and dear to God.

I also know that life is long and people change, or possibly
are exposed to situations that bring out something in them that they never knew
existed.  It is all very
complicated.  I don’t even understand
myself sometimes; how could I possibly fathom someone else?  And so I pray, and follow the truth as best I
can, and love as best I can.

Here’s to no labels, then. 
Let’s stop treating people as a “they”. 
Let’s start treating them as people. 
Even though we are all subject to the same law and have hope through the
same Savior, our weaknesses are as unique as the rest of us.  Sending somebody to stand in the corner with
a sign over his head is neither helpful nor enlightening.
