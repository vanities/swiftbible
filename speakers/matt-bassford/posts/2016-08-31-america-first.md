Over the weekend, San Francisco 49er quarterback Colin
Kaepernick stirred up quite a bit of controversy in the sports world by
refusing to stand while the national anthem was being played before an NFL
preseason game.  Many of my Facebook
friends (most of whom are Christians), posted status updates condemning his
behavior, while a fairly vocal minority defended it (“The American flag stands
for the freedom not to stand and salute it.”).

I can see both sides of the issue, but I think the fact that
it became an issue at all says something interesting about our priorities.  Lots of Christians get frothy-mouthed about
disrespect shown to our nation, even though the only thing you have to do to
become an American is be born here.



However, what about disrespect shown to our God?  Nobody is born into the Lord’s church.  Everyone who joins the kingdom of heaven does
so voluntarily.  However, as we know all
too well, not everyone who is baptized into Christ continues to walk faithfully
with Him.  Hebrews 10:29 testifies that
willful sinners trample underfoot the Son of God, regard the sanctifying blood
of the covenant as unclean, and insult the Spirit of grace.  It’s the spiritual equivalent not of sitting
down during the national anthem, but of using the American flag for toilet
paper.

Where’s the outrage?

Where is the flood of phone calls, cards, and visits for
Christians who haven’t been assembling as they should recently?  Where is the sense that such behavior is not
a personal choice but is actively shameful? 
Where is the conviction that our relationship with such people can never
be the same because of the deep dishonor they have brought on our Lord?

I’m afraid, though, that many brethren who broadcast their
contempt for the likes of Kaepernick (and who probably wouldn’t tolerate a
similar display in their presence) regard the falling away of Christians in
their own congregations with indifference.

“You know where Tom is?”

“No idea; haven’t seen him in months.”

“Me neither.  Oh;
well.”

As serious as this problem is, I think it points to an even
larger issue.  We all know what the
church answer is.  We all know that our
citizenship is in heaven, and that our earthly citizenship, wherever it may be,
is less important.

‘Problem is, if those are actually our priorities, they often
aren’t evident in the way we live.  We
follow the ups and downs of election season with bated breath, yet can’t find
the time to read the Bible and pray every day (and we know what happens if we
don’t read our Bibles and pray every day). 
Our Facebook walls are filled with political posts, but evidence of our
concern for the things of the spirit is hard to find.  We insist that Everyone Must Vote (“If you
don’t vote for X, you’re voting for Y!”), yet regard attending church services
every time the doors are open as an extra. 
We worry more about who will be the next Supreme Court justices than who
will be our congregation’s next elders.

Don’t get me wrong; I love my earthly country.  I’m thankful that I was born in a place where
I could proclaim and practice my faith without interference from the government,
even though those days may be coming to an end.

However, the United States simply isn’t what matters most to
me, and it shouldn’t be.  One day,
whether through cataclysm or slow decline, this earthly nation will be
destroyed, but the kingdom of heaven will continue.  It is far better to receive a kingdom that
cannot be shaken than a kingdom that inevitably will be.  We need to make sure that our actions show we
are putting our hopes in the right place.
