I’ve heard it said that history doesn’t repeat itself, but
it does rhyme.  That certainly seems to
be the case in the modern-day United States. 
The secular elite in our society think they’re breaking new ground for
the human race, but in reality, they’re accomplishing nothing more than the
degeneracy of paganism in the Roman Empire of the first century.  People back then were wretched.

This isn’t as obvious in the gospels, but it becomes much
clearer once the gospel starts going out among the Gentiles.  In the church in Corinth, we see a whole
bunch of people who come to the Lord carrying a whole lot of baggage.  In dealing with them, Paul can’t take
anything for granted.  He has to spend
several chapters of 1 Corinthians explaining morality from the ground up.

Today, much of this moral instruction has become relevant in
a way it wasn’t 75 years ago.  Back then,
nobody had to explain that fornication, casual divorce, and the practice of
homosexuality were wrong.  Now, we have
to.  Let’s look, then, at Paul’s take on
morality in Christ.



**Worldliness in the
Church**

Paul begins this section of the text by looking at two
problems that reveal worldliness in the church. 
The first of these problems is **TOLERATING
IMMORALITY.** Paul explains in 1
Corinthians 5:1-13.  Really, there are
two problems here.  The first is the man
who has his father’s wife.  This probably
means that he has gotten intimately involved with his stepmother.  As Paul observes, that’s too much even for
the Gentiles.  Yuck!

The second problem, one that is arguably even more serious,
is that the church is OK with this. 
Instead of casting out this unrepentant sinner, they’re celebrating his
sin as a symbol of the freedom they have in Christ.  This too is culturally familiar, as more and
more churches in our country celebrate and exalt every form of sexual
immorality.

Paul deals with the problem in Corinth by informing the Corinthians
that because they haven’t passed judgment on the unrepentant sinner, he’s going
to do it for them.  Rather than
continuing to boast about him, the Corinthians need to have nothing to do with
him, refusing even to eat with him.  If
they don’t, his bad behavior will corrupt all of them.

Today, we have to hold one another to a higher standard than
we hold people in the world to.  We can’t
be like the people around us, and we can’t let our brothers and sisters become
like the people around us.  So much of
the time, churches withdraw from Christians only after they’ve become
completely uninterested and stopped attending services altogether.  That’s not what this passage is about, and it
looks an awful lot like locking the barn door after the horse has been stolen.

Instead, this is a passage about relationship.  We have to know our brothers and sisters well
enough to know what they’re up to, and we have to matter to them enough that
our disapproval can shame them into better conduct.  If not, they may have failed God, but we’ve also
failed them.

The second main problem that Paul identifies is **LAWSUITS BETWEEN BRETHREN.** He explains the situation in 1
Corinthians 6:1-11.  Apparently, the
Corinthian church is busy illustrating the principle that two wrongs don’t make
a right.  The first wrong is that one of
the Christians in the congregation somehow defrauded another one.  However, rather than bringing the problem
before other Christians, the defrauded brother takes the first one to
court!

Paul sees this as a disaster.  Even though the church should have been
perfectly competent to sort out the situation, now the whole mess has been
dragged out into public view.  One can
almost hear the locals sneering, “Yeah, they love each other so much they’re
suing each other!”  This brings shame on
the church and on its Lord both.

Today, the same thing holds true.  Christians shouldn’t sue Christians.  Instead, we should keep family business in
the family, and even if that doesn’t work out the way we want, it’s still a
whole lot better than dragging the church’s reputation through the mud.

However, this text has nothing to do with criminal
cases.  God has given the government the
power to mete out justice, and we don’t have the authority to take that power
out of its hands.  If we become aware
that a Christian has committed a crime, though there may be spiritual
consequences as well, we need to tell them that either they turn themselves in
or we will do it for them.  If we don’t
bring the truth to light, the church may be implicated in a cover-up.

Paul points out, though, that the lawsuit problem indicates
a larger problem.  These are people with
the name of saints but the conduct of the worldly.  God did not cleanse us so that we could go on
being swindlers or drunkards or practicing homosexuals.  He cleansed us so that we could leave behind
those things.  If we cling to them, we
will only end up losing our reward.

Before the Lord’s Supper, let’s reflect on this dual truth
about Christianity.  God expects us to
live holy and righteous lives, but ultimately, our holiness and righteousness do
not depend on that.  Instead, we need to
look to Jesus to do for us what we could not do for ourselves.  It is His sacrifice on the cross that made it
possible for us to be washed, sanctified, and justified.  We must consider our own lives, then, with deep
humility.  This morning, let’s reveal
this humility within ourselves as we honor Jesus by remembering Him.  Second, let’s show it by resolving to
dedicate our lives to Him.  Let’s reflect
on these things as we partake.

**God and the Body**

Next, Paul moves on to an even touchier subject, God and the
body.  He begins by discussing **SEXUAL IMMORALITY.** This appears in 1 Corinthians
6:12-20.  One thing that I like about the
ESV is that it puts the first statement of the reading in quotation marks.  In context, it seems clear that at least some
in the Corinthian church are arguing that Jesus has freed them from all the
rules so they can do whatever they want.

Paul responds by pointing out how this applies in a
qualified sense and then moving on to how it doesn’t apply at all.  Yes, Jesus did make a bunch of things lawful
that had been unlawful under the Law of Moses, especially when it came to clean
and unclean foods.  However, those things
aren’t supposed to control us, and in the long run, they don’t matter.

By contrast, sexual immorality is a very different subject.  When we were baptized and rose to walk in
newness of life, we dedicated our bodies to God and to His service.  We became united in spirit with Him.  How shameful, then, for us to take these holy
vessels for God’s glory and desecrate them by offering them to the service of
lust!  How awful for us to turn our backs
on a spiritual union that’s supposed to last forever for the sake of a fleshly
union with no real meaning!

Indeed, Paul argues here that there’s something unique about
sexual sin.  He says that no other sin
has this same body-desecrating effect. 
People like to use his statement that our body is a temple to prove that
we shouldn’t smoke and drink and so on, and even though I agree with the
conclusion, I don’t think this is the place to prove it.  Instead, we need both to confine the temple
language to the realm of sexual sin and understand its importance there.  Sex as part of God’s plan for marriage honors
Him.  Sex outside of marriage shows Him
contempt.

In fact, given that distinction, it’s hardly surprising that
Paul next addresses **MARITAL
RELATIONS.** Look at 1 Corinthians
7:1-7.  Once again, I like the ESV
formatting, where it puts “It is good for a man not to have sexual relations
with a woman” in quotation marks.  I
think this reflects the vast diversity of beliefs in the Corinthian
church.  Some flung themselves into
sexual immorality, while others rejected sex entirely, even in marriage.

In response to this pie-in-the-sky advocacy of celibacy,
Paul offers the obvious rebuttal.  Sure,
it’s a nice idea in theory, but in practice, an awful lot of folks who dedicate
themselves to celibacy end up in sexual sin. 
This too is something that our modern experience bears out.

If celibacy doesn’t work, what does is sex in marriage.  When Paul says that every man should have his
own wife and so on, he’s speaking euphemistically, just as he does at the
beginning of 1 Corinthians 5.  His point
is not that the unmarried should get married. 
It’s that the married should have sex. 
That way, married people won’t go looking for trouble elsewhere.

However, this plan for defeating the devil only works if
both spouses are satisfied with the result. 
To this end, Paul offers three additional instructions:  offer your spouse what they need, regard your
body as belonging to them, not you, and only go without for a limited time, for
a spiritual purpose, and when both spouses have agreed to it.

In marriage, sex isn’t supposed to be a weapon.  It’s supposed to be a gift, and we must not
weaponize it, either on the terms of this text or outside them.  When one spouse withholds sex and the other
demands it because “the Bible says you have to,” both are wrong.  Paul is calling us to consider how we can
serve our spouses, not how they can serve us. 
I suspect this is a difficult balance to strike in every marriage, but
when we get it right, God is glorified.

**Earthly Relationships**

The final section of the text that we’ll be covering this
morning concerns earthly relationships. 
It first discusses **MARRIAGE AND
DIVORCE.** Let’s read here from 1
Corinthians 7:8-16.  There are three main
groups addressed here:  the unmarried,
Christians married to other Christians, and Christians married to non-Christians.  Paul calls the first of these groups to
self-understanding.  He tells them that
if they want a sexual relationship, they need to get married.  If they can live without one (or if they must
live without one), they should do so. More on this later.

Second, Paul writes to Christians married to Christians.  His point is simple:  don’t get divorced.  This is not merely a wise thing to do; this
is a command from the Lord.  However, if
a Christian ignores the Lord’s command and gets divorced anyway, they shouldn’t
make matters worse by going out and getting remarried.  Instead, they should reconcile.

This passage is important because it makes clear to us that
God doesn’t only have a problem with unscriptural divorce and remarriage.  He has a problem with unscriptural divorce,
period.  In cases where one partner defiles
the marriage bond by cheating on the other, God reluctantly allows separation, but
other than that, divorce is a sin.  His
plan is one husband, one wife, for life.

Finally, Paul addresses the difficult situation of
Christians with non-Christian spouses. 
Note, by the way, that when he says, “Not the Lord, but I”, he doesn’t
mean that he’s offering his own opinion. 
Instead, he’s speaking as an inspired apostle, but one who is delivering
a new revelation, rather than repeating one that Christ has already made.

He tells Christians married to non-Christians not to get
divorced either.  However, if the
unbelieving spouse wants a divorce, the Christian should let them go.  Paul says that the Christian isn’t enslaved
in such a matter.  This doesn’t mean that
they have a right to remarry—unless, of course, there has been adultery—but it
does mean that they can let the unbeliever depart peacefully.

After this, Paul urges Christians to **REMAIN WHERE THEY ARE.** Look
at 1 Corinthians 7:17-24.  Interestingly,
we aren’t particularly concerned with any of the specific examples he
cites.  Today, circumcision versus
uncircumcision is a non-issue, and in our country, slavery is outlawed, so
there isn’t any bondservant versus freedman distinction either.

However, I think there’s a more general lesson we can learn
here about what becoming a Christian doesn’t do.  In Corinth, the brethren got the message that
being baptized freed them from their sins and from the Law of Moses, but a
number of them appear also to have gotten the idea that Christianity freed them
from a host of other things besides.  The
creepy guy in 1 Corinthians 5 who has his father’s wife is only one symptom of
a serious problem.  Paul, then, is trying
to get the Corinthians to understand that Jesus does not set them free from
taboo, from social convention, or even from the life situation where they have
found themselves.

This still holds true for us.  Christ is King, but we still live in the
United States of America.  We must obey
God rather than men, but we still can’t drive on the left side of the street.  Christianity should change the way that we
serve our employers and our spouses, but we are still employed and still
married, and we don’t get to throw off those things.  Being called out of the world doesn’t mean
that we have been called out of human society, and we should never forget it.

Finally, let’s consider Paul’s instructions to **THE UNMARRIED.** These appear in 1 Corinthians
7:25-40.  This text is an interesting
blend of instructions tied to a particular place and time and general
principles that remain true today.  Let’s
start with the first half of what we see here. 
Paul is very plain that he writes what he does “in view of the present distress”.  He also observes that the time is very short
and the present form of the world was passing away.

It doesn’t take much imagination to fill in the blanks
here.  Paul wrote 1 Corinthians around 53
AD, and within the span of 20 years, the world the Corinthians inhabited would
be changed dramatically.  Many of them
were Jewish, and in only about 15 years, the Jewish nation would rise in revolt
against Rome, only to have their rebellion brutally crushed and the temple that
was the center of their religious practice destroyed.  Also, even though Rome is still relatively
benevolent toward Christians when Paul is writing, the Roman persecution of the
church would begin even sooner.  In that
context, it made perfect sense for Christians not to get married and start
businesses.  Things could change so
quickly that in a very short time, they would find themselves with nothing.

However, Paul’s next paragraph is equally applicable, even
in our hopefully much less distressing time. 
No matter how stable your nation may be, it’s still true that unmarried
people have more time to devote to God than married people do.  In fact, Paul suggests that for Christians
who don’t feel particularly driven to get married, choosing to remain single is
the best option.

This is important for us to recognize, particularly when the
perspective of the church today is so pro-family.  It’s easy for us to fall into the trap of
thinking of Christians who are single, whether by necessity or by choice, as
second-class saints.  Nothing could be
further from the truth!  There are gifts
from God that they don’t have, but they do possess the extremely valuable gift
of time.  As a result, they can do more
for the Lord than married Christians can. 
Whatever our situation in life, let’s make sure that we’re using what we
have to glorify God!
