If there has been a theme to my preaching these past several
years, it is that in our study of the Bible, we have to be careful not to miss
the forest for the trees.  It is
certainly true that each verse of the Bible has meaning.  Jesus wasn’t misusing the Scripture when He
cited a verse or two to prove His point. 
However, it is equally true that the Bible is more than a collection of sound
bites.  There are some parts of the book
of Proverbs that are like that, but most of the rest of the Bible is not, and
we do it a disservice when we ignore the overall arguments in favor of single
verses.

I’ve also found, though, that when I merely read the Bible
for 45 minutes without commentary, that’s not necessarily effective at
highlighting those overall arguments either. 
Perhaps the problem is that we’re too used to verse-by-verse study.  When we listen too actively to every verse in
a chapter, we only succeed in wearing ourselves out.  This morning, then, I’d like to take a
different tack:  lots of reading, but
with a fair amount of exposition too. 
Let’s look at John’s epistles.



**The Meaning of
Discipleship**

When we consider the first half of 1 John using this
view-from-20,000-feet approach, we see that in general, it’s about the meaning
of discipleship.  Through about the first
two and a half chapters, John defines discipleship in three different
ways.  The first of these is **WALKING IN THE LIGHT.** Let’s read here from 1 John 1:1-2:6.  Here, John is warning us about two extremes,
both of which can easily be problematic for us today.  The first is believing that discipleship
requires sinlessness.  That’s not
true.  As John points out, even though
we’re not supposed to sin, Jesus came to be the propitiation for when we do sin.  Likewise, forgiveness of sins is one of the
blessings available to those who walk in the light.

Of course, we can go too far in the opposite direction and
use the grace of Jesus as a license for sin. 
John proves that this isn’t part of discipleship either.  We might not be sinless, but we still  show that we have come to know with God by
the way we keep His commandments.  If we
don’t, we don’t abide in Jesus, and if we don’t abide in Jesus, our claims to
discipleship are vain.

Second, John defines discipleship as **LOVING OUR BROTHER**.  Consider
1 John 2:7-24.  Once again, John
structures his argument here in terms of a positive and two negatives.  He argues that on earth, above all else, we
should direct our love toward one another. 
Only as we love one another can we truly understand what it means to
walk in the light of Christ.

However, we have to be careful not to love the world.  This doesn’t mean the physical planet.  It means “world” in the sense of those who
live apart from the kingdom of Christ. 
His kingdom will endure, but the world and everything it loves are going
to be destroyed.

Third, we must be careful not to have fellowship with those
who aren’t our brethren.  In particular,
John is concerned with those whom he calls antichrists, whom he defines in 2
John as those who deny that Jesus has come in the flesh.  However, this principle has application
beyond those who are antichrists. 
Instead, anytime we encounter anyone who claims to be a disciple but
doesn’t have the word of God abiding in him, we run into a potential spiritual
problem.  Let’s be wary of people like
that.

Finally, John describes discipleship as **SEEKING ETERNAL LIFE.** This is
his theme in 1 John 2:25-3:10.  This text
is about the promise of eternal life and the way we react to it.  As John points out at the beginning of the
third chapter, it’s truly mind-boggling. 
When the Lord returns, we’re going to see Him as He is and in the act of
seeing be made like Him.  We can’t even
understand what that’s going to be like, but it’s still true.

Once we grasp how amazing it is, how great the hope of our
faith is, we’re going to live in a certain way. 
How terrible it would be to know the promise of eternal life but fall
short of it!  That’s not what the true
disciple does.  Instead, he lives his
life in reliance on the promise of God. 
This isn’t a matter of occasional action.  It’s a matter of overall practice.  When we make a practice of living
righteously, when we make a practice of loving our brother, we show that we are
among the children of God who will be transformed.  When we make a practice of sin, though, we
show only that we belong to the devil.

**Love and Discipleship**

The second half of 1 John focuses particularly on the
connection between love and discipleship. 
This begins with his analysis of **TRUTH
AND LOVE.** It appears in 1 John
3:11-4:6.  In this text, John tells us
that the ultimate spiritual litmus test is the way we love.  If we truly belong to God, we will love, not
only in word, but in deed and in truth.

This isn’t love as we define it.  Certainly, in our day, the world uses the
word “love” in so many ways that it has become meaningless, little more than a
rhetorical football.  Everybody asserts
that they’re the loving ones, and people who disagree with them are
hateful.  Without a standard, of course,
there’s no way to tell whose claims are correct, but the Bible gives us the
standard we need.  Only when we express
love by keeping God’s commandments are we truly loving.

From here, John moves off into an apparently non-sequitur
discussion of true and false spirits, but I don’t think it’s a non
sequitur.  If God’s Spirit leads us to
genuine love, it follows that any spirit of error will lead us into worldly
deceit that only pretends to be love.  We
need to be wary, then, of any form of worldliness that appears to be
loving.  That’s not truth.  It’s deceit.

Second, John points us to **THE EXAMPLE OF LOVE**, God. 
Let’s read his explanation in 1 John 4:7-5:4.  We’ve already seen the importance of truth as
a definition for love.  Here is what that
definition is.  Love is God selflessly
sending His Son to die for us, because He loved us.

That single, towering act of self-sacrifice and grace is
what we’re supposed to be building our lives around.  It’s supposed to set the pattern for our
conduct.  If we want to know how far we
should go for one another, the dying love of Jesus provides our measuring
stick.  It also reassures us when we
don’t measure up, which none of us actually do. 
It’s not like God stopped loving His people 2000 years ago.  His goal is not to find a reason to condemn
us.  He wants to rescue us.  If we spend our lives seeking Him, no matter
how many times we slip up, we can place our confidence in His love to do the
rest.  Of course, if we aren’t seeking
God, if we don’t use our lives as instruments to express His love for our
brethren, any hope we have is only self-deceit.

Finally, John returns in the second half of the book to the
subject that concluded our study of the first half:  **ETERNAL
LIFE.** Here, consider 1 John
5:5-21.  The road to eternal life begins
with faith in Jesus.  However, this faith
isn’t merely mental acknowledgement that Jesus exists, but trust in the witnesses
He brings that testify to our salvation. 
There are three of these.  The first
is the Spirit of God, speaking through the truth, which defines the children of
God.  The second is the water of
baptism.  The third is the cleansing
blood of Jesus.  In this threefold
witness, we have the testimony of God that we belong to Him and that He has
given us eternal life.

John goes on to observe that the hope of eternal life means
that we have the right to seek God in prayer. 
It means that we need to be concerned for the sin of our brother,
because if he falls, he will lose a prize beyond imagining.  It means finally that we live righteous lives
as a means of coming to know Jesus.

All of this obviously gives us a massive amount to
contemplate before the Lord’s Supper. 
We’re here to remember Jesus, who in His death on the cross literally became
the example of love forever.  We’re here
to remind ourselves of the responsibility His love places on us:  that we are to love one another according to His
word and example.  We do this not simply
because it’s good to do so, but because eternal life with God is at stake, both
for them and for us.  Let’s reflect on
all this as we partake.

**Walking in the Truth**

In the final portion of our time together this morning,
let’s ponder 2 and 3 John.  Even though
they’re different books of the Bible, they both address a common theme:  walking in the truth.  This begins with John’s exploration of the
connections among **TRUTH, LOVE, AND
OBEDIENCE**.  Let’s read here from 2
John 1-13.  Lots of people in our time
are spiritual splitters.  They like love,
at least their version, but they’re leery of the concept of doctrinal truth,
and they certainly don’t want to have anything to do with the icky “legalism”
of obedience!

By contrast, John regards these things as part of an
inseparable whole.  Truth is something
you obey, not merely something you know. 
The truth defines what love is, and obedience is an expression of
love.  You take any one of the three out,
and the whole thing doesn’t work.  This
explains John’s antipathy toward the antichrists.  It might seem awfully abstract to reject
people because they don’t believe that Jesus came in the flesh, but John
believes that accepting them will have practical and deadly results.  Whether we’re dealing with antichrists today
(and they still exist!) or some other similarly abstract idea, let’s remember
the danger that abstractions can pose too!

Finally, let’s consider the issues of **TRUTH AND FELLOWSHIP** that John raises in 3 John 1-15.  First of all, he wants us to recognize that
walking in the truth isn’t something that you can do alone.  Instead, it is revealed especially in the way
that we interact with one another.  For
instance, John points out that walking in the truth means caring for the
traveling evangelists who come Gaius’s way. 
Likewise for us.  It’s meaningless
for us to proclaim our devotion to the truth if we don’t show the devotion by
the way that we treat one another.

This is evident in the sad story of Diotrephes.  He was apparently using the excuse of
doctrinal purity to seek supremacy in the church by driving others out.  I wish I could say that this was a practice
limited to the first century, but I don’t think it is.  All too often, church splits today are driven
by men who claim to be acting from doctrinal concerns but really are mainly
interested in getting their own way. 
Brethren, the body of the Lord already has a head.  It doesn’t need two, and when we seek headship
in our local congregations, our behavior is as godless as it can be.
