One of the problems with blogging is the illusion of
settledness and certainty that it gives to the reader.  Here I am; here are my views.  This is how I feel; this is how I always will
feel.  Maybe that’s true for some, but
it’s not true for me.  I’m with Emerson
when he said that a foolish consistency is the hobgoblin of little minds.  What’s important to me is not that I retain
the same opinion.  It’s that I attain the
right opinion.

As a result, I often find myself rethinking things I’ve said
before, especially in instances where I see merit in the words of those who
don’t agree with me.  A recent bout of
such rethinking was brought on by, of all things, a Facebook comparison between
Tom Bombadil and Jar Jar Binks.



Jar Jar isn’t terribly important to this (or any other)
argument, but for those who aren’t Tolkienites, a brief explanation of Tom
Bombadil may be in order.  He appears in
the novel version of *The Fellowship of
the Ring* as a basically good character who nonetheless refuses to get
involved in the struggle between the forces of good and the forces of evil.  Because he isn’t a major part of the plot,
when *The Fellowship of the Ring* became
a movie, the filmmakers cut the character.

In the Facebook thread about him, though, someone replied
with an extended quotation from Tolkien about the purpose of the character,
which I found fascinating:

"Tom Bombadil is not an important person – to the
narrative. I suppose he has some importance as a 'comment'. I mean, I do not
really write like that: he is just an invention (who first appeared in the
Oxford Magazine about 1933), and he represents something that I feel important,
though I would not be prepared to analyze the feeling precisely.

“I would not,
however, have left him in, if he did not have some kind of function. I might
put it this way. The story is cast in terms of a good side, and a bad side,
beauty against ruthless ugliness, tyranny against kingship, moderated freedom
with consent against compulsion that has long lost any object save mere power,
and so on; but both sides in some degree, conservative or destructive, want a
measure of control. but if you have, as it were taken 'a vow of poverty',
renounced control, and take your delight in things for themselves without
reference to yourself, watching, observing, and to some extent knowing, then
the question of the rights and wrongs of power and control might become utterly
meaningless to you, and the means of power quite valueless.

“It is a natural pacifist view, which always arises in the
mind when there is a war. But the view of Rivendell seems to be that it is an
excellent thing to have represented, but that there are in fact things with
which it cannot cope; and upon which its existence nonetheless depends.
Ultimately only the victory of the West will allow Bombadil to continue, or
even to survive. Nothing would be left for him in the world of Sauron."

(Letter 144 to Naomi Mitchison)

Even though Tolkien has the question of pacifism in mind, I
think there are even larger applications here. 
In *The Lord of the Rings*, the
One Ring stands for the corrupting nature of power.  Even the most powerful good characters in the
series don’t dare take up the Ring because they know that it will corrupt
them.  They could use it to defeat the
evil of Sauron, but only at the cost of becoming evil themselves.

The one exception to the rule is Tom Bombadil.  Because he’s uninterested in power, the Ring
has no power over him.  However, if he
retains possession of the Ring, Sauron will win the war and eventually defeat
even him.

I have to acknowledge that in some ways, this is a powerful
critique of my moral/political/religious position.  The Lord said, “My kingdom is not of this
world,” and I tend to read that as a call to avoid entanglement in the world.  When it comes to issues of right and wrong,
I’m a diehard idealist, and when I’m faced with a choice between political
candidates who are at best flawed reflections of those ideals, I am drawn to "none of the above".  It seems clear to
me that politics often leads even godly men and women into behavior that is
ungodly (the ballot box as the One Ring?).

And yet.  I hardly
exist in a vacuum.  I live a life of
prosperity and peace that is not independent of the American system of
government, but is rather dependent on it. 
I am not political, but the older I get, the more patriotic I
become.

This spring, my family and I went to the National Archives
in Washington D.C., where, among other things, we saw the originals of the
Declaration of Independence and the Constitution.  The room where they are displayed struck me
as the Holy of Holies of the U.S.  We are
a nation founded on an idea, and as ideas go, the idea that men have been
endowed by God with unalienable rights is a beautiful one.

Nor have I inherited these things without cost.  I’m currently working my way through David
McCullough’s biography of John Adams, and even though Adams never shouldered a
musket, he sacrificed a great deal for the sake of liberty.  Flawed? 
Yes.  Basically a man of good will?  Also yes.

When I am confronted with these things, I struggle with my
refusal to engage.  It’s easy to be a
smug idealist when others have lived and died to afford me that luxury.  I’m free-riding off of their contributions,
and without them, my stand on my principles might well be literally
short-lived.

Where does that leave me? 
I don’t know.  Maybe Tom Bombadil
should have thrown off his neutrality to fight with the forces of the West against
Mordor, even at the cost of becoming corruptible.  But on the other hand, what if the foes of
Sauron look less like Gandalf and Aragorn and more like Saruman (“Make Isengard
great again!”)?  Do I want to give an
account for supporting the lesser of two evils?

That’s a tough
one.  It really is.  Perhaps in future our political/electoral
choices will become easier, though that’s not the way things have been
going.  Some things about being a
Christian are difficult but simple. 
“Take up your cross and follow Me,” is hardly a complex concept!  Sometimes, though, it’s hard even to see the
right, let alone do it.

The only thing certain in all of this is that the way
forward is uncertain.  We all need to
acknowledge the difficulty of the choices we face (and sin has a way of making
choices ever more difficult), and when we see others choosing differently, we
still must treat them with sympathy and understanding.  We ought to have our eyes fixed on the life
to come, but that doesn’t absolve us of responsibility in this life either.
