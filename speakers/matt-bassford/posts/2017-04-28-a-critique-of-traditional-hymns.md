One of the great temptations of the Internet is that it
gives us the opportunity to jump to conclusions about people we’ve never met on
the basis of a few lines of written comment. 
None of us enjoy being on the receiving end of this, but before we get
too righteoused up, we should remember Ecclesiastes 7:21-22.  It’s tough to get too mad about people making
assumptions about me when I know I’ve done the same thing to others!

As a result, I don’t take it too seriously when somebody
who’s never seen my face announces that because I criticize “I Come Broken”,
I’ve forgotten the second half of the Great Commandment, am a harsh, bitter
person, etc.  There’s probably nothing I
can do to correct such misapprehensions, but thankfully, it doesn’t matter a
whole lot!



It’s somewhat easier to address those who think they can
figure out my whole hymn philosophy from having read (skimmed?) one post.  Generally, because I like some traditional
hymns, people want to assign me to the traditionalist side of the worship
wars.  They assume that if it wasn’t
written before the birth of Queen Elizabeth (II, maybe even I!), I’m going to
reject it out of hand.

In real life, there is no party line I toe.  My approach to hymns is pragmatic and inductive.  I don’t care what the publication date on a
hymn is; I’m concerned whether it works in the congregation (according to both
human and divine standards).  I’m
particularly interested in hymns that have worked for a long time, even after
any appeal from cultural timeliness has passed. 
I study hymns, ask what makes them work, and from them, abstract
principles that I apply primarily to my own writing and secondarily to the
writing of others.

This approach is not original to me.  I learned it from Craig Roberts, whose
background as a scientist may be responsible for the scientific flavor of the
exercise.  However, over the past 20
years, I’ve done plenty of thinking on my own too.

The pragmatic take doesn’t spare anybody’s sacred
cows.  This week, I hacked off the
praise-and-worship crowd by criticizing “I Come Broken” as a step down from
“Just As I Am”.  A couple weeks back, I
made the traditionalists mad by arguing that 99 percent of Charles Wesley’s
hymn output is mediocre-to-bad and shouldn’t be used in worship (99 percent may
be a kind estimate, actually).  I’m an
equal-opportunity outrage, but a lot of folks only seem to notice and react
when their ox gets gored.

Because I’ve developed my position from study, rather than picking
a side and then marshalling arguments to support it, I think that both sides in
the worship wars have a point.  Neither
has all the points they think they have, but there is some truth to each.  It’s tough to critically consider hundreds of
contemporary hymns and not acknowledge the problems there, but it’s equally
tough to critically consider hundreds of hymns by Wesley, Fanny J. Crosby, etc.
and not acknowledge the problems there too (I suspect that most people with
strong opinions on the subject haven’t critically considered either body).

The truth is that a good hymn is hard to find, no matter
what time period you’re considering (though when it comes to older hymns, most
of the mediocre-to-bad has already been filtered out), and even the best hymns
aren’t perfect.  Because traditionalists
need their blood pressure elevated this week too, here are what I think are the
strongest critiques against traditional hymns.

**Use of “Thee” and
“Thou”.** Most people associate the
use of “Thee” and “Thou” in hymns (nearly always referring to God) with the
King James Version, but they typically don’t know why it appeared in the
KJV.  Unlike modern English, Greek is a
language with a second-person plural (like the Southern “y’all”).  The KJV communicates this by using “thee” and
“thou” for second-person singular (accusative/dative and nominative cases,
respectively) and “ye” and “you” for second-person plural.

I’m not even sure that everyday English in 1611 used a
second-person singular/plural distinction (Shakespeare doesn’t), but it had
certainly dropped out by the Victorian era. 
Nonetheless, Victorian writers still used the KJV, and in any
translation of the Bible, the most common second-person singular addressee is
God.  As a result, “Thee” and “Thou”
shifted in use from an indication of second-person singular to special pronouns
used only for God (a process that reached its full flowering in the 1977
NASB).  I’m sure that hymnists gladly
adopted this convention at least in part because “Thee” is so easy to rhyme
with (and is a pretty sound to end a line on too!).

I think this is silly. 
Hebrew and *koine* Greek don’t have
special pronouns for God, and yet the original writers of the Bible were hardly
irreverent!  It also introduces the
complication of archaic verb forms (“Thou heardest” and so on), which make
older hymns even harder to understand. 
There’s no good reason for modern hymnists to use “Thee” and “Thou”, and
we sing older hymns despite their use of “Thee” and “Thou”, not because of it.  Note, by the way, that in *Psalms, Hymns, and Spiritual Songs*,
older hymns that the hymnal reintroduced were quietly recast from using “Thee”
and “Thou” to using “You”.

**Tangled Syntax.** The culprit here is rhyme.  As others have observed, English is a
rhyme-poor language (probably because it draws words from so many different
languages).  It’s much harder for an
English-language hymnist to rhyme than, for instance, a German-language hymnist.

As a result, authors of traditional hymns often fell prey to
the temptation to twist up their sentences so that lines would end on
rhymes.  For instance, Joseph Addison
took, “The heavens declare the glory of God, and the sky above proclaims His
handiwork” (Psalm 19:1) and turned it into:

The spacious firmament on high,

With all the blue, ethereal sky,

And spangled heav’ns, a shining frame,

Their great Original proclaim.

My five-year-old son can grasp Psalm 19:1, but I think that
even most adults would have to sit down and think about the first quatrain of
“The Spacious Firmament” to understand it. 
It’s pretty writing, certainly, but it lacks clarity because of its
inverted sentence structure.  The verb in
the sentence is the last word in the quatrain, and no English speaker would
ever adopt that word order unless they were driven to it by something else—like
the pressure to rhyme.

Honestly, I think this a sign of laziness and slipshod
editing.  It’s possible to rhyme in
English without tortured syntax (and the best traditional hymns are written
with clear, direct sentence structure), but writers of bygone centuries too
often took the easy way out.  This is a
serious problem because it interferes with the ability of the congregation to
sing with understanding.  I am suspicious
of any hymn that is harder to understand than it needs to be.  Pointless complexity is. . . pointless.

**Unappealing
Music.** I’m a lyricist, not a
composer, but I’ve spent even more time poring over old hymn tunes than I have
over old hymns.  I’ve been through, for
instance, The Cyber Hymnal’s entire collection of common-meter tunes (of which
there are hundreds).  My goals were not
particularly abstract; instead, I was hoping to find a good match for a CM hymn
I’d written.

In case any other lyricists out there get the same bright
idea, this almost never works.  There is
not some great stock of magnificent Victorian, etc., hymn tunes out there
waiting to be pressed into service.  What
there is, is a big pile of mediocre, unmemorable music.  Despite 15 years of effort, I have succeeded
in getting a hymn paired with an older hymn tune off the ground exactly once (the
tune was Johann Silcher’s SO NIMM DENN MEINE HÄNDE, and Silcher was a second-rank
professional composer).  I suspect that
when it comes to more familiar tunes, we sing many of them only because they
are familiar.  If they were introduced
cold, they wouldn’t succeed either.

I think there are a couple of reasons for this.  The first is that tastes have changed.  My composer friend Glenda Schales tells me
that modern melodies center more around SOL than older ones do.  Nobody is going to confuse the sound of
Disney with the sound of Mozart.  It’s a
different musical idiom, and people naturally prefer the idiom of their own
time (and before anybody starts hating on Disney, the lyrical and musical
quality of Disney soundtracks is quite high. 
By contrast, the skill level of the Disney wannabes who write praise-and-worship
songs is very. . . varied.).

Second, I think the goals for worship music are different
than they used to be.  150 years ago,
HURSLEY, the tune commonly used for “Sun of My Soul”, attracted criticism for
being vulgar, unsuited to sacred words, and resembling a drinking song.  Too rowdy! 
Today, comments like these make us wonder whether the commenters are talking
about the same HURSLEY, which strikes us as being about as vulgar as a china
pattern.

The commenter was Herbert Oakeley, a Victorian hymn-tune composer,
and I think it reveals something telling about the mindset of Victorian composers
generally.  They churned out thousands of
undistinguished tunes because they were suspicious generally of striking,
emotionally evocative church music.  What
we want, they thought was a problem.  Today,
what they wanted often strikes us as a problem.

**Conclusion.** The point here, of course, is not that
the corpus of traditional hymns is worthless and we shouldn’t use any of
them.  I haven’t changed my stripes (not
that I had any to change in the first place!). 
Instead, it’s that no corpus of sacred song is perfect.  Anybody who embraces all the old hymns or all
the new praise-and-worship songs isn’t paying attention to the real problems
that are actually there.  When we respond
to criticism with a dismissive, “Well, I like it!”, we reveal that we are no different
from those with whom we do not agree at all.

Worshiping God is an important business, deserving of all
the thoughtfulness we can muster. 
Relying on unschooled subjectivity won’t help us offer a better
sacrifice, but honest, unsparing examination of the songs we sing will.  “I like it!” makes for a wonderful battle cry
for the self-absorbed, godless multitudes around us.  It is, after all, what they are about.  However, if we claim that pleasing God is our
first priority, surely we are called to higher ground than that!
