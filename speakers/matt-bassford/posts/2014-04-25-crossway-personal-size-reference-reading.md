Even though
the PSR only proved to be an adequate Bible for preaching, teaching, and study,
its attributes led me to a very different take on its suitability as a reading
Bible.

For one
thing, because the PSR is a smaller Bible, it’s in my sweet spot for
reading-Bible form.  Its relatively light
weight enables me to easily prop it against my stomach, hold it in the air, or
do whatever else pleases me with it while I’m reading.  In size, shape, and heft, it differs little
from a normal paperback novel.  In fact,
my perfect Bible for reading would probably be about this size.

However, I
can’t speak as warmly of its format.  The
PSR is single-column, which certainly leads to a more pleasing layout for the
poetry in the Old Testament.  However,
that format leads to some other problems. 
A lot of other commentators have complained about the number of
characters per line, which, on average, is about 84—far above the 40-66
characters per line range that is considered optimal.  To be honest, I’ve never found the large
number of characters and words per line to be a problem.

Instead, my
issues have to do with brute width.  Even
though it isn’t far over, measuring in at 3 ⅝”, the PSR’s column width is on
the wrong side of the 3 ⅜” paperback line. 
This isn’t a huge problem.  On
average, it means that if I scan straight down the page, I’m missing only the
last word.  A lot of the time, I can
figure out that last word from context without even looking at it and move on
to the next line.  Sometimes, though, I
have to stop and determine whether that last pesky word is “apostles” or
“goddess”, which look surprisingly similar when they’re at the very edge of your
visual field.

This doesn’t
make the PSR a disaster for me as a reader. 
In fact, I’m doing my daily readings out of it right now as I wait for a
double-column NASB to return from being rebound.  Even before this, I did my whole-book
readings out of it for five years without significant problems.  However, the column width does keep it from
being optimal.

On the credit
side of things, the PSR is in paragraphed form. 
As much as I like verse-by-verse Bibles for auditorium and office use,
they would never be my first choice as a reader.  The paragraphing of the PSR makes it
generally easy for me to follow the flow of the narrative and get lost in the
text.

The exception
to this easy flow comes not from the paragraphing but from the bolded section
headings.  These headings are pretty
sparse in the Pentateuch, where they allow the text to flow freely, but in the
Sermon on the Mount, for instance, the translators elected to include a new
section heading with every paragraph. 
This may make it easier to locate Jesus’ teaching on lust (although why
are you trying to track down a particular passage in a reading Bible?), but it
does so at the cost of introducing nearly as much distraction into the text as
verse-by-verse format would.  To be fair,
I don’t think this is a PSR-specific issue. 
Instead, I believe the vast majority of ESV’s suffer from
section-heading-itis.

The PSR does
have cross-references, which are not really a feature I like in a reader, but
it hides them gracefully along the inner margin so that I don’t really notice
them.

In terms of type,
the 7.4-point typeface in the PSR isn’t a problem for me, although it may be
for many people.  The newer PRB has
increased this to 8-point type. 
The paper in my PSR is pretty good, so there isn’t much show-through of
text from the other side.  I’ve heard,
though, that the PSR’s printed in China instead of Belgium have much worse
ghosting.  The PSR isn’t line-matched
(although the PRB is), but because the paper is decent, the lack of
line-matching is never a huge deal.

Overall, the
PSR in many ways exhibits the characteristics of my ideal reading Bible.  It falls short of perfection only in its
barely-too-large column width and overabundance of section headings.  I rate it at 4/5 as a reader.
