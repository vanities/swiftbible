Last week’s Supreme Court decision legalizing gay marriage
in all 50 states was certainly a major clash in the culture wars.  However, some of the combatants took the
opportunity to look ahead to further battles. 
For instance, in his dissent to the *Obergefell*decision, Chief Justice John Roberts wrote,

*Hard questions arise when people of faith exercise religion in ways that may be
seen to conflict with the new right to same-sex marriage—when, for example, a
religious college provides married student housing only to opposite-sex married
couples, or a religious adoption agency declines to place children with
same-sex married couples. Indeed, the Solicitor General candidly acknowledged
that the tax exemptions of some religious institutions would be in question if
they opposed same-sex marriage. See Tr. of Oral Arg. on Question 1, at 36–38.
There is little doubt that these and similar questions will soon be before this
Court. Unfortunately, people of faith can take no comfort in the treatment they
receive from the majority today.*

Many advocates of traditional marriage have gone so far as
to warn that even federal tax-exempt status for churches will be under threat
as the legal scope of gay rights continues to expand.  Up until recently, this has been met with a
great deal of pooh-poohing from the liberal side of the aisle.  “Interfere with the religious practice of
others?!  Heavens, no!  We’d NEVER do that!”

***Bob Jones University* and Its Consequences**

However, in the aftermath of *Obergefell*, Mark Oppenheimer, writing for *TIME*, proposed exactly the elimination of tax-exempt status that
everyone else has been fervently denying is even thinkable.  You can find the article [here](http://time.com/3939143/nows-the-time-to-end-tax-exemptions-for-religious-institutions/).  He doesn’t want to blow up all tax exemptions
for charitable institutions, of course—just the ones belonging to institutions
that he believes deviate from “settled public policy”.

His rationale for this is based on a 1983 Supreme Court
case, *Bob Jones University v. United
States*.  In that case, the court
decided that the IRS could deny tax-exempt status to Bob Jones University
because the university forbade interracial dating among its students.  Because the government had a compelling
public-policy interest in eliminating racial discrimination, organizations that
promoted racial discrimination, such as the university, were not entitled to
tax-exempt charitable status.

This case seems particularly relevant because of the
frequent efforts by gay-rights supporters to link their cause to the crusades
of the African-American civil-rights movement. 
If *Obergefell* is an expansion
of civil rights to an oppressed class in the same way that *Brown v. Board of Education* was, it stands to reason that
faith-based organizations that oppose gay marriage are opposing a compelling
interest of public policy.  A court could
take the *Bob Jones University* opinion,
replace “interracial dating” with “gay marriage” and deny those faith-based
organizations their federal tax-exempt status. 
I didn’t see anything in the opinion that would except even a church
from its logic.

**Losing Federal
Tax-Exempt Status**

That might well prove disastrous to a host of
religiously-based charities.  I don’t
know; my religious tradition doesn’t have any religious institutions beyond
churches themselves, so I am utterly unfamiliar with the charitable world.

However, I'm not at all sure that losing federal tax-exempt
status would hurt churches, especially smaller churches, that much.  Presumably, a church that loses tax-exempt
status would become subject to the same tax law that applies to secular
businesses.  Most notably, churches would
be allowed to deduct business expenses from income.  Money spent on ministerial and staff
salaries, building maintenance, classroom supplies, etc., would not be taxable.

The congregation for which I preach is fairly typical,
statistically speaking.  This past Sunday
morning, we had 96 in attendance, a little above the national norm of 80ish.  I don't know what anybody else's financial
situation looks like, but I know what ours does.  As a rule, we are a non-profit in fact as
well as in legal parlance.  Everything
that we take in, we spend.  If we find
ourselves running a surplus, we send a check to a preacher in need of financial
support.  There simply isn't much, if any
(net) income to tax.

Of course, in order to prove its expenses, a church would
have to document them.  However, churches
really should be doing that anyway, whether the federal government requires them
to do so or not.  A church that doesn’t
keep track of its finances is inviting embezzlement and all sorts of other
corruption.  For many congregations, a
federal mandate that they record their expenses might be the best thing that
ever happened to them.

A second issue here is that, if a church were to lose
tax-exempt status, the contributions it received would no longer be deductible
by the contributor.  No more write-offs
for the check you put in the plate on Sunday! 
For many other charitable organizations, this would be a huge loss.  After all, many orchestras, zoos, etc., are
supported by wealthy individuals and corporations who are donating in part for
the tax deduction.

However, my sense is that people who donate to their
churches are a little bit different. 
Generally, rather than being fat cats, they’re just regular folks, for
whom tax deductions are not nearly so precious. 
Many of them probably take the standard deduction rather than itemizing
deductions on their income taxes anyway, at which point the change has zero effect.  Even church members who do itemize will
probably just start taking the standard deduction, which will hurt a little
bit, but not very much, and likely not enough to deter them from their
religious duty.

The biggest issue at the federal level would probably be
with the ministerial housing allowance, if somehow the government could figure
out a way to deny said allowance to preachers who preached against the practice
of homosexuality (“Check Box C if you agree with Romans 1:26-27.”).  That would be a blow to my personal finances,
no doubt.  However, if the government
reaches the point where it starts taxing individuals because they express their
religious convictions, the First Amendment is D-E-A-D dead, and we probably
have many more serious problems than the housing allowance.

**Losing State
Tax-Exempt Status**

State taxation offers more serious potential problems.  On the one hand, the analysis of federal
income tax liability above applies to state income taxes too (though the
details probably vary from state to state). 
You still can't get blood out of a turnip.

On the other hand, the loss of state-level exemptions from
sales and (especially) property taxes would be a much bigger deal.  Our church doesn't really buy all that many
things that are subject to sales tax (mainly office supplies), but our church
building is a fairly substantial structure that would probably be subject to
$10,000 or more in property taxes every year. 
Of course, churches that didn't own their meeting place wouldn't have
this problem.

I don’t think this is something that the federal government
could impose from the top down.  I can’t
imagine how the feds could force a state *not*to offer a given church a tax exemption. 
Instead, it would be up to each state. 
It’s hard to imagine the Tennessees and Alabamas of the US denying
churches tax-exempt status in our lifetimes, but I suppose that there’s a
possibility that blue states might try.

I suspect, though, that even the bluest of the blue would
hesitate to take such a step.  In many
ways, states are in economic competition with each other, and states that
denied tax-exempt status to churches would be at a disadvantage compared to
those that did not.  Living in Illinois
as I do, I already hear plenty of, “Come to Indiana!  Our taxes are lower here!”  I doubt that the Illinois state government
wants to add to its troubles by allowing our neighbors to the east to add,
“Come to Indiana!  We won’t persecute you
for your faith here!”

In conclusion, I think it’s quite possible that churches
that oppose gay marriage will lose federal tax-exempt status, but I don’t think
the loss would be very damaging.  By
contrast, a loss of state tax-exempt status would be damaging, but I don’t
think it’s very likely.  However, it’s
been a long time since law school for me, and I haven’t thought about legal
matters very much since.  If you think
there’s something I’m missing, point it out so I can modify this accordingly.
