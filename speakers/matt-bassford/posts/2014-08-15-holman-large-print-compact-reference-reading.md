As is my wont, I spent two weeks using the LPCR for my daily
Bible reading each morning.  Well, except
for one Sunday morning that was so hectic I didn’t have time for it and had to
double up the next morning instead. 
Excluding that, the Bible got the usual tour of duty.  Here is what I thought of it:

**SIZE.** What can I say?  I love reading out of small Bibles, and the
LPCR is unquestionably small.  It has the
same length and width as other compact Bibles on the market.  I’ve read paperbacks that were substantially
larger than the LPCR.  It is definitely a
stout little volume, but even that feels more pleasant in the hand than my checkbook-width
ESV New Testament.  It’s hard to imagine
a Bible that feels more like a book you would casually pick up and read, even
if the cowhide cover and spine hubs lend a more formal air.



**LAYOUT.** The LPCR is a two-column Bible.  Because it tucks references in at the end of
paragraphs or at the bottom of the page, it has essentially the same attributes
as a two-column text Bible.  The columns
have the entire width of the page in which to run free.  As a result, two-column text Bibles normally
have character-per-line counts slightly in excess of 40, which is a nearly
ideal match for my reading preferences.

The LPCR doesn’t fare quite that well as a result of its
large-for-a-compact 8-point print.  This
is a perfect example of the compromises inherent in Bible design.  It’s great that 2K could kick the print size
up like that, but as a result, they couldn’t fit as many characters on each
line.  As far as I can tell, the LPCR
averages about 37 cpl.

Of course, this isn’t going to be a problem in prose.  Poetry is another matter.  The Bibles I review must take their victim
and his reading plan as they find him. 
At the moment, I’m working my way through Job, which is 90 percent
poetry.  A line length of 37 cpl isn’t
terrible, but it isn’t wonderful, either, and it has consequences for the
layout.  I would estimate that about half
of the poetic lines in the LPCR’s setting of Job spill over onto the next
line.  To 2K’s credit, I don’t see any
places where they allowed a widow consisting of a single disconnected syllable,
but there are plenty of single words.

Interestingly enough, I found that this bothered me less in
reading than it did in study.  The
difference probably has to do with intensity of attention.  When I’m studying in preparation for a sermon
or a workbook, I’m determined to wring every last nuance out of the text.  Phrases and even single words are important
to me.

Not so when I’m reading. 
By design, I read with a relaxed state of mind.  I’m not trying to analyze every scrap of
information.  If something jumps out at
me, great.  If not, that’s fine, so long
as I come away from the text with the gist of what the author is trying to
say.  From my perspective, “Job thinks
his friends are jerks” is a fine takeaway from Job 16.

As a result, I didn’t feel like I was losing too much
comprehension as a result of the line breaks. 
There were a couple of times, especially when I hadn’t slept well the
night before, when Job had my eyes crossing, but generally, the layout was
unobtrusive.  To me, Bible layout is like
an offensive line in football.  If it’s
doing its job, you don’t notice it.

**READABILITY.** There are a few good things to talk about
here and the usual (for this Bible) bad thing. 
On the credit side of the ledger, 8-point print in a Bible that has true
compact size (as opposed to a hand-sized Bible that the publisher calls a
compact) is phenomenal.  Likewise, the
LPCR is about 99 percent line-matched. 
That’s nice to see in a mass-market Bible.  Hopefully, Foundation, Zondervan, etc., will
get the memo about this eventually. 
Combine that with paper that looks slightly better than the thinline
norm, and there’s a lot to like about the readability of the LPCR.

Buuuut.  Once again,
the LPCR gives us an illustration of why glue-bound Bibles are sub-optimal
Bibles.  When I’m reading, I’m either on
my own or in the presence of toddlers, so I’m not terribly concerned about
maintaining a dignified appearance.  As a
result, I found myself turning the Bible this way and that to get at the text
trapped in the gutter.  One of my
favorite tricks was to open the LPCR at about a 90-degree angle, press the
bulges in the two sides down with my thumbs, and sight along one page to read
the gutter text on the other page.  I
noticed myself doing this, and noticing is bad.

**CONCLUSION.** There’s actually quite a bit to like
about the LPCR as a reader.  The size is
great, the layout works even in the poetry sections, and a lot of the details
are right.  Once again, though the fatal
flaw of the Bible is the glued binding. 
I judge that it deserves a 3.5/5 as a reader.
