[Apparently so.](http://www.hymnsoflight.com/)  In addition to writing in a host of other genres, the author of the Hugo-Award-winning science-fiction novel *Ender's Game* has obviously devoted a fair amount of time and thought to hymnody.  There are certainly caveats here.  Scott Card is famously a Mormon, so his work unsurprisingly features a lot of Mormon doctrine (the odd-to-our-ears celebration of the Lord's Supper with bread and water, for instance).  I'm not posting this because of my sympathies for the teachings of Joseph Smith; I'm posting it because I'm a hymn geek. :)


There are two aspects to this site that are particularly interesting to me.  The first is the collection of Scott Card's hymns.  It's interesting to me that even a writer who is a master of his usual genre hasn't mastered the art of writing hymns.  All of these are thoughtful; some of them are good; some are. . . strange, and they're not just strange because they're written by a Mormon.  Scott Card himself notes the importance of universal appeal in hymns, but I wonder if such a gifted writer who is so good at speaking for himself therefore struggles to speak for everybody.  In addition, though his technique is good, it's not spectacular.

Also, Scott Card needs a hymn editor in a big way.  He needs somebody who will tell him, "That line is bad.  Don't say that."  No matter how talented a hymnist may be, none of us can see our own work objectively enough to keep from lying to ourselves.  I've been doing this for 20 years, and I still lie to myself all the time:  "That rhyme's OK.  Really; it is!"

Second, and even more interesting, is the collection of a dozen essays on hymnody.  The approach here is fascinating.  Scott Card is a smart, smart dude and a world-class writer, and it shows in the way he analyzes hymns.  With a few exceptions, he's not that interested in looking to the Mormon repertoire and saying, "This works, so I should write like that."  Instead, he learns by writing and then studying what he has written:  "I wrote this, and it didn't work, so I need to tweak it."  His Muse speaks a lot more loudly than mine does, alas.

Additionally, even though he notes correctly that writing hymns is not like writing poetry, his hymns are as much poetic as hymnic.  This shows up in three places:  clever rhyme schemes that won't line up with musical phrasing and resonate with the congregation, run-on lines that don't match musical phrasing, and lines with different rhythms in the same hymn.  I think he will probably figure this stuff out as he continues to write, but it does put him in the reinventing-the-wheel stage.

Regardless, both the hymns and the essays make for fascinating reading.  Enjoy!
