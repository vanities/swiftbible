*About a month ago, I sent an email to Foundation Publications suggesting that they publish their Large-Print Pew Bible with a sewn binding.  In her reply to me, Foundation's Carole Holdinski noted that over the next few years, Foundation is planning to develop new settings for its entire line of Bibles.*

*For me, this was exciting news.  Of all the translations out there, the NASB is my sentimental favorite ("Translation of our fathers!  Holy translation!"), and it is also among the best for close study.  However, even the most dedicated NASB/Foundation partisan must acknowledge that current NASB settings, most of which were developed around the time of the NASB update in 1995, have since become dated.  Thoughtful re-settings will do much to increase the appeal of the translation, and indeed of Foundation's lineup.*

*In theory, I could have taken this information and sat on it.  In reality, I promptly replied with a lengthy presentation of what I thought Foundation should do with the re-setting.  I thought that these ideas would be of interest to the Bible-dork community, so I decided to re-post them here.  However, I am equally interested in your thoughts on all of this.  What do you think Foundation should do?  What am I right about?  What am I missing?  Let me know in the comments section!*

*My original email is as follows:*

Dear Carole:

I was thinking about Foundation's re-setting projects this morning, and I had a few thoughts I wanted to pass along about what I would like to see:

ALL BIBLES:

- The typeface should be ITC Weidemann.  Foundation has already used this typeface in places, and it’s one of my favorite Bible typefaces.  It scales much better than Crossway’s Lexicon No. 1, which looks fine at smaller sizes but gets too heavy in larger-print Bibles.

- All Bibles should be line-matched.  Other mass-market publishers have figured out how to do this, so it is no longer the exclusive domain of premium Bibles.  If you can do this, there’s no reason not to.

- Prominent book titles.  In many of the current Foundation settings, the titles aren’t much larger than the text, and they don’t stand out.  This is a missed opportunity to make a simple yet elegant statement at the beginning of each book.  I think Foundation’s Large Print Pew Bible does a decent job with this, and those titles would look even better if set in ITC Weidemann rather than Times New Roman, which is not a particularly gracious typeface.  However, even the Large Print Pew Bible could be more generous with white space around book titles.

- Use text figures in cross-references.  This is another simple touch that adds class to a setting.

- Paragraph out dialogue like in a novel, with each speaker in turn getting his own paragraph.

- Minimize in-text headings as much as possible.  Prominent text headings channel my attention when I am reading, even when I would rather make up my own mind about the logical divisions of the text.  It’s better to have in-text headings that are small and relatively inconspicuous, as in the current Foundation SCR.  It might be better still to avoid in-text headings entirely in favor of running headers.

- Even in text Bibles, it is helpful to include cross-references for Old-Testament prophecies when they are cited in the New Testament, as well as citations for parallel passages in the Synoptic gospels.  Put those at the bottom of each page along with the translators’ notes.  They don’t take up much room.  The Crossway Thinline does an excellent job with this, and Zondervan has followed suit in many of its text NIV settings.

- Left-align all text.  It’s not as pretty to my eye, but studies have shown that left-aligning text increases reading comprehension.

- In paragraphed Bibles, superscript verse numbers.  Ideally, the tops of the superscript numbers will extend above the tops of the capital letters.  This makes the verse numbers easier to locate.

- Continue italicizing words supplied by the translators.  This is one of my favorite features of the current Foundation settings, and the NASB is certainly the translation for people who don’t want to take the translators’ word for it!

- Keep as many Bibles as possible black-letter.  I know that all sorts of people love their red-letter Bibles, but red-letter is hard to execute properly.  Usually, it comes out looking like The Words of Christ in Pink.  Also, in line-matched Bibles, red text with black text on the opposite side of the page is hard to read.

- Sewn bindings for everything.  Foundation generally does a great job with this, and it should continue to do so.


I think that Foundation’s lineup should include at least the following Bibles:

- Thinline (though I don’t think you can call it that because of the Zondervan Thinline NASB).  This one should be a double-column, paragraphed text Bible with 8-point print.  You could market a hardcover trim of this as a pew Bible.

- Large-Print Compact.  The existing Foundation Large Print Compact is already in ITC Weidemann, so there’s no reason to re-set this.  It might be best to change the name, though.  “Large-Print Compact” leads people to expect genuinely large print, rather than large-for-a-compact print.  There are lots of unhappy reviews on Amazon, christianbook.com, and similar sites fussing about this very issue, both for the Foundation LPC and other Bibles with similar marketing problems.

- Text.  This setting should be similar to Foundation’s existing Large Print Pew Bible:  double-column, verse-by-verse, with about 9-point print.  This should appear in both hardback and softcover (poly or genuine leather), the latter for the sake of ministers who are looking for a smaller verse-by-verse Bible for pulpit use.

- Center-Column Reference.  This is your standard hand-sized, double-column, paragraphed reference Bible.  I don’t particularly like center-column reference Bibles, but plenty of people do.  Resist the temptation to try to make this into a thinline.  Use a reference column more like the Cambridge Pitt Minion’s.  I find the reference column in the current Foundation Ultrathin Reference to be confusing.  Try to keep the characters-per-line count in this Bible as high as possible; otherwise, the OT poetry will be a mess.

- Side-Column Reference.  No reason to make this much different from the current Foundation SCR.  Keep the text at about 12-point and the text column relatively narrow.  One of the great strengths of the SCR is that it only contains about 57 characters per line.  The SCR wannabes on the market try to save space by using smaller text, but this leads them to have lines of text that are more than 70 characters long.  I find that Bibles like that are much more difficult to read than the SCR.

- Giant-Print Reference.  I recommend the current Foundation GPR nearly as often as I recommend the SCR.  14-point print is a godsend for Christians with failing eyesight!  The new setting probably should be paragraphed (though be careful to make sure that the verse numbers are still legible even to those with poor vision), and it conceivably should also be single-column.  The double columns of the existing GPR are a little too narrow to be ideal, and a single-column GPR would probably have about 60 characters per line, which isn't bad.
