I am a giant fantasy geek. 
Ever since I was five or so, I’ve been fascinated with elves and dragons
and magic swords.  As books go, the
influence that *The Lord of the Rings* has
had on my life is second only to that of the Bible.  In addition to binge-reading fantasy novels,
I am also perfectly willing to devour historical fiction, provided that the
main characters come equipped with the requisite number of swords, axes, spears,
and shields.  All of this is to say
nothing of my fascination with *Dungeons
& Dragons*, as well as with every computer-based fantasy role-playing
game to emerge in the past 20 years.

To repeat, I am a giant fantasy geek.  If the genre has any conventions (and let’s
be honest; a lot of fantasy is color-by-number stuff), I’ve seen them a hundred
times.  Part of my brain knows that they
are ridiculous, but the majority of my brain, the part that picks out books at
the library, thinks they’re awesome.



One such fantasy trope is the dual-wielder, the hero who
wades into battle gripping not one death-dealing sword, but two.  Generally, both swords have names, just to
make sure that all those fantasy-geek hearts go pitty-pat.  Examples abound:  Twinkle and Icingdeath in R. A. Salvatore’s
Drizzt novels; Serpent-Breath and Wasp-Sting, employed by Bernard Cornwell’s
Uhtred of Bebbanburg; The Rose’s Thorn and The Edge in *Dragon Age:  Origins*.

At this point, about 10 percent of you are nodding in
recognition.  The other 90 percent of you
are wondering what on (Middle-) earth I’m talking about.  If that’s you, trust me.  Dual-wielding is awesome.

Those with secular jobs may find it possible not to take
their work home with them, but as a preacher, I’ve found that I can’t help but
bring myself into my work.  My
personality and interests determine the illustrations I use in sermons, and
sometimes they even influence my interaction with the text itself.

This is certainly the case when I read 2 Corinthians
6:1-10.  When I look at this text, the
spiritual part of me rejoices in Paul’s ringing description of
discipleship.  The writerly part of me
rejoices in Paul’s masterful use of parallelism to advance his argument.  However, the fantasy-geek part of me rejoices
in 2 Corinthians 6:7, particularly in the phrase, “by the weapons of
righteousness for the right hand and the left.” 
It says, “Paul was a dual-wielder! 
Awesome!”

Now, in real life, I should not be trusted with one sword,
let alone two.  My lack of coordination is
such that I’d probably do Peter one better and cut my own ear off.  However, I think I could manage to brandish a
couple of weapons of righteousness.

All of this takes us, via a roundabout and silly path, back
to the realm of Bible design, specifically to the impossibility of Bible
design.  Trying to design a single Bible
that contains everything that everybody wants is like trying to pour two
gallons of water into a one-gallon bucket. 
The laws of nature won’t allow it.

Most Bible designers address the problem through a series of
compromises.  You can’t have large print
and opaque paper in a small Bible, but you can have medium print and medium
paper opacity in a medium Bible.  Bibles
with different sizes and characteristics represent different emphases and
compromises.

However, about ten years ago, the designers at Cambridge
tried a different tack.  Rather than
developing a single compromise Bible, they developed two Bibles, designed to be
used together, that in tandem didn’t have to compromise as much.  These two forms, of course, are the Cambridge
Pitt Minion and the Cambridge Wide-Margin Reference, both of which are
available in a number of different translations.  The Pitt Minion is a classic small
center-column reference Bible.  The
Wide-Margin takes the Pitt Minion’s setting, enlarges it slightly, prints it on
much better paper, and surrounds it with an inch or more of white space
designed for note-taking.

The Internet is full of reviews of Pitt Minions.  It’s easily the most popular small premium
Bible currently in print.  Proponents of
the Wide-Margin aren’t quite as numerous, but it has still attracted the
attention of a respectable tribe of reviewers. 
Bible geek that I am (you may have noticed something about my
personality by this point), I’ve read plenty of each kind of review, but I’ve
never seen anyone attempt to review the two Bibles together, in the
complementary roles intended by their designers.

Thanks to the generosity of Cambridge, I’m now in possession
of a black calf-split Pitt Minion NASB and a black calf-split Wide-Margin
Reference NASB—a matched set.  I intend
to review them as a set, putting both Bibles through my usual two weeks of
intensive review, comparing them to one another, and explaining which Bible I
found most suitable for each role. 
Instead of using one weapon of righteousness, I’ll employ two.

I’ll be dual-wielding Bibles.  It’s going to be awesome.
