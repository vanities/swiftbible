As per my usual practice, I took the Thinline to the church
building for two weeks and used it both during assemblies and in my private
study.  Here’s how I thought it handled:

**PREACHING.** The size of the Thinline is almost, but
not quite, ideal.  I love the proportions
of the text block in isolation.  It’s a
little shorter than Crossway’s Thinline ESV, for instance, which makes it an
extremely handy volume.  It makes me feel
like I’m holding a rapier of the Spirit rather than a two-handed
broadsword!  However, the text block is
just a tiny bit smaller than a sheet of letter paper folded in half.  When I am trotting up to the pulpit, sermon
outline tucked into my Bible, a tiny fringe of white protrudes all the way
around.  Perhaps that shouldn’t bother
me, but it does.



Normally, I prefer my pulpit Bibles to be in verse-by-verse
format.  The Thinline is
paragraphed.  However, I found that it
gave me less trouble than other paragraphed Bibles of my acquaintance.  I think the difference lies in the
details.

Even though the Thinline has superscript verse numbers, just
like nearly every Crossway Bible out there, the type that Zondervan used for
those verse numbers is more readable.  A
side-by-side comparison of the Zondervan and Crossway Thinlines reveals that
the verse numbers in the Zondervan are perhaps a point larger than in the
Crossway.  Also, the Crossway’s verse
numbers are boldfaced, whereas the Zondervan’s are not.

Both of these factors are important.  Crossway’s verse numbers look like quotation
marks to me, and I have to focus momentarily to distinguish them from the
actual quotation marks.  Worse still, they
may blend in with the actual quotation marks, and I may overlook them entirely.  I don’t have either of these problems with
the Zondervan Thinline.  Because the
verse numbers are noticeably taller and thinner in line than the quotation
marks, I don’t get the two confused.

I didn’t expect the Zondervan to give me much trouble in
preaching, then, but just to make sure, I put it through its paces in a
completely textual, expository sermon.  I
didn’t have as easy a time locating verses as I do with the Foundation SCR, but
it was considerably easier than with any other paragraphed Bible I’ve
used.

Similarly, the Thinline doesn’t line up with my preaching
preferences in that it is double-column rather than single-column.  I thought that added some confusion, but not
much.  This may have had something to do
with the fact that the Thinline is a text-only setting.  In my search for numbers, I didn’t have to
consciously disregard the big column of letters and numbers down the middle.

**TEACHING.**  I didn’t have the opportunity to teach any
Bible classes during the Thinline’s two-week rotation, but I certainly used it *in* several Bible classes.  Even sitting in a pew, the Thinline’s
handiness was apparent.  Not only is it a
smaller Bible, but it’s also light for its size.  This may be a tribute to the flyaway paper
that Zondervan used, which I’m guessing is under 20 gsm, but the text block as
a whole is a featherweight.  If I
remember rightly, at 1 pound 2 ounces, even my rebind comes in at only an ounce
more than a Pitt Minion.  That’s the kind
of Bible that you can wave around like a pointer for 45 minutes and not even
notice!

However, though I was mostly listening rather than talking,
I felt myself hampered by having a text-only Bible without
cross-references.  The subject of the
class in which I used the Thinline was the book of Jeremiah, which is closely
linked with the reign of the last few kings of the nation of Judah.  I often found myself wanting to refer back to
what 2 Kings or 2 Chronicles had to say about a particular ruler, but without
marginal references, I had to thrash around in those books myself until I
located what I was looking for.

In fairness, though, this was probably a larger issue for me
as a student than it would have been as a teacher.  When I’m the guy up front, I don’t have the
leisure to spend five minutes running down whatever spiritual bunny trail
strikes my fancy.  Either I’ve studied up
beforehand on those last four kings of Judah and worked them into my
presentation already, or they’re just going to have to wait for another
time.  When I’m teaching, the cross-references
are only significant when somebody blindsides me, and in these two weeks, I
don’t think any of my thrashing was motivated by somebody else’s question.

**STUDY.** Size isn’t as significant when I’m
sitting in my office all week long, but even there, I appreciated the slimness
of the Thinline.  The smaller a Bible is,
the less conspicuous it is as a physical object, and I could one-hand the
Thinline for hours with no problem.

Despite this small size, though, I found the Thinline to be
fairly readable.  Yes, the paper is thin
enough to make Kleenex look like cardstock. 
No, the setting isn’t line-matched. 
However, the loveliness of the setting is enough to overcome these
problems.  Many of Zondervan’s settings
are overly trendy, which means that they quickly become dated.  If you don’t believe me, try going back and
looking at the book titles in the NIV84 Thinlines that Zondervan set about ten
years ago.  Classic, they weren’t.

Even though the copyright on the Thinline NASB is 2002, the
years have been much kinder to its setting. 
According to my photographer, who is a devotee of all things
typographical (he really ought to be writing a Bible-review blog instead of
me), the typeface that it employs is ITC Weidemann.  This typeface was designed for a German Bible
society in the 1980’s.  With a large
x-height, thin lines, and subtle but effective serifs, it is indeed well-suited
for use in a Bible.  Even at 7.8 points,
I found it easy to use for hours at a time.

Paragraphed Bibles aren’t my first choice for study either,
but here too, the way that Zondervan set the verse numbers diminished the
problems I had with it.  Though I used it
for two weeks while writing sermons and various other materials of a spiritual
nature, it never caused me to miss a citation. 
For me, that’s the primary litmus test for a Bible’s usefulness in
study.  I introduce enough errors into my
materials by myself; I don’t need help from my Bible in introducing more!

To my surprise, the feature of the Thinline that gave me the
most trouble in study was its lack of cross-references.  I wouldn’t have thought that a text-only
Bible would be sub-optimal in a setting where I have the Internet at my
fingertips, but so it proved to be.

The thing is, I’m lazy. 
When I’m kicked back in my desk chair, reading John 19, and I come to
the prophecy that predicts that not a bone of the body of Jesus would be
broken, I don’t want to sit up, fire up a Web browser, and type in the verse
citation to figure out where the original prophecy was.  That’s too much trouble!  Instead, I want to have the cross-reference
supply me with the answer without making me move.

This sounds ridiculous, but it’s true.  Indeed, as the week progressed, here’s what I
found myself doing.  When I started using
the Thinline, I didn’t bother removing my normal office Bible, a Foundation
SCR, from my office, and to me, the SCR became a stumbling block.  Rather than going to All The Bother of
sitting up to locate a cross-reference, I reached over, grabbed the SCR, and
looked up the cross-reference in it.  As
always, when I find myself using a different Bible than the Bible I’m reviewing
during the review period, that’s probably a sign that the review Bible is not
optimal for that particular purpose.

Overall, I loved the size and the layout of the
Thinline.  However, its lack of
cross-references did give me trouble in teaching and study.  As my church-building Bible, I rate it at
4/5.
