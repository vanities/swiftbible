The Bible is a timeless book.  It is relevant in every era and in every
culture.  However, at any given time,
some parts of it may seem more relevant to our situation than others.  For instance, I don’t imagine that brethren
50 years ago talked too much about persecution. 
At that time, even though most people in America weren’t New-Testament
Christians, at least they honored the Bible and those who followed it.  Biblical counsel about handling persecution
didn’t matter too much.

Today, however, this has changed pretty significantly.  A growing number of people in our country are
openly hostile to those who “look for the ancient paths”.  Despite their claim to tolerance, they’re
only tolerant of those who agree with them. 
Given enough power and opportunity, they will happily persecute
Christians.

All of a sudden, then, the many passages about bearing up
under persecution and suffering have become much more relevant to us.  We need to learn from our first-century
brethren how to bear up under insult, slander, and bad treatment.  With this in mind, let’s consider the first
three chapters of 1 Peter this morning, to learn how we can follow godliness no
matter what.

 **What Makes Us Different**

The first chapter of 1 Peter is concerned with what makes us
different, what distinguishes disciples of Christ from the worldly people round
about us.  The first difference that he
identifies is our **LIVING HOPE.** Let’s read here from 1 Peter 1:1-9.  Basically Peter wants us to understand that
we ought to joyful aliens.  Most of us
probably hear “alien” and think “little green men”, but instead, we should be
thinking of this in the sense of “people who don’t belong here.”  As Christians, wherever we are on earth, we
don’t belong and can’t belong.

Peter explains that we can’t belong because we have
something that nobody else on the planet has. 
We have an imperishable inheritance waiting for us in heaven.  No matter what happens to us on earth, if we
are faithful, we can be certain that God will bring us to that
inheritance.  Because of our heavenly
reward, we’re able to view earthly events with some detachment.

This applies even to the suffering and trials that we
undergo for the sake of Christ.  People
without a hope wouldn’t even bother to go on through persecution, but we have
it, so we do.  Peter compares this
process to the purification of gold in fire. 
The hotter the fire, the purer the gold.

So too for Christians. 
Every Christian in heaven will glorify God, but the Christians who
endured through persecution will particularly glorify Him.  By their continued obedience in the face of
suffering, they show their faith in the faithfulness of God.  Even if we are suffering now, we can still
rejoice because we know that one day, the joys of heaven will be all the
sweeter.

Next, Peter discusses our **ASSURANCE OF SALVATION.** Consider
1 Peter 1:10-16.  This context divides
into two main parts.  The first discusses
our reason for confidence; the second, what we ought to do about it.  In the first section, Peter particularly
calls our attention to the prophets who predicted the coming of the Lord.  None of them had a full understanding of the
gospel, but by means of the Spirit, they searched diligently to determine
everything about Christ that they could. 
Sometimes, their findings contained a startling level of detail.  It’s amazing to realize that 1000 years
before Christ was born, His ancestor David predicted in Psalm 22 that He would
be pierced through His hands and feet and that His enemies would cast lots for
His clothes.

Today, this is important to us for two reasons.  First, it shows us how thankful we should be
for the full revelation we have today. 
Every one of us can open our Bibles to the Gospel of Matthew and learn
more about Jesus than David ever found out in a lifetime of searching.  Second, this record of fulfilled prophecies
stretching back for millennia does much to confirm our faith.

In the second main section, on the basis of the certainty of
our salvation, Peter urges us to greater and greater holiness.  This makes perfect sense.  If I thought somebody maybe possibly left me
a million-dollar check at the bank, I might go check to see if they did.  However, if I were sure that they had, I’d be
at that bank as fast as I could drive over! 
So too for us.  Because the
evidence for the truth of the gospel is so good, we need to excel in our
service to God.

In the third main part of this context, Peter works out the
logical implications of our salvation when it comes to **PURIFICATION AND LOVE.** Let’s
read on in 1 Peter 1:17-2:3.  He begins
here with a startling statement:  that we
ought to live our lives in fear.  He
bases this, though, on the truism that the more we pay for something, the more
we value it and the more we hate to see it destroyed.  If Marky takes a ballpoint pen to the
cheapest Bible I own, I’ll be a little bit upset about it, but I won’t be too
angry.  However, if he does the same
thing to the most expensive Bible in my collection, I’ll skin him and use his
hide for the rebind!

What, then, is true of us? 
God didn’t buy us out of sin with any amount of money.  Instead, it cost Him the blood of His Son.  When we have been so perfectly purified, who
of us would dare to take our souls for which so much was paid and corrupt them
again with sin?  Brethren, that’s about
like spitting in the face of almighty God, and we should be afraid of that!

Because we have been purified with such a great price, we
need to devote ourselves to the work before us, and Peter wants us to
understand that we do this best by attending to the word.  After all, it is the gospel that is the
instrument of our spiritual rebirth, and just like any creature is born for a
purpose, so we are reborn for a purpose. 
Christians are reborn to love, and the more we feed on the word, the
more we put off wickedness and the more we adopt the traits of God.

**A Chosen People**

In the second main section of his discussion, Peter focuses
on Christians’ identity as a chosen people. 
He emphasizes our special nature by describing us as **LIVING STONES.** Let’s continue on here with 2 Peter
3:4-10.  The kingdom that Peter depicts
here is unique.  It begins with Christ, a
king who was rejected by His own nation but was confirmed by God.  He is the cornerstone on which the church is
built.  His people aren’t made up of the
Jews who rejected Him, but of those who answer the call from everywhere.  Even though we’re different, He builds us all
up together into a single spiritual house that devotes itself and its worship
to God.

In fact, it’s appropriate for us to think of ourselves as
monuments.  Probably all of us have been
to Chicago and seen the statues of General Grant and whoever else, set up 100
years ago so that even today, we can look at the statues and be reminded of the
greatness of the men they represent. 
Jesus, by contrast, isn’t interested in monuments made out of something
as meaningless as metal or stone. 
Instead, the enduring reminder of His greatness and glory that He has
left behind is the people who follow in His footsteps.  We are the image of Christ to everybody else.

Because this is so, it’s our job to proclaim the
excellencies of the One who saved us.  We
don’t do this by physical imitation of Jesus—growing a beard or wearing
sandals.  That’s not how we reveal Him to
those around us.  Instead, we do it by
spiritual imitation, by reflecting the nature and the character of our
Savior.  If others can’t see Him in us, we
aren’t glorifying Him.

Next, Peter discusses
how we can **FULFILL OUR PURPOSE.** Let’s look at 1 Peter 2:11-25.  This text looks at two primary ways we
display the image of Christ to those around us. 
The first is through morally blameless behavior.  There were people then, and there are people
now, who are convinced that Christians are a bunch of hypocrites.  Our responsibility is to live such righteous
lives that even these people, people who are looking to find fault with us
because of our faith, can’t find anything to pick on.  Our gentle and upright behavior should lead
even Christ’s enemies to Him.

Second, Peter emphasizes the importance of godly
submission.  This takes first of all the
form of submission to government.  Back
then, this question had particular significance because the Romans assumed that
anybody in their empire who went around talking about a king was plotting
rebellion.  Peter wanted his readers to
understand, though, that the correct response even to an outside tyranny like
the Romans was still to pay taxes and otherwise honor their overlords.  The best way to prove that Christians were
trustworthy was to be trustworthy.  Our
position today is not quite the same, though I know quite a few Christians who
think the American government is increasingly tyrannical, but our responsibility
is.  Good Christians are good citizens.

Similarly, good Christians are good servants.  This was true for Christians 2000 years ago
who were slaves, and it’s true for Christians today who are employees.  We’re to honor those who are over us, not
only when they are kind and reasonable, but especially when they aren’t.  When our boss is a jerk and punishes us for
no reason, but we continue serving him faithfully, it pleases God.

All this is important because of the example of Jesus.  We’re supposed to be His monument,
remember?  We’re supposed to bear His
image.  When we bear up patiently under
those who are oppressing us, that’s exactly what we’re doing.  Jesus did no wrong, yet He suffered great
wrong, because He had a higher purpose in mind. 
He suffered and died to heal the wounds of our sin.

When we suffer unjustly yet continue to do good, we reveal
that we have a higher purpose in mind too, to glorify the One who died for
us.  Before the Lord’s Supper, let’s
evaluate ourselves in this.  Can the
people around us see Jesus in us?  Let’s
reflect on this as we partake.

**Godly Suffering**

Peter concludes our context today with a further discussion
of godly suffering.  This begins with his
advice for **HUSBANDS AND WIVES.** Let’s read from 1 Peter 3:1-7.  One of the most important lessons about
marriage that the Bible teaches us is that every time a New Testament author
talks about the responsibilities of husbands and wives, it appears in a context
of submission.  That’s true in Ephesians,
it’s true in Colossians, and it’s true here. 
According to God’s word, marriage isn’t fundamentally about love or
romance or companionship or any of those other things.  Marriage is fundamentally about
submission.  For men and women both, it’s
about taking our good and our desires and putting those things below the good
and desires of our spouses.

Of course, because men and women are different, this plays
out in different ways for each.  Women
married to unbelievers are to lead their husbands to the Lord not by nagging,
but by respectful, godly behavior.  It’s
not the responsibility of Christian wives to be concerned for themselves or
afraid for themselves.  It’s their role
to fearlessly do what they know is right.

Similarly, husbands are to put their wives before themselves
by treating them with understanding. 
Hopefully, every married man here has realized by now that women don’t
think the way that we do.  When it comes
to relationships particularly, I’m playing checkers, but Lauren is playing
three-dimensional chess.  Things that
seem simple to me are anything but to her. 
Even if I don’t get it, I’m still supposed to honor that
difference.  This selfless love is
exactly counter to the selfish wisdom of the world, but it is the only possible
foundation for a good marriage.

More generally, Peter calls all of us to live like those who
are **INHERITING A BLESSING.** Consider 1 Peter 3:8-17.  The point here is pretty plain.  There is a certain way that Jesus expects His
disciples to live, no matter what.  Even
when others insult us and visit evil upon us, we don’t do the same to
them.  Instead, we are as kind,
sympathetic, and humble as if they had treated us well.  We who have a heavenly inheritance don’t need
to worry about earthly slights.

However, we should not mistake kindness for lack of
conviction.  When others attack us for
our faith, we don’t roll over.  Instead,
we patiently continue in what we know is right, and if we suffer for it, we
suffer for it.  In fact, under such circumstances,
we have an even greater obligation to be godly than normal.  We well know that those who are inclined to
persecute us are going to look for any excuse they can to justify their bad
behavior.  It will help them sleep at
night if they can prove to their satisfaction that we deserve persecution.  Whatever we do, we can’t hand them that
excuse.  Instead, we glorify God by our
conduct, so that they are ashamed and not us.

Similarly, when others call us to account for what we
believe, we must be ready to answer. 
These questions could be anything from honest curiosity to an attempt to
embarrass, and we need to be prepared for either one.  As in so many things, the key here is
Biblical knowledge.  If we know the word
inside and out, we need never fear that our answers will bring discredit to our
Lord.

In this also, we are called to share in **THE WORK OF CHRIST.** Peter
describes this work in 1 Peter 3:18-22. 
Here, he reminds us once again that suffering for what is right is part
of our legacy as disciples of Jesus. 
Jesus suffered for what was right, we follow in His footsteps, so we
should expect the same.  Peter then goes
on to describe the work of Jesus in some terms that we don’t understand and
some terms that we do.  The idea of Jesus
making proclamation to the spirits now in prison is at the least a debatable
one.  My view on it is that this is
figurative.  By His perfectly obedient
life, Jesus sealed the condemnation of everyone who was disobedient because He
proved that obedience was possible.  Like
I said, not the easiest passage to unpack!

What is certain, though, is that the death of Jesus has
brought us near to God.  Likewise, just
as Noah was brought safely through the waters of the flood, Jesus brings us to
salvation through the waters of baptism. 
Baptism is effective for the forgiveness of sins because in it we are
united with the death, burial, and resurrection of Christ.

Jesus’ resurrection and subsequent exaltation make up
Peter’s final implied point.  Jesus was
willing to humble Himself to the point of death, but God took care of Him and
saw that He was richly repaid for His suffering for the sake of
righteousness.  If we are steadfast like
Jesus was, even to the point of suffering ourselves, God will see to it that we
are richly repaid too.
