*Obviously, the subject
is too complex to make blanket statements like, “Everyone who writes hymns is
Exactly Like This.”  However, I’ve found
these things to be true for me, and I’ve seen them be true in varying degrees
for other hymnists of my acquaintance. 
Take it for what it’s worth.*



**It means feeling compelled to write hymns.**

In my experience, the mass of Christians divides up into three groups.  First, there are those who those who have no
desire to write a hymn.  Instead, they
prefer to serve the Lord in other ways, which is acceptable and indeed
praiseworthy. 

Second, there are those who would like to write a hymn but don’t really feel
compelled to do so.  These are the
brethren who might write a verse and put it down, never to return to it again,
or who might work on a single hymn verrry slowly over the space of 10 or 15 years. 

Third, there are those who feel driven to write hymns and can’t stop.  This is by far the smallest category, and to
be honest, I’m not really sure what its identifying features are.  Some are obvious.  Christians like this tend to be pretty intense
about their faith.  They are generally
creative, either musically, verbally, or both. 


However, I know plenty of intense, creative Christians who still aren’t
hymnists.  I think the difference is that
the hymnist who is always writing uses writing as a mental and emotional
release.  Life starts getting to you, and
so you sit down and channel those negative energies into a hymn.

As coping strategies go, writing hymns is better than drugs or alcohol, but it
has its downside too.  When I’m stressed
out and pressed for time, the very last thing I need to do is add more to my
plate by beginning work on another hymn, but that’s invariably what I end up
doing.  Rarely does that improve the
situation that gave rise to the hymn in the first place!

**It means being bad at what you do.**

As every graduate of Craig Roberts’s Hymninar knows, Tennyson once said, “A
good hymn is the most difficult thing in the world to write.”  Whatever one might think of Tennyson’s own
skill as a hymnist, he was certainly right about that! 

The difficulty begins with the restrictive nature of the hymn as a form.  A hymn has to be a spiritual thought
accessible to the congregation, written and paired with music in such a way
that the result can easily be used by untrained singers.  There are all kinds of things that one might
write, both lyrically and musically, that are too complicated for the
congregation.  A good hymn, by contrast,
is filled with lyrical and musical patterns that free the worshiper from focusing
on technique and allow him to concentrate on the content.

Hymnists, then, have to learn a certain method of writing and composing, which
is akin to learning a new language.  This
skill, however, is only the first and lowest bar.  A good hymn is not only something that the
congregation can sing.  It has to be
something that the congregation wants to sing.

This is fiendishly difficult.  Like any
other art form, a hymn is self-expressive. 
You can’t write from anything other than your own soul and expect it to
be any good.  Nobody is a skilled enough
writer to lie in a hymn about who they are. 
However, unlike any other art form, a hymn can’t merely speak for the
self.  It’s not enough for other
Christians to appreciate a hymn, like a painting or a song on the radio.  Instead, they have to identify with it and
want to adopt its message as their own. 

Some hymnists are better at anticipating what people want to sing than
others.  Glenda Schales has a gift for
this.  I, by contrast, am pretty
miserable at it.  I never have any idea
whether a hymn will work until I actually hear people singing it. 

However, even the best technicians in the history of English hymnody, even the
writers with the surest finger on the pulse of the worshiper, don’t succeed in
writing a repertoire-level hymn more than a tiny percentage of the time.  A couple years back, I went through the
hymnal *Songs, Hymns, and Spiritual Songs*
and compared the number of hymns by a particular author that it contained to
the author’s total output.  Excluding
modern authors (most of whose work will not become standard even though it is
currently published), the most successful hymnist of all time was Frances
Havergal. 

Her success rate was something like 4 percent.

From their own experiences, most hymnists know that the vast majority of what
they write will not work.  I, at least,
believe fervently at the time of writing that This One Is Going To Make It, but
after a few weeks, the optimistic glow generally wears off.  If I spend a year writing and emerge from
that with one or two that look like they might have a chance at success, I’m
pleased with the result.

**It means you have an ambivalent relationship with your work
that is used.**

A few years back, I attended a gospel meeting, and it so happened that during
the singing, the songleader selected and led one of my hymns.  After the meeting was over, I was talking to
a brother who asked me, “What are you thinking when one of your hymns is sung?”

My reply, which I’ve regretted ever since, was, “I’m hoping
that no one is thinking about me.”

Hymnists are contrary folk. 
We spend our lives hoping that the hymns we write will enter
circulation, but when they do, we aren’t happy with that either.  Or, more accurately, we’re happy when
Christians far away are singing our hymns, but not when that’s happening where
we are.

Except sometimes we are happy about it.  It’s complicated, and we’re confused.

To me, the experience feels a lot like the equally
unpleasant sensation of listening to your own recorded voice.  It’s you, but it’s coming from someplace
else.  Both experiences also make me
hyper-aware of all the quirks in my normal manner of speaking and writing.  After one of my hymns is sung, I commonly
find my fingers itching for a red pen, even (especially?) when the hymn is
published and I can’t change anything in it.

I suspect that older, more spiritually mature writers handle
the experience much more gracefully than I, but between being aware that This
Is My Hymn and yearning to engage in some slash-and-burn editing, it’s easy for
me to forget that I’m supposed to worship with the hymns I write too!

**It means that you are humbled by the experience of having
your hymns used in worship.**

The extreme difficulty of writing hymns does have one
spiritually useful consequence.  It makes
you aware of how little your success depends on you and how much it depends on
God.  I don’t know what the template for
a repertoire-level hymn is.  Indeed, I
don’t believe that such a template exists.

When I don’t even know what the standard is, much less have
an idea of how to attain to it, I can only attribute any success I have to the
mercy of God rather than my own efforts. 
For reasons of His own, He chooses to bless my work sometimes, and I can
only be thankful.

It reminds me, in fact, of a couple of texts from the Old
Testament.  The first is David’s reply to
God’s great Messianic promise in 2 Samuel 7. 
The 18th verse of the chapter reads, “Then David the king
went in and sat before the Lord, and he said, ‘Who am I, O Lord God, and what
is my house, that you have brought me this far?’”

I’m no king, but I’ve heard the people of God use my work to
worship Him.  Who am I, indeed?

The second comes from Ezra’s prayer of confession in Ezra
9:8, which reads, “But now for a brief moment grace has been shown from the
Lord our God, to leave us an escaped remnant and to give us a peg in His holy
place, that our God may enlighten our eyes and grant us a little reviving in
our bondage.”

What is the experience of writing a hymn, if not a brief
moment of grace?  What is a hymn that is
sung, if not a peg in God’s holy place, driven there by the sincerity of
worshipful hearts and by His own kind intention?



A peg isn’t much. 
It’s just a dowel in a hole.  But
for me, when I long for enlightenment, when I yearn for a little reviving in my
exile here, it is enough.
