Several months ago, I sent an e-mail to Tyndale House’s
marketing department, soliciting an NLT for review.  They replied that they were no longer sending
out review copies of that particular Bible, and they commended me to their
blog-for-books program, which offered plenty of inspirational romance novels
for review, but no Bibles.  With all due
respect to those who write and enjoy such things, I would rather gargle battery
acid than read an inspirational romance novel.

Nor was I particularly inclined to buy a Tyndale NLT.  I’ve spent enough time nosing around
religious bookstores to know that when a Tyndale Bible proclaims its “Durable
Binding”, what it often (but not always) means is “Glued Binding”.  No thanks.

But I still wanted an NLT! 
What, then, was a poor Bible blogger to do?  As it happened, my longing for an NLT joined
forces with another one of my Bible longings—my longing for a Pitt Minion.  I had given away my only Pitt a year before,
and I couldn’t justify buying another one in a translation I already own.

However (and here was the genius of the thing), I did not
own an NLT!  I burned an Amazon gift card
I had gotten for my birthday, thus solving my NLT problem and my Pitt Minion
problem in one swell foop.

**VITAL STATISTICS**

Translation:  New
Living Translation

Cover:  French Morocco

Binding:  Sewn

Trim Size:  4 ¾” x 6
¾”

Text Block Width:  ¾ ”

Weight:  15 ounces

Column:  Double

Layout:  Paragraphed

Print Size:  6.75

Words of Christ:  Red

Cross-References:  Yes

Ribbon Marker:  1

**SIZE.** Among Bible geeks, the Pitt Minion is
almost certainly the best-known small Bible in the world.  Of course it did well here!  The glove-box test gave the impression that I
could have put two Pitt Minions in the glove box instead of one and still have
been able to close the compartment door just fine.

Indeed, I was able even to store the Pitt in an article of
clothing.  I see pictures online of guys
with Pitt Minions in their jeans pockets. 
Sorry; can’t do that.  However, I
did manage to fit it in the front pocket of my winter coat, which, taking into
account Illinois’ nine-month winters, is considerably more useful.  I don’t own another full Bible that I could
have carried in that way, so for Luddites like me who don’t own a smartphone, a
Pitt Minion does offer an extra dimension of portability.

**BINDING.** My NLT Pitt is what I think of as a
low-premium fine Bible.  The cover is
French Morocco rather than goatskin.  The
binding is paste-down rather than edge-lined. 
It has normal gilt rather than art gilt. 
Really, though, I’m perfectly fine with all of those things.  I’m much more interested in the inside of a
Bible than the outside.  I care much more
about how a Bible functions than how it looks. 
Plus, I’m not willing to pay the extra $30 for goatskin and art gilt!

I gather from Internet chatter that Cambridge French Morocco
has gained an ill reputation in the past. 
“Too stiff!” cry the critics. 
Admittedly, my Pitt is a pretty stiff Bible.  However, I don’t think it’s any more stiff
than the calf-split ESV Pitt I used to have, or the calfskin TBS Windsor I
still own.  Indeed, all three Bibles are
quite similar, with the same deeply impressed grain that almost inevitably
leads to stiffness.  They’re all
Jongbloed products (Jongbloed being a renowned Bible printer in the
Netherlands), and this is what Jongbloed does with calf-split-grade
Bibles.

However, the stiffness doesn’t bother me.  I only really mind stiff Bibles when they’re
unrepentantly stiff, like the genuine-leather Crossway Legacy I used to
own.  Trying to break that Bible in was
like trying to break in a sheet of Plexiglass! 
That isn’t true of this Pitt.  I
unconsciously fidget with stiff Bibles when I’m holding them, flexing them back
and forth, and under this treatment, the Pitt NLT has loosened up considerably
already.  I imagine that if I were to
adopt it as my one-and-only, it would soon become about as floppy as a small
can get.

I do have one final note about the exterior of the Bible,
which has nothing to do with Cambridge and everything to do with Amazon.  As I said earlier, I bought the Pitt with an
Amazon gift card I got for my birthday. 
Amazon is certainly not my first choice for online Bible purchases.  For one thing, their prices are nearly always
higher than those of specialty outlets like Evangelical Bible or Christian Book
Distributors.  However, an Amazon gift
card was what I had, so to Amazon I went.

When I received the Bible and took it out of its clamshell
box, one of the first things I noticed about it was that the interior of the
box looked dirty.  I thought this was strange,
but shrugged it off in favor of examining my new prize.  I decided I liked it and promptly razored a
side off the box to convert it into a slipcase.

After I had glued and clamped the slipcase-to-be, I returned
to the Pitt.  To my horror, I figured out
where the “dirt” in the box had come from. 
At some point in transit, the Bible (which was packaged only in a box
and a bubble envelope) had gotten crushed hard enough that the polish on either
side of the spine had transferred to the box, leaving a crushed, scuffed gouge
running the length of the cover on both sides.

Because I had already destroyed the box, I felt like I
couldn’t return the Bible, so I got out my black shoe polish and used it to
touch up the cover.  After a couple of
coats of polish, the scuffing is pretty well gone, but the gouged-out trench
remains.  I don’t think anybody else
would notice it, but I do.

I suppose the scrape is good for me spiritually, in that it
reminds me that even beautiful things in this world will inevitably be marred
and broken.  However, I am annoyed that
my premium product did not receive premium treatment, at least from Amazon, and
other Bible geeks I know online have experienced similar problems with them.

The moral of the story is pretty plain:  when you buy a Bible online, go to a
specialty Bible site.  I’ve bought Bibles
from both evbible.com and christianbook.com, and I’ve never had reason to
complain of the products I received from either one.  If you’re stuck with an Amazon gift card instead.
. .  May the odds be ever in your favor,
I guess.

**TEXT BLOCK.** The Pitt Minion is a Cambridge
product.  Therefore, I confidently
expected it to be two things:  sewn, and
cockling-free.  It was both, as any fine
Bible ought to be.  The paper is so thin
that it has a slight tendency to curl, especially in the presence of moisture,
but this is a minor problem.

**VALUE.** My NLT Pitt is a well-made Bible.  However, you certainly pay for the
privilege.  Even the French-Morocco
version retails for a cool $100.  A real
online Bible retailer will sell it for $65-$75. 
I’m happy to pay that for a Pitt, but compared to a Windsor, for
instance, the value doesn’t exactly bowl me over.

**CONCLUSION.** The NLT gets stellar marks for size,
binding, and text block.  I can only
criticize it for being a bit pricier than I would prefer.  For Physical Form, the Bible merits a 9/10.

Amazon, however, gets a 1/10 for crunching up my Bible in
shipment (or, rather, for packing it so that it would be crunchable).  Thanks a million, guys.  See if I buy another Bible from you anytime
soon.
