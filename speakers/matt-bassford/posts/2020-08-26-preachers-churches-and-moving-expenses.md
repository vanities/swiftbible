When I moved to middle Tennessee to work with the Jackson
Heights church in October 2017, the congregation very graciously paid my moving
expenses for me.  At least in some parts
of the brotherhood, this is a common practice, and one that relocating
preachers invariably appreciate!  Better
still, under previous tax law, the amount of expenses paid by the church was
excluded from the preacher’s income.

Today, that is no longer the case.  When the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 was
passed, it made many changes to the tax law. 
Among them, it suspended the exclusion from income of moving-expense
reimbursements from 2018 to 2025.  Those
who want to read more about this (because they are suffering from insomnia,
perhaps), can turn to IRS Publication 521  (which is available [here](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p521#en_US_2018_publink1000203444))
for further details.

Even though this change was not aimed at ministers, it still
affects us.  It’s a good news-bad news
situation.  The good news is that
churches still can pay our moving expenses for us.  The bad news is that the expense amount now
is included in income.

Let’s say, for instance, that Fred is moving from Colorado
to work with the Lonely Road church someplace in west Texas.  The elders at Lonely Road thoughtfully offer
to pay Fred’s moving expenses.  He
already has arranged with a moving company to transport his possessions for the
cost of $10,000.  Thus, when he reaches
Lonely Road, the elders hand him a check for $10,000.

So far, so good. 
However, next April, when Fred is doing his taxes, he must include that
$10,000 as ordinary income.  He has to
pay income tax on it, and, worse still, he has to pay FICA and self-employment
taxes on it.  If he does otherwise, he is
not rendering unto Caesar what is Caesar’s.

Note also that if the Lonely Road elders make the moving
arrangements rather than Fred, this still does not circumvent the law.  Even if Fred’s bank account never sees that
check, he must pay taxes on it anyway. 

This is an obnoxious change. 
At least in other areas (housing allowance, business expenses), the
complexity imposed on preachers by the government often allows for tax
savings.  Here, there is no upside.  It’s simply a substantial and unavoidable tax
bill.  Nonetheless, because we strive to
honor God and man by following the law, it’s a bill that we must pay.
