I acquired a whole bunch of Crossway Value Thinlines via the
process described [here](http://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2017/01/a-changeup-in-crossways-lineup.html).  Even though I ended up giving almost all of
them away, I held on to one of them to review. 
The setting, which is native to the Crossway Ultrathin, is one I’ve
never reviewed before, so I figured “Why not?” 
My impressions are as follows:



**VITAL STATISTICS.**

**Translation:**  English Standard Version (2011)

**Cover:**  Polyurethane

**Binding:**  Sewn

**Trim Size:**  5⅜”
x  8 ⅜”

**Text Block Width:**  ⅝”

**Weight:**  15 ounces

**Column:**  Double

**Layout:**  Paragraphed

**Print Size:**  7.5 Lexicon No. 1

**Words of Christ:**  Black

**Cross-References:**  Minimal

**Ribbon Marker:**  None

**SIZE.** This is probably the thinnest Bible I’ve
ever reviewed, and its other dimensions aren’t exactly huge either.  It’s not a true compact Bible, but it’s
smaller than nearly anything else would be. 
As a result, it’s very easy to fit into a glove box.  This is a good thing, too.  After I had stashed it in our van’s glove box
to take to my wife’s grandmother’s memorial service, I received word that my
father had passed on, so in the course of doing all kinds of other things, I
forgot about the Bible and left it in the glove box for two weeks.  It emerged with a couple of dents but was
otherwise unscathed.  You can’t put this
Bible in a pocket, and it might or might not fit in a purse, but it will go
nearly anywhere else.

**COVER.** This is another value-priced poly-cover
offering from Crossway.  This is
completely subjective, and I don’t have any previous specimens around for
comparison, but my impression is that the covers aren’t quite as sturdy-feeling
as Crossway poly covers from five years ago. 
The poly itself may not be as thick, and I’m fairly certain that the
bookboard inside the cover is thinner.

As a result, the Bible is a little more abusable than its
predecessors.  During the review period,
I accidentally dropped it on the floor from desk height (I reached behind me to
set it on my desk but missed), and the resultant collision flared one of the
corners up permanently.  This doesn’t
impair the usefulness of the Bible, but it is a constant and annoying reminder
of my own clumsiness!

On the plus side, there aren’t any seams in the cover, so
none of the seams are going to fail. 
Instead, I think a Value Thinline cover will fail in the way that poly
covers usually fail.  The poly will start
to crack and flake off at the edges of the spine, and eventually the cover will
fall off.  This will take a lot of use,
though!  Of course, you can kill a poly
Bible cover a lot more quickly by leaving it out in the sun so that it will
bubble and melt and generally look like somebody threw acid on it.  Protip: 
don’t leave your poly-cover Bible out in the sun.

The endpapers are what you would expect from a value-level
Bible:  thick paper rather than
leatherette.  This should be OK.  Typically, the larger the Bible, the sturdier
the endpaper has to be, but the Value Thinline isn’t a very large Bible at
all.  That anorexic text block just isn’t
going to exert much force on the endpapers. 
Of course, if you make a habit of bending the cover backward or jamming
five years’ worth of church bulletins into your Bible, you can destroy just
about any endpapers, but that’s your fault, not the Bible’s.

As far as lovability goes, the cover isn’t terrible.  It doesn’t have any kind of stamped grain,
but the blind-stamping on the spine and a blind-stamped line around the
perimeter add some distinctiveness to it. 
All the kids at church who ended up with all the other Value Thinlines
really seem to like them, though, and if the Bible’s looks are a hit with the
tween set, it’s probably succeeding with its target demographic.

**TEXT BLOCK.** Here, we come to my favorite feature of value-level
Crossway Bibles.  Any Crossway product
above the level of paperback giveaway New Testaments and pew Bibles is going to
have a sewn binding.  I care deeply about
this because of my loathing of Bibles with glued bindings.  Those are the ones where 1 John starts
falling out in a chunk about six months after you bought the Bible.

Sewn Bibles, by contrast, will retain all their pages
indefinitely unless you physically remove said pages (“Out, book of
Hebrews!  Out!”) or destroy the binding
by cramming stuff inside the covers (that again).  True to form, the Value Thinline has a sewn
binding, which means that even though you can buy one online for less than $10,
no matter how hard you use it, it isn’t going to start shedding pages.  It’s a Bible that’s meant to be used, and
used hard.

Interestingly, the Value Thinline is another indication of
Crossway’s move away from gilded page edges in some of its Bibles.  The Value Thinline of 10 years ago had edge
gilding; in this one, the page edges are white. 
As a result, it’s going to show use a little more easily than a Bible
with gilding, and it’s a little less expensive for Crossway to make, but it’s
no less effective at communicating eternal truth.

**VALUE.** As of the time of writing, it’s possible
to purchase a Value Thinline on christianbook.com for $9.99.  With some frequency, the site runs sales that
make them available for much less.

Just about all the other publishers out there produce
value-line Bibles with glued bindings. 
They might be a little cheaper, but they won’t survive actual use.  They’re kind of like shovels that break the
tenth time you stick them in the ground. 
I don’t like shovels like that, and I don’t like Bibles like that.  The Value Thinline isn’t like that, which
makes it immeasurably better than Bibles that are.

I’m a preacher.  One
of the great goals of my life is to get people to read the word of God.  Low-cost Bibles with decent quality
accomplish that goal.  I love that
Crossway cares enough that even some of the humblest members of its lineup can
be used and used for years.  I tip my cap
to the Value Thinline and award it a 9.5/10 for Physical Form.
