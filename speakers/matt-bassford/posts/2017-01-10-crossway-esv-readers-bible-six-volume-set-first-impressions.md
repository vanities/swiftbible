I know that the appearance of the six-volume set of the ESV
Reader’s Bible is old news.  It came out
months and months ago, and various worthy Bible bloggers have already written
volumes of their own about it.  However,
I elected to splash my Christmas money on buying a set of my own, and it
arrived on my doorstep less than 24 hours ago. 
I find myself simply *dying* to
write about it, and I’m gonna.  So there!



It’s too bad that I didn’t get a video of the unboxing
process; this was simply the most ferociously packaged Bible that I’ve ever
encountered.  I bought it from wtsbooks.com,
the bookstore attached to Westminster Theological Seminary, for the simple
reason that they’re currently selling it for $10 cheaper than anybody else I
could find.

WTS was really, *really*determined to make sure the set arrived unscathed.  I opened the box they sent me (1) to find
another box shrink-wrapped with heavy-duty plastic to a cardboard pallet (2).  I sliced open the plastic, opened the second
box (3) to find yet another box inside it (4). 
This one was black and covered with ESV logos.  Then, I opened the black box, which revealed
a wrapping of black paper (5).  Inside
the black paper were the six volumes. . . in a slipcase (6).  It was kind of like opening a set of
matryoshka dolls that were filled with the Holy Spirit.

The good:  there was
absolutely no damage to any books in the set. 
The bad:  I’d started opening them
because I wanted to take the gospels to Zoë’s karate practice, and the epic
unboxing process made us late.  Maybe WTS
should start putting a sticker on the outside that reads, WARNING:  BOX TAKES AT LEAST 15 MINUTES TO OPEN.

During karate practice, I read most of the book of
Matthew.  Later that evening and earlier
this morning, I read from about Genesis 24 (as far as I’d gotten in my daily
reading) through to the end of the book.

Honestly, I was blown away. 
I’m no stranger to reading the Bible; over the past three years, I’ve
read the whole thing four times. 
However, reading the six-volume ESV Reader’s Bible was a completely
different experience.

I intend to talk more about this in a later post, but the
whole idea behind the edition is to make the Bible as readable and accessible
as possible.  Chapter and verse divisions
are nowhere to be seen.  Almost all the
headings are gone (and in the heading-happy ESV, this is an extremely
significant and welcome change!).  What’s
more, the text isn’t crammed into a single volume on flimsy paper.  Instead, Crossway set the text for maximum
readability and chose the paper for maximum readability, which is why the
result is six volumes long instead of one (and six volumes is about what you
would expect when the Bible is around 750,000 words long).

These decisions have a literally transformational
impact.  I love the Bible, and I love to
read.  However, reading the Bible has
never felt like reading.  I don’t like
the way that many settings read, and even more than that, chapter and verse
numbers are distracting.  No matter how
determined you are to focus on the text, they’re always going to be nagging at
your subconscious.

I didn’t realize how big a problem this was until I read
from a well-set Bible without them.  I
didn’t really get lost in the story of Matthew (maybe because I’m so familiar with
it and spent the whole time mentally labeling the reading with chapter numbers;
maybe because a gym filled with shouting karate students isn’t the best place
for concentration), but I did in Genesis. 
For the first time in my life, really, I felt the sweep of the
narrative, just as I do with any good book.

It moved me the way that good books do—I didn’t want to put
it down, even when I needed to.  I was
supposed to be downstairs, supervising the children as they got ready for
co-op.  Instead, I was upstairs on the
couch, reading, because I HAD to get to the end of Genesis before I got
up.  I know the storyline of Genesis
perfectly well, but I still felt compelled to read it.  I’ve never felt that way about reading the
Bible before.

What a difference design makes!  I know that many Christians struggle with
reading the Bible regularly.  Self-control
freak that I am, I tend to chalk that up to lack of spiritual discipline.  I find myself wondering, though, if the
problem is that most Bibles are optimized for reference and fight against
attempts to read them.

My recommendation, then, is this:  If you really want to read the Bible, get a
multi-volume edition to read.  The
six-volume ESV Reader’s Bible definitely works; Bibliotheca (which some of my
friends have) may work.  I haven’t really
gotten the chance to test it, though, so I can’t recommend it yet.

This is a little pricey; even from WTS, my set cost me $100 (and, when you’re buying six hardbound volumes, that's how it's going to be).  However, if spending
the money is the difference between reading the Bible or not, Spend The
Money.  There will probably be people in
hell who regret reading the Bible too little, but I can’t imagine that anybody
in heaven will regret reading it too much.
