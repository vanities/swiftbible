Pop quiz.  What do all
of the first lines/couplets below have in common?

*Magnify, oh, magnify,*

*Oh, magnify our God
and Father!*

*When I survey the
wondrous cross*

*On which the Prince of
glory died. . .*

*O Lord, prepare me*

*To be a sanctuary.*

*I am Thine, O Lord;*

*I have heard Thy
voice.*

*Lord, we come before
Thee now;*

*At Thy feet we humbly
bow.*

*As I journey here mid
the toil and tears,*

*There’s a rainbow in
the cloud.*

*This is my Father’s
world,*

*And to my listening
ears. . .*



It’s not style.  Some
of those hymns are very formal; others aren’t. 
It’s not era.  Some of those hymns
were written centuries apart from each other. 
It’s not even overall hymn quality. 
There’s, um, quite a bit of variation on that metric too.

Instead, the common element in every one of those opening
phrases is that they are good.  The
hymnist may proceed to corkscrew their hymn into the ground, but they start off
right.  All of them are paired with good
music, but the initial appeal isn’t really based on the music in any of these
cases.  It’s based on the opening
thought.

Oh.  There’s another
similarity.  All of these hymns, good or
not, are sung.  In fact, I think they are
sung precisely because the opening thought is good.

Last lines are important. 
They are, after all, the closing message of the hymn, the part that will
leave an aftertaste in your mouth after you’re done singing.  In good hymns, last lines are almost always
strong, and most of the skilled writers of my acquaintance will rig things so
that they end up with powerful last lines.

However, I don’t think last lines are what determine whether
a hymn is sung.  I think the decision is
driven by the first line instead.

I first started thinking in this direction about 2004-2005.  Then, I was writing voluminously (more than
at any other time in my life before or since), but very little of what I wrote
worked.  This was frustrating, so I
started reflecting about what I might be doing wrong.  I noticed, among other things, that most hymns
that were sung had good titles.  My
hymns, by contrast, didn’t.  They had
awful, uninspiring, clunky titles that were generally derived from awful,
uninspiring, clunky first lines.

I concluded that my problem was that my hymns didn’t have
punchy, compelling core thoughts that I could summarize in a strong title.  In retrospect, I think I was half right.  Many of my hymns were vague excursions
through boring, unrelatable topics. 
Nobody wanted to sing them.  I
didn’t even want to sing them.  I just
wanted to say, “Ooo!  I wrote another
hymn!”

Side note:  of all the
possible milestones, writing another mediocre hymn is among the most pointless.

Content and focus were problems for me, yes, but in
retrospect, I think I was overthinking things too.  If my hymns were distinguishable from good
hymns by their clunky titles from clunky first lines, could it be that the
problem was. . . the clunky first line?

Naturally, the thought didn’t cross my mind.  Instead, I tried changing other things about
my writing, but even though my focus and technique improved, it still seemed to
me that I was writing too many duds.  In
order to avoid the disgrace of bringing yet another collection of duds to the
yearly hymnwriting seminar I attend, I started trying out my work with a test
group of singers beforehand.

I ask my tryout group to fill out ballots on the hymns we
test, but during the tryout session itself, I also do my very best to listen to
how the hymn sounded while it was being sung. 
Two hymns that sound pretty much the same when you play them back in
Finale can sound completely different when people actually sing them.  One clicks; the other doesn’t.  Sometimes this has to do with musical difficulty,
but more commonly, it’s the difference between “I want to sing this in worship!”
and, “It’s OK, I guess.”

Christians make that decision about every new hymn they
encounter, and I think they often make it very early.  When I tried out an early version of a hymn
called “Come, Jesus Christ” a few years back, I knew it was going to work
because I heard it working by the end of the first line.

There were still plenty of problems in the hymn at that
point.  In fact, there was a line in the
second verse that was such a horrible mismatch with the music that the members
of my tryout group sounded surprised as they sang it.  However, because they had made up their minds
about the hymn before they hit the clunker line, they still gave it high
ratings.

The moral of the story here is pretty simple.  If you want to write a hymn that will be
sung, you have to nail the first line.  Have
to.  If you want to figure out whether a
hymn will be sung, look at the first line. 
A hymn with lots of lovely content in the middle but a lame first line
is like the stereotypical girl with the nice personality.  Buy that hymn a cat, ‘cause nobody’s going to
be interested.

In fact, I will venture a prediction.  “Powerful in Making Us Wise” (#281 in *Psalms, Hymns, and Spiritual Songs)* is a
lovely hymn with lovely music.  However,
the first line (“Powerful in making us wise to salvation”) doesn’t work.  Even though it’s quoting from 2 Timothy 3:15,
the wording is inelegant, and it “buries the lede” until the third line, which
is much too late.  I think the first line
will doom it, and it will not be sung.

The point here is not that the church ought to sing bad
hymns with good first lines.  It is that
it will.  An excellent first line will punch
the ticket for your hymn, even if you spend the next three verses committing
hymnic malpractice.  How much better,
then, to use the power of a strong first line to get the church to sing
something that is actually good?
