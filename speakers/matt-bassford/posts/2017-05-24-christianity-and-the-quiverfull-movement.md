The other day, my wife sent me [an article](http://www.rawstory.com/2014/09/escape-duggarville-good-christian-wife/) written by a woman who had fled the Quiverfull Movement.  Her experience within it was so embittering
that she argues not only that the Movement itself is bad, but that it is a
logical extension of the principles of Christianity, so that Christianity
itself is bad.

This is, of course, an extremely important question.  Did this woman face so much misery in her
life because she followed a dangerous distortion of the gospel, or did that
misery come from the gospel itself?  I won’t
deny that following Christ can lead to great suffering.  This is amply evident in the lives of men
like Paul, Stephen, and indeed Christ Himself.

However, the suffering that comes from false doctrine is far
greater because those who are deceived by it have unwittingly placed themselves
in the dominion of the devil.  God loves
us and seeks our ultimate good; Satan emphatically does not.  God loves to give us good things and will not
allow us to suffer needlessly; Satan delights in our unnecessary
suffering.  Indeed, distortion of the
truth of Scripture is one of his favorite tools for making people miserable.



**Understanding the
Quiverfull Movement**

In order to determine whether the Quiverfull Movement is
based on truth or distortion, we must consider the Biblical justification that
its adherents advance.  Normally, I’m
reluctant to use the words of the enemy of some group to describe that group’s
beliefs.  However, in this case, the
article’s author aims to tie the Movement to the Bible, so I think she’s making
the best Scriptural case she knows how. 
She writes,

*That verse in Psalm
127 says, “Blessed is the MAN who has his quiver full of them” … and it goes on
to say, “he shall not be ashamed, but will speak with the enemies in the
gates.”  We were taught that in Bible
times, the city gate was the place where male leaders made decisions regarding
local government.*

*So this was about
political domination.  The whole point of
having a quiver full of babies is to … out-populate the “enemy,” … that would
be all of you; and to shoot those many arrows “straight into the heart of the enemy.”  And by that, we meant that our children would
grow up to be leaders in all the major institutions of our society.  This was our plan for taking back America for
God. So the children were like arrows (which is the ammunition) in God’s holy
war.*

Sarcastic, yes, but so far as I understand things,
fundamentally (get it?) accurate.  The
Quiverfull Movement takes Psalm 127:3-5 and uses it as a blueprint for a
religiously motivated takeover of American society.

**What Psalm 127 Means**

There are lots of problems with this.  The argument above shows some awareness of
the cultural context of Psalm 127 (it is true that people in Bible times
settled disputes in the city gate), but this awareness is curiously, perhaps
willfully, lopsided.  There are many more
things in the cultural context that the Quiverfull Movement doesn’t talk about
because they work against its argument.

For instance, it assumes that the primary, perhaps the only,
reason why people in the Old Testament wanted to have lots of kids was to
accumulate political power.  This is
incorrect.  Instead, their primary
concern was economic.

Children back then weren’t an economic drain as they are in
our society ($233,610 per child, according to the latest figures from the
USDA).  Instead, they were farmhands.  My dad was born in the 1930s, at the tail end
of pre-industrial agriculture, and he reported that his dad had him working
from about the time he could walk.  You
don’t have to be very big to stand behind the cows in the dairy with a shovel
to keep the manure from hitting the floor when they poop (though as my Aunt
Doris learned, when they sneeze in mid-bowel movement, you won’t enjoy the
result).

My dad left the family farm as soon as he turned 18 and
never went back, which tells you something about the amount of free labor that
the family patriarch extracted.  However,
children 3000 years ago were much less likely to do that.  Typically, people didn’t leave their home communities
unless they were thrown out (as in Job 30:1-8) or forced out by famine (as in
Ruth 1:1-2), and neither one of those was a particularly secure way to
live.  The more kids you had, the more
people you had working your land, and the more prosperous you were going to be
(which always leads to political influence and power too).

Lots of kids also meant something else.  It meant you had largely avoided infant
mortality.  We’re horrified when we see
statistics that the average man in the ancient world only lived about 40 years,
but even though those statistics are true, they’re misleading.  People then who made it to adulthood often
lived nearly as long as people do today, but the ancient-world average is dragged
down by the vast number of infants who used to die before their first
birthday.  I’ve lost a child, and I’m
here to tell you:  Blessed are those who
don’t!

As a result, we shouldn’t read Psalm 127:3-5 as a
commandment at all, even for its original audience.  It is an observation of sociological reality
thousands of years ago, no more, no less. 
Canaanite peasants who never even heard of Psalm 127 were every bit as
interested in kids as Israelite peasants were.

When we treat Psalm 127 as a commandment, then, we distort
the text.  If a Christian family today
wants to have lots of children, good for them! 
However, there’s no call for us to conclude that this is what we’re
morally obliged to do.

**Political
Christianity**

In fact, this distortion of the text is driven by a larger
distortion of Christianity:  its
diversion to political rather than spiritual ends.  Old-Testament Judaism and Islam are both
state religions.  They lay down rules by
which society ought to be governed. 
New-Testament Christianity, however, isn’t like that.  Jesus Himself repeatedly disclaims any
interest in political power (most notably in Luke 12:13-14 and John
18:35-36).  His earliest disciples were
concerned with governing themselves and their congregations, not with governing
society.

This disinterest was based on both practical and ethical
grounds.  As a minority religion in a
pagan society, it would have been fatal for early Christians to try to set up
their own government.  Second, it’s
impossible to coerce anybody into genuine discipleship.  If the state makes church attendance
compulsory, you will make more pew-sitters, but you won’t make more
Christians.

I don’t think it’s wrong for Christians in our democratic
society to try to apply their principles in the voting booth (though I think
this proves quite difficult in practice). 
However, it is extremely dangerous for us to make the pursuit of
political goals the primary expression of our faith, and this is clearly what
the Quiverfull Movement is about.  It’s
an attempt to breed an army of (let’s be honest) Republican voters and
activists.

We see the fruits of this approach in the article author’s
observation that her family was named the 2003 Nebraska Family of the Year by
the Nebraska Family Council.  This didn’t
happen because they were so active evangelistically or worked so hard at feeding
and clothing the poor—the things that the Bible explicitly tells us to do.  As future events revealed, they weren’t even
developing the love and unity that would keep them together as a family.  Instead, their “Christian” work was lobbying
to have the Nebraska Defense of Marriage Act passed.  They abandoned the things that were truly
important in pursuit of a political achievement that proved to have no lasting
effect.

**Conclusion**

I think this is tragic. 
However, I deny that it, or the Quiverfull Movement generally, is a
faithful enactment of New-Testament Christianity.  It is based on a fundamental misapplication
of Scripture (which then distorts the way the rest of the Bible is read) and is
directed toward earthly ends rather than heavenly ones.  Such a misappropriation of the name of Christ
can never please Him.

It’s fine for Christians to have opinions on who should be
president.  However, those views must
never lead us to forget who is King.
