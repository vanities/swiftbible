Yesterday, a friend of mine linked to [this article](http://fivethirtyeight.com/features/the-sun-is-always-shining-in-modern-christian-pop/#fn-5) on my Facebook wall.  It confirms
something that I’ve suspected:  modern
song worship, especially modern song worship with modern songs, is slanted
toward the positive.  I think that’s a
problem, and I’ve written about it [here](http://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2014/09/darkness-in-hymns.html) and [here](http://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2016/05/bono-honesty-and-worship.html).  The complex emotional tapestry of the Bible
and indeed of our lives can’t be adequately expressed by hymns that emphasize
the bright threads and ignore the dark ones.

So, then, we’ve found ourselves with a worship repertoire
that doesn’t capture what our lives are truly like.  This is weird.  It suggests that all the major players in the
worship world, hymnal editors, songwriters, worship leaders, and even
worshipers, have worked together to produce a caricature of human emotion, a
caricature that even they must recognize as such.  We’re singing “I’m Happy Today” every day when
we know perfectly well that half the time we are not, in fact, happy today.

What gives?



I think the answer is that the happy happy happy all the
time is a form of advertising.  On some
levels, this is entirely intentional. 
Some years ago, back during my preacher-trainee days, my mentors had me
read Rick Warren’s *The Purpose-Driven
Church*.  In many ways, the book is
the bible of the megachurch movement.  It
urges churches to redesign themselves to appeal to as many of the unchurched as
possible.

If people in your
community like listening to country-and-western music, ditch the hymns and
bring in a country-and-western praise band. 
The more you do what people like, the more of them will come.  It’s marketing for the church.  It’s advertising.

Recently, a number of my friends have taken vacations to
Disneyworld.  Personally, I’d rather go
to Advertising Land.  I don’t know what
world all those people in the TV commercials inhabit, but it sure isn’t this
one!  The sun is brighter, the grass is
greener, the people are prettier, and the food tastes better.  There are no drunks in the beer
commercials.  There are no repo men in
the car commercials.  All the tears have
not been wiped away, but that’s only because there never were any tears to
begin with.

Advertisers are not stupid. 
They have one goal.  They want you
to fork over your money in exchange for whatever product they’re hawking.  They know they can do that if they succeed in
convincing you that their product will make you happy.  It works for all the people in the
commercials, so surely it’ll work for me too! 
Advertisers don’t really care what you do when you come home from the
store and discover that the product does not actually make you happy after
all.  From their perspective, the
transaction has already been completed.

Church-based marketing, then, is inevitably going to find
itself pointed at the same goal.  The
object is to make the deal, to close the sale, to convince the unchurched that
if he becomes churched, he’ll find himself in a religious version of Advertising
Land.

As a result, there aren’t any more
downer praise songs than there are drunks in beer commercials.  People don’t like sad.  It’s a turnoff.  It drives them away.  You need to keep the songs happy to keep the
theater-style seating filled up.

In many places, the ordinary pew-sitters are complicit in
this.  They never appear in church
without what I’ve heard described as “the Christian smile”.  It is the smile that has been lacquered in
place, beaming resolutely on despite the most awful of circumstances.  My wife is dying of cancer?  Doesn’t matter, ‘cause I’ve got Jesus in my
heart!

This is the advertising push, the push to sell Jesus.  Sadly, it has next to nothing to do with what
being a disciple of Jesus is really like. 
I can’t think of any significant Christian in the New Testament who
believed that the world was a fundamentally pleasant place, or that following
Jesus would make it so.  Joel Osteens are
conspicuously absent from the pages of Scripture.  During the second missionary journey, Paul
was only in Thessalonica for three weeks, yet he reports in 1 Thessalonians 3:4
that he repeatedly told the Thessalonians that they would suffer affliction for
Christ’s sake.  He warned the believers
in Acts 14:22 that through many tribulations they must enter the kingdom of
God.

That’s rotten advertising. 
If Paul had been a Madison Avenue account manager, the “you’re going to
suffer a lot” marketing campaign would have gotten him fired.  However, it was fundamentally genuine and
true, and it prepared his hearers for what they would soon encounter.

Conversely, it is fundamentally untrue for us to pretend, by
what we sing or how we behave, that our walk with God is going to be all blue
skies and rainbows and sunbeams from heaven. 
God works in our lives to lead us to eternity with Him, not to make
those lives as pleasant as they possibly can be.  Indeed, sometimes those two goals are
directly opposed.

New Christians don’t get transported to Advertising
Land.  Instead, they will still find
themselves on trashed-up planet Earth, and in many ways, their earthly
situation will be a lot worse because they have surrendered their lives to
Jesus.  We don’t face state persecution
in the U.S., but Matthew 10:34-39 is definitely still a thing.  I’ve baptized people who didn’t stay faithful
a week because of the abuse they got from their families.  The life of any Christian is going to have
its share of disappointments, tragedies, and defeats.

When we pretend that these things are not so, we leave
ourselves and those around us unprepared. 
What’s more, we deny the reality of our own sometimes-troubled walk with
God.  It’s hardly surprising, then, when
some brethren, deceived by the Christian smile, decide that they’re the only
imperfect ones in a perfect church and leave. 
Others see through the Christian smile, decide that its practitioners
are hypocrites, and leave.  Still others
just get fed up with repeating something they know isn’t true.  I’d love to know how many people Joel
Osteen’s church loses out the back door every year.

If being a good Christian requires us to put on a happy
front all the time, Paul, with his weakness and fear and trembling and tears,
was a bad Christian.  Peter, who wrote a
whole book of the Bible about suffering, was a bad Christian.  How about the One who was a man of sorrows
and acquainted with grief?  Was Jesus a
bad Christian?

When we remain silent about the difficult parts of our
pilgrimage, we do everyone a disservice. 
Instead, it’s important for us to sing about them, because songs about
those things are useful, necessary, and genuine.  Heaven is a wonderful place, yes, but we
aren’t there yet.  It’s great to
celebrate where we want to be, but we need to speak truth in our songs about
where we are too.
