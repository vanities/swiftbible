During World War I, the United States was the first major
power to field infantry armed with pump-action shotguns.  In the short-range world of trench warfare,
the deficiencies of the shotgun as a weapon were masked and its usefulness
magnified.  This was all the more true
because the shotgun issued to American troops, the Winchester Model 1897,
lacked a trigger disconnector.  As a
result, if the pump handle is moved forward while the trigger is held down, the
Model 1897 will fire immediately.  This
process, known as slam-firing, allowed soldiers to fire it very quickly.

I was reminded of slam-firing during a conversation with D.
J. Bulls, a brother from Texas.  D. J.
has forgotten more about sacred music than I will ever know, and he argues that
the best way to determine whether a hymn is congregationally suitable is to
sing it straight through with all four parts from the beginning.  If the hymn flies, it will work in the
congregation.  If it doesn’t, it won’t.

Frankly, this approach strikes me as rather daring.  Can’t you at least run through the melody
once before trying the hymn with all four? 
However, I think D. J. has a point, and it has to do with the abundance
of false positives in today’s sacred-music world.

These days, there are a lot of formats that will make a hymn
sound great, even if the hymn is too musically complicated to work
congregationally.  This starts with
music-notation software, like Finale and Dorico.  All music-notation programs have playback
functionality.  Once you’ve entered a piece
of music, the program will play it for you exactly as you have entered it. 

Metronomic soullessness aside, the results are often quite
beautiful.  Every rhythm, every chord,
every note will be rendered perfectly. 
The computer will do this for you even if a choir of crack sightreaders
with perfect pitch couldn’t handle what you’ve written.  Consequently, it’s quite common for tunes
that sound great in Finale to crater spectacularly when handed to actual
real-life Christians.

 The same holds true
of recorded performances, whether of denominational praise bands using
instruments or Christians with musical training singing in a chorus.  In both of those cases, the ability of the
performers can mask musical difficulties that will become glaringly obvious during
congregational worship.  A lot of music
that sounds great on the radio doesn’t work as well when a bunch of untrained
singers have to work together.

Indeed, the same holds true for the “tryout group” at
church.  When I’m looking for people to
help me try out a hymn I’ve written, I look for brethren who either can read
music or have a good ear for it.  Most of
us aren’t going to record a hit single, probably, but the average level of
musical ability is considerably higher not only than that of the world (these
days, zero) but that of the larger congregation. 

The solution, then, is to slam-fire an unfamiliar song.  If the good singers can get it immediately,
the congregation will get it eventually. 
If, on the other hand, the best singers in the group struggle by
themselves, the congregation is going to struggle epically. 

Five minutes will tell the truth.  If your hymn goes, it will go.  If it doesn’t, at least it’s been given the
opportunity to die a quieter, more dignified death!
