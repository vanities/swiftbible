*Then the servant took
ten of his master's camels and departed, for all his master's goods were in his
hand. And he arose and went to Mesopotamia, to the city of Nahor.*

*--Genesis 24:10*

In modern America, employers and employees have an almost
antagonistic relationship.  Employers, on
the one hand, are often as demanding as they think they can get away with.  They require ever-increasing commitments of
time and energy, especially from salaried employees who aren’t compensated for
it, and anyone who isn’t willing to work all the unpaid overtime is next on the
chopping block at layoff time.  For their
part, many workers engage in constant low-grade guerrilla warfare against their
bosses.  Workplace humor from “Dilbert”
to “The Office” reflects the temperament of a workforce that is frequently
determined to slack as much as it can. 
The resulting dysfunctional work environment does neither side any
favors.



We get a glimpse into a far more wholesome way of doing
things in the interaction of Abraham and his servant.  Although Abraham does require an oath first,
he entrusts a vast amount of material wealth to this servant, in the
expectation that the servant will travel hundreds of miles to Mesopotamia and
faithfully fulfill his master’s commands. 
The servant could have wandered off to North Africa instead to live off
of his ill-gotten gains, but he did precisely as Abraham had asked.

Instead of the mutual exploitation we see around us, the
relationship between Abraham and his servant appears to be marked by
trustworthiness and trust.   We don’t know much about this servant except
that he had been with Abraham for a long time, and in those years of service,
they’d evidently forged a connection that could withstand tremendous
stress.  Abraham trusted his servant
because he had already seen the servant was trustworthy, and the servant was
trustworthy because he knew his master would reward him for so being.  Instead of a vicious cycle of betrayal, Abraham
and his servant established a virtuous cycle of loyalty toward one another.

As Christians, whether we are employers or employees, we
can’t change the workplace climate by ourselves, but we can be loyal.   When we work for another, no matter how much
our employer oppresses or mistreats us, we should never use that as an excuse
to give less than one hundred percent nor even engage in water-cooler grumbling
about it.  As Peter tells us in 1 Peter
2:18-20, even when our masters are plainly abusive and unjust, we still owe
them obedience as to the Lord.  Likewise,
as employers or supervisors, we must remember that those under us are people,
not commodities.  How much production we
squeeze out of them will not shape our eternal destiny, but the kindness and
respect with which we treat them will. 
As with any other area of our lives, we must temper justice with mercy,
and be faithful in both.  Most people
will respond favorably to loyal treatment, but even if not, righteousness in
the workplace will certainly win favor with God.
