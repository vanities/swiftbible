One of my favorite things about my blog is that I seem to
have carved out a space for niceness on the Internet.  Most online comments sections make me recoil
in horror, but the comments that I get, whether the commenter agrees with me or
not, are uniformly thoughtful and polite. 
I don’t know why I’ve been spared the usual troll avalanche, but I’m not
going to complain!

I think that such high-caliber commenters deserve equal
thoughtfulness and courtesy from me, and it’s in that spirit that I write
today.  A couple days back, I posted [this article](http://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2016/08/an-idol-named-jesus.html),
my second rebuttal to Bromleigh McCleneghan’s version of Christian sexual
ethics.  Below it, a commenter replied,
“I'd like to see your writing help the Church make a difference for the people
Jesus would stand up for the today, ie. confront human trafficking.”

I think the implied question (“Why don’t you write about
human traffic and other social ills instead of sexual ethics, etc.?”) deserves
honest consideration and an honest answer. 
After all, human trafficking is inarguably bad.  As per [this HuffPo piece](http://www.huffingtonpost.com/entry/taking-a-photo-of-your-hotel-room-could-help-save-a-trafficking-victims-life_us_57714091e4b0f168323a1ed7),
every time my family stays in a hotel room, my wife takes and uploads a picture
of the room to help the authorities combat human trafficking.  So. . . why do I write about the likes of
Bromleigh McCleneghan instead?



There are several reasons. 
Let me, as they say, name them one by one:

**Because it doesn’t
align with my understanding of the purpose of organized religion.** As the original comment implies, fighting
human trafficking isn’t really something that individual Christians can do by
themselves.  I don’t know, and I don’t
think I’ve ever known, anyone who was involved in human trafficking.  I would be appalled to learn that anybody I
went to church with was involved, and as a condition of repentance, I would
require them to turn themselves in to the police.  However, it’s simply not part of the suburban
middle-class bubble I inhabit.

Instead, this is a problem with a political, not a personal
solution.  It is not I who should
act.  It is “the Church”.

However, from my perspective, at least, that’s a solution
that creates more problems than it solves. 
First of all, I don’t believe that earthly religious organizations
larger than a local congregation should exist. 
I don’t belong to any denomination, and I am adamantly opposed to so
belonging.  For me, there is no “the
Church”.

Second, even on a local and congregational level, I believe
the work of the lower-case-c church is strictly limited.  In my view, churches should concern
themselves with proclaiming the gospel and providing for needy saints.  Anything beyond that is a departure from the
practices of the early church.

I understand the temptation to do differently.  After all, if only churches would band
together, concentrate their powers, and move beyond the limited objectives of
first-century congregations, they could accomplish all sorts of good!  However, if a congregation’s foremost goal is
teaching truth, and all its resources are devoted to that, pursuing any other
goal means less focus on what should be most important.  I think that’s going in exactly the wrong
direction.

In addition, I’m ambivalent about political involvement for
individual Christians, much less churches. 
What if standing strong against human trafficking requires a church to
make common cause with those who support abortion (as would likely be the
case)?  I’m with Paul in 2 Corinthians
6:14.  What communion has light with
darkness?  It’s far better to avoid
unsavory compromises.

**Because it wasn’t Jesus’
first concern either.** Jesus lived in
a world where human trafficking was not only a problem but quite literally the
foundation of the economy.  The Roman
Empire was built on the backs of slaves. 
Victorious Roman generals made their fortunes by selling countless
thousands of captives into slavery.

And yet, not one word did Jesus ever say against the
institution of slavery.  I think it’s
easy to make the case that Jesus would have said, if asked, that slavery was
evil.  However, He never raised the
issue, probably because He would have been tried and killed as an
insurrectionist (for obvious reasons, the Romans dreaded slave rebellions)
without having had the opportunity to accomplish His true mission.

Jesus came for the sake of slaves, just like He came for the
sake of everyone who was lost.  However,
His goal was never to redeem them from earthly oppression.  Instead, it was to rescue them from the
bondage of sin.  To argue that He would
have “stood up for them” is to read a modern concern into ancient texts.

**Because it doesn’t
align with my goals for my blog.** I
don’t write from an apologetic perspective in an attempt to convince
unbelievers.  Instead, I write to those
who are already Christians, particularly those who belong to my own religious
tradition.  I don’t write about the
problems and issues such people don’t have. 
I write about the problems and issues they do have.

I think sexual immorality is a big problem among Christians,
so I write to refute the Bromleigh McCleneghans of the world.  I think many/most Christians don’t have a
proper understanding of hymns and song worship, so I write about that a
lot.  I write about pornography, racism,
and hardheartedness toward the poor because I think there are plenty of
believers who struggle with those things.

Human trafficking, though? 
I would guess that many Christians are so naïve about the dark
underbelly of the world that they aren’t even aware that such a thing still
exists.  I would expect 100 percent of
those who are aware of it to be opposed to and even revolted by it, and they
would reach that conclusion quickly and easily.

Raising the issue, then, isn’t going to result in any kind
of spiritual change.  People get that
already, just like they get that Christians shouldn’t be thieves or murderers
or drug dealers.  It’s a problem for
somebody else, not a problem for us. 
Generally, I’m suspicious of talking about the sins we don’t struggle with.  It leads to self-congratulation rather than
self-examination, repentance, and growth.

I think human trafficking is an important issue, along with
many other social ills.  As I have
opportunity, I try to be a friend to the disadvantaged and oppressed, just as
Jesus was.  However, my first concern is
for souls, not bodies, so I choose to write about that one needful thing rather
than anything else.
