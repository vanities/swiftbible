After one of my hymn-related blog posts last week, my
brother and friend B.J. Young asked me what I thought could be done to increase
our appreciation and understanding of hymns. 
In particular, it sounded like he thought leading hymns in particular
ways might help us to grasp them better, and I think there’s something to be
said for that (indeed, I may say it in a later post).  However, my mind immediately took me to the
things that we as individual Christians can do outside of the assembly to
become better worshipers inside it.



**Read.** In the first place, I think that
understanding hymns better has to start with reading them more.  There are hymns that we’ve been singing all
our lives that are tattooed in our memories, but any hymnal is going to contain
hundreds of hymns we don’t know by heart.

It’s also possible to sing a hymn since forever and still
not understand it.  For instance, the
verse of “By Christ Redeemed” that begins “And thus that dark betrayal night”
is famously impenetrable.  I once asked
an auditorium-sized adult Bible class what it meant, and either nobody
understood it or was willing to admit that they did.

Whether we don’t know a hymn or know it but don’t comprehend
it, the assembly isn’t the best place to solve the problem.  When we’re singing together, there are many
different things competing for our attention, from singing the tune correctly
to ignoring the howling baby the next pew over, and we often lose focus and
lose the thread of the meaning as a result.

Instead, it’s much easier to figure out a hymn when we’re
reading quietly by ourselves.  There are
no (or at least fewer) distractions. 
There’s no song leader rushing us on to the next verse, so that if we
want to go back and reread a line, we can easily do so.  Craig Roberts, editor of the hymnal *Psalms, Hymns, and Spiritual Songs*,
recommends keeping a hymnal on the bedside table.  It doesn’t take very long to read a few hymns
before bed, and the practice benefits both the mind and the soul.

It’s not surprising, then, that *Psalms, Hymns, and Spiritual Songs* is uniquely suited for this kind
of individual devotion (full disclosure: 
I was one of the technical editors for the hymnal, but I didn’t make any
of the decisions during its compilation, nor do I have any financial interest
in it).  The editors of *PHASS* laid it out in what they call
“phrased notation”, so that hymns in the staff still maintain the same line
breaks that they would have in a book of poetry.

Consider the following two examples:

Abide with me:  fast
falls the eventide; The darkness

deepens:  Lord with me
abide!  When other helpers

fail and comforts flee, Help of the helpless, O abide with
me!

Abide with me: fast falls the eventide;

The darkness deepens; Lord with me abide.

When other helpers fail and comforts flee,

Help of the helpless, O abide with me.

The first is the way the text of “Abide With Me” is set in *Great Songs of the Church* (a wonderful
hymnal in its day, to be sure).  The
second is the way the same text is set in *PHASS.* Both settings contain the same words, but
the second highlights its logical and poetic structure.  It does a better job of revealing the beauty
and meaning that already exists in the hymn. 
There are certainly books out there that set hymns like poetry, without
music, and accomplish the same thing. 
However, if you want to read hymns out of a hymnal, read out of *PHASS*.

**Study.** Little-known fact:  most authors of great hymns knew their Bible
well.  Like, *really* well.  Like, “all
kinds of cool, obscure Scriptural phrases stuck here, there, and everywhere
because that’s how they thought” kind of well. 
As a result, those who are interested in upping their hymn-comprehension
game can take the hymnal off the bedside table, put it on the kitchen table, and
read it with a Bible and a digital concordance alongside.

It probably helps to use the translation the hymnist was
using when he wrote the hymn.  For any
writer before 1975 or so, the language of the hymn is probably tracking the KJV
(hence, for instance, “fightings and fears” in “Just as I Am”, which is cooler
than “conflicts” in the NASB version of 2 Corinthians 7:5 anyways).  A couple of hours with the hymns you thought
you knew best will probably turn up all kinds of Biblical connections you didn’t
know existed.

**Meditate.** Hymns certainly fall under the “these
things” of Philippians 4:8, and the better the quality of the hymn, the more
true this is.  A great hymn both has spiritually
useful things to say and says them beautifully and well.  C.S. Lewis reported that, as a new Christian,
he sneered at the hymn repertoire as being “fifth-rate poems set to sixth-rate
music,” and, as he continues his discussion, never really retracts the
sentiment.  It’s worth noting, though,
that although Lewis wrote successfully in all kinds of other spiritually
related genres, he never wrote a repertoire-level hymn.  A great hymn is great art, and those who look
down their noses at the achievements of hymnists past are cordially invited to
improve upon them (hint:  the conviction
that you can do so is probably enough to keep you from ever succeeding).

As a result, in the body of great hymns, we have some of the
most enduring achievements of the human pen (the most well known and widely
read English-language poets of the eighteenth century, for instance, are all
hymnists) directed toward the most important human endeavor:  the glorification of God.  The more we dwell on hymns, the more they
will enrich our lives, almost without limit. 
Our good days and our bad days alike will benefit if we call to mind a
hymn to go with them.
