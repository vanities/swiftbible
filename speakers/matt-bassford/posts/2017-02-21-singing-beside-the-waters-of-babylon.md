Most Christians are well aware that increasingly, their
values do not line up with the values of the society around us.  Sometimes, this is obvious; there are
millions today who revel in rejecting the thou-shalt-nots of Scripture.  At other times, though, the difference is
more subtle but no less important.

Take, for instance, the difference between the Biblical
perspective on singing and the world’s perspective on singing.  According to the Scripture, singing is supposed
to be for everyone.  Every Christian is
supposed to participate in “teaching and admonishing one another in psalms and
hymns and spiritual songs.”  It doesn’t
matter whether we have the voice of an opera singer, couldn’t carry a tune in a
bucket, or are somewhere in between. 
Christians are a people of song.

However, Americans (white, middle-class Americans, at least)
are not a people of song. There’s plenty of music all around us, but it’s music
that is performed and listened to rather than sung.  Our culture rarely calls upon ordinary people to
sing.  When it does, they’re so unused to
singing together, and even to singing at all, that they make a hash out of
it.  “The Star-Spangled Banner” before
sporting events sounds like vague, undifferentiated moaning.  Tens of millions of our neighbors couldn’t sing “Happy Birthday”
correctly if their lives depended on it.

This raises a vital question, a question that Jim Duke
originally raised in a comment after [this post](http://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2017/01/hymn-recordings-and-congregational.html) (and it’s worth going back and reading the original comment).  How do we preserve and strengthen the
apostolic tradition of song worship when singing is something that people in
our society never do, except maybe along with the radio or in the shower?  How do we sing the Lord’s song in a foreign
land, a land that has forgotten how to sing?



I think the first step is awareness of the problem.  Sadly, many church leaders have fallen into
the habit of believing, in the words of 2 Peter 3:4, that “all things are
continuing as they were from the beginning of creation.”  They overlook the effect that cultural
changes have on the church, and they take for granted that our round of worship
will continue as it always has with minimal effort on our part.

This is hardly the case. 
If churches don’t invest any corporate effort in song worship, then
their worship will drift with the cultural tide.  Fewer and fewer Christians will sing, and
those who do sing will sing more timidly. 
Eventually, somebody will bring in the praise-and-worship band out of
sheer disgust.  Mediocre rock music isn’t
according to the first-century pattern, but it’s marginally more pleasant than
awkward murmuring, which isn’t according to the first-century pattern either.

If churches want to avoid this fate, they’re going to have
to work to avoid it.  Church leaders
communicate the importance of song worship by calling members to invest time
and energy in it.  There are many ways to
do this.  One of the easiest is to make a
practice of regularly learning new hymns. 
This is simpler if a congregation has a song leader who can read music,
but it’s possible even if it doesn’t. 
However this gets accomplished, the addition of new hymns to a
congregation’s worship repertoire infuses it with new life.

Similarly, congregations can hold hymn-themed gospel
meetings.  There are many capable men in
the brotherhood who can visit for a week and instruct members in the mechanics
and the significance of song worship.  A
meeting like this can provide the emotional impetus needed to begin more
frequent hymn-learning sessions.

Another strategy, and one that the Joliet church has
adopted, is to host a yearly area-wide singing. 
I think it’s fair to say that this singing is the spiritual high point
of our year.  The attendance that Friday
night can be almost double our normal Sunday-morning attendance, and the
atmosphere is saturated with energy and enthusiasm.  It shows the members of the congregation what
the mountaintop experience of congregational singing is like, and I believe it
helps the congregation to worship more enthusiastically all the time.

Song leader-training sessions also have considerable merit
here.  No matter how willing the
congregation, an unskilled song leader can put a lid on worship.  Every man has different levels of ability,
but all of us can benefit from learning how best to use what gifts we
have.  Some churches do this with a
separate class during normal Sunday and Wednesday Bible classes.  Others do it outside of regular service
times.  The former might be better
attended, but in either case, it will raise awareness of how to conduct worship
effectively.

As part of this, song leaders need to be encouraged to lead
good hymns.  It might be fun to raise the
rafters with an old favorite that doesn’t say a whole lot, but “worship” like
that doesn’t have a lot of staying power. 
By contrast, hymns that engage both the intellect and the emotions have
the power to transform us by the renewing of our minds.  If song leaders simply learn to select hymns
that say something (and there are such hymns in any genre), the church will
benefit immeasurably.

Finally, and most importantly, congregations need to return
again and again to what the Bible says about song worship.  This certainly includes the study of
authority passages such as Ephesians 5:19 and Colossians 3:16, but it goes well
beyond that.  One of the most important
texts for our consideration is the description of our worship in Hebrews 13:15
as a sacrifice of praise.

If our song worship is to be a sacrifice (and Hebrews 13:15
certainly describes song worship, even if it also describes other things), then
the Biblical principles governing sacrifice also apply.  Jesus died for our sins because His life was
the best thing He had to offer, and nothing less would do.  Similarly, we must approach our singing with
the mindset that we are going to give to our Creator the best we have to offer,
and nothing less will do.

Ultimately, our attitude toward song worship reflects our
attitude toward God.  No one who truly
loves and rejoices in the Holy One of Israel will turn aside from an
opportunity to glorify Him with all they have. 
Yes, we might not be very good at singing.  So what? 
No human voice nor human heart is perfect enough to give Him the glory
He deserves.  Once we grasp this, the
relative extent of our inadequacies stops mattering very much.

The people of God are a people of song.  There’s no way around that.  In the words of Isaac Watts, “Let those
refuse to sing who never knew our God.” 
If people in the world don’t sing, well, they don’t have much to sing
about.  We do.  Both now and in the years to come, may His
church dedicate itself to His worship, so that He will continually be exalted
by the praises of His redeemed people!
