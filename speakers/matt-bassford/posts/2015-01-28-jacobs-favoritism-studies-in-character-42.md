*Now Israel loved
Joseph more than all his children, because he was the son of his old age. Also
he made him a tunic of many colors.*

--Genesis 37:3

A number of posts ago, we looked at another story of favoritism.  Isaac loved Esau, but Rebekah loved
Jacob.  This family rivalry caused
decades of strife between the two brothers and eventually led to Jacob’s exile
in Mesopotamia.  However, now that Jacob
is raising his own family, he shows that he has learned to imitate his father’s
mistakes, not to learn from them.  Joseph
is the son of his old age, and as nearly the baby of the family, gets
preferential treatment.  It may also have
been a factor that Joseph was the son of Rachel, who was his favorite
wife.  Regardless, this is an instance
where we see Jacob, who was so shrewd about so many other things, being
clueless about his own family relationships.



When we consider this tale of multi-generational favoritism, it teaches us two main
lessons.  First of all, it should lead us
to understand the scope of the disaster we cause when we play favorites.  It’s bad enough when we distort the character
of our own children, but the wreckage usually doesn’t end there.  Just as soon as our children have children,
they will most likely repeat in their own child-rearing the mistakes we made
with them, which certainly includes favoritism.

I knew an old assistant county
attorney in Texas who had worked in the same office for decades, and he said
that in some instances, he had prosecuted three or four successive generations
from the same family.  Great-Grandpa went
through the court system 45 years ago, Grandpa made his appearance 30 years
ago, Dad was sent down 15 years ago, and now, here’s Junior, walking in the
footsteps of his less-than-upright ancestors. 
Even though our families may not have that same rich heritage of
lawbreaking, it’s all too easy for our child-rearing mistakes to continue for
generations.

Of course, we are not just
ancestors.  We are also descendants, and
all of us can look back and see things that our parents didn’t do quite right
with us.  In some cases, they may have
given us some truly devastating burdens to bear, like a legacy of
alcoholism.  In other situations, it may
be that they should have done a better job of teaching us to think for
ourselves or of being less mouthy. 
Whatever that undesirable trait may be, we must remember that neither
heredity nor upbringing is destiny. 
Certainly, those things exert a great influence on us, but they do not
control us.  No matter who our parents
were, no matter what they taught us, we can choose to be different.  We can choose not to carry out those mistakes
in our lives, and we can choose not to pass them on to our children.  Some of the greatest kings of Judah were men
who had wretched fathers.  Even if our
parents were similarly evil, we can still resolve to be righteous.
