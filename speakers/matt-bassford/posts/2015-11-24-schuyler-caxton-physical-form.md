**OVERVIEW.** Like the Tyndale Select, the Caxton is an
example of high-end premium goodness.  In
fact, “ultra-premium” might even be a fair description.  Once again, though, this isn’t a Bible that
comes cheaply.



As with all the high-premium Bibles I review, I didn’t buy
this one.  Instead, I solicited a review
copy from Schuyler, and they graciously provided one.  As always, nobody placed any conditions on
the content of my review.

**VITAL STATISTICS**

Translation:  New
Living Translation (2015)

Cover:  Goatskin

Binding:  Sewn

Trim Size:  5 ⅜” x 8 ¼”

Text Block Width:  1 ½
”

Weight:  2 pounds, 5
ounces

Column:  Single

Layout:  Paragraphed

Print Size:  8.75/11.5
Lexicon No. 1

Words of Christ: 
Black

Cross-References:  Yes

Ribbon Markers:  3

**SIZE.** Cut-and-paste here from my description of
the size of the Tyndale Select.  The book
blocks in these two Bibles are identical, so they are the same size—a
substantial, mid-sized Bible.  The Caxton
won’t pass the glove-box test either, though if you’re willing to shove a
high-premium Bible into a glove box to keep it safe, you have a steelier soul
than I do.  The Caxton wouldn’t be any
problem to hold, but it’s not really a grab-and-go Bible either.

**COVER.** In broad strokes, the Caxton has pretty
much the same cover as the Select.  Both
are edge-lined goatskin Bibles, printed and bound by Jongbloed.  The biggest distinction between the two is
that the lining material in the Caxton is calfskin rather than
polyurethane.  Beth Rhodes at Evangelical
Bible notes that the switch to the calfskin liner was an attempt to reduce the
hinge squeaking that some people complained about in previous Schuyler
Bibles.  It is true that my Caxton
doesn’t squeak when I open it, but neither does my poly-lined Select.  I’m not sure, then, that the calfskin makes a
difference functionally, but a goatskin cover lined with calf undeniably makes
a statement that no effort has been spared in making the Caxton a premium
Bible.

Like the Select, then, the Caxton appears to be a Bible that
might last until the Lord returns.  I
guess that in a few generations, if the leather isn’t cared for, it will start
to dry out and crack.  Other than that, I
don’t see a likely source of cover failure. 
At this price point, I shouldn’t see such a source, but it’s good that I
don’t.

In terms of lovability, the Caxton offers a slightly
different vibe than the Select.  When I
compare the copies that I have, the cover on the Caxton appears to be slightly
thicker and slightly harder, with a more pronounced grain and slightly less
flexibility.  It has a more rugged feel
than the Select.  This is particularly
noticeable for me where the Schuyler cross has been imprinted on the front
cover.  Because the leather has been
compressed down there, it doesn’t want to bend as easily.  Of course, because there is variation in the
skins of individual animals, my observations may not hold true for other
Selects and Caxtons.

If I were to draw a distinction between the Select and the
Caxton, I would say that the Caxton is a little bit showier.  My Caxton is dark green; my Select is
black.  Indeed, the Caxton overall offers
a wider selection of colors beyond the Select’s sober black and brown.  The Select has nothing on the cover; the
Caxton has that blind-stamped Schuyler cross. 
The Select’s simple spine contrasts with the ribbing and Schuyler logo
on the spine of the Caxton.  Neither
Bible is gaudy or tacky by any means, but the Caxton is definitely the more
noticeable of the two.

Personally, I could go either way, maybe leaning a little
bit toward the slightly blingier Caxton. 
However, that’s totally a matter of individual preference on my
part.  Both Bibles are luscious, gorgeous,
and smell enticingly of dead goat.  If
you don’t like either one, you probably don’t like Bibles.

**TEXT BLOCK**.  Once again, there’s not a whole lot of
difference between the Select and the Caxton here.  Both text blocks are sewn.  Both text blocks have red-under-gold art
gilt.  For some reason, the red in my
Select looks a little bit darker than the red in my Caxton.  Because Jongbloed is the gilder in question
for both Bibles, I’m sure this is nothing more than coincidence.

However, the two Bibles do not offer the same quality of
ribbon.  As I said last week, I wasn’t
thrilled with the quality of the Select’s two ribbons:  too short and too fray-prone.  By contrast, my dark-green Caxton came with
three ribbons in three different shades of gold (a subtle but interesting
touch).  They were all considerably
longer than the ribbons in the Select, and the ribbon ends were either heated
or treated with something to keep them from fraying.  I’m not really a ribbon guy (I never use more
than one), but the ribbons in the Caxton are clearly superior.

**VALUE.** Here we go again.  The high-premium Select retails for
$290.  The ultra-premium Caxton retails
for $350.  At the moment,
evangelicalbible.com, which is the sole retailer for Schuyler Bibles, has them
on sale for $186, but that’s still a sizable amount of money.  However, if you’re determined to have the
best NLT available, and indeed one of the best-designed Bibles available,
period, this is it.  It might also make a
marvelous Christmas gift for the Bible geek in your life.

In terms of physical form, I’ll assign this one the same
score as the Select:  8.5/10.  The better-quality liner and ribbons are
cancelled out by the higher retail price.
