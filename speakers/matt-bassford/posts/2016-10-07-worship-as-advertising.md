Earlier this week, I [blogged](http://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2016/10/church-or-corporation.html) about the problems with
approaching religion from a corporate perspective.  One of my commenters observed that some of
the biggest problems with corporatizing church appear in the realm of sacred
music.  Megachurches quite literally take
polls and consult focus groups to figure out what the preferred style of music
in their community is, and if need be, they will nuke their current repertoire
and replace it with songs that outsiders will find more congenial.



I admit that this reveals a considerable amount of worldly
wisdom.  If the unchurched-but-seeking
are trying to decide between two different churches, and they are utterly
ignorant of Scriptural or traditional bases for making that decision, then they
may well opt for the church whose band they like better.  It’s kind of like leaving a five-dollar bill
taped under one of the seats of the church bus for the lucky finder to take
home.  When you appeal to worldly people
using worldly strategies, you’re likely to find success.

However, this shrewd approach fails to reckon with the
Biblical perspective on song worship. 
I’m not aware of a single text in the New Testament that links sacred
music with seeking the lost.  Instead,
the Scripture identifies three main objects for it.

The first of these is the self.  In James 5:13, James advises, “Is anyone
cheerful?  He is to sing praises.”  God’s people are supposed to be a people of
song.  The Biblical model of worship is
not one in which the congregation sits and passively observes the performers on
stage.  Instead, every member of the
congregation is supposed to be actively involved in glorifying God.

A focus-grouped repertoire makes this impossible.  Most people are not trained musicians and
cannot read music.  As a result, they are
only able to participate when the songs selected are familiar to them.

This will be the case with a traditional repertoire
containing hymns that in some cases have been continuously sung for
centuries.  However, it will not be the
case with a repertoire modeled on Top 40 radio, in which some of the songs were
probably written by the church band last Tuesday.  When presented with music like that,
Christians who have been attending for decades will have no choice but to sit
and listen.  In fact, because the
repertoire turns over so much, there’s not much point in attempting to learn
any songs by ear.  Five years down the
road, they’ll all be replaced with a new batch anyway.

This isn’t a problem for the visitor.  He wouldn’t know any of the old hymns, even
if everybody else did.  In fact, he might
feel uncomfortable in a setting where everyone else is enthusiastically joining
in the worship.  By contrast, if nobody
else is singing because they don’t know the songs, he’s on an equal footing and
doesn’t have to do anything but sit back and listen to the style of music he
enjoys.

Indeed, I wonder if one of the unstated purposes of
contemporary Christian music is to keep the congregation from singing so that
outsiders won’t feel like outsiders. 
That’s great for the outsider. 
It’s less great for the congregants who have been robbed of an
opportunity to glorify God.

Second, sacred music is supposed to be for the church.  This is plain from the “one another” language
of Ephesians 5:19 and Colossians 3:16. 
We worship in part for the benefit of the body of Christ.

This benefit comes first of all through the straightforward
teaching that hymns do.  By their very
nature, hymns are easier to remember than Scriptural texts are.  When Christians are given the opportunity to
commit hymns to memory through regular repetition, they are also committing to
memory the fundamental truths of Christianity.

However, not all that we gain is strictly intellectual.  A repertoire of familiar hymns becomes part
of our sense of what it means to “do church” and be a Christian.  As we sing these hymns through the decades,
we come to a deeper understanding of their meaning and accumulate emotional
associations along the way.

I can tell you without thinking very hard that the three
hymns we sang at my daughter’s funeral were “The Sweet By and By”, “Be Still,
My Soul”, and “Nearer, My God, to Thee”, and I will remember that every time I
sing any of those hymns.  Even though
it’s not a happy connection, it is still deeply important to me, and if I am
deprived of those hymns, I am also deprived of a link to one of the most
significant events of my life.  More
generally, the hymns I sing are part of my identity, and in losing those hymns,
I would lose part of myself too.

God is mindful of our frame, and I think He intended for us
to react that way to the hymns we sing.  Our
hymn repertoire is one of the ties that bind us to the church and to Him.  Without that repertoire, we lose our sense of
place and belonging.  We feel like we
have been set adrift.  Its wholesale
demolition might work to the benefit of those who are not part of the church and
do not have such emotional connections, but it cannot fail to grieve and
diminish those who are and do.

As always, I’m a hymn enthusiast, not a hymn
taxidermist.  I don’t think I need to
sing exactly the same hymns I sang when I was five years old.  That’s not healthy either.  However, I believe that repertoire change
should be thoughtful, incremental, and additive rather than destructive.

A hymn like “When I Survey the Wondrous Cross” has already
proven its worth in a dizzying variety of places and times.  When we evaluate such works, we must respect
the judgment of centuries.  We ought to
look for modern spiritual songs to set beside them, hymns with enough merit
that we can thoughtfully hand them too down to our grandchildren and
great-grandchildren.  However, when we
dynamite this musical treasury because the focus group said that people in our
city prefer country-and-western music, we show that we have left the path of
wisdom.
