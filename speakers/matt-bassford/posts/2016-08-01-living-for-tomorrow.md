For a moment, let’s turn our attention to a grim
hypothetical.  Let’s imagine that you go
to the doctor, and, after a round of tests, he informs you that you have a
massive brain aneurysm.  It is
inoperable; there is nothing to be done. 
He estimates that you have two weeks of perfectly healthy life
left.  Then, the aneurysm will rupture,
and you will be dead in moments.

Now that you’ve got the disastrous diagnosis, do you go into
work the next day?  For most people, the
answer is probably no.  Instead, you’re
going to pack as much living as you can into that two-week span, because it’s
going to be your last chance to try all the things you wanted to do but haven’t
been able to before.  Things like saving
for retirement are no longer going to concern you.

Generally, when preachers tell fun stories like this one,
their point is that we ought to consider the fragility of life, and I’m
certainly not going to discourage such reflections.  However, that’s not really my point.  Instead, I mean for it to signal an important
difference between Christians and non-Christians.  They’re the ones who are going all-out
because they think they’re going to be dead in two weeks; we’re the ones who
are living more measuredly because we anticipate a future that they don’t.  Our looking ahead, in fact, ought to be one
of the defining features of our earthly lives. 
Let’s consider, then, what Peter has to say to us about living for
tomorrow.

**Earthly Relationships**

Our text this morning will be 1 Peter 4-5, and the first
section of this reading concerns earthly relationships.  Peter’s discussion of the subject begins with
his instructions about how to handle **THE
UNGODLY.** These appear in 1 Peter
4:1-6.  Peter starts his argument here by
pointing, as he does throughout the entire letter, to the example of
Christ.  Jesus didn’t have a good time in
the flesh.  Instead, He suffered, and if
we want to follow Him, we must be willing to suffer too.  If we aren’t willing to face the unpleasant
for His sake, we’ll surely lose our souls over it.

In particular, Peter is concerned with the suffering that Christians
face because of the contempt of non-Christians. This is something that is a
particular struggle for new converts. 
Generally, they have all sorts of ungodly friends with whom they pursued
the pleasures of the flesh.  Now that the
new disciples aren’t living that way anymore, those old friends notice, don’t
appreciate the change in behavior, and start mocking them over it.

This is hard to take. 
Nobody enjoys bad treatment at the hands of those whom they thought were
their friends, and new Christians and young people are especially vulnerable.

However, lest any of us be tempted to return to worldliness
because worldliness is easier, Peter reminds us that all of those worldly
people are going to have to give an account of their behavior before God
too.  If there were no God and no
judgment, sure, why not live it up here? 
However, because both are real, we have to remember that our time before
the throne is coming. Refusing sinful pleasure and facing the contempt of
others is a small price to pay for eternal life.

Next, Peter instructs us in **GODLY CONDUCT.** Here,
consider 1 Peter 4:7-9.  This section
starts with Peter’s observation that the end of all things is near.  I think Peter is probably writing this before
the great cataclysm of 70 AD and so is referring to the downfall of the Jewish
nation.  However, because our earthly
lives are so brief, it is equally true that the end is near for all of us, so
near that the first goal of our lives needs to be preparation for the future.

In particular, Peter says there are four things that we need
to do to prepare.  First, he urges sound
judgment and a sober spirit on us. 
Obviously, these are useful for keeping us out of trouble generally, but
Peter is particularly concerned with their good effect on our prayers.  When we live wise, thoughtful, righteous
lives, we ensure that we are the kind of people whom God is most willing to
hear.  Peter lived in a troubled time,
and so do we.  In both cases, Christians
need all the help they can get to make it through, and we must make sure that by
our sin, we aren’t cutting ourselves off from the One who can truly help us.

Similarly, Peter encourages us to fervent love and eager
hospitality.  Once again, this is timely
advice.  Both love and hospitality are
about relationship-building, and in our time particularly, people are suffering
from poverty in their relationships. 
They simply don’t have many people in their lives who love them and care
about them.  I think this is what is
behind our recent epidemic of mass murderers—alienated, unhappy souls who hate
those whom they perceive as indifferent to their suffering.  Of course, loneliness and fear can have a
toxic effect even on those who will never kill. 
As disciples, our role is to make sure that our brethren know they
matter to us.

Finally in this section, Peter instructs us concerning **WORK IN THE CHURCH.** Let’s continue in 1 Peter 4:10-12.  In this text, he sets out a general principle
followed by two specific examples.  The
principle is that whatever gift God gives you, you’d better be using it for
Him.  This extends beyond the
obvious.  This past week, I was amazed,
as I often am, by the sheer ingenuity displayed by the many people who helped
decorate for our Vacation Bible School. 
As anybody who looks at my PowerPoints can tell, I’m not very visually
creative, and I’m glad to see those who are using their skills to bring stories
about Jesus to life for our children.

There are many other ways in which Christians can work for
the Lord, but Peter zooms in on two.  The
first of them is speaking, and in Peter’s view, excellence in speaking isn’t
about presentation or demeanor.  Instead,
it’s about speaking as the oracles of God. 
Today, of course, none of us actually are oracles, but we can still
speak like them by relying on the word of God.

Similarly, those who serve are to do it by the strength
which God supplies.  It’s often the case,
I think, that Christians excuse themselves from service on the basis of lack of
energy.  I get home from work and I’m so
tired that I can’t do all these good things I know I should be doing.

Brethren, if we’re looking to ourselves for the strength to
serve God, we’re looking in the wrong place. 
Remember what Peter just said about living righteously to make our
prayers effective?  This is one of the
things we ought to be praying for.  There
have certainly been times in my life when I felt like I simply didn’t have enough
strength to do the godly work that was before me.  However, every time when I prayed, God gave
me what I needed.  He’ll do the same for
all of us.

**Looking Ahead**

In the next main section of our reading, we see Peter
considering the general subject of looking ahead.  First, he urges Christians once again to
consider the value of **SUFFERING.** Here, let’s read 1 Peter 4:12-14.  Interestingly enough, in this context, the
first thing Peter says about suffering is that we should not be surprised by
it.  This was certainly appropriate in
the first century, when persecution was a reality for so many brethren, but it
still holds true for us today.  In 2
Timothy 3, Paul reports that all who desire to live godly in Christ Jesus will
be persecuted.  That’s a promise.  If we have the desire, we can expect to see
the persecution.  Conversely, if we go
through our lives as Christians persecution-free, that should lead us to
question our desire to follow.

As Peter points out, persecution doesn’t have to mean
becoming a human torch for one of the Emperor Nero’s garden parties.  Instead, our suffering might come when we are
reviled for the name of Christ, which certainly does happen today.  All of us face the choice to stand up and get
mocked or hide and blend in, and we shouldn’t minimize the problems we face
when we do right.

We need to be ready for that so that we can react
appropriately to it.  Rather than being
afraid of or intimidated by the opposition we face, we should rejoice because
of it.  After all, we’re not somehow
blazing the trail of godly suffering. 
Instead, we’re sharing in the sufferings of Christ.  When we do right, we’re going to get grief
from the same kinds of people who gave Him grief.  The suffering itself is hardly pleasant, but
the knowledge that we’re following Jesus should be sweet.

Peter next connects our perseverance through suffering to **THE COMING JUDGMENT.** Consider 1 Peter 4:15-19.  He begins by pointing out that not all
suffering is equal in value.  If we
suffer because we are doing evil, that has absolutely no value in the eyes of
God.  We’re only getting what we
deserve.  However, when we endure
suffering for righteousness’ sake, that pleases Him.

This is important because God’s judgment is for
everyone.  I tend to think that Peter here
is still referring to the destruction of Jerusalem, but his words apply to the
judgment that we know is waiting for us. 
He reminds us that the righteous are saved only with difficulty.  The difficulty, of course, is not because of
God’s inability.  It’s because of our stubborn
determination to do evil.  The only thing
that could rescue us from disaster was the blood of Jesus.  Even the preacher, even the elder of the
church, must rest his hope on that, on the suffering and death of the Son of
God.

From there, Peter reasons that if the “good” people need so
much help, then what will come of the people who aren’t even trying to be
good?  This is a warning not only for the
people of the world, but for Christians who feel so secure in their salvation
that they complacently flirt with sin. 
Whether they suffer for their sin in this life or not, they will
certainly suffer in the life to come.

Even the most righteous disciple, then, must contemplate the
day of the Lord with sobriety and godly fear. 
This is nothing for us to be fooling with, brethren, and when we have
the opportunity to suffer for Jesus, or not, we must remember that He is
watching and will remember.  Whether
that’s a good thing or a bad thing is entirely up to us.

Finally, Peter calls **ELDERS**to consider what the judgment means for them.  Look here at 1 Peter 5:1-4.  It’s always important to read passages in
context, and this one is bookended with discussions of the judgment.  I think the point is clear.  The only way to be a good elder is to anticipate
the end.  A man who has his eye on
anything other than the glory to be revealed and the unfading crown of glory
that the Chief Shepherd will award is going to get it wrong.

Sadly, as we all know, our congregation currently does not
have elders.  However, it is certainly my
hope and prayer that some of the men who are now members here will rise up to
shepherd the flock.  I know, in fact,
that several of you have that goal very much in mind.

If that’s our goal, then, we need to consider what the work
is, and this is one of the two longest passages in the whole New Testament that
defines the work of elders.  In
particular, Peter defines that work using three sets of opposites:  voluntarily, not under compulsion; eagerly,
not for the sake of sordid gain; and as examples, not as lords.  Serving as an elder is both difficult and
thankless, yet the elder must honestly desire to do it for the sake of the
souls he will help.  As he serves, he
can’t do so for because of earthly motivations, either money or a power
trip.  Godly elders are humble men who
lead by moral authority and force of example, not because the work is
financially profitable, but because it is spiritually profitable.  May this congregation be blessed in the
future with many such men as these!

Before the Lord’s Supper, though, let’s turn our attention
from shepherds to the Chief Shepherd. 
This context isn’t really about Jesus, but Jesus is all through it.  It calls us first of all to remember the
sufferings of Christ as recorded by Peter and other witnesses.  The death of the Son of God on the cross is
no myth.  It’s historical fact.  That suffering serves as an example for us.  Even in the face of cruelty, we are called to
respond with meekness and love.  It is
also our only hope in the Day of Judgment. 
Without Jesus, we would be eternally lost.  Let’s remember, then, the way that this
sacrifice has transformed our lives as we partake.

**Standing Firm**

The third and final reading we’ll be contemplating this
morning is about standing firm.  It
begins with advice about **RESISTING THE
DEVIL.** Here, let’s read from 1 Peter
5:6-9.  Just as it is true that elders
have responsibilities toward the church, so too the church has responsibilities
toward elders.  Younger men in particular
need to remember that God has set those elders over them and submit to
them.  Furthermore, Peter calls us all to
humility.  There are few sins that have
caused as much trouble for the Lord’s people through the centuries as pride
has.  If we do not make a determined
effort to stay humble, it is certain that Satan will lead us astray too.

After all, as Peter points out, God is never far from any
one of us, but neither is the devil. 
Sometimes he’s sneaky and slithers around like a serpent.  Sometimes, though, he’s not at all sneaky and
stalks around like a roaring lion.  He’ll
let us know he’s coming because he wants us to be afraid.  Peter is speaking in the specific context of
persecution, and persecution is like that. 
The devil wants Christians to break under the strain, to avoid doing
what they know is right for the sake of avoiding physical suffering.  However, when we do, the devil has already
attacked and devoured us.

Instead, we have to sacrifice the passing joys of this life
for the eternal delights of the life to come. 
Like Jesus, we are not to resist the evil men who want to slander or
even harm us, but we are to resist the devil. 
We must remember that the real battle isn’t the one we can see.  It’s the one we can’t see.  When Christians are insulted, dispossessed,
imprisoned, or even killed for the Lord’s sake without fighting back or even
mocking their tormentors, that looks like a defeat for them.  In reality, though, it’s a victory for God,
and when we endure suffering for His sake, we are walking in the same path that
the victorious Christians of ages past also trod.

Peter then reminds us that all that we face is only for **A LITTLE WHILE.** Let’s read here from 1 Peter
5:10-11.  Here, Peter highlights the
crucial difference between passive resistance by Christians and passive
resistance by anybody else.  We have a
future; they don’t.  Those Buddhist monks
who light themselves on fire to make political statements aren’t going to get
anything for that, but when we stand firm in the face of trial, we store up
glory for ourselves.

I’ve heard it said that people can endure anything if they
know it’s not going to last, and we know that our problems in this life
won’t.  As Peter observes, next to what
we have waiting for us, our time on earth is insignificant, and when that time
is over, we will receive a reward.

This is true not only of persecution, but of all the
unpleasantness that accompanies life under the sun.  Whether we’re dealing with family problems,
friendship problems, or even a rough day at work, we can say to ourselves,
“This isn’t going to last” and keep going. 
The trial may seem long to us now, but when God wipes our tears away,
we’ll know it for the trifling thing it truly was.

As is common, Peter concludes the epistle with a few **CLOSING REMARKS.** They appear in 1 Peter 5:12-14.  Here, first of all, we are reminded of the
manner of composition of most of the epistles of the New Testament.  They weren’t written.  Instead, they were dictated to a scribe.  In this case, the scribe is Silvanus, better
known to us as Paul’s companion Silas. 
Peter also refers to “she who is in Babylon”, which is probably the
church in Rome.  A less opaque greeting
comes from Mark, probably not Peter’s literal son, but probably the same John
Mark who flakes out on the first missionary journey but later works to restore
his usefulness to the Lord.

These greetings, though, should not obscure Peter’s last
point.  He urges us to recognize that
because the grace of God is true, we must stand firm in it.  If the gospel were a lie, there would be no
point in being a Christian, but because it is true, there is no point in being
anything else.
