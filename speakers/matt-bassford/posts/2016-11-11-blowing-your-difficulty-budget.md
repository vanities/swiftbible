In [my last post](http://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2016/11/hymns-and-difficulty-budget.html) about about hymns,  I opined that every hymn has a difficulty budget.  In other words, every hymn that is adopted
for use gets adopted because its good qualities outweigh the difficulty of
learning it.  By contrast, if a writer
overspends his difficulty budget—if the hymn is more difficult than it is
worthwhile—congregations will not sing it.

I also noted that there are several attributes that a hymn
can have that increase its difficulty budget. 
They appeal to the Christians singing it, so they are more willing to
fight through difficulty to add it to their repertoire.  However, just as a hymn can have attributes
that increase its “spending limit”, so too it can have attributes that spend
down the accumulated goodwill.

I think it’s important to distinguish these positive and
negative attributes from each other because they don’t work in the same
way.  The absence of a negative attribute
does not increase the difficulty budget in the same way that the presence of a
positive attribute does.  Nobody says,
“Wow!  The lyrical meter in that hymn was
so perfect that I want to sing it over and over again!”  However, a flawed lyrical meter will lead
singers to reject a hymn.



With that in mind, here are several elements whose absence
will not help, but whose presence will harm, the chances of a hymn being
adopted:

**Extreme Melodic Range**.  One of the hymn-tune composers who taught me
to write claims that hymn melodies should range between Eb4 and Eb5.  Another says that the ideal range is between
D4 and D5.  The first writes to his tenor
range; the second to her alto range.

Both, though, convey the same basic truth:  an ideal hymn tune has a range of about an
octave.  This is the range that we see in
almost all the great hymn tunes of the past because it is the range that a
congregation of untrained singers finds most comfortable.  These singers don’t practice vocal exercises
to extend their ranges, and their voices have different natural pitches.  As a result, a range limited to an octave
will keep higher-pitched singers from grunting and lower-pitched singers from
squeaking.  Everybody is comfortable;
everybody is willing to sing.

By contrast, every note of range beyond the ideal octave increases
singer discomfort and demands more from the hymn.  The hymn with perhaps the largest range in my
congregation’s repertoire is “In Christ Alone”, which has a melody that
stretches from Bb3 to Eb5.  “In Christ
Alone” gets away with this larger range for two reasons.  First, all soprano notes below a D are
doubled with the alto, which gives true sopranos cover when they start to
gravel out.

Second, “In Christ Alone” is an exceptional hymn.  Christians are willing to sing it despite its
range problems because the content is so good, particularly for a piece written
in this century.  However, unless you’ve
written an “In Christ Alone” (and even Townend and Getty aren’t that
consistently good), imitating its range is a good way to make sure your hymn
won’t be sung.

**Awkward Musical
Rhythm.**  My wife is an ace
sightreader.  She comes from a musical
family, she has had plenty of musical training to go with it, and being married
to me, she gets lots of opportunities to keep her skills sharp!  However, nothing will drive her crazier
faster than trying to sightread one of those contemporary praise songs with
sheet music that looks like somebody dumped a cup of sixteenth notes on
it.  I call it “Morse Code
sightreading”:  dash dash dotdotdot dash
dotdot dash dash, etc.  Even if you’re
really good, getting those rhythms right can be a struggle.  If you’re less than really good (like I am),
it’s hopeless.

Certainly, the rhythms of older, traditional hymns can be
monotonous.  A friend of mine, since
departed, liked to compare the usual quarter-half rhythm of a hymn in 3/4 to
the sound of one of the presses in the print shop that he owned.  Ka-thunk, ka-thunk, ka-thunk, ka-thunk.  Innovative and interesting rhythm can do much
to add to the appeal of a tune (though it’s not an attribute that can ensure
use on its own)

However, wacky, counter-intuitive rhythm, whether to cover
up poor lyrical technique or just for the sake of weirdness, can wreck a hymn’s
chances all by itself.  It forces the
worshiper to concentrate on rhythm instead of worship.  This can work OK for a lead singer up on the
stage.  Nobody’s singing along with him,
so if he botches the rhythm, who cares?  However,
when a worshiper isn’t singing the same rhythm as the Christian sitting next to
him, it’s distracting and even embarrassing.

Congregations will put up with that for an exceptional hymn,
but such hymns are necessarily the exceptions.

**Poor
Arrangement.** A good friend of mine
is the musical editor for a multivolume psalter currently under
production.  He requires the composers
with whom he works to write according to common-practice rules of harmony.  He’s told me that every time he has allowed
an exception to the rules, he’s regretted it because the exception made the
tune more difficult to sing.

This is true not only for actual part-writing errors
(parallel fifths, cross-voicing, etc.), but also for times when the arrangement
of a tune does not follow strong progressions. 
I generally sing bass when worshiping (though I’m really a baritone
without a home), and I find that the bass lines of hymns tell me all I need to
know about the skill level of the composer. 
Some lines move with purpose toward an intuitive and emotionally
satisfying completion; others appear nearly random.  It’s as though the composer said, “The bass
has been on this note long enough; I guess it’s time for them to move.”

Not surprisingly, tunes in the latter category are much
harder to read and sing.  This is true
even when the apparent difficulty of a well-written arrangement is much
greater.  When Bach sends you to a note,
he has a reason for sending you there, and as you sing, the reason will become
apparent.  It will make sense for you to
go there.

By contrast, John Q. Praise-Song Writer will send you to a
note on a whim, even though you’re moving in a parallel octave with the alto
and you’re going to be doubling the third once you get there.  Like the peace of God, such arrangements
surpass all understanding.  Bad music
makes for hard music, and hard music makes for a hymn not being sung.

**Complex
Arrangement.** I think most composers
these days still use Finale (at least the older ones do; we sank so much effort
into learning how to use the wretched program that we are NOT going to
switch!), and as useful as Finale can be, there is a very important lie that it
tells.  When it plays back the intricate
tune that you have written without a single error, it leads you to believe that
the tune will sound that way when it is actually sung.

Maybe that’s true if you’re writing for the Mormon
Tabernacle Choir, and if you are, well, enjoy the experience!  However, most hymn-tune composers aren’t
writing for the Mormon Tabernacle Choir. 
They’re writing for the average congregation, which has about 80 people
in it, most of whom are not skilled singers, some of whom don’t like to sing or
simply can’t carry a tune in a bucket. 
In my religious tradition, which relies on untrained singers to manage
four-part a-cappella harmony, the point has particular force.  If Average Joe can’t manage it, there is no
choir or organ or praise band to save you.

This means, then, that the wise composer will adopt a
brutally simple style.  If a tune exceeds
the harmonic complexity of most folk songs, it is likely no longer suitable for
the ordinary folks who will be trying to sing it.  This can be a real struggle for composers,
particularly those blessed (cursed?) with a phenomenal ear:  “This tune makes so much sense to me!  How could anyone not understand it???”  For those who don’t have perfect pitch and
can’t glance at an unfamiliar piece of SATB sheet music and hear all four parts
in their head, comprehension is much more of a struggle!  If they don’t get it, they won’t sing it.

**Broken Lyrical
Meter.** Interestingly, this seems to
be the only attribute of lyrics with a purely negative effect.  Bad content can doom a hymn; good content can
ensure that it is sung, almost in the face of everything else.

Not so with meter (which, in hymnody, is defined as the
number of syllables per line).  Regular
lyrical meter is unremarkable.  It flows
smoothly, and the congregation does not notice or care.  Introduce an irregular meter, where the
number of syllables differs from phrase to phrase or from verse to verse, and
problems start appearing.

When a hymn establishes a metrical pattern and then breaks
it, it will divide the congregation. 
Sightreaders will sing the hymn as it is written, breaking the
pattern.  Non-sightreaders will follow
the pattern, ignoring the hymn as it is written.  In my experience, the most conspicuous
example of the problems this causes is the Ira Sankey hymn, “The Ninety and
Nine”.  The content is great; the meter
isn’t.  As a result, unless the hymn is
led by an exceptional song leader, the congregation will sing it in fits and
starts.  Poorly metered hymns without
great content won’t survive to be sung.
