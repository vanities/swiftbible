Once again, let me state for the record that I view the
decision to vaccinate (or not) as a matter of personal conscience.  Indeed, of all the Romans 14 issues that
arise today, the vaccination debate most closely resembles the first-century
quarrel over meat sacrificed to idols. 
Our responsibility as Christians is simple.  Do not judge. 
Do not regard with contempt. 

However, there are some among the opponents of vaccination
who want to make it a sin issue rather than a conscience issue.  Over the past several months, I’ve been told
repeatedly that the Lord will judge me for my own tolerance of
vaccination.  Well, that does raise the
spiritual stakes!  If they’re right, I’m
in sin.  If they’re wrong, they are.  They’ve violated Romans 14:3.  It’s important, then, for us to assess this
issue, not according to our emotions, but according to the standard of the
word.

The argument that support of vaccination is sinful is based
on the fact that some vaccines are manufactured using cell cultures taken from
aborted fetuses.  There is disagreement
about how many abortions are involved (Two? 
80?  More?) and how recent the
abortions were (1972?  1985?  Later?). 
Because there is no Biblical way to distinguish between an insignificant
and significant connection to abortion, the disputes about these disputed facts
are remarkable for their futility even by the standards of arguing on the
Internet.

However, there’s a more fundamental issue here.  Nearly all Christians agree that abortion is
wrong.  Even the brethren who accept
vaccination would express their disapproval of the abortions leading to the
vaccine cultures and would regret that such abortions occurred.  None of us would will for abortions to
continue so that vaccines could as well.

Vaccination opponents, though, would say that this
disapproval and regret does not matter.  They
argue that the mere act of benefiting from vaccination, or even of defending
vaccination, contaminates Christians with the guilt of abortion.  Thus, the sin of the abortionist is imputed to
their opponents.

That’s a very, very significant claim about the nature of
sin.  Calvinists have been arguing for
centuries that the sin of Adam is imputed to all of his descendants.  However, I’m not a Calvinist, and I’m
skeptical that I can be charged with sin simply because I come into contact
with the byproducts of sin. 

This logic does appear in the New Testament.  However, it shows up in the
meat-sacrificed-to-idols debate.  The
vegetable-eaters argued that you shouldn’t eat meat because the meat may have
been sacrificed to an idol, and eating such meat would bring you into
fellowship with the idol.  However, Paul
reveals that meat is meat.  The only
issues with sin when it came to eating meat sacrificed to idols were 1) leading
a brother to sin against their conscience, and 2) participating in an
idolatrous feast oneself.

Additionally, when it comes to downstream contamination with
sin, there’s a very significant dog that does not bark in the New
Testament.  It has to do with money.  Back in the days before fiat currency, all
money was made of precious metal.  The
metal came from mines, and the mines often were worked by slaves. 

The treatment of mining slaves was conspicuous for its
inhumanity even in such an inhumane age. 
The work was difficult and dangerous; the conditions were brutal.  Slaves (some of whom were Christians
condemned for their faith) remained in the mines until they perished. 

Today, we have no trouble recognizing the evil of such a horrific
practice.  And yet, even though the currency
of the ancient world was created at the cost of terrible suffering, we never
see first-century Christians refusing to use money.  No inspired writer taught that touching
coinage would taint brethren with the guilt of the mine bosses.  Nobody thought that benefiting from the use
of money was the same thing as condoning abuse of mining slaves.  Consequently, I struggle to believe that
using vaccines is the same thing as condoning abortion either.

Though I am neither a prophet nor the son of a prophet, I
predict that some who read this will not agree. 
If that’s you, I urge you to be consistent in your convictions, not
merely with respect to what’s downstream from abortion, but with respect to
what’s downstream from any great evil.

For instance, the Chinese government is currently engaged in
the brutal eradication of an ethnic minority known as the Uighurs.  The crimes against the Uighurs are
manifold:  murder, rape, confinement in
indoctrination camps, and yes, forced abortion. 
I do not hesitate to describe it as the great genocide of the 21st
century so far.  Note, by the way, that
the Chinese government is up to all sorts of evil stuff.  Its treatment of the Uighurs is merely its
most egregious policy.

If you think it’s wrong to use vaccine because of its
association with abortion, surely it is equally wrong to use anything made in
China because of its association with genocide. 
That abortion may have occurred in 1985, but the genocide is going on
right now.  You need to get that evil
stuff out of your house and out of your life!

If, on the other hand, you’re not OK with vaccine, but
you’re OK with your iPhone, it may be time to consider whether your beliefs
really are as principled as you think they are.
