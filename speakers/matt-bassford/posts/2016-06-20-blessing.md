Ever since I came to Joliet, I’ve made a practice of
preaching on topics that members of the congregation request.  There are lots of reasons why I like to do
this, but one of them is that it takes me to places in my preaching that I
wouldn’t otherwise go.  Like everybody, I
see and talk about the Bible from my own perspective, but sometimes, that leads
me to assume that others understand things the way that I do or to overlook a
question that others have.  Preaching
sermons on topics that other people suggest is a nice cure for this disease.

Our topic this morning comes from Carolyn, who called me a
couple weeks back wanting to know about the Biblical use of the word
“blessing”.  Apparently, this subject
caused some confusion at the ladies’ class a few weeks ago, and Carolyn wanted
to know whether, for instance, it was appropriate to bless God.  When she asked me for this, I realized first
of all that it was a complicated subject, and second, that others in the
congregation might well like to know my answer too.  With these things in mind, I decided that
this morning we would spend some time studying blessing.



***Eulogeō***

Once I sat down with all of my Bible helps spread around me
and began my word study of “blessing”, it quickly became apparent where the
trouble lay.  The simple use of “bless”,
“blessing”, “blessed” and so forth in our English Bibles obscures a lot of
complexity in the Greek.  In the Greek,
we’re not dealing with one word with one basic definition.  We’re dealing with two main words, one of
which has a whole bunch of different definitions.

The first Greek word that we’re going to be considering
today is *eulogeō*.  It’s a verb that means “to bless”, and it’s
made up of the word parts for “good” and “speak”.  Basically, to *eulogeō* is to say good things. 
It’s the word from which we get our English “eulogy”.

This is the word that has a whole bunch of different
definitions, at least four of them, in fact, and the first thing that *eulogeō* can mean is **“TO PRAISE”**.  The word in
this sense is only ever applied to God, or at least to those who are closely
connected to God.  For instance, when the
angel says to Mary “Blessed are you among women,” that’s *eulogeō*.  A pretty classic
use of the word appears in James 3:8-9. 
This pretty directly clears up the issue of whether we can/should bless
God.  James obviously thinks we ought to,
and he regards God as so worthy of blessing that it’s a problem even to curse
those who have been created in the image of God.  As a result, when we sing “Blessed Be the
Name” or any other hymns that speak of blessing God, we’re doing what we
should.

The second definition of *eulogeō*
is **“TO CALL DOWN GOD’S FAVOR ON”**.  We see *eulogeō*
used in this way with reference to the interaction between Melchizedek and
Abraham in Hebrews 7:4-7.  According to
the Hebrews writer here, it is always the greater who blesses the lesser.  I wasn’t in the ladies’ Bible study,
obviously, but my guess is that somebody in the class remembered this passage,
took it at face value, and said, “Well, this means that we can’t bless God,
because God is greater than we are, and it’s supposed to be the greater
blessing the lesser.”

What we have to understand, though, is that the “good words”
definition of *eulogeō* is quite
elastic, and it fits just as easily the circumstance of our asking God’s help
as it does the circumstance of our praising God.  Both words, though different, are equally
good.  Here, then, is what the Hebrews writer
is saying.  He’s saying that whenever you
have two people who are together, a lesser and a greater, it is the greater who
intercedes for the lesser.  The lesser is
the one who needs help.

Look at how this actually plays out in the meeting between Melchizedek
and Abraham in Genesis 14:18-20. 
Melchizedek is the priest of God; Abraham isn’t.  As a result, he’s on a higher level than
Abraham is, and this is shown in two ways. 
First, Abraham pays tithes to Melchizedek.  Second, Melchizedek calls down God’s blessing
on Abraham.

This might be a little tough to understand because we don’t
have much in the way of hierarchy today. 
Every Christian is equal as a priest under Christ our High Priest, so
there aren’t status divisions among us. 
However, I can think of one situation where this does come up.  When I’m studying the Bible or eating with a
man who is not a Christian, I will always offer the blessing at the beginning
of the study or meal.  I am a priest of
God, and I have a favored relationship with God.  The non-Christian I’m with isn’t and
doesn’t.  It wouldn’t be appropriate for
him to intercede for me.  It’s my place
instead to intercede for him.  This is
basically the same relationship that existed between Melchizedek and
Abraham.  Melchizedek was on a higher
spiritual level than Abraham was, so it was Melchizedek’s role to bless
Abraham, not vice versa.

Third, *eulogeō* can
mean **“TO CONSECRATE”**.  This is applied only to things.  We see Jesus doing this in Mark 6:41.  This is part of the story of Jesus feeding
the 5000, and this is the point at which the miracle begins.  In blessing the bread and fish, Jesus is
acknowledging that it came from God, who is the source of every good thing.  He is also, however, devoting that bread and
fish to God’s use.  Because it has been
offered to God, God will now use His power, working through Jesus, to multiply
the substance of the food many times over.

Today, of course, none of us should anticipate working a
miracle, but it’s still appropriate for us to bless things in this sense.  This happens most obviously, of course, with
the prayers that nearly all of us offer before meals.  We want to give glory to God as the source of
our material blessings, and we also want Him to see to it that the food we are
about to eat will nourish and strengthen us.

Finally, *eulogeō*
can mean, with reference to God, **“TO ACT
KINDLY TOWARD”**.  Consider, for
instance, its appearance in Hebrews 6:13-14. 
This might seem like a departure from our good-words definition of *eulogeō*, but before we get too upset
about the untidiness, we need to remember whom we’re talking about.  God is the one who creates things by speaking
them into existence.  He said, “Let there
be light,” and there was light.  When God
blesses us, He’s not merely aiming gracious words in our direction.  He’s adding actual, tangible blessings to our
lives.

For this reason, then, we should always remember to seek the
blessing of God.  Sometimes, when we pray
according to promises that God has made, we can be sure that we will receive
what we’re asking for if we’re praying in faith.  These things fall into the category of the
“every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places” of Ephesians 1.  Even when we’re not praying according to
God’s promises, though, we shouldn’t be shy of asking His blessing.  Granting us that blessing may or may not be
within His will, but we can’t tell one way or the other, and it never hurts to
ask!

***Eulogētos* and *Makarios***

The second part of our study will be framed by two more
Greek words, *eulogētos* and *makarios*.  Let’s start with ***eulogētos***.  As you can probably tell by looking, this is
a word that is derived from *eulogeō*,
and it is translated in our Bibles as “blessed”.  However, it’s never used to refer to someone
who has been blessed by God.  Instead, it
only ever refers to God Himself.  As a
result, it means pretty much the same thing as our first definition of *eulogeō.*When we see “blessed” in our Bibles, and it’s referring to God, it’s
always going to mean “worthy of praise.”

Let’s illustrate this by looking at Ephesians 1:3, which was
one of the passages in the ladies’ class that touched off this whole
sermon.  As we can tell by looking, this
is a verse that’s full of words that have “bless” as their root, and in the
Greek, all of those “bless” words have *eulogeō*as their root.  When we take all of
the various meanings of these words into account, the verse is actually saying
something like, “God is worthy of praise, because He has acted kindly toward us
in giving us every spiritual sign of His favor.”  Obviously, we’re talking about a versatile
word here!  Paul, of course, was well
aware of its many meanings.  He stacked
them all up like this because it sounds pretty and he liked to play with words.

From here, though, we’re going to move on to a different
Greek word entirely, ***makarios***.  As you can tell, this one doesn’t look a
thing like *eulogeō*, and it doesn’t
have a thing to do with good words.  Even
though it’s also translated in our Bibles as “blessed”, it doesn’t carry a
connotation of “worthy of praise”. 
Instead, it has the sense of “happy” or “fortunate”.  *Makarios*doesn’t mean that you’re innately wonderful.  It means that you’re in a good position.

Usually, when *makarios*shows up in Scripture, it’s about people, not about God, and usually, when
a human is described as “blessed”, *makarios*is the Greek word underlying that. 
This is what’s going on in the Beatitudes.  For instance, look at Matthew 5:11-12.  Much of the time, when we read the
Beatitudes, we breeze right though all the “blesseds”, but when we do that, we
miss the point.

Here’s what Jesus is actually saying.  He starts with a group of people who don’t
look like they’re in a good position. 
They’re following Christ, so the world treats them like dirt.  They’re insulted.  They’re persecuted.  They’re slandered.  That’s not normally a position that we seek!

However, Jesus wants us to understand, when we’re in this
situation that doesn’t look so good to worldly eyes, it’s actually a wonderful
situation to be in.  We should rejoice
over our good fortune in being persecuted for two reasons.  First, it’s a spiritual endorsement from the
least likely source—the world.  As Jesus
points out, the world has always persecuted God’s most faithful followers, so
if the world is harassing us, it shows we’re on the right track too.  Second, the earthly misfortune we’re
experiencing now will add up to great heavenly fortune later.  The worldly Greeks of the first century would
not have thought Christians were *makarios*.  Jesus begs to differ.

Even though *makarios*
is commonly applied to people in the New Testament, it is not universally
so.  God is described as *makarios* twice in the Bible, both of
them in 1 Timothy.  Even there, though,
they’re about position and circumstance. 
Let’s see how this works in 1 Timothy 6:15-16.  God here is not *eulogētos*, worthy of praise because He has blessed us.  Instead, He is *makarios,* happy because of the attributes He possesses.  He is the only Sovereign.  He is the King of kings and Lord of lords.  Only He has immortality.  Only He dwells in unapproachable light.  In short, nobody has a position and
circumstances like God does, so He is worthy of honor and eternal
dominion.  We can only properly
appreciate God’s uniqueness when we compare Him to everything else.

**Blessing and the
Lord’s Supper**

Finally this morning, let’s consider the relationship
between the concept of blessing and the Lord’s Supper.  As I was doing my word study, I couldn’t help
but notice how often the various “bless” words appear in a Lord’s Supper
context, so I thought it would be worth looking at.  The first place one of these words shows up
is during **JESUS’ ENTRY.** Here, let’s read from Mark 11:7-11.  This is the celebration of Jesus’ entry into
Jerusalem on the Sunday before His Friday crucifixion.  In many of our Bibles, the sentence “Blessed
is He who comes in the name of the Lord,” is in small caps.  This indicates that it’s a quotation from the
Old Testament, coming, in fact, from Psalm 118:26-27.  This text is particularly interesting because
it suggests that Jesus is blessed in three ways.  First, He is worthy of praise as the Lord’s
anointed.  Second, God has shown His
kindness to Him.

Third, though, I think that Jesus is also being blessed not
as a person, but as a thing.  Notice that
v. 27 in Psalm 118 concludes by saying, “Bind the festival sacrifice with cords
to the horns of the altar.”  The people
didn’t realize it, but God and Jesus both knew perfectly well that He was
coming to Jerusalem to die.  He is also
blessed here in the sense of being consecrated as the sacrifice for sin.

This theme of consecration continues in Jesus’ interactions
with **THE BREAD** during the Last
Supper.  Let’s read here from Mark
14:22.  Today, we follow Jesus’
instructions here each time we partake of the Lord’s Supper, as we do each
first day of the week.  In 1 Corinthians
11, Paul makes clear that the Lord’s Supper isn’t meant to be a physical meal,
a physical feast, but rather a feast for the spirit.  When we ask God’s blessing on our Sunday
dinners, we’ll be asking Him to ensure that the meal will physically nourish
us.  Here, though, the blessing that we
seek is that the unleavened bread will spiritually nourish us.  We want to be reminded of the body that He
gave for us, and we want to reflect on the communion we have with one another
through Jesus our Head, as the metaphorical body of Christ.  Our prayer is that the Lord’s Supper will be
effective in this.

The concept of blessing is also associated with **THE CUP** in which we are all about to
share.  Let’s consider Paul’s comments on
this in 1 Corinthians 10:16.  When he
calls the cup “the cup of blessing”, that’s a reference to one of the Jewish
customs of the Passover.  After all, the
Last Supper was a Passover meal, which is why we still use unleavened bread in
the Lord’s Supper today—the bread that Jesus originally distributed was
unleavened.

Similarly, another Jewish Passover ritual, one not described
in the Hebrew Scriptures but in the Mishnah, involved four cups.  The third cup, the cup of blessing, came
after the meal.  Today, we ask God’s
blessing on the food before we eat it; 2000 years ago, the Jews thanked God for
His blessing, both in providing the meal and in providing their freedom from
slavery, after eating it.  During the
Passover, they drank the cup of blessing in conjunction with that prayer of
thanks.

When we drink the cup of blessing today, then, it doesn’t
mean that the cup is conveying some blessing to us.  It means that we are remembering the blessing
we have already received, the blessing of forgiveness of sins through the blood
of Jesus.  We should humbly consider the
great sacrifice that has been offered on our behalf and rejoice that God loved
us enough to offer it.

Last of all, let’s consider **OUR RESPONSIBILITY** as Christians, our dedication in Christ.  Peter sums it up well in 1 Peter 3:9.  Because we have been blessed with every
spiritual blessing in the heavenly places, there’s a particular way that we’re
supposed to live.  As people who have
benefited so much from our Lord, we’re supposed to imitate Him.  Not for us are the thoughtless, hot-tempered,
vengeful ways of the world.  When we
people curse us, we don’t curse them back. 
Instead, we bless them, sincerely, in the hope that they will come to
know God’s blessing too.  “May the Lord
bless you” should be our prayer for everyone we encounter.

If we live in this way, Peter tells us that we will fulfill
our call and our purpose.  As we give
blessings, so we will inherit one.  This
blessing is the final and greatest of God’s kind actions toward us.  If we are faithful to Jesus, through Jesus we
will receive the gift of eternal life.

In just a few moments, we’re going to partake of the Lord’s
Supper.  As we do, let’s remember the great
blessing that we have received through Him. 
Let’s see His body and His blood in the bread and the cup of blessing
which we bless.  Finally, let’s consider
ourselves and ask whether we are living the lives of a blessed people.  Let’s think about all of these things as we
partake.
