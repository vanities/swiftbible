About a month ago, John Meyer and I were talking about our
proposed change to our order of services, and in the course of the discussion,
he suggested that I preach a sermon on the work of the church.  “That’s a great idea!” I replied, and so here
we are this morning.

For those who were raised by Christian parents as I was,
this topic might seem like old hat.  I
can’t even tell you how many sermons I heard growing up on Biblical authority
and the work of the church.  However,
even if we are familiar with the subject, we can’t let familiarity with it
obscure its importance.  Time and time
again, mankind has proven to have a genuine gift for apostasy.  Name a church past or present, and most likely,
it is drifting farther away from the Bible and from the will of Christ.  The only way for us to keep ourselves from
apostasy is to return again and again to the Bible and God’s plan for the
church, to make sure that we ourselves aren’t straying.  With this important goal in mind, let’s
consider Christ, the church, and its work.



**The Authority of
Christ**

Let’s begin this morning by considering the extent of the
authority of Christ.  The Scripture
presents this authority as **SUPREME.** Look here at Matthew 28:18.  This statement could not be stronger.  To rephrase the old saying, this is Christ’s
world, and we’re just living in it.  The
world has seen absolute monarchs and totalitarian dictators, but the authority
of Christ overshadows them all.  For
instance, in Hebrews 1, the Hebrews writer reports that Jesus upholds all
things by the word of His power.  In
other words, the universe continues to exist only because of the exercise of
the will of Jesus.  If He chose to stop
upholding it even for a moment, it would cease to exist.  Jesus would never do that because He doesn’t
want to violate the will of His Father, but He could.

When the authority of Jesus is that great, it necessarily
follows that every one of us puny humans is subject to Him too.  It’s true that God has given us free will so
that we can choose to obey Christ or not, but we must not mistake freedom to
sin for permission to sin.  Jesus doesn’t
vaporize us instantly as soon as we commit our first evil action—for which we
should all be grateful—but the day will come when He will judge the actions of
everyone, good or bad.

We’re always subject to the authority of Jesus, but we
become subject to it in a special sense when we enter **HIS KINGDOM.** Consider the
way that Paul puts things in Colossians 1:13. 
Here, as elsewhere in Scripture, we have to be careful to consider words
in their original sense, and not as we understand them today.  When the Bible speaks of the kingdom of
Christ, that’s not a kingdom like the kingdom of England.  It’s not about lines on a map.  It’s about personal authority.

When we become Christians, we don’t pledge our allegiance to
the church.  The church that Jesus
established is not an institution.  It’s
simply a collection of saved people. 
Instead, we pledge our allegiance to Jesus personally.  We promise Him personally that we are going
to obey Him, no matter what He asks of us. 
When we sin, and particularly when we fall away, we bring dishonor on
Him personally.  If ever we wonder about
the wrath of God and why He condemns the wicked to hell, I think we find the
answer here.  King Jesus can’t accept
unrepentant rebels into heaven and still be King.  If He did, He would make a mockery of His own
authority.

Because Jesus is King of each of us individually, He is also
**HEAD TO THE CHURCH.** Let’s read here from Ephesians
1:22-23.  Jesus exercises His authority
as King over Christians individually, and He continues to exercise that
authority when we assemble together.  The
metaphor that Paul employs here to define the relationship between Christ and
the church is a telling one.  He compares
Christ to a head and the church to that head’s body.

Note that Jesus is a real head, not a figurehead.  We don’t get to pay Him lip service and then
do whatever we want when we come together. 
Instead, Paul tells us that Christ is head in all things to the
church.  He controls the church with the
same degree of absolute authority with which we control our own bodies.  Even though we have various physical
limitations, it is still basically true that our bodies do what we tell them to
and only what we tell them to.  If I were
up here preaching a sermon, and my body decided that it wanted to walk out the
door and stroll down the street, I would be extremely alarmed!  That’s not how things are supposed to
work.  In the same way, when the church
gets ideas that Jesus didn’t give it, that’s not how things are supposed to
work either.

All of this might leave us wondering who Jesus thinks He
is.  What gives Him the right to boss us
around like that?  Here, we must remember
that Jesus is not merely the head but also the **SAVIOR OF THE BODY.** Let’s
read together from Ephesians 5:22-27. 
First of all, note that Paul here once again emphasizes the complete
subjection in all things of the church to Christ.  From this we can conclude that any group of
people that is not completely subject to Christ is not actually the true
church.  Second, though, let’s consider
the apostle’s testimony about what Christ has done for the church.  He loved her. 
He gave up His own life for her. 
He washed her clean.  He made her
beautiful.  When He has done so much for
her, how could the church possibly refuse Him?

Before the Lord’s Supper, let’s consider these things.  What is true of us collectively is true of us
individually.  Jesus loved each one of us
and died for each one of us to wash us clean from our sins.  We’re obliged to obey Him, but even more than
that, we should feel compelled to obey Him. 
He more than deserves everything we have to give.  Is that what we do?  Do we gratefully submit our lives in
everything to Him, or do we selfishly try to keep things back for ourselves,
even though He gave everything for us? 
Let’s remember His sacrifice and ponder this as we partake.

**The Work of the
Church**

Now that we’ve seen that Christ has authority to direct the
church, let’s see what the Scripture has to say about the work He assigns to
the church.  Basically, the church has
one core mission, and that is **SUPPORTING
THE TRUTH.** Consider 1 Timothy
3:15.  As we’ve already seen, the church
obeys Christ in all things, so of the many organizations out there that claim
to be churches belonging to Christ, we can determine which of those claims are
accurate by reference to the will of Christ expressed in Scripture.  If a group of people obeys Him, it can
legitimately claim to be a church belonging to Him.  If it does not, that claim is false.

This recognition has important consequences for the way we
read this verse.  Some people take the
passage and use it to argue that because they claim to be the church, whatever
they say is true.  Friends, that’s
exactly backwards.  It’s not the church
that identifies the truth.  It is the
truth that identifies the church.  After
all, even if there were not a single church on earth that taught and followed
the Bible, the Bible would still be true. 
It does not need a church to authenticate it.

This text, then, describes not the nature of the church, but
the mission of the church.  If we want to
be a church belonging to Jesus, our first, last, and only job is to proclaim
His will.  This work is so important that
literally nothing should be allowed to distract us from it.  There are all kinds of good works out there
that people might come together to do. 
However, worthwhile though they may be, none of them are as important as
the work of getting souls to heaven.  We
can’t engage in any of those things because they can only distract us from what
truly matters.

The first way that the church supports the truth is through **BUILDING UP THE BODY** and its members.  Here, look at Ephesians 4:15-16.  Here too, there are all sorts of avenues and
expedients that human wisdom might suggest. 
There are all kinds of churches out there that have Starbuckses and
bowling alleys and everything else you can imagine, presumably because they
think it’s beneficial for their members to have access to those things.  However, look again at what this passage
says.  There’s only one thing that the
body can use to build itself up, and that’s speaking the truth in love.  Here too, we see that everything gets back to
the gospel of Christ.

Everything that we do when we come together is dedicated to
this goal.  We build ourselves up in our
assemblies by following every command that the Lord gives us.  We sing together, we study His word, we pray,
we share in the Lord’s Supper, and we contribute to the Lord’s work.  If we had opportunity, we would appoint
elders and deacons here for the more effective upbuilding of the church.  If we are required to, we will withdraw from
unfaithful members to protect the church from contamination.  The primary work of the church is to use the
truth to encourage its members to follow Jesus.

Of course, the gospel of Christ is not only for the
saved.  It is for the lost too, so the
church can rightfully devote itself to **SHARING
THE GOSPEL** with outsiders.  For an
example of how this works, look at Philippians 4:15-16.  There’s a lot that we can glean from this
text.  First of all, note that this is
explicitly about the church in Philippi. 
Paul is not merely receiving funds from concerned Christians there; he’s
getting money from the church.

This reveals the existence of a second avenue for church
action.  “Church” is just another word
for “assembly” or “congregation”.  We
build one another up in the assembly. 
However, even when we aren’t actually assembled, our local congregation
can still continue to work through the money that we collect.  We use that money for things that make our
edification possible and effective—like a church building to meet in.  However, we also use it to provide support
for preachers of the gospel—me locally and other preachers in other
places.

Preachers are often engaged in edification, it’s true, but
we also spend time and effort on evangelism, just as preachers in the first century
did.  We have it on good authority that
Christ came to the world to save sinners, so obviously we should seize every
opportunity that presents itself to tell sinners the good news.  Every Christian should be involved in this
work, but the church itself shares in it through the resources that it shares
with evangelists.  I’m free to study the
Bible with somebody at 3:00 on a Tuesday afternoon in a way that those of you
with day jobs are not, but you participate in that work through the funds that
the church uses to support me.

Finally, we also see in Scripture the use of church funds in
**PROVIDING FOR NEEDY SAINTS.** Among other places, we see this occurring
in Romans 15:25-26.  When Paul says
“Macedonia and Achaia” here, he is of course referring to the churches in those
regions, like the church in Corinth.  It
was entirely right for those churches to send some of the funds they had
gathered to relieve the suffering of needy saints who belonged to the church in
Jerusalem.

Although it’s not immediately obvious, even this is
connected to the church’s role as the pillar and support of the truth.  Every one of us has a role in building one
another up, and if we’re hungry or homeless, we’re clearly not going to be very
good at participating in the work of the church!  Only when our physical needs are met can we
be spiritually useful.

**The Biblical Pattern**

During the final portion of our time together this morning,
we’re going to consider the Biblical pattern and the problems that arise from
breaking it.  Within this, the first
point that we need to consider is **THE
EVIDENCE FOR THE PATTERN.** Read with
me from 1 Corinthians 4:16-17.  This
tells us something critical about the first-century church.  It wasn’t some ad-hoc, cobbled-together
organization.  Paul didn’t go to the
church in Corinth and tell it to worship with instruments, and then go to the
church in Ephesus and tell the brethren there to sing a cappella.  Instead, everywhere that Paul went, he taught
the same thing in every church.

Here’s why this is important.  Nowhere in the Bible do we find a set of
church regulations in one spot.  We don’t
have any ancient orders of services. 
However, if Paul taught the same thing to everybody, everything he said
has to be part of the same overall plan. 
For instance, nowhere in the book of Galatians do we see Paul teaching
the Galatians that they ought to financially support gospel preachers.  However, we know from 1 Corinthians that Paul
did teach that gospel preachers ought to be supported.  Because Paul taught the same thing in every
church, we can conclude that at some point, he taught the Galatians about supporting
gospel preachers too.  We don’t know
everything that Paul, or Peter, or any of the others said to everybody.  However, through study of the whole New
Testament, we can figure out everything that God wants His people to know.

There is, then, a pattern. 
There is a distinctively New-Testament way of “doing church”.  However, why should we follow it?  Why should we limit our churches to only the
activities and methods that the Bible describes?  After all, very few churches even claim to
make the attempt.

Sadly, once we leave the New Testament behind, a number of
problems start emerging.  The first of
these we can group under the heading of **DISCIPLESHIP
PROBLEMS.** To illustrate what I mean,
let’s start by looking at Hebrews 12:2. 
The point here is quite clear. 
Christians are supposed to be hospitable people.  We’re supposed to invite strangers into our
homes.

However, let’s say that that’s not happening.  Let’s say that the Joliet church is an
inhospitable church.  There are a couple
of solutions that we might adopt.  The
first is the Ephesians 4 solution.  We
speak the truth to one another in love. 
We say, “Brethren, we need to be more hospitable!”  We stir one another up to love and good
works.

The other is to adopt an institutional solution to an
individual problem.  The brethren here
aren’t inviting others into their homes for meals, so we need to use church
funds to build a fellowship hall on our property here to take over the
hospitality function.  Even though we
don’t ever see church funds being used to provide meals for the non-needy, this
is commonly done.

However, it gives rise to two problems.  The first is that it probably isn’t going to
work.  No institution can ever provide a
substitute for the love of an actual human being.  The second, though, is that it allows the
Christians of Joliet to go on being crummy disciples.  We still aren’t hospitable, but we can salve
our consciences by pointing to that big new fellowship hall.  We can be satisfied that the church has taken
over hospitality for us, so that we never learn to be more like Jesus.

This is a common problem, and it doesn’t only emerge in the
area of hospitality.  It shows up in
caring for the world’s poor, in improving our worship, and in a dozen other
areas besides.  The point is plain.  If we have a discipleship problem in this
church, we need to solve it by becoming better disciples, not by turning to the
church so that it will do our work for us.

Second, and even more importantly, departure from the
pattern quickly produces **OBEDIENCE
PROBLEMS.** Our starting point here is
2 Corinthians 9:6-7.  This will
illustrate, I think, the problem with a common objection to teaching on Bible
authority.  I’ve often heard people say, “Yeah,
I get why we can’t do what God forbids, and why we have to do what He says, but
I don’t see why we can’t take His silence as permission.”  That’s a nice theory, but in practice, it
doesn’t work, and this text shows why. 
This too is a pretty plain passage. 
When it comes to giving to the Lord’s work, how much we give is up to
us.  There is no set dollar or percentage
amount.

However, I’m not aware of a single denominational church
that follows the New Testament pattern on this point.  Rather than following the law of Christ, they
go all the way back to the Law of Moses. 
Even though they aren’t Israelites, they take Moses’ commandment to the
Israelites on tithing and use it to demand that their members give 10 percent
too.  They’ll preach all the time on
Malachi 3:10, which is another Old-Testament passage on tithing, but 2
Corinthians 9 is absent.

I don’t think the denominations do this because they’re
greedy, although I suppose they may be. 
Instead, I think they demand tithing from their members because they
have to.  The first-century pattern of
giving is adequate to support the works of the first-century church, but it’s
not enough to support all those human institutions that the denominations add
on.  Somebody has to pay for all those
Starbuckses and bowling alleys!  Because
they took the silence of the Scripture as permission to build all those things,
they have to violate the Scripture in order to maintain them.
