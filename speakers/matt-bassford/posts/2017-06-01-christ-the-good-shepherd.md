There are many ways that our society is off the tracks when
it comes to religion, and one of the most subtle yet important of these is its
habit of re-creating God in its own image. 
The people around us have all of these ideas about what they think are
good, so they project those ideas onto God and then argue on the basis of their
invented God that what they’re doing is good and right.  We saw this a few weeks back when we
considered those who make arguments like “God just wants me to be happy” and
“God is a God of love” in order to justify their favorite sins.

The only way for us to keep from falling into the same trap
is to turn to the word and develop our conception of God from what He says
about Himself.  That way, the conclusions
we draw will be reliable instead of being nothing more than a projection of our
own desires.  This morning, then, let’s
consider John 10 and Jesus’ self-description as the good shepherd.



**The Parable of the
Shepherd**

This context begins with the parable of the shepherd.  Jesus introduces it by talking about **THE VOICE OF THE SHEPHERD.** This discussion appears in John
10:1-5.  Let’s start our consideration of
this by looking at the context.  There
doesn’t appear to be any break between John 9 and John 10 other than the
chapter number, so we ought to read all of this as following right on the heels
of the story of the man born blind whom Jesus healed.

If you’ll recall, one of the big themes of that story is
authority.  The Pharisees insist that
Jesus can’t possibly be from God because He healed on the Sabbath.  To this, the formerly blind man retorts that
Jesus is obviously from God because He gave him sight, and nobody else but God
could do that.  As a result, the
Pharisees put him out of the synagogue.

These two attitudes toward Jesus could not be more
different, and I think we should read Jesus’ reply as an attempt to explain
them.  The Pharisees are the thieves and
the robbers who have climbed over the wall. 
They are among the sheep, but they don’t have a right to be there, and
the sheep don’t recognize them.

Jesus, by contrast, is the shepherd.  He’s among the sheep legitimately because the
doorkeeper let Him in.  He has the
authority and right to be there.  His
sheep have no interest in following anybody else, but they hear His voice and
will eagerly follow Him.

This is important, brethren. 
It tells us that those who belong to Jesus are also those who obey and
follow Him.  There are plenty of people
who want to break that connection.  They
insist that they belong to Jesus, and it’s all Jesus this and Jesus that with
them, but they don’t go to a church that honors Him, and they don’t keep His
commandments in their personal lives either.

How about us?  Let’s
say that somebody set a drone to following us for an entire week, 24-7.  The drone is with us when we’re in the
assembly, but it’s also with us on the job and when we’re by ourselves at home,
and the whole time, it’s recording us. 
Would somebody who watched the recording conclude that we belong to
Jesus, or would they say that we belong to ourselves?

Next, Jesus describes Himself as **THE DOOR OF THE SHEEP.** We
read about this in John 10:6-10.  This
starts off, unsurprisingly, with Jesus’ audience being confused about the first
part of the parable.  At the end of John
9, He was talking about blindness, and now He’s shifted to agriculture!  He sees that He’s lost His audience, but His
solution to the problem is to describe Himself as the door of the sheep, which
as explanations go, quickly loses us!

In order to understand this, we have to put ourselves in the
position of His audience 2000 years ago. 
They knew much more about the shepherding business than we do, and they
would have been very familiar with sheepfolds like the one pictured above.  Looking at it, we might be confused about how
this would keep sheep safe without a door. 
What we don’t understand and what the Jews would have is that the shepherd
*was* the door.  After he had led the sheep into the fold, he
would lie down in the entrance to keep the sheep in and anything else out.  In the morning, he would only let the sheep
out once he had checked that the area was safe. 
Thieves and robbers, by contrast, had no interest in the safety of the
sheep.  In fact, they themselves were the
danger!

In this text, I want us to pay particular attention to the
claims that Jesus makes.  He tells us
that His work for us is to lead us to pasture and give us abundant life.  In other words, He’s constantly working for
our good.

I’m sure all of us would agree that yes, this is what Jesus
does for us, but I’m not at all sure that all of us actually believe that
following Jesus is the path to the abundant life.  For instance, Jesus and the Holy Spirit teach
that we will benefit by assembling with other Christians as much as possible,
but an awful lot of brethren here act like the path to the abundant life on
Wednesday nights leads not to the church building, but to the recliner.  In this as in all things, we show our trust
in Jesus not by amenning what He says, but by getting out there and doing it.

Finally, the Lord describes Himself as **THE GOOD SHEPHERD.** Here,
consider John 10:11-18.  Before, the
other characters in the parable were thieves and robbers, but this section
introduces us to the hired hand.  The
hired hand is OK as long as nothing’s happening, but as soon as he sees a wolf
coming, he quickly decides that he isn’t being paid enough for this and flees
for his life.  The good shepherd, by
contrast, sees the wolf coming and dies to protect the sheep.

Jesus is very plain that this is what He is going to
do.  As the good shepherd lays down his
life for the sheep, so Jesus is going to lay down His life, and He’s going to
do it for several reasons.  First, unlike
the hired man who doesn’t care about the sheep, Jesus does.  He knows them, and they know Him.  He’s willing to die so that they can
live.

Second, Jesus is willing to lay down His life in order to
bring other sheep that aren’t of this fold into His flock.  I’m sure that none of His listeners then had
a clue what He was talking about, but with the benefit of hindsight, we easily
understand that He’s talking about the Gentiles.  Even while He was still alive, He planned for
His gospel to reach the whole world.

Third, Jesus is going to lay down His life in order to
please His Father.  God loves Him for the
greatness of His self-sacrificing heart. 
Indeed, the Father loves Jesus so much that He has given Him authority
to take up His life again after He has laid it down.

We’ve been talking all through this about the importance of
hearing and following Jesus, but here, we see how far the Lord was willing to
go even though we didn’t hear and follow. 
In the words of Isaiah 53, all of us like sheep have gone astray.  Every one of us has turned to his own
way.  We weren’t innocently eating grass
in the pasture.  We were bumbling around
in the wilderness when the wolf caught us, and yet Jesus still interposed Himself
between us and danger at the cost of His own life.  We were foolish and evil, but He paid the
ultimate price.

**“Are You the Christ?”**

The discussion in the next part of the text centers around
the question, “Are you the Christ?” 
John’s account of this begins with describing **JEWISH REACTIONS** to all that Jesus has said.  Read with me from John 10:19-24.  It’s obvious from this account that Jesus is,
to say the least, a polarizing figure. 
We’re used to the idea that Jesus rose from the dead.  However, before He actually did it, His claim
that He was going to rise struck many of His hearers as proof that He had been
possessed by a demon.  I can see their
point.  After all, if somebody went
around today claiming that he had the authority in himself to rise from the
dead, wouldn’t we think he was crazy?

However, as the other faction of the Jews points out, that’s
not the only relevant piece of information. 
First of all, Jesus is a whole lot more coherent than demon-possessed
people usually were.  Second, it’s not
like Jesus is making these claims without evidence.  Instead, He has established that He is from
God by giving sight to the blind.

At this point, we see additional information about the time
when all this is taking place.  We know
from John 9 that the story of the blind man takes place in Jerusalem—it’s where
the pool of Siloam was—and now we learn that this is all happening about the
time of the Feast of the Dedication. 
Today, we typically call this Jewish festival Hanukkah, and John’s
description of all of this taking place in the winter jibes with the modern
celebration of Hanukkah in early December.

Regardless, there’s this festival going on, Jesus is out in
public, and all these arguing factions of Jews descend on Him and demand that
He tell them whether He is the Christ or not. 
This is a big deal.  Today,
“Christ” has sort of become Jesus’ last name, but back then, it meant “Anointed
One”, particularly someone who had been anointed as king.  As a result, the question that the Jews are
asking is as much a political question as it is a religious question, and if
Jesus replies in the affirmative, He will certainly bring the Romans down on
Himself.

All this helps us to understand **JESUS’ REPLY**, which is found in John 10:25-30.  As usual, He doesn’t answer the question
directly.  He tells the Jews first of all
that He has told them already.  This is,
to say the least, a strange claim for Him to make, especially given that He
does not publicly declare Himself the Christ until literally the last day of
His life, when He is on trial.

Obviously, Jesus isn’t trying to lie here to throw the Jews
off the scent.  Instead, His point is
that He has already told the Jews all they need to know about Him.  If He’s identified Himself as the bread of
life and light of the world, it’s pretty obvious who He is, regardless of
whether He adopts a politically charged title or not.  His sheep have recognized this, heard, and
followed.  The Jews who are still fussing
over details have proven by this that they don’t belong to His sheep.

Next, Jesus makes a new claim about His sheep that has
proven theologically significant.  He
reveals that because of His unity with the Father, no one is able to snatch His
sheep out of His hand.  Not surprisingly,
this has become a favorite proof text for Calvinists.  They argue that because no one can snatch
Jesus’ sheep out of His hand, it’s impossible for a Christian to fall away.

Contextually, though, there are problems with this
logic.  Remember, these same
un-snatchable sheep are also those who hear the voice of Jesus and follow
Him.  By definition, the Christian who
falls away is no longer listening to Jesus and is no longer following Him, so
this text simply doesn’t apply to them.

Now, I certainly believe that this is a text that should
offer all of us tremendous comfort.  It
tells us that through all the trials and temptations of our lives, if we are
looking to hold on to Jesus, He isn’t going to let us go.  He’s our Friend, not our prosecutor!  However, there’s a big “if” in that previous
statement.  It only applies as long as we
are seeking Him.  If we decide to turn
away, there’s nothing in the Bible that implies that Jesus will force us back.

**Claims of Deity**

The final part of our text this morning concerns Jesus’
claims of deity.Not surprisingly in a Jewish context,
this is introduced with **A THREAT OF
STONING.** Look at John 10:31-38.  If you’ll recall the end of our last section,
Jesus there declared that He and the Father were one.  This gets the Jews in a killing mood almost
immediately.  Jesus sees what they’re
about to do and asks them, provocatively, which of the good works He’s done is
the reason why they want to kill Him.

Not surprisingly, His audience misses the point.  They dismiss the good works and get right to
what matters to them:  the fact that
Jesus claims to be God, which they think is impossible and blasphemous.  The Lord answers this by exposing the
inconsistency of their behavior..  The
Law of Moses itself calls not just Jesus but everybody gods, and the Jews are
OK with that, but when Jesus, whom God sent into the world, claims to be God,
they get murderous.

From there, Jesus diverts His attention back to where He
wants it:  the works that He has
done.  He urges the Jews to assess
them.  If Jesus is a fraud and hasn’t
done the works of God at all, then they should reject Him.  If, however, His works show that He is from
God, then His hearers need to believe Him, even when He is making challenging
claims about His relationship with God.

Even today, it is critically important for us to base our
faith not on what we think we already know, but on the evidence.  This, after all, is the mistake that the Jews
made.  They had the evidence of the works
of Jesus right in front of them, but they knew—*knew!*—that He couldn’t be the Son of God, and in the end, their
conviction won out over what Jesus had shown to be true.

We don’t have the evidence of Jesus’ miracles before us, but
we do have the evidence of the word, and we must be open to that evidence,
rather than blindly going along with what we’ve always believed.  Let me give you a personal example.  All through my childhood and young adulthood,
I’d been associated with churches that offered a second serving of the Lord’s
Supper.  To be frank, I didn’t give it
much thought and assumed that it was OK because it was what I’d always seen
done.

Then, about six or seven years ago, I heard Al Diestelkamp give
a presentation in which he explained his opposition to the second serving.  He pointed to the repeated use of the word
“together” in reference to the Lord’s Supper in 1 Corinthians 11 and argued
that any partaking of the Lord’s Supper that isn’t together isn’t according to
the pattern.  After he was done, I went
up to him, shook his hand, and said, “You’ve convinced me, brother.”  To this day, I can no longer conscientiously participate
in the second serving because of the evidence.

It is this, I think, that must define our attitude toward
every aspect of our worship and service to God. 
What we’ve always done counts for nothing.  What the Bible says counts for everything.

Finally, let’s look at **THE
RESULTS** of Jesus’ provocative words. 
John explains in John 10:39-42. 
As this text tells us, some of Jesus’ hearers became enraged and sought
unsuccessfully to arrest Him.  As a
result of this, He went off to the wilderness across the Jordan, well out of
reach of the Jerusalem authorities. 
There, though, many came to Him and believed in Him.

Metaphorically, every one of us is doing one of these two
things too.  Either we are driving Jesus
out of our lives, or we are seeking Him to invite Him in.  This is true even of Christians.  There are plenty of people who come to church
on Sundays who spend the rest of the week keeping Jesus out of their
business.  They live lives that are
filled with materialism, selfishness, and secret sin.  I’m sure the Jews in Jerusalem were pleased
to get rid of Jesus too, but in the end, it cost them their salvation.  We need to make sure that we aren’t making
the same mistake.
