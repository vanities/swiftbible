Sometimes, we fall into the trap of assuming that all
children come off the divine assembly line exactly the same.  To this way of thinking, they are little
blank slates, little empty vessels waiting to be filled with whatever their
parents choose.  Thus, this line of
reasoning triumphantly concludes, whenever a child fails to meet some standard
of behavior, it’s the parents’ fault.  If
only Mom and Dad were willing to train up that child in the way in which he
should go, everything would be fine.  Too
bad those parents are morally deficient!



Now, certainly, proper parenting is critically important to
a child’s development, but the simplistic analysis above overlooks one key
factor:  kids are different.  Some are amiable and biddable; others come
into this world determined to do what they wanna do.  I see this difference every time I’m around
my sister and her family.  My nephew,
Luke, simply isn’t a boy with much interest in coloring outside the lines.  He will often mend his ways after a murmured
admonition from one of his parents.  My
children, on the other hand, have been known to steadfastly disobey even in the
face of threats of nuclear annihilation!

I get that.  I am,
after all, a strong-willed adult.  I have
a high degree of internal motivation, I love to work independently, and I hate
being forced into a routine I haven’t chosen for myself.

I was a strong-willed child too.  My childhood was one long battle.  At times, I skirmished with my mother, who
was no weak reed herself.  She constantly
strove to schedule and order my life (“Piano practice at 6; drum practice at
6:30; homework at 7”), I constantly strove to overthrow her schedules, and I
generally succeeded.

Mostly, though, I fought with my teachers.  I was the student that teachers pray they
don’t get:  precocious, talkative, loud,
and utterly impervious to correction. 
One day, my sixth-grade history teacher got so sick of me talking out in
class that she asked me if I wanted to teach in her place, handed me the chalk,
and sat down at her desk.   That didn’t work out quite the way she
intended.  I missed the sarcasm and
happily lectured the other students on the history of the Assyrian Empire until
she told me to stop five minutes later. 
My seventh-grade English teacher once sent me to the principal’s office
every day for a week.

When I was interested in the material, I was engaged and
committed.  When I was bored (which I
usually was), I was a holy terror.  My
parents and teachers tried everything short of child abuse to get me to
behave.  I was, at one point, grounded
for a year.  Nothing worked.

Surely, with a childhood like that, I was going to be dead
or in prison by the time I turned 30, right? 
That’s not quite how things happened. 
Instead, today I am a committed Christian, a devoted husband and father,
and a productive member of society.  I
ended up becoming everything that my parents wanted me to be.

So. . . what happened? 
Perhaps more to the point, what can the parents of strong-willed children
do to make sure that their holy terrors end up committed, faithful, and
productive rather than dead or in prison?

**Remember that a
strong will is a character trait, not a character flaw.** The problem is, in children, it looks
like a character flaw.  Here, you have
this kid who is determined to do his own thing, no matter what his parents or
teachers have to say.  He compares poorly
to his sibling/cousin/classmate who is quiet, obedient, and easily led.  Strong-Willed Kid sure looks like a goat, not
a sheep.

It is certainly true that the greatest villains of Scripture
were strong-willed.  Pharaoh, Jezebel,
Athaliah, and Caiaphas all had willpower to burn.  However, it is equally true that the greatest
*heroes* of Scripture were
strong-willed too.  How about Moses?  How about Ruth?  How about Elijah?  How about Paul?

For that matter, how about the Lord Himself?  Jesus had a truly indomitable will.  Otherwise, He never could have kept Himself
sinless and submitted Himself to God to the point of death, even death on a
cross.  Jesus didn’t do that because
somebody else made Him do it.  He did it
because He chose to.

Strikingly, the only story we have about Jesus growing up,
the story of the boy Jesus teaching in the temple in Luke 2:41-52, is a story
about a strong-willed, independent child. 
If we allow ourselves to forget that this is a story about the Son of
God and merely read it as a story about a determined 12-year-old who goes off
to do his own thing for four days (in the big city, no less!) without telling
anybody, a lot of us will start saying to ourselves, “I could totally see my
kid doing that.”  Jesus simply would not
have been the man He was unless He had first been a child who could and did act
that way sometimes.

Even today, I am quite confident that the church of the
future will have great need of strong-willed men and strong-willed women.  Otherwise, how can God’s people possibly
resist being destroyed by an increasingly evil society?  A strong will that is strong for the Lord is
a blessing, not a flaw.  It is the
responsibility of the parent not to crush that strong will, but to aim it in
the right direction.
