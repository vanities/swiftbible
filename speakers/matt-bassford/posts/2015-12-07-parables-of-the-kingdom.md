It almost goes without saying that if we want to call
ourselves Christians, we ought to be following Christ.  However, this obvious no-brainer, once we
apply it to the wider religious world, somehow becomes a brainer.  There are plenty of self-identified Christians
out there who also manage to be blissfully ignorant of the actual teachings of
Jesus.

Rather than puffing ourselves up because of them, though, we
need to take warning from their example. 
The sign out front that says “church of Christ” doesn’t mean that all of
us in here are doing what He wants us to do. 
We can only establish that we belong to Him by living for Him, by
learning His will for us and putting it into practice.

With this in mind, let’s turn our attention to a section of
Scripture where Jesus spells out what the behavior and the goals of His
disciples are supposed to be.  Much of
what He says has a particular first-century application, but it applies to us
too in a larger sense.  This morning,
then, let’s consider the teachings of our Lord in the parables of the kingdom.

 **The Parable of the
Sower**

Our text this morning will be the thirteenth chapter of
Matthew, and it begins with the parable of the sower.  We see **THE
PARABLE ITSELF** introduced in Matthew 13:1-9.  The parable of the sower is found in all
three Synoptic gospels, but only in Matthew is it the beginning of a lengthy
discourse about the kingdom.  I see at
least two possible explanations for this. 
It could be that Mark and Luke are recounting different events, or that Matthew
is simply taking advantage of this opportunity to thematically collect the
parables of the kingdom together.

The outline of the parable itself is probably familiar to
most of us:  one sower, the same seed, and
four different kinds of soils, only one of which bears a worthwhile crop.  To Jesus’ listeners, this would have been
both familiar and opaque.  The scene of a
farmer broadcasting seed happened every year all over Galilee, but few of
Jesus’ listeners would have understood that this simple story had a spiritual
point.

In fact, this is why the few words in v. 9 are among the
most significant in Jesus’ entire speech. 
His message isn’t for everybody. 
He’s not putting things plainly so that every schmoe in the multitude
can understand it.  Instead, He is
concerned with those who have an ear to hear, those who are already concerned
with spiritual things, those who are determined enough to tease out the meaning
of a parable that is difficult to understand.

The same thing remains true today.  There’s a sense in which the gospel is for
all, but there’s also a sense in which it is only for those who have ears to
hear.  Being here doesn’t ensure that
we’re in that group.  Only our
determination to find the truth can put us in it.

From here, Jesus moves on to discuss His **SPEAKING IN PARABLES** generally.  Read with me from Matthew 13:10-17.  As this text shows, it’s not only obvious to
us that Jesus isn’t being as clear as He could be.  It’s obvious to the disciples too.  Naturally, they want to know why.  Why is the Teacher teaching in a way that
makes it more difficult for His hearers to learn?

Jesus’ response is a harsh indictment of all those who
haven’t come to Him seeking answers.  His
truth isn’t for the multitudes.  It’s for
His disciples only.  Like we forget
factoids we don’t care about, in time, the multitudes are going to lose even
the little glimmers of understanding they had. 
They heard, but they don’t understand, and they don’t care enough to
understand.

Jesus then moves on to point out that there is nothing new
about this.  God said the same thing to
Isaiah when he began his prophetic ministry. 
The people of Judah in Isaiah’s day weren’t interested enough in God’s
word to avert disaster, and in their heedlessness, they provided a type for the
equally uninterested Jews of Jesus’ day. 
When God’s word is brought to people like this, it cannot save.  They won’t let it.  Instead, it can only condemn.  The very fact that they had the opportunity
to be enlightened and turned it down is the final confirmation of their
guilt.  The disciples, by contrast, will
hear even more than Isaiah and the other prophets did.

We need to remember that the gospel is a two-edged sword for
us too.  It can still save.  It can still enlighten.  However, once we hear the truth, there’s no
going back from that.  If we are
disobedient, we can no longer plead ignorance, and we will be punished
accordingly.

Jesus concludes this section by **EXPLAINING THE PARABLE.** Here,
consider Matthew 13:18-23. 
Interestingly, His words here reveal that this is a parable about
parables.  Farmers all over Galilee may
have been acting out the parable literally, but the multitudes and His
disciples are acting it out spiritually. 
Once again, the message is that the gospel will do no good for most
people.  Some hear the gospel and it just
bounces off.  Some hear it, are really
excited about serving God for a little bit, then drift back into
worldliness.  Others hear it and become
disciples, but never really accomplish anything as disciples, preferring
instead to drift around in the gray area between the kingdom and the
world.  This is a literally fruitless
endeavor.  Finally, though, some hear it,
take it to heart, and transform their lives accordingly, bearing the fruit that
God wants.

This should remind us, friends, that there are only two
kinds of Christians.  Either we are
fruitful disciples, or we are failed disciples. 
Even the thorny-ground folks, who don’t quite fall away, don’t end up
pleasing God.  I don’t plant tomatoes in
my garden because I think they look pretty. 
I plant them because I want them to produce tomatoes, and if they don’t,
I consider them failures, no matter how pretty they look.  As with tomatoes, so with Christians.  What matters with us, friends, is not that we
show up and sit in a pew.  What matters
is that we glorify God with every day of our lives.  Only then are we truly accomplishing the will
of Jesus.

Before the Lord’s Supper, let’s reflect on all this.  In a similarly agricultural discussion, the
discussion of the grain of wheat that falls to earth in John 12, Jesus reveals
that He came to earth to die.  However,
that death wasn’t the purpose of His coming. 
Instead, He died so that His death would bear much fruit, a harvest of
souls dedicated to Him.

We’re gathered here on the first day of the week, as we
always are, in order to remember and honor Jesus.  However, there’s a sense in which the most
significant way that we remember Jesus is by living according to His word.  If we eat our crackers and grape juice here
on Sunday and live unrighteously the rest of the week, we aren’t honoring Him
at all.  Let’s reflect, then, on our
lives.  Let’s ask ourselves whether we
are bearing the kind of fruit His sacrifice demands.

**The Parable of the
Tares**

The second main discourse of the parables of the kingdom
centers around the parable of the tares. 
Jesus relates His story about **TARES
AND WHEAT** in Matthew 13:24-30.  The
first thing to note about this one is that this time, Jesus’ language makes
clear from the beginning that He isn’t merely talking about agriculture.  Instead, He gives meaning to the parable by
saying that it is like the kingdom of heaven.

Whenever we see the word “kingdom”, I suspect there’s a part
in many of our brains that automatically wants to sub in the word “church” for
it.  However, it simply doesn’t make
sense to reduce this parable to being about the church.  This event takes place in the middle of
Jesus’ ministry.  This is years before
the establishment of the church on the day of Pentecost following Jesus’
resurrection from the dead.  He is
talking to people who do not belong to the church.  In fact, at this point, they have no notion
that Christ is going to establish a church. 
Even Jesus’ closest followers during His ministry believed that He was
going to establish an earthly kingdom. 
It makes no sense for Jesus to be talking at this time about something
so alien to His hearers.

Instead, it makes much more sense for us to understand
“kingdom” here in the much broader sense of “God’s authority and
dominion”.  When Jesus in the Lord’s
Prayer says, “Your kingdom come; Your will be done on earth as it is in
heaven,” he’s praying the same thing two different ways.

This parable, then, is about the time when God would carry
out His will on earth as He already did in heaven.  Even before the explanation, Jesus’ listeners
would have understood it as being about God coming in judgment.  However, God isn’t coming in judgment against
a field that belongs to somebody else. 
Instead, He’s coming in judgment against a field that belongs to Him,
that through the action of an enemy has produced a crop He didn’t want.  On its face, this is an ominous beginning,
and we’ll wait for Jesus’ explanation to learn more.

Before Jesus reaches the explanation, though, He holds forth
about **SEED AND LEAVEN**.  Here, let’s read from Matthew 13:31-35.  On their face, the two parables seem to be
inconsistent.  In the first, the kingdom
starts out small and inconspicuous, but becomes large and obvious.  In the second, even though the kingdom is at
work, it can’t be seen directly, only observed by the effects that it has on
other things.

However, these two kinds of growth in the kingdom aren’t
contradictory, but complementary. 
Sometimes God’s dominion increases in ways that are obvious to us all,
but sometimes, we only know the growth by its indirect effects.  For instance, whenever somebody obeys the
gospel and is added to the church, the kingdom grows by one.  One soul has submitted itself to the
authority of God.

Even in a situation, though, where a church hasn’t seen any
baptisms for a while, God’s kingdom may still be increasing.  If His word is working on the hearts of the
faithful so that they devote more and more of their lives to Him, the kingdom is
growing there too.

We must be careful, though, not to assume that the kingdom
is growing here or anywhere else because people are showing up and listening to
sermons.  Let me explain.  Unlike most men, I’ve actually made bread from
scratch before.  My mother considered it
an essential part of my education.  As is
true for most bakers, there were times when I didn’t get the water hot enough
for the yeast or whatever, and the bread didn’t rise.  That wasn’t a success simply because I added
leaven.  It was a failure because the
leaven had no effect on the bread. 
Likewise, if the word does not continue to change our lives, the kingdom
isn’t growing in us either.

Finally, we see here another illustration of Jesus’ use of
parables as sorting methods.  He’s not
revealing everything that we know about the kingdom of God today.  Instead, He’s offering up a verbal appetizer to
tantalize the taste buds of those who have an interest in spiritual things.

In the last part of this section, we come to **AN EXPLANATION OF THE TARES.** Here, consider Matthew 13:36-43.  First of all, note that where we were
expecting to see God as the farmer, instead, we encounter the Son of Man—Jesus
Himself.  This is important.  If the field is the world, and the field
belongs to the farmer, than the world belongs to Jesus.  We weren’t wrong in saying that the parable
is about God.  Instead, Jesus is using
the parable to imply that He is God.

Second, we see that this is indeed a parable about judgment,
about the people of the world and the people of the kingdom all mixed up
together, who will only be sorted out at the coming of the Son of Man.  As always, we need to be careful not to leap
to the conclusion that this is specifically about the final judgment.  All the way through the Old Testament, God
describes His various judgments against Israel and Judah as those rebellious
nations being burned with fire.  I think
the specific application here is similar. 
Jesus is again predicting the destruction of Jerusalem about 40 years in
the future, at the end of the age, which will reveal the true people of God.

However, we need to remember that this applies generally to
us.  God always allows the wicked and the
righteous to flourish together until He comes in judgment.  So too for us.  If we’re determined to be wicked, He’s not
going to follow us around zapping us with tiny lightning bolts.  He will let us go our own way and suffer the
consequences of our actions.  If we
continue in wickedness until the end of our lives, even if we succeeded in
fooling everybody the whole time, we won’t be fooling anybody on the Day of
Judgment.

**Good and Bad Examples**

Matthew concludes this passage on parables with a good and a
bad example for how we might react to the words of Jesus.  This section begins with **THE GOOD EXAMPLE.** Here,
ponder the events of Matthew 13:44-52. 
He starts this off with a trio of rapid-fire parables.  The kingdom of heaven is like a
treasure.  It’s like a pearl.  In either case, it’s so valuable that it’s
worth giving up everything you have in order to get it.  The next parable explains this.  The kingdom of heaven is so valuable because
it’s binary.  Either you are in the
kingdom or you aren’t, and if you aren’t, only torment awaits you.

We need to remember this whenever the devil tempts us to
compromise with sin, to continue on with our old comfortable worldly way of
life because seeking the kingdom first is too hard.  It reminds me of the way I studied for the bar
exam when I graduated from law school.  Like the Day of Judgment, the bar is
pass-fail.  Either you get a license to
practice law, or you don’t.  However, I
didn’t treat that pass-fail as an incentive to slack, to try to skate through
the exam with the minimum amount of effort. 
Instead, I slaved for three months. 
The consequences of failure were so dire that I did everything I could
to ensure that I would succeed.  That,
friends, is how we should treat our discipleship.  It’s about something too important for us to
take lightly.

Before we leave this section, though, let’s notice the
disciples’ reaction.  This time, they
don’t ask for an explanation.  They
understand on their own.  They’ve
progressed enough under Jesus’ teaching that they don’t need training wheels
anymore.  The same thing should happen to
us, so that we reach the point where we are equipped to be teachers too.  If we get there, like the head of the
household in Jesus’ story, we will be able to use our understanding to bless
others.

Finally, let’s consider **THE
BAD EXAMPLE**, which is supplied when Jesus returns to Nazareth.  Matthew 13:53-58 tells the tale.  The people there are so caught up in
remembering Him and knowing His family that they don’t listen to what He says
or even offer themselves for healing. 
They just can’t believe that somebody who grew up in Nazareth is a
prophet of God.

For some of us, at least, this is a familiar
experience.  Whenever we try to talk
religion to our family or close friends, it seems like they give us less
attention than they would anybody else. 
They know us so well that they can’t get past the us to focus on our
message.  As annoying as this is, I
suppose it’s reassuring to note that even Jesus had the same problem.

Second, in a more general sense, this should alert us to the
problem of rejecting Jesus because of our baggage instead of His message.  Sometimes we refuse to accept the truth because
we’d rather cling to what we had heard before, or because it will require us to
make changes, or because it will alienate us from people we’d rather be close
to.  Brethren, we need to stop worrying
less about the baggage and more about the truth.  Whatever keeps us from hearing and obeying
the truth is a problem, and we need to jettison it from our lives.
