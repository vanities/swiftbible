Last Sunday, a friend of mine linked to an article entitled
“What Not to Say to Someone Who Has Been Hurt by Church”.  Normally, I’m not a fan of what-not-to-say
articles.  They insist that all people
who are in a particular situation should be dealt with in the same way, and
they encourage interaction based on fear rather than on love.  I think it’s much better to lean on
understanding and compassion than on following a list of rules.  My reaction to this article was much the
same.  I came away from it with a lot of
“It depends.”

However, the author of the article did use a phrase that
resonated with me.  He described
circumstances in his past as “spiritual abuse”. 
I don’t think that everyone who has been church-hurt has suffered
spiritual abuse.  I’ve encountered (as
any church leader has) Christians who were determined to be offended.  I feel sorry for people who go through life
with that mindset, but I don’t feel any particular guilt that they found in my
conduct some reason for offense.  If you
can’t make somebody happy, ain’t no point in trying!

Church hurt and spiritual abuse aren’t the same thing, but I
believe that there is such a thing as spiritual abuse.  The author of the article I mentioned didn’t
define what spiritual abuse looks like, but I think it’s spiritually useful for
us to attempt a definition.  I think
spiritual abuse involves at least these elements:

**A spiritual leader
misusing his position.** In order for
spiritual abuse to be explicitly spiritual, as opposed to garden-variety
physical or verbal abuse, I think it has to have some distinctly religious
element.  If you’ve got a mean “friend”
at church who is always insulting and undermining you, that’s no different from
a similar relationship at work or in your free time.  It’s still evil, but it’s not
distinctive.  However, the dynamic
changes when a church leader (in my religious tradition, this means preachers,
elders, and deacons) uses his religious authority in an abusive way.  That works in different ways and needs a
different label.

**A pattern of abusive
behavior.** I think there’s a sliding
scale here.  The less intense a
particular abusive behavior is, the more it must be repeated in order to rise
to the level of abuse.  For instance, if
a church leader uses his position to intimidate a church member into having sex
with him once, that one incident is enough to qualify as abuse.  However, one thoughtless, even wounding
remark, if it occurs in isolation and not as part of a pattern of similar remarks,
doesn’t rise to the level of abuse.

**One of the following
behaviors, or a behavior similar to them:**

**Sexual misconduct.**  It is unbelievably tawdry and evil for a
church leader to become sexually involved in any way with one of the members of
his church.  Adultery, fornication,
sexual impurity, and filthy speech/sexual harassment are all unacceptable.

Of course, those things are sinful no matter who practices
them, but they’re particularly unpalatable in a church leader.  There is always a power imbalance, and often
an implied threat, when he makes sexual overtures to an ordinary Christian,
particularly when that Christian is a woman. 
He counts; she doesn’t, so he gets to do what he wants with her.  If she repeats the story, who’s going to
believe her?

It is true that sometimes the church leader is the pursued
rather than the pursuer, but even in those circumstances, his religious
position is never far out of sight.  As
James observes in James 3:1, men in authority are held to a higher
standard.  For such men to misuse their
authority as they defile their souls is doubly inexcusable.

**Financial
exploitation.** This occurs when a
religious leader uses shame, guilt, and intimidation to extract money from a
church member, whether that money goes to him personally or to the church
treasury.  Paul emphasizes in 2
Corinthians 9 that all such contributions are to be purposeful and
cheerful.  It’s up to each member to
decide how much he or she wants to give, not to the church leader.  A church leader does not have the right to
coerce Sister Jane into coughing up a couple grand to help with the new roof,
either because he knows that Sister Jane is loaded or because he knows that
Sister Jane has a soft heart and will give him the money whether or not she can
afford to.  All such appeals must leave
room for church members to say “No” and feel completely comfortable in doing
so.

**Isolation.** It is true that evil companions corrupt
good morals.  It is equally true, however,
that even though we are not of the world, God does not take us out of the
world.  Christians can and indeed should
have relationships with non-Christian family members, co-workers, and even
friends.  How can we let our light shine
if we hide it under the bushel basket of the church?

It’s problematic, then, for church leaders to discourage relationships
with non-Christians.  I haven’t seen this
in churches of Christ, but a church leader who demands that you no longer
associate with outsider family members is trouble with a capital T.  People who are isolated like that are much
easier to manipulate, and that’s probably what the church leader has in mind.

**Moving the
Goalposts/Inconsistency.** The church
leader should always be consistent and clear in what he says to a member.  So far as is possible, his message should be
consistent overall as well.  Of course,
as 1 Thessalonians 5:14 makes clear, different people require different
approaches, and it’s appropriate to expect more from an elder than from a new
convert.  When those differences are
taken into account, though, the church leader’s message should be the same for
everybody.  It’s unlawful to clobber
drunks while giving fornicators a free pass.

It’s even worse to be inconsistent in dealing with one
member over time.  Imagine, for instance,
that Mary is a fringe Christian who only attends Sunday services once or twice
a month.  On Facebook, she posts a number
of pictures of her in a skimpy bathing suit for all to see.  The elders meet with her and warn her that
unless she takes the pictures down, she will be withdrawn from.  Mary is shaken and complies.  However, rather than praising her for
listening, the elders figure that they’ve got her on the run, so they tell her
that now she has to start attending services every week or be withdrawn
from.  These shifting expectations create
confusion, uncertainty, and vulnerability. 
They may appear to be useful, but they emotionally damage their target.

**Belittling.** To borrow a phrase from 1 Peter 3:7,
church leaders should show members honor as fellow heirs of the grace of
life.  Anybody for whom Christ died is
worthy of courtesy and respect and should be treated accordingly.  Being a church leader is sometimes (often?)
frustrating, but the man of God should not allow that frustration to boil over
in rude, ungracious speech.  Still worse
is when a religious leader uses his position to try to bolster his insecurities
by tearing other Christians down. 
Insults and shoddy treatment carry more weight when they come from an
authority figure, and they have the potential to cause far more harm than he
intends.

**Withholding
love.** The love of Christ is
unconditional.  He may well be extremely
angry at us because of our sin, but He never ceases to love us.  The same should be true of the love of the
church leader.  “Unless you do what I
tell you, I’m going to shun you and treat you like an enemy!” is never an
acceptable message to send.   Even in the
extremely painful times when withdrawal becomes necessary, marking a disorderly
member should be an expression of “tough love”. 
All involved must understand and believe that the leader’s goal is
restoration of relationship, not ending that relationship.

**Blame-shifting.** Church leaders are human and make
mistakes.  Paradoxically, their value as
church leaders is dependent on the way in which they handle these
mistakes.  The honest church leader will
openly acknowledge error, even when the acknowledgement is painful.  In fact, he will welcome those who approach
him about mistakes.

The dishonest church leader, however, doesn’t have the mental
toughness necessary to admit that he was wrong. 
Usually, he can’t make this stick in a congregational setting.  80 Christians probably aren’t going to
believe that they’re all crazy and the preacher is sane!  However, individuals and small groups are
more vulnerable to selective rewriting of the past.  Once the religious leader gets the ball
rolling in such a setting, he might be able to keep it going for a while.  Everything becomes somebody else’s fault,
with predictable difficulties for all the somebody elses.

**Conclusion.** As lists go, this is a very rough
draft!  I don’t have an advanced degree
in a psychology-related field, though I’m married to someone who does.  I hardly qualify as an expert.

However, I think the list has value even from me, even at
this point.  It can help us to evaluate
the actions of others and, perhaps even more importantly, to evaluate
ourselves.  Being a church leader is the
most important work a man can do. 
However, it’s up to him to make sure that he’s doing not harm, but good.
