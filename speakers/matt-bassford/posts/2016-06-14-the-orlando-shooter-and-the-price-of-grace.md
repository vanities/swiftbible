In some ways, senseless violence becomes even harder to deal
with once it starts making sense.  The
more I learn about Omar Mateen, the Orlando shooter, the more I find this to be
true.  At first, he seemed to be one more
in a line of brutal, interchangeable Islamist killers (insert
banning-guns-versus-banning-Muslims debate here; I’m not interested).  As a rule, we’re willing to allow our lack of
understanding of such people to substitute for true comprehension.  They’re apparently driven to kill and die for
their conception of God, we don’t get it, so we leave it at that.  They do alien things, so we’re content for
them to remain alien people.



However, the more we find out about Mateen, the more the
stereotype of the hateful fanatic begins to crumble.  The gay nightclub shooter may well have been
gay himself.  According to [this article](http://www.msn.com/en-us/news/us/orlando-gunman-had-used-gay-dating-app-visited-nightclub-witnesses-say/ar-AAh0eVN), he had visited (and gotten drunk at) the Pulse on a regular basis and used a
gay dating app.  [This article](http://www.palmbeachpost.com/news/news/orlando-shooter-omar-mateen-was-gay-former-classma/nrfwW/?ecmp=pbp_social_twitter_2015_sfp) quotes a former police-academy classmate as saying that Mateen was gay and had
asked him out romantically.

This, combined with what we know about Mateen’s Muslim
faith, makes him sound like a man leading a double life.  He would attend Friday prayers at the local
mosque, then go out and get drunk on the weekends.  He sought romantic involvement with other
men, but had a brief, disastrous marriage with a woman.  He wanted to be two things that were mutually
contradictory.  When seen through this lens, his many inflammatory anti-gay
remarks take on a sheen not of bigoted ignorance, but of envy and resentment.

Speculation is perilous, but Mateen may well have thought
himself caught between a rock and a hard place: 
his attraction to other men on the one hand, and his religion, which
forbade him to act on his urges, on the other.

This conflict could only have been worsened by the
works-based nature of Islam.  The Qur’an,
sura 99 (Az-Zalzala), ayat 7-8, says that in the divine judgment, everyone will
be judged according to all the good deeds they have done and all the evil deeds
they have done.  There’s no way to erase
the record of even a single action.  Even
repentance involves the performance of good works that will outweigh the sin
committed.  Those who have done more good
will be rewarded; those who have done more evil will be punished.

If I were an observant Muslim to the extent that I am an
observant Christian, I wouldn’t be too worried about the day of judgment in a
scheme like that.  I am by no means
perfect, but I probably spend more time doing good than I do sinning.  When your primary occupation involves
studying the Bible, it helps!

However, Mateen knew that he regularly betrayed his commitment
to his faith.  He likely considered
eternity not with complacency, but with fear. 
We will probably never know, but I suspect that he did what he did
because he thought it was his only hope for a reset button.  According to the Quran, sura 3 (Al Imran),
avat 169–170, the *shahid*, those who
die as martyrs for the sake of Allah, are automatically granted entrance to
Paradise.  If tempted, tortured,
hard-drinking Omar Mateen dies for his faith, he gets eternal life regardless
of what else he has done.

Helped on by transoceanic whispers from the good people of
ISIS, Mateen decided to do exactly that. 
He committed suicide by cop, prefaced by a murderous rejection of the
sin he struggled with.  In mid-spree, he
proclaimed his allegiance to the Islamic State with the fervor of a would-be
Baptist reciting the sinner’s prayer.  He
was so angry and resentful and confused and afraid that he tried to purchase
his absolution with the blood of others.

I can never excuse his actions, but I think I understand
them.

Those actions raise the age-old question.  What do you do when you’re convinced you’re a
sinner?  My father likes to joke that if
prostitute is the world’s oldest profession, priest must be the world’s
second-oldest.  Once you’ve surrendered
to your urges, you need some way to deal with the guilt.  Mateen thought he found a way.  He tried to redeem himself by offering his
own life and the lives of as many people as he could shoot.

If you believed that killing and dying for your faith was
your only hope of eternal reward, would you do it?

I think Paul answers the question in the negative in Romans
9:1-3.  Those who went on the Second
Crusade at the urging of Bernard of Clairvaux (“Hasten then to expiate your
sins by victories over the Infidels, and let the deliverance of the holy places
be the reward of your repentance!”) answered it in the affirmative.  Either way, it certainly brings into
razor-sharp focus the grace that we have in Christ.

In some ways, the death of Jesus on the cross conforms to Mateen’s
twisted intuition.  The memory of some
things can only be blotted out with blood. 
However, it isn’t our own blood, nor the blood of our victims, whether
animal or human.  It is the blood of
Jesus, the death of the innocent, freely offered by Him, that pays the debt for
sin.

The good we do does not have to be proportionate to the evil
we have done.  There is no hole so deep
that Jesus can’t get us out of it.  His
grace is the answer to every sin.  It is
the whole-life reset button for anybody who wants to push it.

I’m not a Muslim.  I
have no hope of justifying myself.  I
have sinned and fall short of the glory of God. 
However, because I’m a Christian, I believe that I don’t have to justify
myself.  The light at the end of my
tunnel isn’t a murderous rampage.  It is
the glory of the grace of Christ.
