**OVERVIEW.** The LPPSR has unusually large print for a
Bible of its size and a smooth-reading double-column layout.  However, its claims to be a reference Bible
are somewhat misleading.  It’s really
closer to a tweener Bible or even a text Bible.

For a variety of reasons, I’ve had the Large-Print Personal
Size Reference (tragically abbreviated “LPPSR”) at the church building for
three weeks-ish, rather than my usual two. 
Here’s what I’ve learned about it:



**PREACHING.** In terms of size, the LPPSR is juuust
about perfect from my perspective.  Its
trim size is a hair above 5" x 8" in both dimensions, which means that a
teeny-tiny little fringe of white protrudes when I tuck one of my half-sheet
outlines inside the cover.  However, this
isn’t nearly large enough to bother me.

Also, the comparatively small size of the LPPSR makes it
handier in the pulpit.  This was actually
something I noticed after I shifted back to my usual preaching standby, the 6 x
9 Foundation SCR.  As it happened,
technology betrayed me on that Sunday morning, so I found myself preaching a
topical sermon from my paper Bible, which necessitated a lot of
page-turning.  I could tell that it was
more difficult to turn pages in the SCR than it had been in the LPPSR, and I
reached passages more slowly as a result. 
I suppose that all other things being equal, a small Bible is always
going to handle better.

**LAYOUT.** The LPPSR is a double-column, paragraphed
Bible.  These are not my favorite kind of
Bible to use in the pulpit, but they generally work fairly well.  However, I thought the LPPSR did better than
most.

This may be due to the LPPSR’s typesetting.  Like so many Bibles, the LPPSR claims to be a
large-print Bible.  Most Bible
aficionados have learned to take such claims with a grain of salt.  When you order a Bible that purports to be
large-print, but when it shows up and you open it, it has eight-point print
inside, that’s liable to make you a little bit cynical about Bible marketers.

Unlike those Bibles, the LPPSR actually is in large print,
fully 12-point, and it is set in a slab-serif typeface.  I spent several hours online trying to
identify said typeface before I gave up and e-mailed Andreas Krautwald, Art Director
for 2K Denmark.  He quickly and
graciously replied that the LPPSR is set in Bible Slab, a typeface that 2K
designed in-house.

The combination of print size and typeface is phenomenally
readable.  I set the LPPSR down atop my
SCR to compare the two.  The SCR is set
in 11-point Times New Roman and is generally considered to be an easy-to-read
Bible.  The LPPSR, though its trim size
is much smaller, has significantly clearer print.  I’ve never seen a Bible with a better
combination of small size and high readability.

This is important.  As
my wife’s uncle has noted to me, one of the problems with old age is that as
your eyes get worse and need Bibles with larger print, your hands get less
steady and less able to handle large-print Bibles, which are inevitably larger
and heavier.  For preachers whose eyes
and hands both aren’t what they used to be, the LPPSR may be the perfect
solution.

I ended up using the LPPSR for about six weeks beyond the
end of the review period, and throughout that time, it continued to perform
well in the pulpit.  The only passage I
missed was because the end of the reading I had selected was on the other side
of a line break and a boldfaced section heading, and pretty much every Bible I
might use is going to give me trouble with those.

**TEACHING.** The LPPSR is a pretty standard hand-sized
Bible.  At thinline length and width and
slightly more than thinline thickness, it’s comparable in size to the Cambridge
Standard Text REB or the Crossway New Classic Reference ESV.  That makes it relatively easy to tote, even
when I’m roaming around up at the front of an auditorium class.  Generally, I’m not going to notice any Bible
that weighs less than two pounds, and the LPPSR certainly falls beneath that
line.

In terms of references, the “reference” in the title of the
LPPSR is a trifle misleading.  Ad copy
for the LPPSR claims that it “features many of the same helps as other editions
twice its size,” and that’s true, sort of. 
The LPPSR has references, just like the SCR has references.

However, the similarity ends there.  Every page of the SCR bears a robust
inch-wide column of references, 95,000 in all. 
By contrast, the LPPSR only contains a few references per page.  Some of them are citations for Old-Testament
prophecy cited in the New Testament, parallel passages in other synoptic
gospels, and that sort of thing.  Those appear
at the bottom of the page.  More general
cross-references are tucked in at the ends of paragraphs.

On the one hand, that makes for a much prettier layout.  I think reference columns are ugly.  On the other hand, it makes for a much less
thorough cross-reference system.  I don’t
know how many total references the LPPSR has (and none of Holman’s advertising
is willing to inform me, which makes me think that the number does not compare
well), but I’m pretty sure it’s considerably less than 95,000.

Simply put, that’s the tradeoff you have to make.  Bibles are not magical artifacts that defy
the laws of physics.  The LPPSR is a
medium-sized Bible with large print, and that means that there can’t possibly
be much room left for other things.

For myself, I’m fine with that.  The references I use the most are the ones
that the LPPSR places at the bottom of the page, and I’d be willing to consider
teaching a Bible class from a Bible that contained no other references than those.  To me, smaller size is more important, but
the needs and opinions of others may certainly vary here.

It is worth noting, though, that for a class I happened to
be teaching on Revelation, I did feel the need to go through and write
citations for some of the more obscure Old-Testament prophecies in the
margin.  It may be that no other subject
of study would lead me to do this; the way I teach Revelation relies more on
cross-references than my presentation of any other book in the Bible.  As much as I like small Bibles, though; I did
feel the trade-off.

**STUDY.** Again, the size of the LPPSR is every bit
as comfortable for reading in my office chair as it is for preaching a sermon
or teaching a Bible class.  No issues
here.  Also, other than Foundation’s
sprawling Giant-Print Reference, the LPPSR has the most readable type of any
Bible I have yet reviewed.  Even if I did
struggle with eyestrain after prolonged reading, the LPPSR would make continued
reading as easy on me as possible.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEjXSRJfF2_gIFyPgzuBfv_nO1xY9SxLgNHp_ePMvUkhwWhCao8Pyox-kfm8D2txGksmXnbVkGCo3oDrnzpmlAqbvF7YL9ggticJlb9p141iSCzv6nZrrXG3DsMxv-fuzyqpWuHFvJNrTA/s1600/PTS6.jpeg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEjXSRJfF2_gIFyPgzuBfv_nO1xY9SxLgNHp_ePMvUkhwWhCao8Pyox-kfm8D2txGksmXnbVkGCo3oDrnzpmlAqbvF7YL9ggticJlb9p141iSCzv6nZrrXG3DsMxv-fuzyqpWuHFvJNrTA/s1600/PTS6.jpeg)



The layout of the LPPSR also seemed to work well in the
office.  If I made any citation errors
due to the paragraphed format, I haven’t spotted them yet.

**CONCLUSION.** In short, I found that the LPPSR excels
as a Bible for preaching, teaching, and study. 
The paucity of references may bother others, but it didn’t bother
me.  Also, I’m a huge fan of its
setting.  I’m no artist when it comes to
layout, but I know enough to tell when somebody does a truly excellent job, and
2K’s work with the LPPSR is outstanding. 
The layout is pretty enough that it makes me want to use the Bible.

In fact, that’s exactly what I ended up doing.  The SCR has been my go-to pulpit Bible for a
couple of years now, but I gave the LPPSR an extended trial to see if it would
serve even better.  I can’t bestow a
higher accolade on a Bible than that.  In
time, I decided not to use it, but that was primarily because of the
thumb-indexing that I inadvertently requested but don’t like.  In this role, the LPPSR earns a 10/10.
