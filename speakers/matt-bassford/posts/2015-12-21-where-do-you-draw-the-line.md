Late last week, a YouTube started circulating on my Facebook
news feed.  It depicts part of the
Christmas extravaganza staged throughout December by Hillsong Church
London.  I’d never heard of Hillsong
Church London before this, but I assume the organization is in some way
connected to the Hillsong Church in Australia from which the music of Hillsong
United has gone out unto all the earth.



This YouTube presents Hillsong Church London’s take on the
venerable Christmas carol “Silent Night”. 
When I think “Silent Night”, I think cozy, peaceful, calming, and
intimate.  Apparently Hillsong Church
London thinks differently.  Some things
are better experienced than told, so [here's the link.](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SbUezIFAb3A)

Let me give the devil his due.  From an aesthetic standpoint, this is very
well executed.  Connecting “Silent Night”
to the Roaring Twenties displays remarkable imagination, to say the least.  Everything from the costumes of the
performers to the arrangement of the music fits the theme perfectly.  The musicians are good; the singers are good;
the dancers are good.  It’s quite a
performance!

It is also a nearly complete secularization of the
sacred.  The dancers aren’t sleazy,
quite, but they are certainly sexy.  The
singers are singing about Jesus, but throughout the entire spectacle, I never
find myself thinking about Jesus. 
Indeed, the only out-of-place element in the performance is the carol
itself.  The simple words and the simple
melody come across as shabby, worn, behind the times, like an Iowa farm girl
wandering into a speakeasy.  We’re wayyy
too sophisticated here to want to reflect quietly on a baby in a feed trough in
a barn in Judea!

But then again, who am I to judge?  There were thousands of people there at the
“Silent Night” concert.  They shrieked
uproariously when the show was over. 
I’ll never have that many people coming to hear me preach.  Maybe the Iowa farm girl isn’t the only one
who is shabby, worn, and behind the times.

And yet.  It is
possible to win over thousands and still miss the point.  The gospel transformed into Hollywood
extravaganza (I kept on expecting Fred and Ginger to come trotting out onto the
stage) does not represent the triumph of the gospel.  It represents the triumph of Hollywood.  It wasn’t God who pioneered giving the people
what they want.  It was the devil.

I suspect most of the people who are reading this are on
board with me.  What a shameful display
that was!  Shocking!  Wherever the line is, we’re sure that
Hillsong Church London is on the wrong side of it.

However, we might be better served to give more thought to
the line.  I’m sure the Hillsong Church
London people would defend their performance of “Silent Night” by pointing to
all the people who like it.  The
millennials aren’t going to their boring Anglican parish churches.  They’re going to Hillsong Church London
because they *like* “Silent Night”
performed like that.

Is that really so different from arguing that we should sing
Hymn X because “the young people really like it”?

Similarly, I’m sure that the Hillsong folks would say that
their seasonal spectacular is a worship service.  We’re singing about Jesus, aren’t we?  It meets the minimum standards for worship,
so it must be fully as worshippy as any other worship.

Is that really so different from saying, “This hymn doesn’t
teach false doctrine, so it’s fine to use in worship”?

How do we draw the line between them and us?

In reality, this is a trick question.  When it comes to serving God, questions about
lines are always trick questions.  A
question about lines is never actually a question about serving God at
all.  Once we start asking about lines,
once we start defining our spiritual lives in terms of the minimum, we’re
really asking about pleasing ourselves. 
We’d follow our selfish desires all the way to the devil if we could,
but we know God will blast us if we go that far, so we try to define how far we
can go before getting blasted.  We start
drawing lines.

As my brother Edwin Crozier likes to say, “Draw near to the
Lord, not to the line.”  This is no less
applicable to worship than it is to any other spiritual subject.  The first question we ask about worship
should not be “Do I like this?”  It
should not be, “Does somebody else like this?” 
It should be, “Does this glorify God?”



Only when we’re certain that a particular hymn gives God all
the glory that it is within us to give, only then should we even bother asking,
“Do I like it?”  This may be harsh, but
I’m pretty sure that Hillsong Church London put the second question first.  We need to be careful that we don’t do the
same.
