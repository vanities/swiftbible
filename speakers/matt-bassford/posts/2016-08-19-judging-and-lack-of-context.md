A couple days ago, a Facebook friend of mine posted [this link](https://themighty.com/2016/08/why-i-wont-apologize-for-having-fun-while-sick/) in her news feed.  Thankfully, I’ve never
been chronically ill, and so never had to suffer this sort of misplaced
judgment, but I think it does highlight a wider and more worrisome social
trend.

For all the tub-thumping about tolerance, we do not actually
live in a very tolerant society.  This is
true even beyond the culture wars over sexuality.  Consider, for instance, the currently ongoing
pillorying of Ryan Lochte.  Sure, he was
a liar and an idiot and should have known better, but a chorus of op-ed writers
has turned him into the Worst Guy Ever. 
All the rest of us (who are not in the national news for being drunk and
stupid, even if in times past we ourselves have been drunk and stupid) get the
satisfaction of nodding our heads sanctimoniously at his downfall.



Matthew 7:1 is one of the most abused passages in the
Bible.  The abuse begins with those who
are otherwise uninterested in Scripture, but, when accused of some sin, will
reply by crying, “You’re judging me!” 
However, many well-meaning Christians will continue abuse of the text by
stripping it of all meaning.  They cite John
7:24 (which, by the way, is about the Jews judging Jesus, not us judging
others) and argue that it leaves them free to judge.

I don’t believe that Jesus wanted us to reach either one of
these results.  There are times in our
lives when both the Biblical principle and its application are clear.  When I tell a man who’s cheating on his wife
that he is committing adultery and is in sin, that’s not my judgment.  It’s God’s. 
I have the responsibility to speak truth in love, but I’m still obliged
to speak the truth.

However, there are times when the application is much
fuzzier.  Consider, for instance, the
people in the original article who take it upon themselves to be the disability
police.  When they see someone with a hang
tag who doesn’t act disabled, they feel free to engage in some self-righteous
finger-wagging.

I think the Biblical principle here (if such people are even
worried about the Bible) has to do with honesty and lack of deceit.  Someone who isn’t disabled but takes
advantage of the “perks” available to the disabled (who would much rather have
their health than the perk) is engaged in fraudulent behavior.  I agree that such behavior is wrong.  It’s exploitative to borrow your grandma’s
hang tag so you can get a good parking spot at the mall during the holidays.

However, I don’t think it’s my place to survey the users of
disabled parking spaces with a stern eye, watching for wrongdoers.  I simply don’t have the information I need to
make the correct judgment.  Maybe that
kid in the disabled space at Target is using a relative’s hang tag.

Maybe not, though. 
Maybe he has a congenital, irreparable heart defect that will leave him
blue-faced and gasping by the time he reaches the front doors, even from that
close spot.  He’ll never have a normal
life, but disabled status makes some parts of a normal life barely available to
him, which is the whole point of disabled status.  If I get on his case without knowing all the
facts, I’m accomplishing nothing more than adding to the sum total of misery in
the world.  Do we really need more of
that?

The problem is even worse online.  At least in real life, there is some
opportunity to consider context clues (though vital pieces of the puzzle may
not present themselves unless we know somebody well).  On Facebook and Instagram, though, context is
absent.  All we have are cryptic posts,
comments, and status updates.

These updates may be carefully thought-out and constructed
by people who are very good writers. 
Even then, though, they are still open to misinterpretation.  Funny how often we miss words like “not” when
we’re reading!

Alternatively, though, the post may come from someone we don’t
know very well who is not a good writer and has not taken thought for how it
will be received.  That’s not
particularly wise, but who of us can claim to be above speaking thoughtlessly
sometimes?

Ironically, these impulsive posts are often received with equal
impulsiveness.  Our blood pressure rises,
our vision narrows, and we pound out a sarcastic response taking the original
posters to task for whatever norm we feel they have violated.  In reality, of course, they may be guilty of
no greater crime than doing something ambiguous (or even completely acceptable)
and writing a status update that we have read in the worst possible light.  The anger of men doesn’t approach the
righteousness of God on Facebook either, and the certainty that we so often
feel is nothing more than a dangerous illusion.

It’s equally important to consider context when someone has
done something wrong.  It can lead us, if
not to excuse, at least to understand and sympathize.  When it comes to Ryan Lochte, I’m not a fan
of drunk and disorderly.  I’m not a fan
of liars.  If he were a member of my
congregation, he and I would have a talk.

However.  I’ve never
been an international-caliber athlete in anything, but I can well imagine that
the pressures on such people are intense. 
That’s certainly been the case, at a slightly lower level, for the various
athletes at Mizzou that I’ve known.  Some
of the coping strategies that people under stress employ are healthy; others aren’t.  Even beyond that, it can’t have been a whole
lot of fun spending an otherwise illustrious competitive career as Michael
Phelps’s bridesmaid.  Second place, even
to the greatest swimmer of all time, gets old after a while.

Like I said, I can’t excuse Lochte’s behavior.  I think, though, that I can understand
it.  This too may be an illusion (the guy
could just be an oaf, though I suspect he’s more complex than that), but at
least it is an illusion that leads me to grace rather than condemnation.  Jesus never said this, but I think He would
agree that when we are in doubt, we should be merciful.  Unlike misery, mercy is something the world can
never have too much of.
