I think it’s true that most of us have certain familiar,
comfortable practices to which we retreat when life seems to be against
us.  Maybe it’s a favorite movie and a
pint of ice cream after a stressful day at work.  Maybe it’s massacring the foe in an online
video game.

I find that this is certainly true for me as a
preacher.  The past couple of weeks
haven’t been easy, what with illness in the family, power outages, and ComEd
personnel digging up my backyard with a backhoe at 10 at night.  When I was preparing this sermon, I felt a
need to get back to what is most comfortable to me, and that is preaching about
the stories of Jesus.

What’s more, I think that all of us can and should find
comfort in these stories.  They remind us
of the nature and character of our Lord, they impress us with His power and
greatness, and they encourage us to trust in His promises.  With these things in mind, let’s turn to a
story from early on in the book of Luke. 
Let’s consider his account of the beginning of the ministry of Jesus.



**The Temptation of
Jesus**

Our text this morning will come from Luke 4, and the first
subsection in this chapter is about the temptation of Jesus.  It begins with the story of how the devil
attempts to trap Jesus with **THE LUST OF
THE FLESH.** This appears in Luke
4:1-4.  This is a story that I can
remember hearing as a little kid, but I think there are plenty of grown-up
lessons here for us.

The first is that even serving God can expose us to temptation.  Jesus isn’t in the wilderness because He felt
like going on vacation.  Instead, He has
been led there by the Holy Spirit.  Even
though the text doesn’t say so, it’s almost certainly true that He’s fasting
for spiritual reasons.  However, although
He’s doing the right thing, the devil is still able to use that to attack him.

Similarly, we must watch out for the times when the devil
tries to use our service to God to attack us. 
There are temptations that Christians face that the irreligious do not.  They don’t care about the church, but we do,
and the devil will use that fact to stir up antipathy toward other Christians
whom we think are lazy or uncommitted or simply doing it wrong.  Devotion to the truth can lead us to
self-righteousness.  There are many other
such temptations, and we must be careful!

Second, notice that the temptation here is to fulfill a
godly appetite in an ungodly way.  There
was nothing wrong with Jesus being hungry, but it would have been an abuse of
His power for Him to use it to feed Himself. 
This is always going to be true of the lust of the flesh.  God created us with all sorts of fleshly
desires.  We’re free to satisfy them
provided that we do so according to God’s will. 
However, the mere fact that we feel the desire does not mean that its
satisfaction will always be right.

Second, we see Satan turn to **THE LUST OF THE EYE.** Luke
4:5-8 explains.  Of all three of the
temptations, this is the most obviously wrong one.  Probably even my kids are able to see the
problem with bowing down and worshiping Satan! 
Similarly, most of us are able to brush off the prize that Satan is
offering.  What does it profit a man if
he should gain the whole world and lose his own soul?

In real life, though, this one is much trickier.  Our country is filled with people who have
bowed down and worshiped Satan for a much smaller prize without even realizing
that they’re doing it.  After all, as
Paul says, covetousness is idolatry too, and we’re surrounded by
money-worshipers.

Even at this point, most Christians are likely to brush off
the warning.  We know that covetousness
is a problem, and because we know it, it’s not going to happen to us, right?  Sadly, experience teaches us the
opposite.  Sometimes, the devil uses fear
to make covetousness stick.  I have to
work and miss church services all the time because if I don’t, I won’t have the
money I need to provide for myself.  I
can’t help my brother in need because I have to protect my future by building
up this massive retirement account.  Other
times, the devil uses desire.  If I get
this and this and this, I’ll finally be happy, so I need to gear my life around
earning money instead of discipleship. 
Brethren, it is not only people in the world who can end up unknowingly
worshiping Satan!

We can only protect ourselves from this temptation by
looking to Jesus first.  He must be where
we turn for protection.  He must be where
we turn for contentment and happiness. 
If we have learned to turn anywhere else, we are opening a door for
spiritual disaster.

Third, the devil employs **THE PRIDE OF LIFE.** The story
continues in Luke 4:9-13.  This too is a
strange one.  I hate heights, so if I
were put on the pinnacle of the temple, I would want nothing more than a way
back down that was as quick and safe as possible!

However, I’m not the Son of God, and Jesus knew He was.  He knew that His death was the only way that
God could redeem the human race from sin, so He was the One whom God could not
allow to die before His time.  If Jesus
felt like it, the temptation goes, He could jump off the tower and God would
have to save Him.  It’s kind of like
bungee jumping without a cord.

None of us are the Son of God, but deep down inside, every
one of us believes that we’re special. 
Without any trouble, we could come up with a list of all the ways that
God and man alike ought to be treating us better.  That makes us vulnerable, but I think the
parallel temptation here is the temptation to insist on our own specialness, to
demand that God do this for us, to demand that somebody else give us what we
deserve.  After all, if Jesus had jumped,
He would have been saying to God, “God, you must do this for Me!  I am leaving you no choice!”

That’s what pride is. 
It’s the conviction that our own will matters more than anybody
else’s.  It is refusing to listen to
those who are in authority over us, whether God or man, and it is seeking to
assert our authority over those who are not subject to it.  What’s worse is that we do these things in
the full conviction that we’re doing right. 
There’s no easy fix for pride.  We
simply have to be self-aware enough to listen to ourselves and recognize when
we’re being proud.

**Return to Nazareth**

The next scene in the chapter describes Jesus’ return to
Nazareth.  It opens with **A READING** of the Scriptures on the
Sabbath.  Look at Luke 4:14-19.  As this text makes clear, Jesus isn’t coming
back to His hometown as an unknown figure. 
Instead, He has already begun His ministry throughout Galilee.  Luke explicitly says that He has been preaching,
and it’s clear from His words a little later on in the chapter that He has been
working miracles too.

However, upon returning to a familiar city, Jesus also
returns to a familiar role.  I don’t know
how many Jewish peasants were literate—estimates vary widely—but it’s clear
from this that Jesus is literate and is known to be literate.  The other worshipers in the synagogue are
used to having Him read from the Law for them.

This time, they ask Him to read from the scroll of
Isaiah.  Apparently, this is their idea,
not His.  Maybe they were on a yearly
rotation or something.  In any event,
Jesus opens the scroll, locates a Messianic prophecy—those aren’t very hard to
find in Isaiah—and reads it.

There are places in the New Testament where you can almost
feel the world start to shift as you read the story, and this is one of
them.  Ever since the Babylonian
Captivity, and with increased fervor since the Roman occupation, the Jews have
been waiting anxiously for the fulfillment of God’s promises.  They are eager to see the return of the glory
of the kingdom of David.

In this context, the opening verses of Isaiah 61 aren’t just
an abstract religious text the way that they are to us today.  Instead, they have real and profound social,
political, and spiritual consequences. 
If indeed, the kingdom is going to be restored, dramatic times are
ahead!

This is interesting stuff, but what’s even more interesting
is **THE REACTION** of the Nazarenes to
this message.  It appears in Luke
4:20-23.  As the text begins, we see that
every eye in the synagogue is fixed on Jesus. 
They want to hear all about the coming of the guy who is going to kick
out the Romans and deliver them.  They
want to know who he is and when he’s coming.

However, Jesus throws them a curveball when He tells them
that the text is about Him.  This is not
what they are expecting from the Expected One! 
They believe that Jesus is a prophet and a miracle worker, but the
Messiah is supposed to be a warrior like David, and Jesus isn’t.

As a result, even though they initially praise Him, soon
they start picking at the image He’s presenting.  He’s sure talking awful pretty for a hometown
boy, for one thing, and for another, isn’t He the carpenter’s kid?  They can’t believe that a nobody like Jesus
could be the Promised One.

Jesus sees clearly where this will head.  He knows that in a few minutes, because they
don’t believe His claim to be the Messiah, they’re going to start questioning
what they’ve heard about His miracles too. 
After all, if He really has the power to heal, how come He isn’t
spreading it around for the benefit of the hometown folks?  They’ve heard the truth, but they’re about to
reject it.

2000 years later, we can still fall into the same trap.  Familiarity does breed contempt.  We can refuse to listen to an important
spiritual message because it comes from a friend or family member we know too
well.  Even our familiarity with the
teachings of Scripture can cause them to lose their power over us.  If we’re already in the habit of disregarding
the commandments to care for one another or teach the lost, we may well meet
their repetition with a shrug. 
Sometimes, the most important thing we can do is learn to listen with a
fresh set of ears.

Sadly, the story ends with Jesus’ **REJECTION** by the people who knew Him best.  We see the explanation in Luke 4:24-30.  As we observed earlier, Jesus knows what
these people are thinking.  Ironically,
the way they are treating the one and only Messiah is no different than the way
that any prophet historically has ever been treated.  Usually, we don’t know a prophet’s hometown,
but when we do, as with Jeremiah and Anathoth, the relationship is bad.  So too with Jesus.

Second, Jesus explains that the hometown folks don’t
necessarily get miraculous goodies. 
Naaman and the widow of Zarephath weren’t even Jews!  As we saw during the temptation, the powers
of prophets serve the will of God, not the interests of the prophet and those
close to him.

When the Nazarenes hear this, they listen thoughtfully and
agree that they should recalibrate their expectations for Jesus.  No, actually, they don’t.  Instead, they get mad and try to kill Him by
throwing Him off a cliff.  The truth
doesn’t persuade them.  The truth enrages
them.

We must be careful, friends, lest a Nazarene spirit make its
way into our hearts.  At times, every one
of us is going to be confronted with truth we don’t want to hear.  Maybe it’s truth about the Bible.  Maybe it’s truth about a personal
failing.  Whatever that truth may be,
when truth makes us angry, we must recognize that the devil is stirring us up
to think, say, and do evil.

We must also be aware of the similar danger of shooting the
messenger because we don’t like the message. 
It is certainly true that not all truth-bearers are kind, empathetic,
and thoughtful.  However, truth bluntly
and even rudely presented is still truth, and God still expects us to hear
it.

Before the Lord’s Supper, then, let’s spend some time
reflecting on Jesus, expectations, and truth. 
Jesus told the truth to the Nazarenes, but it wasn’t the truth they
wanted to hear, and He wasn’t the Messiah they wanted to follow.  Of course, things would get far more baffling
than that.  Ultimately, Jesus would prove
Himself the Anointed One by dying the death of a common criminal.  Today, let’s remember that sacrifice, and
let’s consider the Scriptures and our hearts to determine whether the Jesus we
are following is the Jesus of the Bible or simply one we’ve made up.

**Miracles of Healing**

The final section of the text describes various miracles of
healing that Jesus works.  First, we see
Him **CASTING OUT A DEMON.** Let’s read here from Luke 4:31-37.  One of the striking things about this passage
is its chiastic structure.  Basically,
this means that the logic of the text looks like an X.  From both beginning and end, the text moves
symmetrically to a point in the middle.

In this case, we see that the text both begins and ends with
people being amazed at the authority of Jesus. 
In the beginning, they’re amazed at the authority of His teaching; in
the end, they’re amazed at the authority with which He casts out demons.  The parallels continue as we work
inward.  From both ends, we next come to
information about the demon-possessed man. 
In the beginning, he has come into the synagogue, and in the end, the
demon has come out of him.  Next, we come
to two exclamations.  The demon says,
“Leave us alone!”  Jesus says to the
demon, “Come out of that man!”  All of
this interplay, though, is meant to focus our attention on the central exclamation
of the narrative.  The most important
thing in the story is that the demon exclaims, for the first time in the
ministry of Jesus, that He is the Holy One of God.  What the Nazarenes couldn’t understand even
with the help of the Scripture, an evil spirit announces.

There are two points here. 
The first is formal.  Here, as in
so many other places in the gospels, we are reminded that they aren’t simply a
collection of stories about Jesus. 
Instead, the stories are both told and arranged with considerable craft
and skill.  The more we pay attention to
these things, the more they will highlight the literary quality and indeed the
inspiration of Scripture.

Second, we must also pay attention to the content.  There is no other time period in Scripture as
filled with demons as is the ministry of Jesus. 
One gets the impression that there’s some kind of spiritual equivalency
going on here.  Because God sent His Son
to earth, Satan got to send bushels of demons. 
However, Jesus uses those demons to demonstrate His authority over them
and us both.

Last of all, let’s look at the story of **THE MULTITUDES COMING** in Luke 4:38-44.  What is to be a busy day of healing, though,
begins with the healing of the relative of a familiar figure:  Peter’s mother-in-law.  She is sick, but Jesus heals her so
completely that she pops up out of bed and starts waiting on the guests in her
house.

This is worth noting for a couple of different reasons.  Along with various other passages, it shows
that Peter was married.  During his life,
Peter was lots of things, but celibate wasn’t on the list.  Second, it shows the complexity of the gospel
narratives.  In Luke, this story occurs
even before Jesus calls Peter to be a fisher of men.  In Mark, it’s afterward, and in Matthew, it’s
long afterward, with the Sermon on the Mount in between.  Today, we assume that when someone sets out
to write a history, they’re going to do so in strict chronological order, but
strict chronology clearly wasn’t important to most of the gospel writers, and it’s
not fair to hold them to our culture’s standard.

The healing of Peter’s mother-in-law, though, appears to
open the floodgates.  Now that word has
spread that Jesus can work genuine miracles, people who have apparently been
traveling all day start showing up in Capernaum at sunset.  Jesus literally heals them all, whether they
are diseased or demon-possessed.  This
leads to Him being mobbed by crowds, which will continue until the end of His
ministry.  Ultimately, though, He
declares that His purpose is not healing, but preaching.

Let’s pause for a moment to reflect on this.  Never before in the history of God’s people
has there been a healer like Jesus, and I don’t think this was accidental on
God’s part.  The earthly healing that
Jesus has already brought foreshadows the spiritual healing He offers to all of
us.
