As I knew it would, my post yesterday about hymn structure
and “Sanctuary” stirred up a considerable amount of discussion and even
disagreement.  One of the most thoughtful
examples of the latter came from my brother and friend Dominic Venuso, who
graciously gave me permission to quote from his comment in this post.  I think both his comments and my replies will
serve to illuminate many of the issues I’ve been exploring.  Dominic's words are italicized.



*The
reason I keep responding is less to do with your actual ideas, and more with
what I’ve seen people do with similar ideas. I think we’ve all seen the hymn
snob who mocks and looks down on others who sing lesser hymns. I don’t think
you or your articles are doing this, but I’ve seen people use things like your
article as fuel to a fire of pride. If we're not careful, our carefully thought
out standards of what is best in hymn-writing can lead us to see the
imperfections in hymns more clearly than we see the hearts of the people who
somehow or other express their gratitude toward God in lesser hymns. When I
gather with other Christians, many of whom are different than me, am I more
concerned about the bond between us or about the abstract quality of the hymns
we sing? Again, this isn’t aimed at you, but at people I’ve seen who let their
hymn standards create a distance between them and less intellectual people.*

This is a problem, no doubt. 
I believe it can be a soul-destroying problem.  I know one man in particular who is not a
Christian at least in part because he thinks he’s smarter than the people he’d
be going to church with.  What can a
bunch of duds like that have to say to him?

However, if a hymn like “Sanctuary” is going to be a
spiritual stumbling block to people, why sing it?  You acknowledge that people who object to its
intellectual poverty have a point.  Why
not replace it with hymns that offer intellectual content, accessibility, and
emotional power all at once?  I don’t
think that “Sanctuary” gives us anything unique, so I don’t see the rationale
for favoring it over hymns without its drawbacks.

*If
I was more confident that the standards you've argued were absolute and clear,
then maybe I'd be less hesitant. So, for example, what should we make of your
“Jesus Loves Me” test? Personally–as far as my own preferences go–I agree with
you and prefer a song with logical development, but do I have confidence that
"sing with my mind" actually has anything to do with hymns with
logical progression from verse to verse? No. While I think your Jesus Loves Me
test is rhetorically powerful, and I find it amusing, when you say hymns that
don’t pass it interfere “with God’s goals for our worship and so should not be
used congregationally”, I think you go too far. The rule may be a good personal
one, but the rule goes well beyond what can actually be established from the
texts cited.*

I invented the “Jesus Loves Me!” test not because I thought
it was a particularly high bar for “singing with the mind”, but because I
thought it was a very low one.  In fact,
when I linked to the 1 Corinthians 14:15 post on an ecumenical Facebook group
dedicated to worship, some of the commenters thought that “Jesus Loves Me!”
shouldn’t be used in congregational worship either.

“Jesus Loves Me!” is literally written at a four-year-old’s
level of comprehension.  I checked with
my four-year-old this morning.  The
conversation went like this:

ME:  Marky, you know the hymn “Jesus
Loves Me!”?

MARKY:  Yeah?
ME:  What’s it about?
MARKY:  Jesus!
ME:  What does it say about Jesus?
MARKY:  He loves us!
ME:  So what did He do?
MARKY:  He died on the cross and cares
for everyone.

Marky can’t consistently put his shoes on the correct foot,
but he understands “Jesus Loves Me!”  If
“singing with the mind” doesn’t at least demand hymns written at the
four-year-old level, what does it demand? 
It seems to me that if we go any lower than the “Jesus Loves Me!” test,
we have read the meaning right out of 1 Corinthians 14:15.

*We
serve the God who uses the oxgoad, the tent peg, the millstone, and the jawbone
of a donkey to bring victory. We serve the God of the foolishness of the cross.
God has a way of skirting our intellectual and stylistic standards. His power
is made perfect in weakness.*

Agreed that God can use oxgoads and millstones to accomplish
His purpose.  However, we also serve the
God who demanded that only skilled craftsmen construct His tabernacle, required
unblemished sacrifices, was enraged when worshipers presented the sick and the
lame, and drove moneychangers and animal sellers out of His temple.  In the end, God’s power is all-important, but
He has never allowed that to become an excuse for His people to offer less than
their best.

*I
honestly don’t know what pleases God more based on the abstract quality of a
hymn. I can’t say for sure that our applications of what is best actually
reveals what God delights in most. I think of the widow and her two mites.
Perhaps, when a well-educated man carefully selects the best-written hymns and
sings them to God, it does not please God more than when the poorly educated
man sings a song that verse by verse (without logical progression) expresses
biblical truth that he identifies with. I don’t see how 1 Cor 14:15 shows me
that God delights more in the praise of the first man than the second. I’ve
seen plenty of people in churches who could not manage much better than a D+
history paper. And I still think they can offer acceptable worship to God,
worship that exceeds my own.*

There are several issues here.  The first is the strange agnosticism that
prevails among members of churches of Christ when it comes to Scriptural
standards for hymns.  We read Acts 20:7
and confidently argue that it requires us to partake of the Lord’s Supper on
each first day of the week, even though the text on its face doesn’t say
that.  We read Colossians 3:16 and insist
that it’s wrong to worship God using musical instruments, even though the text
on its face doesn’t say that.

However, when the text does say on its face that our singing
should express a rich indwelling of the word of God and that we should sing
with the understanding as well as the spirit, we shrug and say, “I don’t know
what that means.  It must just be a
matter of preference.”

Can the same spring send forth both fresh and salt water?

Also, I think it’s worth noting the way that “singing” in
your reply turned into “praise” and “worship” (though I admit that I do a fair
amount of switching back and forth myself, mainly for stylistic reasons).  Our singing always has two elements.  There is a praise-and-worship element that is
directed toward God, and there is a teaching-and-admonishing element that is
directed toward other Christians—vertical and horizontal fellowship, if you
will.

Problems with hymns don’t usually arise with respect to the
vertical elements.  Worship is super-subjective.  Who can say whether any particular singer is
worshiping God in spirit and in truth? 
If you tell me that you feel the subjective emotion of worship while
singing “Sanctuary”, who am I to argue?

Both Colossians 3:16 and 1 Corinthians 14:15, however, have
a strong horizontal focus.  Indeed, in
the context of the latter, Paul explicitly addresses the problem of public
expressions of worship that are vertical but not horizontal.  1 Corinthians 14:17 reads, “For you are
giving thanks well enough, but the other person is not edified.”

It is beyond dispute that teaching that is well structured
and filled with content is better than teaching that is poorly structured with
weak content.  If hymns are supposed to
teach, it follows that hymns with good structure and content are better than
hymns with bad structure and shallow content—hymns like “Sanctuary”.

Maybe “Sanctuary” provides a fitting vehicle for carrying
worship to God.  Again, who am I to
argue?  However, it doesn’t provide a
fitting vehicle for edifying me.  Rather
than building me up, it leaves me wondering, “What in the world is that third
verse about?”  There are so many hymns
that do build me up that I once again find myself asking why the repertoire
should include “Sanctuary” and similarly shallow efforts.  If somebody really wants to worship with "Sanctuary", perhaps it would be best if they sang it when they were by themselves, a setting that does not expose its drawbacks in edification.

*I’ve
been thinking a lot of C. S. Lewis’ experience here: “When I first became a
Christian, about fourteen years ago, I thought that I could do it on my own, by
retiring to my rooms and reading theology, and I wouldn’t go to the churches and
Gospel Halls; . . . I disliked very much their hymns, which I considered to be
fifth-rate poems set to sixth-rate music. But as I went on I saw the great
merit of it. I came up against different people of quite different outlooks and
different education, and then gradually my conceit just began peeling off. I
realized that the hymns (which were just sixth-rate music) were, nevertheless,
being sung with devotion and benefit by an old saint in elastic-side boots in
the opposite pew, and then you realize that you aren’t fit to clean those
boots. It gets you out of your solitary conceit.”*

I’ve thought long on this particular C. S. Lewis quotation
too!  Before we get too carried away with
it, though, we need to remember when he wrote it and what hymns he was talking
about.  Even though *God in the Dock* was published posthumously, using the chronology in
the quotation, Lewis wrote this essay in 1944 about his earlier views in
1930.

This tells us a lot about the “fifth-rate poems set to
sixth-rate music” he sneered at.  Lewis
wasn’t writing about “Sanctuary”.  He was
writing about the Anglican hymnody of the time, hymns such as “Abide with Me”,
“Holy, Holy, Holy”, and “Peace, Perfect Peace”. 
The gospel halls in Great Britain in 1930 would have been singing gospel
songs like “I Am Thine, O Lord”, and “I Stand Amazed”.  *Those* are
the “fifth-rate poems set to sixth-rate music”.

Indeed, to a first-rate intellect like Lewis, such efforts
truly would have seemed fifth- or sixth-rate. 
Great poets and composers generally don’t write hymns.  Nobody studies Isaac Watts in literature
class along Shakespeare.  I’m not a
first-rate writer, but I don’t have any difficulty seeing weak spots in every
hymn we sing.  How much more would they
have been obvious to Lewis!



Lewis acknowledges, though, that “Abide with Me” and “Holy,
Holy, Holy”, though they aren’t great works of literature, can still be sung
with “devotion and benefit”.  I agree
that part of this analysis applies to “Sanctuary”.  It certainly can be sung with devotion to the
Lord.  However, I struggle to see its
benefit to me.  Once again, I
repeat:  When there are so many other
hymns in which both the devotion and the benefit are unquestionable, why sing
that one?
