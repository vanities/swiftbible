In life, one of the things I’ve learned is that the only way to have a group of people who all agree on everything and see everything alike is to have a group with one person in it.  Any time you introduce another person, you are inevitably introducing a difference of opinion.  I’ve heard people insist that churches of Christ are particularly given to this, that contentiousness is one of the hallmarks of our brotherhood.  I agree that there are many more church fusses than I would like to see, but honestly, there’s not a religious group on the planet that’s any better than we are.

Given, then, that conflict is inevitable, how do we handle it?  Do we have to splinter off into a bunch of bitter one-person churches, or is there some part of the Scriptural pattern that shows us how to resolve our disagreements and continue to work together?  Thankfully, the latter is the case, and we see one of the best examples of this in Acts 15, during the Jerusalem council.

**The Controversy**

The first part of Acts 15 tells us how the whole controversy gets started.  It begins with a **DEBATE IN ANTIOCH**.  Look with me here at Acts 15:1-3.  What we see erupting here is nothing less than the great religious question of the early church.  Did Gentiles who obeyed the gospel of Christ have to be circumcised in order to be Christians?  Many Jewish Christians hadn’t gotten their heads around the implications of the sacrifice of Christ yet, so they assumed that Gentile converts had to serve God in the way that they had always done:  according to the Law of Moses.  Men like Paul and Barnabas, on the other hand, understood that seeking salvation by the works of the Law was nothing less than a rejection of the sacrifice of Christ.  This was a matter of faith if ever there was one, so naturally it generated heated debate.

It’s important to note, though, how this debate ended.  The two sides didn’t split off into the Eastside and Westside churches of Antioch; instead, they appealed to the authority they had.  They resolved to bring the question before a council of the apostles in Jerusalem.

Today, despite numerous claims, we don’t have apostles any longer.  At least, if somebody wants me to accept him as an apostle, he needs to, the words of Paul in 2 Corinthians 12, perform the signs of a true apostle.  If you want me to accept your claim of authority, I want to see some signs and wonders and miracles!  However, we do have the word of God.  It is our authority today, so we need to settle all of our religious disagreements by appealing to the word.

This, however, only leads to **DEBATE IN JERUSALEM**.  Let’s read here from Acts 15:4-6.  Paul and Barnabas arrive in Jerusalem and report on all the Gentiles who obeyed the gospel during the first missionary journey.  However, it’s immediately clear where all the trouble in Antioch has come from, because as soon as they’re done, some Pharisee Christians stand up and say, in effect, “We’ve got a problem here.  You say that all of these Gentiles are now followers of Christ, but you haven’t circumcised any of them.  They have to be circumcised to be true Christians.”

As we’ll soon see, the Pharisees are wrong about this, but we still have something to learn from their example.  They got the issue out in the open, and they disagreed without being disagreeable.  Especially for people who don’t like conflict, there’s a temptation to paper over the disagreements that do exist, to ignore them in the hope that they will go away.

In reality, though, that’s not what happens.  If we don’t talk about doctrinal disagreements with brethren who disagree with us, the two sides will only become more entrenched and embittered, and the disagreement will only get worse.  Instead, we need to confront those disagreements in a godly way.  If it’s not an essential of the faith, we can agree to disagree, but if it is, we have to go together to the Scripture until we come to a consensus.

Now that the circumcision debate has come before the apostles themselves, **PETER SPEAKS**.  Consider Acts 15:7-9.  Peter doesn’t reach his conclusion here; instead, he’s explaining the evidence on which he bases his conclusion.  God sent Peter to preach the gospel to the Gentile household of Cornelius, God cleansed their hearts because they believed the gospel, and God confirmed that he had done these things by causing Cornelius and his household to speak in tongues, just as He had caused the apostles to speak in tongues on the day of Pentecost in Acts 2.  By this, the Jewish Christians could know that the Gentiles were no less saved than they were.

What’s truly interesting about all of this, though, is what it implies about the question that Peter is asking.  Peter isn’t asking, “What will be most popular with the church in Jerusalem?”  He isn’t asking, “What will help spread the gospel among the Gentiles the most?”  Instead, he’s asking, “How does God see this issue?  What does God want us to do?”

Today, friends, this is precisely the question that we must ask when faced with any disagreement.  It doesn’t matter what seems best to us.  It doesn’t matter what seems most advantageous in earthly terms.  Instead, we must ask what God wants us to do, and there’s only one way to answer that question.  We have to learn His will by studying His word.

After this, we come to **PETER’S CONCLUSION**.  It appears in Acts 15:10-12.  Notice how closely this follows from the evidence that Peter has cited.  God sent Peter to proclaim the gospel to the Gentiles, God saved the Gentiles without having Peter circumcise them, and God confirmed His will by bestowing miraculous spiritual gifts on them.  Therefore, it must be God’s will to have followers who are not merely Christians from among the Gentiles, but Christians who remain Gentiles.  By rejecting this, the Pharisees are doing nothing less than putting God to the test.

Nor is that all.  Peter also calls the Judaizing teachers to consider whether their plan is even workable.  Never have even the Jews themselves succeeded in keeping the Law of Moses, so why in the world are they asking the Gentiles to try?  It would be like me trying to pick up a piece of furniture, discovering that it’s too heavy, and then urging Marky to carry it!  As Paul and Barnabas’s testimony proves, Christ clearly wants Gentile disciples.

This, friends, emphasizes the importance of seeking God’s will above all else.  I don’t think these Pharisee Christians set out to deliberately undermine God’s plan and put Him to the test, but in their thoughtlessness, in their failure to ask the right questions, that’s what they ended up doing.  The same thing could easily happen to us.  If we don’t ask what God wants and search until we find an answer, we are likely to end up putting Him to the test too, and that’s about as safe as playing hopscotch in a minefield.  Thoughtlessness will lead us to spiritual disaster.

**James Speaks**

Now that Peter, Paul, and Barnabas have had their say, James speaks.  There are many Jameses in the New Testament, but this one is James the brother of Jesus.  He begins by **REPLYING TO PETER**.  Look at Acts 15:13-15.  I know this is an awkward context break, but I think it’s important here to look at the form and the substance of James’ argument separately.  Here, we’re only looking at the form.  James begins by acknowledging what Peter has said about God’s will for the Gentiles, but then he does something very important.  He compares Peter’s experience to another expression of God’s will, this time in the Old Testament prophets.

This, friends, is exactly the way that we must seek to understand God’s will today.  God does not contradict Himself, so the entirety of the Scripture speaks with one voice.  However, if we only consider one isolated passage, one part of God’s revelation, we may well miss His meaning, like looking at a single piece of a puzzle might give us the wrong idea about the picture on the puzzle.

Instead, we need to constantly evaluate our understanding of a passage in the light of other passages.  Sure, John 3:16 does say that the one who believes in Jesus will have everlasting life, but that isn’t everything that the Bible has to say on the subject of salvation.  It’s when we put our blinders on and cling to one interpretation of a text that we’re likely to get into trouble.

Second, let’s consider what James has to say about the content of **THE PROPHECY**.  It appears in Acts 15:16-18.  Here, James is quoting from the ninth chapter of the book of Amos, not a part of the Bible that often attracts our attention.  However, this would have been an important prophecy to Jewish Christians in the first century.  After all, they believe that Jesus is the promised Messiah and the church is the restoration of the Davidic kingdom.  Because the New Testament hasn’t been written yet, those prophecies are the only texts they have that define the church.

According to Amos, then, this rebuilt tabernacle of David is going to allow the Gentiles who are called by God’s name to seek Him.  This is a significant turn of phrase.  After all, under the Law of Moses, it was possible for a man who was born a Gentile to become a proselyte, a naturalized Jew, if you will.  However, he did that by following all the Jewish rituals, including circumcision.  Once he was done with that, he wasn’t a Gentile anymore.  He was a Jew.  In Acts 6, Nicolas the proselyte is identified as a Christian before the gospel is preached to Cornelius.

That, however, is not what this text is talking about.  Instead, these are Gentiles who are now called by the name of God.  They’ve become part of His people without sacrificing their Gentile-ness.  This tells James that under Christ, you don’t have to be a Jew to be a Christian.

After this, James relates **HIS CONCLUSION**.  Let’s read here from Acts 15:19-21.  On the one hand, the new Gentile Christians don’t have to follow the Law of Moses.  As James observes, Moses has enough followers already.  However, they do have to avoid four things:  idolatry, fornication, blood, and things that have been strangled.

All four of these things make sense, because all of them appear in God’s will outside of the Israelite covenant with Moses.  From the beginning of time, God’s people have worshiped God, which necessarily excludes the worship of idols.  Second, as Jesus points out in Matthew 19, it is God’s creation of male and female in Genesis 2 that reveals His plan for sex and marriage.  From the beginning, sexual immorality has never been a part of that plan.  Third, God told Noah, who was a Gentile, not to eat blood in Genesis 9.  Fourth, a strangled animal has necessarily died with its blood still in it, so it too falls under the Genesis 9 prohibition.  James, then, is doing nothing more than reiterating the laws that God gave to Gentiles long ago and urging them to obey them.

This ends the debate.  All the noise about Peter as the first pope to the contrary, it is James, not Peter, who provides the definitive answer to the question of Gentile circumcision.  Peter was never the head of God’s church.  Instead, it has always been Jesus.  Also, it’s likely that James’ particular status in early Christianity is important here.  As best as we can tell, James was the leader of the conservative Jews.  In Galatians 2, Paul describes the Judaizing teachers in Antioch as “men from James”.  These men were speaking without his authority, but they were from his party.  When James himself, then, agrees that Gentiles don’t have to be circumcised, it’s like Rush Limbaugh endorsing Obamacare.  He takes the heart right out of the opposition.

Before the Lord’s Supper, though, let’s take our minds off the political subtleties and consider the overall point.  The overall point is this:  Through Christ, anyone can seek and find God.  Neither the divisions that were important to people 2000 years ago nor the divisions that are important to us have any importance with respect to the grace of Jesus.  Jew, Gentile, slave, free, male, female, black, white, rich, or poor—all of us can find salvation in His name, because what matters is not us, but the greatness of His sacrifice.  Let’s remember it now as we partake.

**Settling the Controversy**

In the final part of this story, we see the apostles and the elders settling the controversy.  This begins with **THE DECISION** that they reach.  Here, let’s look at Acts 15:22 only.  The thing that stands out to me here is the importance of consensus.  James’ judgment may have settled the debate, but this is something that seems good to the apostles, the elders, and the whole church.  Today, friends, consensus should be no less important to us.  A local church should not be like the US government, where you have different parties contending to get their way.  Instead, we should see ourselves as all being on the same side.  We all serve the same Master, and we all want the same thing, even if our views on how to get there may differ.  There are very, very few times, whether a church has elders or not, when the majority should override the wishes of a minority to get its way.  We may think that doing X is a good idea, but peace among brethren is an even better idea.

Of course, this cuts both ways.  If we should find ourselves in the minority, we ought to consider whether the majority view is truly such a big problem that we have to steadfastly oppose it.  In a local church, tyranny of the minority is no better than tyranny of the majority.  The importance of our fellowship with one another is second only to the importance of our fellowship with God.

In addition, the council decides to send **A LETTER** to the church in Antioch.  This is described in Acts 15:23-29.  Here, we come to the reason why we don’t have church councils today.  Even though we have certainly been talking about a gathering of men, this is also a gathering of inspired men.  We know that all of the apostles had miraculous spiritual gifts, and it is nearly certain that all of the elders did too.  At this point in the history of the Jerusalem church, why wouldn’t they?  As a result, they can say with confidence, “It seemed good to the Holy Spirit.”  It is the Holy Spirit, not the elders nor even the apostles, that is the true authority here.

Today, of course, our situation is very different.  I suppose that every denomination out there has some kind of governing body that makes decisions for that denomination as a whole.  At least practically speaking, we could do the same thing.  We could get all of the churches of Christ in the Chicago area to send representatives to a church council.  However, from that council, the most crucial element would be missing.  We couldn’t work by the inspiration of the Holy Spirit, because none of us today have miraculous spiritual gifts.  Instead, we would be operating by our own human wisdom, and God does not give us the authority to make decisions for any church beyond our own.

Finally in this story, we see **THE RECEPTION** that this apostolic letter gets.  Here, look at Acts 15:30-33.  What strikes me here is how different the church situation is at the end of this story than it was at the beginning.  We started with great dissension and debate, but we’re wrapping things up with joy and encouragement and strength and peace.  Neither the Jerusalem church nor the church in Antioch was harmed by this discussion.  Instead, they came out better than before.

In the same way, whenever we encounter disagreement in the church, our goal should be for our congregation to come out of it stronger than it was before.  All too often, that’s not the way things go.  All too often, the result is bad blood between two sides or even a church split.  That’s a result that only makes the devil happy.

If, by contrast, we want the result that these two churches got, the result that makes God happy, we have to try to get that same result by the same process.  We have to be determined to remain at peace with each other, and we have to be determined to listen to the guidance of God.  Sometimes, there isn’t a right answer.  I don’t see any evidence in Scripture that God cares about the color of the carpet in the auditorium, and we must keep disagreements like that in perspective.  However, if there is a right answer, we have to seek it, find it, and follow it with a whole heart.  Otherwise, we will never be able to find unity with one another nor unity with God.
