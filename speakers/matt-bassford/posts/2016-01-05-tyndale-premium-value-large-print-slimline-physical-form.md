**OVERVIEW.** This is a mid-sized Bible with an OK
cover and a surprisingly well-made textblock. 
It offers outstanding value for money.

This one isn’t a review copy.  I bought it with my very own money.  My wife began homeschooling our children this
summer, and she wanted an easy-to-follow Bible she could use in their morning
devotions.  Eventually, we settled on
this Bible, and I bought an imperfect copy from christianbook.com at their
usual scratch-and-dent prices.  Here’s
what I thought:

**VITAL STATISTICS**

**Translation:**  New Living Translation (2007)

**Cover:**  “LeatherLike” (polyurethane)

**Binding:** Sewn

**Trim Size:**   6” x 9”

**Text Block Width:**  1”

**Weight:**  1 pound, 8 ounces

**Column:**  Double

**Layout:**  Paragraphed

**Font:**  9-point FF Meta Serif

**Words of Christ:**  Black

**Cross-References:**  No

**Ribbon Marker:**  1

**SIZE.** This one straddles the boundary between
hand-size and full-size Bibles.  At 6” x
9”, it’s generally about as large as I like my Bibles to be, but at only a
pound and a half, it’s light for such a big Bible (spoiler:  the light weight may be a sign that the paper
isn’t the greatest).  It’s also slender
enough that I was able to glove-box it in my Corolla, though I suspect that the
more crowded compartment of my Rondo would have given it trouble.  You’re not going to put this Bible in your
purse, unless you carry one of those mom purses that’s big enough to hold a
Mini Cooper.  However, it will fit quite
nicely in the side pocket of a laptop bag, etc. 
Its light weight also makes it a decent carry Bible.

**COVER.** Tyndale is one of those publishers that
likes long, confusing names for its Bibles. 
After I’ve looked at a listing of Tyndale Bibles, I can feel my eyes
start to cross as I’m trying to sort out the Premium Value Slimlines from the
Premium Reference Slimlines, etc. 
Personally, I prefer the Trinitarian Bible Society’s method of assigning
Bibles one-word names with some resonance. 
Even if I don’t know what a TBS Windsor is before I read the
description, at least I don’t have any trouble distinguishing it from a TBS
Westminster.

In Tyndale-speak, however, a Premium Value Bible is their
lowest-end Bible apart from paperback giveaway Bibles.  These days, that means a Bible with a poly
cover.  That’s not such a bad thing; give
me a poly Bible over a bonded-leather Bible any day and twice on Sundays!  In many ways, the Premium Value Large-Print
Slimline (hereinafter PVLPS) has a cover that is typical of its tribe.  It’s a two-toner, mostly dark brown with
two-thirds of the front cover being medium brown.  It is stitched along the perimeter and along
the join of the two panels.

However, in my estimation, the poly in the PVLPS isn’t quite
as nice as the poly in most other poly Bibles. 
It doesn’t have a texture to it, which makes it much easier to tell that
the Bible is poly and not leather.  Also,
the poly doesn’t seem quite as thick as the poly in the Crossway Verse-by-Verse
Reference that I reviewed a few months back. 
This is no more than one would expect from a low-end Bible, but it’s
worth noting regardless.

Also as one would expect from a value-level Bible, the
endpapers of the PVLPS are actual thick paper instead of coated
leatherette.  This won’t stand up as well
as leatherette would, but as I handle the Bible, I don’t get the sense that the
endpapers would fail first.  Instead, I
think the poly would start to fray and flake off along the edges of the
spine.  This is not a Bible with a cover
that will stand up to years and years of daily use.

Nor is it a Bible with a cover that is particularly
lovable.  I don’t hate it.  In fact, I think the blind-stamping on the
front cover and the spine is well-executed. 
I even like the way that the blind-stamping feels when I touch it, but
that’s the only part of the Bible that is pleasant to touch.  As with any poly Bible, the PVLPS doesn’t
have any discernible smell. 
Bible-sniffers (you know who you are) need not apply.

**TEXT BLOCK.** After the uninspiring cover, the text
block is a welcome surprise.  First of
all, props to Tyndale for making sure that the text block is sewn.  The marketing copy claims it is, and I’ve
seen the binding thread in the gutter myself. 
At this point, I don’t think anyone should ever buy a Bible with a glued
binding, and the PVLPS clears that hurdle easily.  This alone makes it superior to most low-end
Bibles.  In fact, I wouldn’t be surprised
if the text block in these Bibles generally outlasted the cover.

Second, and even more interestingly, the PVLPS is printed
with the grain.  When I squint down the
edge gilding, I see slight waviness on the top and the bottom, but not on the
side.  There is no unsightly cockling in
the gutter.  The PVLPS opens as silently
as the most premium Cambridge.  Nor does
the Bible exhibit the problems with page curling that often accompany
with-the-grain printing.  I don’t know
why Tyndale chose to print the Bible this way instead of cross-grain, much less
execute it so well, but I’m not complaining!

**VALUE.** Here is where the PVLPS shines.  The cover is OK, the text block is
unexpectedly well-made, and this is a Bible that you can basically buy out of
your kid’s lunch money.  It retails for
$21 and is available online for substantially less.  It’s been a few months since we bought ours,
but I seem to recall that we got our scratch-and-dent PVLPS for $15 shipped.

While I’m on the subject, let me note that buying imperfect
Bibles is a *great* way to buy
Bibles.  I don’t think I’d be such a big
fan of buying one of those mis-embossed Bibles (I think the plate over the
mis-embossed name is tacky), but imperfect Bibles are outstanding.  For a big discount, you can get a perfectly
functional Bible with a bent corner or a scuff on the cover or something like
that—in other words, something you’d end up doing to the Bible yourself in two
weeks anyway.  In the case of my PVLPS, I
think the defect is that one of the corners didn’t fold quite right, so that it
has a lump in it.  Bible geek that I am,
even I don’t notice it most of the time. 
It certainly doesn’t interfere with the utility of the Bible.



In any event, I think that the PVLPS, whether imperfect or
no, offers phenomenal value for money.  If
you want to buy little Johnny an NLT, the PVLPS is a great place to start.  I wouldn’t recommend it for long-term heavy
use by an adult, but if you want to have an NLT to use as a daily reader
sometimes or as an exemplar of the translation, the PVLPS will serve well in
both of those roles.  In light of the
low, low price and surprisingly convenient size, I think it merits a 9.5/10 in
Physical Form.
