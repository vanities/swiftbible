*“But regarding the resurrection
of the dead, have you not read what was spoken to you by God: ‘I am the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac,
and the God of Jacob’? He is not the God of the dead but of the living.”*

-- Matthew
22:31-32 (NASB)

If there is
anything that just about every student of the Bible agrees on these days, it is
that Proof-Texting Is Bad.  People from
every shade of the Christian religious spectrum agree that we must seek to
understand the texts of the Bible in their original historical and literary
contexts.  They further assert that when
we take an isolated passage and use it to prove some point never contemplated
by the original author, we are misusing it.

That may be
true, but it is not necessarily true.  Certainly,
we must make sure that we do not use the text in a way that contradicts other
parts of the text or of the body of Scripture as a whole.  We should not read Romans 9:14-24 in a way
that pits it against Romans 10:8-13, nor does the text demand that we do so
(for one thing, Romans 10:8-13 is a discussion of individual salvation, but
Romans 9:14-24 is not).

However, the
single passages of Scripture also have a truth and logic of their own, even in
isolation.  The meaning of John 3:16 is enriched
by its context, but those words stand by themselves too.  It is entirely appropriate to use John 3:16
to show that those who believe in Jesus can have life in His name, provided
that we don’t use the verse to set up a duel with other verses.

In doing
this, we imitate the example of the inspired preachers and writers of the New
Testament.  All of them who referred to
the Old Testament at all, from Jesus Himself on down, used it at least
sometimes to proof-text.  They relied on
the truth of an isolated passage of Scripture to prove a point that did not
contradict the witness of Scripture as a whole.

Take, for
instance, Matthew 22:31-32.  In this
passage, Jesus cites Exodus 3:6 and argues from it.  Exodus 3 has nothing to do with the resurrection
from the dead.  It’s an account of the
calling of Moses.  However, Jesus plucks
Exodus 3:6 from that context and uses it to defend the resurrection of the dead
anyway.

This is not
an appeal to authority.  Jesus doesn’t
say, “Exodus 3:6 means this because I’m the Son of God and I say so!”  Instead, it is an appeal to logic, logic that
makes perfect sense to us, logic that we can imitate.  God is the God of the living.  If, in Exodus 3:6, He was still the God of
men who died hundreds of years ago, then those men must still be alive
somewhere.

Likewise,
Colossians 3 is not a text that defines the work and worship of the
church.  It is a discussion of God’s
expectations for the lives of individual Christians.  However, from Colossians 3:16, we learn that
individual Christians are to teach and admonish one another by singing.  From this, we conclude that if we want to
please God today, the music in our assemblies had better consist of
singing. 

This was not
the point that Paul was seeking to prove, but it is a valid conclusion from one
of his statements, a conclusion that does not contradict anything else in
Scripture.  Proof-texting?  Yes. Appropriate?  Also yes.
