A few weeks ago on the Hymnody Facebook group, a member
posted a question about the hymn “God’s Family”.  He asked if others found it as musically
awkward as he did.  Predictably, a number
of the group members did.  Equally
predictably, a number of group members also began to express their lack of
affection for the hymn generally, some in rather snarky tones.

In reply, another group member wrote, “This comment won't
apply to all of the above comments, but many of them - I hope all the
snarkiness don't discourage someone who is edified by this song, or discourage
someone from writing because they are afraid they will be subjected to the same
childish treatment. Criticism is one thing and is sometimes invited when one of
us offers up a song for critique, but much of this appears to be little more
than prideful attempts to show how superior someone's judgment and/or wit are.”

As always, I’m repeating his comment with permission, and I
think he raises an issue well worth exploring. 
What does the call of Christ to love our neighbor as ourselves have to
say about hymn critique?  On one extreme,
does it mean that critique is never warranted, that we should welcome every
hymn that is offered to us because presumably the author had good intentions?  Or, on the other, does the Second Commandment
have nothing to do with hymn editing at all, so that we’re allowed to let fly
with both barrels at anything we don’t like?



Obviously, there’s a balance to be struck here.  It’s another opportunity to apply the
ubiquitous “Speak truth in love.”  However,
before we get too focused on the Second Commandment, I think we need to
remember the First.  Our love for God
necessarily calls us to honor His holiness. 
The Hebrews writer puts it well in Hebrews 12:28-29:  “Therefore, let us be grateful for receiving
a kingdom that cannot be shaken, and thus let us offer to God acceptable
worship, with reverence and awe, for our God is a consuming fire.” (ESV)  The people of God shouldn’t accept just any
old hymn in worship because God Himself doesn’t accept just any old hymn.  A good hymn, by contrast, expresses reverence,
awe, and gratitude.

There are a number of ways that this attitude can reveal
itself, but one of the most important is in our determination to offer our
best.  The divinely inspired critique of
suboptimal sacrifices in Malachi 1:6-11 is worth considering here in its
entirety.

“A son honors his father, and a servant his master. If then
I am a father, where is my honor? And if I am a master, where is my fear? says
the LORD of hosts to you, O priests, who despise my name.

“But you say, 'How have we despised your name?' By offering
polluted food upon my altar.

“But you say, 'How have we polluted you?' By saying that the
LORD's table may be despised.

“When you offer blind animals in sacrifice, is that not
evil? And when you offer those that are lame or sick, is that not evil? Present
that to your governor; will he accept you or show you favor? says the LORD of
hosts. And now entreat the favor of God, that he may be gracious to us. With
such a gift from your hand, will he show favor to any of you? says the LORD of
hosts. Oh that there were one among you who would shut the doors, that you
might not kindle fire on my altar in vain! I have no pleasure in you, says the
LORD of hosts, and I will not accept an offering from your hand.

“For from the rising of the sun to its setting my name will
be great among the nations, and in every place incense will be offered to my
name, and a pure offering. For my name will be great among the nations, says
the LORD of hosts.” (ESV)

As worship critiques go, this one moves well beyond snarky
to positively scathing.  Neither Malachi
nor God appear to be particularly concerned with the tender sensibilities of
its audience.  It speaks to a timeless tendency
among God’s people to offer better service to the governor (or the employer,
or. . .) than to God because the governor is visible and God isn’t.  This is less than our best, but it’s good
enough for Him!

I appreciate the spirit of the brother who raised the
question, but in my experience, whenever the be-loving card is played, it is
played in defense of a hymn that is less than our best.  That is certainly the case with “God’s
Family”.  It’s not a hymn that has made
its way onto my hate list, but it’s not in the top 100 hymns we sing.  It’s probably not in the top 500.

For starters, it has one giggle-inducing content
problem.  The first couplet of the second
verse reads,

When a brother meets sorrow we all feel his grief;

When he’s passed through the valley we all feel relief;

I find myself reminded of the numerous hymns that use “going
down the valley” as a metaphor for death. 
Are we relieved that our brother has died?  (Well, he was kind of a bore, always going on
about his problems.)

Content isn’t the real issue here, though.  In fact, the generally acceptable content of
the hymn is what keeps it off my hate list. 
It doesn’t quote Scripture much (except for the unfortunate valley
reference), but at least it has something to say.

The real issue is its form. 
“God’s Family” was clearly written for a performer rather than a
congregation.  It doesn’t have a
consistent lyrical meter, which means that it can’t have a consistent musical
rhythm, which means that the congregation is going to spend three verses
guessing what it’s supposed to sing next.

The rhythm also includes everything from eighth notes to
dotted half notes tied to half notes (really a single note for two full
measures).  As a result, the hymn can’t
help but drag.  At its best, it’s a
heartfelt, emotional drag; at its worst,
it’s oppressive.

It’s certainly possible to worship with “God’s Family”.  I’ve done it. 
However, we can’t evaluate hymns in a vacuum.  We have to evaluate them against the other
hymns that could have been sung in their place, and “God’s Family” doesn’t
deserve to be mentioned in the same sentence as “Blest Be the Tie” or “How Sweet,
How Heavenly” (oops).

Those hymns are our best. 
“God’s Family” isn’t.  If a little
snark in the spirit of Malachi 1 helps people to see that, then maybe a little
snark is what people need.
