Psalm 1:1-3 reads, “How blessed is the man who does not walk in the counsel of the wicked, nor stand in the path of sinners, nor sit in the seat of scoffers!  But his delight is in the law of the LORD, and in His law he meditates day and night.  He will be like a tree firmly planted by streams of water, which yields its fruit in its season and its leaf does not wither; and in whatever he does, he prospers.”  I could make this the shortest eulogy in history.  I could read this passage, hold up my father’s Bible, allow everyone to draw the obvious and correct conclusions from its bedraggled appearance, and sit back down.  Though he is dead, his Bible still speaks.

However, I think it’s appropriate for me to add my witness as his son to its witness as his Bible.  I can confirm that he was a man who was faithful to God’s word.  He faithfully studied it; he faithfully lived it.  He was faithful to his wife through 50 years of a storybook marriage.  He was faithful to his family.  He was faithful to his employers.  He was faithful to his church.  He was faithful to his God.

During our time together, he taught me many things, and one of the most important was that there are two kinds of man in the world.  The first kind of man will do the job before him; the second kind won’t.  Throughout his life, whatever the work he had to do was, my father did it, and he did it well.  Several months before he died, he told me that if he had the chance to go back to his youth, when my mother was still alive, and both of them were in their twenties, and live out his life again, he wouldn’t do it.  He regarded himself as having been blessed beyond measure, he was satisfied with the life he’d lived, and he was ready to pass on.

All of this, though, makes my father sound like a pious caricature, and he was anything but that!  He snored, terribly.  He loved Mizzou football and Mizzou basketball, and would get utterly disgusted when the Tigers lost.  He had an incisive intelligence, a sarcastic sense of humor, and a cynical outlook on life.  He had low expectations for humankind, but when he had an opportunity to help others, he took it.

Above all, he loved to argue, not argument with the intent to cause trouble and contention, but simply as an intellectual sparring match.  He loved to argue, most of all about the Bible, most of all about abstractions in the Bible, and most of all about the nature of the Holy Spirit as his favorite abstraction in the Bible.  The arguments about the Holy Spirit went on for decades.  At one point, I threatened him that when he was dead and couldn’t stop me, I was going to carve on his tombstone the quotation from Acts 19:2, where the Ephesian disciples say to Paul, “We have not even heard whether there is a Holy Spirit.”  He thought that was pretty funny.

But now, for all of his delight in spirited discussion, for all of his stubborn, cross-grained nature, for all of his genuine goodness, for all of his deep, abiding love for his family, my father is dead.  This certainly came as no surprise to him.  During the last months of his life, he and I had many conversations about life, death, and what it all meant.  It was obvious that in his Bible study, he was earnestly striving to figure it out, as he had earnestly striven to figure out so many things.

In fact, for a while, our conversations took on a pattern.  I’d be telling him how appreciative I was for all he’d done and the example he’d been for me, and he’d get uncomfortable and embarrassed, and he’d start harrumphing about how he wasn’t a perfect person and he’d done things that he knew weren’t right, and so on.  I knew that, of course, but I don’t think he was really talking to me.  Instead, I think he was grappling with the awesomeness of the realization that he was going to have to give an account to his Creator, and he was going to have to give that account soon.

This pattern continued until one day, he excitedly informed me that he’d been studying 1 John and had seen something he’d never seen before.  It appeared in 1 John 5:10-11, which reads, “The one who believes in the Son of God has the testimony in himself; the one who does not believe God has made Him a liar, because he has not believed in the testimony that God has given concerning His Son. And the testimony is this, that God has given us eternal life, and this life is in His Son.”  He didn’t have to fret about his imperfections, because eternal life had been in Jesus all along.  I say similar things from the pulpit every Sunday, but it’s one thing to study a concept in Bible class and another to realize, as you stand at the gates of death, that this is what gives you the assurance you need to step forward.  My father died peacefully, and he died peacefully because he died in faith, not having received the promises, but having seen and welcomed them regardless.

Here, I think, is the most important lesson of my father’s life:  not that a man should do the job, although that is important.  Instead, it is that at the last, even the good man must turn to the cross, because there is nowhere else to turn.  Who am I, that I should navigate the mysteries of life and death?  Who is any of us?  My father, though, knew who Jesus was, and in the end, he knew it was enough.
