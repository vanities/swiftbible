One of my Facebook friends reposted [a 2013 article](http://www.christianitytoday.com/gleanings/2013/august/wrath-of-god-in-christ-alone-blocked-pcusa-hymnal.html) from *Christianity Today*, and even
though the article is a few years old, it raises an issue well worth
considering.  It concerns the hymn “In
Christ Alone”.  From my perspective, “In
Christ Alone” is about as welcome as lemonade in July.  The bane of contemporary hymnody is lack of
Biblical content, and “In Christ Alone” has heaps of content.  Buckets of it.  Strong content + engaging tune = hymn I want
to sing.  If I were to compile a hymnal,
“In Christ Alone” might well be the first of all hymns written in the past 20
years that I would include.



However, the Presbyterian Church (USA), the largest and
among the more liberal of the various Presbyterian denominations in this
country, saw things very differently.  At
the time of the article, they were putting together a hymnal.  During the hymn-review process, they saw
something in “In Christ Alone” that they really didn’t like, a couplet in the
second verse of the hymn that reads:

But on that cross as
Jesus died,

The wrath of God was
satisfied.

Indeed, it was the line about the wrath of God that aroused
the wrath of the editorial board.  They
objected to the doctrinal soundness of the line, claiming that it taught the
doctrine of penal substitution, the idea that the righteousness of Christ is
imputed to us, while He was killed because our sin was imputed to Him.

This strikes me as strange for two reasons.  First, I don’t believe in penal substitution.  In particular, I object to it because it promotes
the concept of eternal security.  If God
looks at me and only sees Christ, He is going to continue to see Christ no
matter how badly I behave.  However,
though not a fan of penal substitution, I don’t have any problem singing the
line.  It strikes me as Biblical and
accurate.

Second, penal substitution used to be, at least, one of the
core beliefs of Presbyterianism.  John
Knox, the founder of the Presbyterian Church, was a Scottish Calvinist.  He enthusiastically endorsed such
teachings.  It’s strange to see any of
his religious descendants taking a different tack, but I suppose that’s what
hundreds of years of religious drifting will accomplish.

Regardless, the editorial board didn’t like the “wrath”
line.  They asked Stuart Townend and
Keith Getty, the authors of “In Christ Alone”, to change it to “The love of God
was magnified”.  Townend and Getty said
“No!”  The PCUSA committee replied, “All
right; we won’t use your hymn, then.”

I see two lessons here:

**Doctrinal teaching is
divisive.** As every politician knows,
the only way to avoid offending anybody is to say nothing.  This insight is the direct forebear of the
focus-grouped pablum that passes for most public speaking these days, to which
the likes of Lincoln and FDR would respond with contemptuous scorn.  As an inevitable consequence of saying
nothing offensive, you also say nothing worthwhile.

Though I don’t think most contemporary writers are as
deliberate in their pursuit of blandness, they achieve the same result.  They write about how much they love Jesus,
with no thought for what such love should lead them to do.  They write about how much Jesus loves them,
with no thought for the 2 Corinthians 5:14-15 implications of that love.  As a result, the most godless reprobate ever
to darken a church door can sing their work without a twinge of conscience or a
trace of remorse.  I don’t know what that
is, but I’m pretty sure it’s not the gospel of Christ.

By contrast, once you start writing about what the Bible
actually says, you start stepping on toes. 
The notion that Jesus loves us and died to save us?  Popular. 
The notion that by our sin, we incurred the wrath of God and invited
eternal destruction?  Not nearly so
popular, even though the second is precisely what caused the first.  I wonder sometimes what a lot of people in
the religious world think they’re being saved from.

This is where the writing of Townend and Getty has left
them.  The line about the wrath of God is
probably a good litmus test for one’s views about the authority of the Bible in
general.  People who aren’t very Bible-y
aren’t going to like the line, but those who are will.

Like the authors, I have a high view of Scripture.  Even though I don’t agree with them, I
recognize the texts to which they are alluding (Isaiah 53:11 and Romans 5:9
leap immediately to mind as I sing), and I am able to use their Biblical
language to express my own understanding of the gospel.

**We need more teaching
like this in our repertoire.** Hymns
don’t merely express doctrinal understanding. 
They also shape it.  Most
Christians don’t have the ability to quote extensively from the Bible from
memory.  However, they do know hundreds
of hymns by heart.  The combination of
rhythm, rhyme, meter, and music greatly aids in recollection.

This rote-learning process begins very young.  My five-year-old son can’t really read yet,
but he already knows some hymns from memory. 
As a result, most of what most people remember about spiritual things
comes from hymns.

When hymns have so powerful an influence on our spiritual
understanding, it is imperative that they declare the whole counsel of
God.  Content-free hymns lead to ignorant
Christians.  A skewed repertoire that
avoids unpleasant subjects like sin and divine wrath will lead to a skewed
understanding of the faith.  From the
beginning, one of the primary functions of song worship has been to teach, and
the better it teaches, the better off the body of Christ will be.

We need hymns like “In Christ Alone”, hymns that point out
unpalatable truths that we’d rather not think about.  Let somebody object to singing about the
wrath of God.  Let them complain.  That teaching that they least like is
probably what they most need.  Our faith
cannot be founded on our own opinions. 
It must be founded on the word instead, and it’s nearly impossible to
establish a strong foundation without strong hymns.
