Last Tuesday, I tackled the problem of preacher burnout and
made one suggestion about avoiding it. 
Here are two more.  I got, um, eloquent about the third and had to reserve it for still another post.



**Reading.** Note that this point does not say
“study”!  Any preacher worth his salt is
always studying, preparing for sermons, Bible classes, and kitchen-table
studies.  That’s important and good, but
it’s not sufficient.  He also needs to
spend time just plain reading the Scripture, on a daily basis if at all
possible.  Don’t try to wring every last
drop of meaning out of every last verse. 
Don’t struggle with it.  Passively
allow the text to flow through you, taking from it only what it surrenders.

This is obviously going to be useful in the preacher’s work,
but it’s also going to be useful in sustaining him in that work.  On its simplest level, the Bible is God
talking to us.  When we’re reading, we’re
listening.  His words strengthen us,
reassure us, and remind us that He is faithful, even to righteous men who were
in a whole lot worse shape than we are!

Maybe our situation is Job or Jeremiah bad.  Maybe we’ve ended up in our own version of
the cave of Adullam.  Doesn’t
matter.  God never forsook Job or
Jeremiah or David.  He’s not going to
abandon us either.

More subtly, the more we listen to God, the more we take on
the mind of Christ.  All of us who are
married have probably sensed that year by year, we become more like our
spouses.  That’s why people who have been
married for 50 years often look alike. 
They have learned to think alike, so their faces assume similar
expressions, so those faces age in similar ways.

When we listen to God, the same thing happens to us.  We begin to think the way that He
thinks.  Sharing God’s perspective is the
best armor against discouragement that we can have.

**Meditation and
Prayer.** Often, the most valuable
time that I spend is time that I spend not doing anything.  As is true of the whole Bassford tribe, I am
brim-full of nervous energy, so after I’ve spent some time pecking away at the
computer, I have to get up and pace around. 
I could not begin to tell you how many laps I’ve made around the Joliet
building’s auditorium and parking lot!

However, I find that when I cease to concentrate, my mind
continues to work.  I often return to the
computer with just the phrase that had eluded me earlier.  I also find that these periods of meditation
clarify my Biblical thinking in a more general way.  I discover myself making Scriptural
connections in areas that I hadn’t even known I was thinking about Part of the
beauty of daily reading is that it keeps my mind stocked with raw materials for
meditation.

Similarly, these periods of apparent aimlessness help me to
consider both my own life and the lives of others.  They have a way of bringing to my attention
the things that I need to be thinking about. 
They remind me that I haven’t seen Sister So-and-So around for a while,
or that my attitude in speaking with Brother Thus-and-Such isn’t what it ought
to be.  Without the pressure of
immediacy, I’m able to sort out what I should do.  In the words of the psalmist in Psalm 4:4,
I’m able to meditate in my heart and be still.

This is often intertwined with prayer.  Maybe this will change someday, but for right
now, my prayers are nothing like the marathon prayers of Jesus, who frequently
went off by Himself and prayed for hours at a stretch.  Instead, they tend to be micro-prayers, very
short and immediately related to the subject of my meditation.  Think about some spiritual struggle.  Pray about the spiritual struggle.  Think about some brother.  Pray about the brother.  Think about the church.  Pray about the church.  Think about my son.  Pray lots and lots about the little
rascal.  And so on.

All this meditation and prayer might seem to be a low-value
use of time, but I find the opposite to be true.  When I’m so swamped that I can’t let my mind
go off and graze for a few minutes, my work suffers.  My writing isn’t as strong.  I start overlooking things I need to be
doing.  I miss opportunities to influence
others for good.  Perhaps most of all, I
lose that sense of being “rooted and grounded” that helps me to serve the Lord
at my best.
