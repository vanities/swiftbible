By nature, I have a rather gloomy turn of mind.  From time to time, I find myself considering
cheerful, uplifting topics like, “If my whole family died in a horrific car
accident, what would I do?”  It is in
that same lighthearted vein that I want to explore today’s topic, to wit, “If I
could only have one Bible, which one would I pick?”



Thankfully, this is not the case.  I have a shelf full of Bibles, though not as
many as my friends sometimes accuse me of possessing.  Some of them I keep for sentimental reasons,
but most of them have a function. 
Admittedly, most of them fulfill that function only infrequently.  Rarely do I ever want to look at the
translation of some passage in the Revised English Bible, but if I do, my REB is ready
and waiting to serve!

**A Series of
Unfortunate Events**

However, let us imagine instead a sad, grim world in which I
am reduced to one Bible.  Perhaps the
church building catches on fire and I only have the opportunity to rescue one
from the flames.  Perhaps the bank
decides that the balance of my mortgage is due tomorrow, and I have to sell almost
all of my Bible collection to pay my debts. 
Perhaps the government decides that Bible-owning is illegal, and it
becomes too risky for me to attempt to conceal more than one.

|  |
| --- |
|  |
| Holman NKJV Large-Print Personal-Size Reference |

You can tell that I’m good with the whole doomsday-scenario
thing, can’t you?

In any event, regardless of the whys and wherefores, I can
have no more than one Bible.  I do,
however, get to choose which one.  Which
one do I choose?

**Choosing One
Translation**

First, I think it’s appropriate to narrow things down by
translation.  At the moment, I primarily
own multiple Bibles so I can access multiple translations.  Yes, I know, I can look them all up on the Internet
for free (well, not the REB and some others, but most of them), but there’s
something different about holding the actual volume in my hand, and I think
that being able to easily poke around in a context helps me to better
understand individual verses.

|  |
| --- |
|  |
| Crossway Large-Print Compact |

However, in the world where I am, alas, limited to one
Bible, all the translations but one go by the board.  Under those sad circumstances, I think I opt
for an NASB.  It’s not the
smoothest-reading translation out there, but it excels at close study.  I prefer it over the ESV because I think it
offers more precision in study (in particular, I appreciate the NASB’s practice
of italicizing words supplied by the translator), and from what little I know
of the manuscript evidence, I prefer the NASB’s approach to the Greek text to
the NKJV’s.  None of these things keep me
from using any of the other translations, or even recommending them to others,
but if I have to choose, I have to choose on some basis.

Besides, I own more NASB’s than any other translation, so
that gives me more Bibles to choose from!

**No Wide Margins**

Second, and this may surprise regular readers, the Bible
would *not* have wide margins.  Don’t get me wrong; I love wide-margin
Bibles.  My workhorse preaching Bible is
a Cambridge NASB Wide-Margin Reference. 
I love preaching out of a wide-margin; in fact, I think it makes me a
better preacher.

|  |
| --- |
|  |
| Cambridge NASB Wide-Margin Reference |



However, if I’m not allowed to have a specialized Bible, the
wide margins have got to go.  You see, I
use wide-margin Bibles like sermon notebooks. 
As I prepare to preach, I underline my chosen text heavily and write
copious notes in the margin.  That’s
great for preaching. . .

. . . but it’s terrible for reading.  When I return to a text that I’ve underlined
heavily, I feel like the underlined portions are in ALL CAPS, so that every few
words, the Bible is SHOUTING AT ME.  This
is more than mildly annoying.  I would
rather not keep my sermon notes in my Bible than have to read from an
underlined Bible.

|  |
| --- |
|  |
| The Cambridge WM yelling at me |

My sermon-note system is heavily dependent on underlining,
so if I could only write in the margins, it would fall apart.  If I can’t write in the margins effectively,
why use a wide-margin Bible at all?  This
is a shame, because my three wide-margin NASB’s:  the Cambridge WM, a 2002 Foundation Side-Column
Reference, and a 1999 In Touch, are all impressive text blocks.  They have wonderful paper that’s great for
writing and lots of room to write in. 
However, if I’m not writing in my Bible, all that white space is wasted
space.

**Eliminating a Few More**

I’ve given away at least four more NASB’s, but even of my
remaining collection, there are two more that I wouldn’t consider for my One
And Only Bible.  The first is my
Cambridge NASB Pitt Minion.  I love the
idea of the Bible, and it’s beautifully designed and constructed, but my bleary
eyes just can’t hack that 6.75-point font at 6 in the morning, when I get up to
do my daily reading.  I know my eyes
aren’t getting any better, either.

|  |
| --- |
|  |
| Cambridge NASB Pitt Minion Reference |

The next Bible I still own (I actually own a set of them, to
use in studies with people who aren’t familiar with the Bible) is the
Foundation Large-Print Pew Bible.  I like
the layout, the paper’s nice. . . but it’s only ever been available with a
glued binding.  I want my Bibles to be
more than covers in which I carry around a bunch of loose Scripture sheets, so
the Large-Print Pew Bible is out.

At the other end of the scale, the Foundation Giant-Print
Reference isn’t the right answer for me either. 
Yeah, my eyes aren’t what they used to be, but they aren’t terrible enough
that I need a Bible set in 14-point ITC Weidemann, either.  Besides, in order to fit such large print in
a single volume, Foundation had to make all kinds of other compromises in the
design of the GPR.  Giant-print Bibles
are great for those who need them, but they’re less wonderful for anybody else.

**The Contenders**

This brings me down to the two remaining NASB’s in my
collection:  the Zondervan NASB Thinline
and a 2008 narrow-margin Foundation Side-Column Reference.  At first glance, these two Bibles could not
be less similar.  The Thinline is a slim
little travel Bible, and the SCR is a three-pound brute of a utility
Bible.  However, the ways in which they
are similar are equally important.

|  |
| --- |
|  |
| Foundation NASB SCR (2008) |

First, both Bibles have outstanding layouts.  The Thinline has a beautiful, elegant
setting, even for a text Bible (which tend to be beautiful and elegant).  I’m using it for my daily reader at the
moment because it’s such a pleasure to look at. 
The SCR is not beautiful (it looks vaguely like it was laid out by the
federal government), but my goodness, does that design function well!  The line length is right, the reference
column works well, and even though the SCR has a verse-by-verse setting, it’s
somehow still very easy to read.  Other
publishers have tried to capture the SCR magic in other translations, but none
of them have succeeded.

|  |
| --- |
|  |
| Zondervan NASB Thinline |

Second, it is sad but true that neither physical text block
is wonderful.  The Thinline is about what
you would expect from a mass-market China-print thinline:  indifferent paper, words of Christ in pink,
et cetera.  The SCR is a China-print SCR,
which is worlds different in text-block quality from the USA-print 2002
vintage.  The paper in it somehow manages
to be both bulky and not very opaque, so that it takes on a sickly grayish-yellow
hue.  The print is large and dark enough
that the Bible is still quite easy to use, but it’s not very appealing to look
at.

**The Decision**

That notwithstanding, the SCR is still the Bible I would
choose.  Yes, it’s huge, but all that
bulk isn’t wasted space.  You get the
full NASB reference suite, which the Thinline cannot offer, and you also get a
verse-by-verse setting.  Usually,
verse-by-verse Bibles aren’t smooth readers, but the SCR is.  Also, it’s much more difficult to miss a
citation in a verse-by-verse Bible than in a paragraphed Bible.  Sure, the SCR isn’t as convenient to carry as
the Thinline, but ease of carry is a specialty-Bible characteristic.  If you can only have one Bible, you need an
all-rounder, and the SCR is that.

|  |
| --- |
|  |
| The SCR, in all its plus-sized glory |



In real life, though, if I had warning that I was going to
be reduced to one Bible, I would send my wide-margin 2002 SCR off to get
chopped, regilded, and rebound, and then use it.  Why subject yourself to grayish-yellow ghosts
every day if you don’t have to?
