Though it is true that man’s nature has not changed any
since the days of the Bible, there are some significant differences between the
experiences of God’s people then and our experiences today.  One of the most notable of these is the
absence of miracles in our lives.  Around
men like Moses and Elijah and Jesus, miracles were practically an everyday
occurrence.  They could reverse any of
the calamities of life, from hunger to sickness, and even to death.

What do we do, then, when calamity strikes in our lives when
no miracle workers are around to reverse it? 
What do we do when those dearest to us die before their time?  What do we do when we find ourselves among
strangers without any way to provide for ourselves?  Thankfully, the Bible reveals that even in
times where there are no miracles, God continues to work and care for the
righteous.  Let’s look this morning at
one illustration of that principle:  the
book of Ruth.



**Naomi and Ruth**

The first main division of the book describes the
relationship between Naomi and Ruth. 
However, the story opens with a tale of **DISASTER.** Let’s look here at
Ruth 1:1-13.  I feel like I’m reading a
Shakespearean tragedy here, or one of those grim fantasy novels I waste too
much time on.  Basically, by the time we
get to v. 6, if you are a male in Naomi’s immediate family, you’re dead.  Both her husband and her sons have died, and
this is as bad as it gets.  We can
imagine how Elimelech and Naomi must have rejoiced years before, when it became
apparent that both of their boys were going to survive until adulthood.  Those two strong sons would carry on the
lineage of their father, and they would be able to support their parents in old
age.  Instead, both Mahlon and Chilion
are struck down in their prime, so that Naomi loses both family and financial
security.

As a result of this devastating blow, Naomi herself is
plunged into depression.  She tells her
daughters-in-law that the hand of the Lord is against her.  Perhaps most tellingly, even though both of
them try to stick with her, she does her best to drive them away.  It’s not that she dislikes them, it’s that
she has decided that she’s a lightning rod for divine wrath, and she wants to
get them clear.

In this, though, Naomi is making a classic mistake.  She sees where her life is now and assumes
that God is never going to bless her again. 
We too have a bad habit of assuming that where we are now is where we’re
going to be.  We will always be stuck in
this job we hate, with the raw grief caused by the death of a loved one, or
with whatever other burdens we’re carrying.

We have to be careful, though, that we don’t give up on
God.  Life is long, and rarely do people
live lives of unrelenting misery, especially when God is watching over them for
good.  Don’t assume, don’t despair.  Wait on the Lord.  He knows how things will turn out, and we
don’t.

In this dark scene, though, there is one bright spot:  **RUTH’S
COMMITMENT** to Naomi.  We see the
story in Ruth 1:14-22.  Orpah reluctantly
returns to her parents’ home, but Ruth will not leave Naomi, no way, no
how!  In the past few years, I’ve seen I
don’t know how many articles online that assert that Ruth and Naomi are a
Biblical example of a lesbian couple. 
That, I’m afraid, says a lot more about our sin-saturated society than
it does about anything in the actual text. 
This is not a story about Ruth’s unnatural desire—for her mother-in-law,
no less!  It’s a story about her decency
and her daughterly affection and her desire to do the right thing.

That, friends, is the example that we need to take from her
actions here.  When the people close to
us are hurting and in need, we need to be there for them, even when, and
perhaps especially when, they try to shut us out and drive us away.  This is something that Lauren saw during her
hospice internship last year.  Sometimes,
hospice patients would be utter jerks to their families so that when they
finally passed on, nobody would grieve too much.  There are plenty of other times when people
try similar things, when they have unwisely decided that they want to be alone
with their pain.  We should handle their
decisions respectfully, but we must also remember that what they want and what
they need aren’t necessarily the same thing. 
Like the hymn says, I don’t know a thing in this whole wide world that’s
worse than being alone.  We must take
care never to abandon others.

**The Kindness of Boaz**

The next part of the story of Ruth is dominated by the
kindness of Boaz.  It begins with Ruth **GLEANING BARLEY.** Here, consider Ruth 2:1-16.  In order to understand what’s going on here,
we first have to understand the requirements of the Law of Moses, and what Boaz
actually does.  In Leviticus 19, God
commands the Israelites that when they harvest a field, they aren’t supposed to
harvest the corners of the field, nor are they supposed to go back through and
glean the stalks of grain that they missed the first time.  In both cases, these instructions are for the
benefit of the poor.  If somebody didn’t
have a field of their own to harvest, they could go to the field of a more
prosperous neighbor and glean after them. 
It probably wouldn’t be very much food, but it would be enough to keep
body and soul together.

I suspect that many wealthy Israelites disregarded these
commandments, as they disregarded many of the other instructions of the Law
about caring for the poor.  By contrast,
Boaz was a just man, so when Ruth comes to his field, she finds barley left for
her to glean.

However, Boaz is more than merely just.  He tells Ruth to drink from his servants’ water
jars, he feeds her lunch, and he even instructs his servants to intentionally
leave some grain behind for her to glean. 
This is far beyond anything that the Law requires him to do.  It bewilders Ruth, and she asks him why.  His response is telling.  Because he has heard of all the good that
Ruth has done for Naomi, he wants to do her a good turn in return.

Today, we should have the same heart as Boaz.  Whenever we see God’s people in need, our
first impulse should be to be gracious to them. 
Whether the call is for our money, our energy, or our time, we should
not refuse it.  We should be sympathetic
to their needs, and we should do what we can to help.  We need to be merciful to one another in the
image of the mercy of our Father.

When Ruth reports what has happened to Naomi, she quickly
discerns the workings of **PROVIDENCE.** This is apparent in Ruth 2:17-23.  One of my favorite things about this story is
that even though miracles are absent, God is clearly present.  The text says earlier in the chapter that
Ruth “happened” to come to the field of Boaz, but as Naomi discerns, this
wasn’t happenstance at all.  In some
unknowable way, God guided Ruth to the field of a righteous and merciful man
who was also one of Elimelech’s closest relatives, and so was favorably
disposed toward the two widows.  A
chapter earlier, Naomi believed that God had rejected her, but now she sees the
truth.

In the same way, friends, we must understand that God works
in our lives in ways we don’t expect or even see.  When Ruth first set foot on Boaz’s property,
I’m confident that she didn’t have the slightest idea that it was going to be
one of the most important moments of her life. 
Likewise, rarely are we able to discern the importance of the things God
is doing for us at the moment that He is doing them.  Slight, subtle changes in our lives can have
a dramatic effect on us for good later on. 
We can be sure that God wants us to spend eternity with Him in heaven,
but it’s not given to us to see the path between here and there.  As with Naomi, it may well be that at the
very moment when we despair of His help, He is working powerfully to provide us
with what we need.

**Boaz and Ruth**

The final portion of the book revolves around the marriage
between **BOAZ AND RUTH.** This comes up because Ruth seeks Boaz out
at **THE THRESHING FLOOR.** This event is described in Ruth 3:1-13.  Some parts of Ruth are more accessible than
others, and this is one of the less accessible to us.  Why in the world does Naomi tell Ruth to go
to the threshing floor, uncover Boaz’s feet, and lie down at his feet until he
notices her and starts talking to her? 
It starts making a little bit more sense to us, though, once we
recognize that the ancient Israelites often used hands and feet as a euphemism
for the private parts.  In uncovering
Boaz’s feet, Ruth is actually inviting him into the much more intimate
relationship of marriage.

Also, both Ruth and Boaz remark on the fact that Boaz is a
near relative because under the Law of Moses, if a married man without children
died, his brother was supposed to marry his widow and have children in the dead
brother’s name.  Of course, Ruth’s
husband and brother-in-law are both dead at this point, so it falls to the next
closest relative to assume this obligation if he wants to.  Boaz says, though, that if the next closest
relative won’t marry Ruth, he will.

Even though all the stuff about feet and levirate marriage
doesn’t translate well to our day, there are some things that do.  Apparently, Boaz doesn’t feel compelled to
marry Ruth either, but he decides to, because, in his view, she is a woman of
excellence.  Throughout the story, it’s
apparent that Boaz is no dummy, and this is an instance of his wisdom that we
can learn from.  When unmarried
Christians are looking for somebody to marry, the most important thing they can
look for is godliness.  As King Lemuel
observes in Proverbs 31, charm is deceitful and beauty is vain, but the woman
who fears the Lord will be praised, and the same thing is true of the man who
fears the Lord.  A shared sense of humor
may or may not stand the test of time, but a shared commitment to righteousness
will.  When we marry wisely, we spare
ourselves all kinds of trouble and heartache.

Boaz resolves the situation when he comes before the elders
at **THE CITY GATE.** Here, consider Ruth 3:14-4:12.  When Ruth reports to Naomi what has happened
at the threshing floor, Naomi predicts that Boaz is going to resolve the
situation immediately, and indeed he does. 
He finds the man who is actually the closest living relative, he tracks
down ten elders of Bethlehem, and he sits down with them in the city gate.  This too is a custom that is strange for
us.  Throughout Scripture, however, it
was the city gate, rather than in some specialized building, where courts met
and justice was meted out.  The elders of
the city were the judges.

In this particular judicial inquiry, the nearest relative is
summoned, is willing to redeem Elimelech’s land, but then discovers that the
land comes with a wife attached and suddenly isn’t so willing.  Once he steps aside, Boaz claims both the
land and Ruth for himself.

Even though, once again, none of us will ever be in this
precise situation, we can still learn from Boaz’s example to do good
decisively.  Boaz may be motivated here
by some sort of attachment to Ruth, but it’s clear that he’s just as determined
to do right by Ruth and Naomi both, and as Naomi says, he doesn’t rest until
the matter is settled.  Too often, this
isn’t true of us.  We see good things
that we could do—reaching out to a struggling brother, telling a friend about
the gospel, or even obeying the gospel ourselves—but rather than acting
immediately, we sit on things.  We delay
and delay so that rather than doing good, we do nothing.  Good intentions are great, but if that’s all
we’ve got, we haven’t got much.  We need
to pair those good intentions with good actions.

Finally, we come to **THE
MARRIAGE** itself.  Consider Ruth
4:13-22.  First of all, let’s notice how
things have worked out for Naomi.  In
marrying Ruth, Boaz implicitly promises to care for her too, so she can be
assured of a comfortable old age.  Even
more than that, she’s going to have the grandchild that she must have assumed
she would never see.  Back in her despair
in Ruth 1, Naomi should have realized that God wasn’t finished with her yet!

Second, though, let’s look at what takes this story from
being an interesting historical footnote and makes it into an important part of
the story of the Bible, and that’s the brief genealogy at the end.  As it reveals, Ruth was the great-grandmother
of David the king.   One of the most
famous Israelites of all time was, by blood, one-eighth Moabite.  Of course, famous as he was, David is not the
most prominent of the descendants of Ruth. 
The last place in the Bible that her name appears is in Matthew 1, in
the list of the ancestors of Jesus.  The
providence that guided her to the field of Boaz was only part of the larger
providence that accomplished our salvation.
