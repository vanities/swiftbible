**OVERVIEW.** Only a mother could love the ASV as a
reader.  It has many things going for
it—paragraphing, print size, opacity—but I do not love the self-pronouncing
format, and the ASV as a translation is just plain hard to read.

\*             \*             \*

After using the ASV as a reader for several weeks, here are
my impressions:

**SIZE.** No problems here.  I rested the Bible on my chest instead of
holding it up in one hand, but that’s perfectly acceptable, and with a trim
size of 5 ¾” x 8 ¼”, the pages are small enough to turn easily.  Basically, in terms of size, the Teacher’s
edition reads about like a thinline, and thinlines are fine readers.

**LAYOUT.** Strangely enough, the ASV does have many
of the attributes that make for a well-designed reader—for me, at least.  It’s a double-column paragraphed Bible, which
is optimal.  It is a
center-column-reference Bible, though, which is less than optimal.  I prefer my readers to have more than 40
characters per line; the ASV is somewhere in the 37-39 cpl range.  That’s not ideal, but it’s certainly not
enough to sink it as a Bible.

Gutter-wise, the ASV is at least decent.  This is a thick Bible, and generally, thick
Bibles have more gutter problems. 
There’s more material underneath your page on either side to create a
gutter problem.  However, I’d eyeball the
ASV gutter margins as being about 3/8 of an inch wide, which works fine even in
the middle of the Bible.  Even there,
there was white space between the left- and right-hand pages.

Also, the ASV is black-letter.  The print quality isn’t wonderful, which is
to be expected from a Bible printed on an honest-to-goodness mechanical press
long before I was born, but at least I’m not contending with The Words Of
Christ In Pink.  The Bible isn’t line-matched
except coincidentally (which happens with some regularity because it doesn’t
have any section headings), but the paper is so opaque that line-matching or
not makes no difference.

Finally, the text is plenty large enough to be
readable.  Star Bible claims on their
site that it’s 9-point, which looks about right, and with such a heavy, round
typeface, 9-point is more than sufficient. 
As was true of my experience with the Foundation Topical Reference
Bible, the inline verse numbers in the ASV didn’t give me any trouble.

However.  One thing
that I do not like in the ASV, indeed cannot stand, is the self-pronouncing
names.  I understand the logic behind a
self-pronouncing Teacher’s Edition; it’s not too hard to imagine some novice
teacher keeling over after being asked to read Nehemiah 7 out loud.  However, it’s miserable to read.  I probably don’t ever need pronunciation
marks to say “Jesus” correctly when I’m reading out loud.  I’ll generally manage that one OK on my own.  However, I certainly don’t need the
pronunciation marks when I’m reading it!

In fact, the self-pronouncing names actively interfere with
my reading comprehension.  In sections of
the Old Testament with lots of names, I have to bring my mind out of the text
to try to figure out what that name actually is:  “Jehoahaz! 
Ohhh!”  That ain’t no good.

Also, the ASV generally is hard to read.  It’s great to be able to see Greek syntax and
Greek idioms when you’re doing a close, verse-by-verse study of a text.  However, it’s not nearly so wonderful to have
those things when you’re trying to figure out the gist of a context in the
midst of a three-chapter reading.  The
ASV is not as bad as some of those Supreme Court cases I had to read in law
school, but it isn’t anything I’d want to inflict on a normal human being
either.

**CONCLUSION.** It’s funny how a few choices can make the
difference between a great reader and a below-average reader.  The Teacher’s Edition does lots of things
right.  Indeed, it does most things
right.  However, the self-pronouncing
names and translation idiosyncrasies keep it from being a Bible I enjoy
reading.  Reading rating:  4/10.  Overall,
the Bible earns a 20/30, which is average to poor (mainly because of the
reading score).  However, for specific
applications, it’s much more valuable than that.
