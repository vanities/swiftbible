Last month, I read a book entitled Franchising McChurch.  It was a critique of the modern multi-campus megachurch.  Even though the authors are not associated with the churches of Christ, much of their indictment sounded familiar.  A focus on numerical growth leads pastors to minimize or ignore the hard sayings of Scripture.  In encouraging attendees to lead “your best life now”, these preachers de-emphasize the eternal reward of heaven.  Finally, splintered, consumer-centric worship services aimed at specific age groups, ethnic groups, and even those who only want to join in via the Internet destroy a church’s cohesiveness.

To these ear-tickling, materialistic, disconnected churches, the writings of the first century have little to say.  The Bible mindset and the consumer-church mindset don’t have much in common.  However, if our goal is to imitate the earliest disciples of Jesus, those concerns that are irrelevant to much of the religious world are very relevant to us.  In illustration of this, let’s look at a passage that speaks where many don’t want to listen.  Let’s look at 1 Thessalonians 4-5.

**Excelling Still More**

The first subdivision within this text concerns the Christian’s pursuit of greater godliness, what Paul calls “excelling still more”.  He highlights THE NEED FOR IMPROVEMENT in 1 Thessalonians 4:1-2.  In some ways, this is a strange passage.  After all, Paul is encouraging the Thessalonians to change where there is no problem.  As Paul himself says, he taught them what the will of Jesus was, and they are faithfully walking according to that will and pleasing God.  These Christians are doing what they’re supposed to.

However, instead of slapping them on the backs and saying “Mission accomplished,” Paul tells them how they need to change and why.  First, they have to change by excelling still more.  They have to take the things they’re already doing right and do an even better job at them.  Why?  Because these things have been commanded by the authority of the Lord Jesus, and His Lordship is absolute.

The same is true for us today, and we must remember that in the face of the temptation to say, “Good enough.”  This is a temptation that even mature Christians face.  We say to ourselves that we spend enough time studying the Bible because we come to Bible classes on Sunday morning and Wednesday night.  Because we stop after services to encourage a weaker Christian, we excuse ourselves from welcoming that visitor too.  We sign up to teach one Bible class a year and wait for somebody else to teach the rest.

To all those things, brethren, our Lord says, “Excel still more!”  We might not be doing anything wrong, but there is always more to do that’s right.  None of us are entitled to become complacent about our obedience.  If we’re comfortable with our level of service, if we aren’t stretching, aren’t pushing, aren’t feeling the strain, that’s not a good thing.  It’s a very bad thing.  Let’s be Christians who ask “What else can I do?” instead of pointing to what we already do and insisting, “I’ve already done enough.”

Paul first points out the need for excellence in SEXUAL MORALITY.  Let’s read here from 1 Thessalonians 4:3-8.  This is a passage that addresses both sides of the coin.  Let’s start with the good side first, what Paul says about sex in marriage.  On this subject, the entire New Testament speaks with one voice.  There is nothing dirty or impure about marital sex.  Instead, it is a sanctified and honorable thing.  Brethren, we need to own this passage.  Too often, we, and I certainly include myself in this, focus on the sexual don’ts of fornication and adultery and lust while forgetting about the sexual do’s.  As a result, too many marital relationships are tainted by the disgust and shame that should only accompany sin.  Make no mistake about it, friends; when Paul writes that each one is “to possess his own vessel in sanctification and honor,” he is commanding Christians to have sanctified, honorable sexual relationships with their spouses.  Unless it is impossible for some reason for a husband and wife to come together, a sexless marriage is not a personal problem; it is a state of sin.  The marital bedroom is not to be the scene of the selfishness of Satan, but of the selfless love of Christ, and that’s a knife that cuts both ways in every marriage.

The other side of the coin is Paul’s warning against sexual sin.  Sex in marriage is a gift from God; sex outside of marriage is a tool of the devil.  If you want to find the ugly, the dirty, the nasty in sex, here it is.  Look at the words he uses to describe sexual immorality.  It is lustful passion.  It is fraud.  It is transgression.  It is impurity.  All sexual sin, fornication and adultery alike, is an evil that God is going to avenge.  This is true not only of the sin that disgusts us, but also of that which we find enticing.  The allure is a lie.  It is a thin sheet of fleshly appeal stretched over a bottomless well of pain and misery and sorrow.  We honor God in the marriage bed, but we dishonor Him when we depart from His plan for husbands and wives.

Finally, Paul encourages excellence in PROPER BEHAVIOR.  Look at 1 Thessalonians 4:9-12.  We can sum up this passage in a single sentence:  “Take care of others; take care of yourselves.”  We fulfill the first part of this by loving our brothers and by living in accordance with that love.  Just like faith without works is dead, love without action is posturing.  Here’s what this boils down to:  If our brother has a need, he can count on us to fill it.  Whether the problem is financial, emotional, or spiritual, we’ll help him get through it.  Obviously, we must always be guided by holiness and wisdom in the help we provide.  In 2 Thessalonians 3, writing to this same group of Christians, Paul tells them not to help brethren who are lazy instead of being truly in need.  However, we must also take care not to let the exception swallow the rule.  In His church, God intends for Christians to love one another and care for one another.

One of the ways that we show our concern for others is by ordering our own lives appropriately.  We shouldn’t be people who cause trouble at work or school.  We shouldn’t be people who constantly stick our noses where they don’t belong.  Instead, we should mind our own business in both senses of the word.  We should concern ourselves with our own doings and work hard at everything we put our hands to.

I’ve mentioned before how blessed I feel to be able to support my family by preaching the gospel.  I know that there are Christians here who wish they were able to spend as much time with the Bible as I do, but can’t because of the requirements of their secular jobs.  What Paul wants us to understand here is that there is no such thing as a secular job.  It might not be as obvious as it is with a preacher, but every Christian who does honorable work is doing that work to and for the Lord.  He is glorified just as much by a brother or sister who works hard sweeping floors as He is when I get up in the pulpit and preach a sermon.  When we work in this way and God rewards our labor, we won’t need to look for help from anyone.

**The Day of the Lord**

In the second main section of this text, Paul turns his attention to the day of the Lord.  He begins by explaining THE RESURRECTION OF THE DEAD.  Let’s read here from 1 Thessalonians 4:13-18.  First of all, put this one on the list of my 500 Favorite Passages.  This is such a hopeful text!  It reminds us that Christ has solved mankind’s biggest problem for us and for all who love Him.  Sure; unless He returns, we’re going to die.  However, we aren’t going to stay dead, nor will anyone else who falls asleep in Him.

This isn’t some abstract issue for us, and apparently, it wasn’t some abstract issue for the Thessalonians.  V. 13 here makes it sound like the church was in mourning for some of its members who had recently died, perhaps even members who had been killed during the recent persecution.  Unlike us, the Thessalonians didn’t have 1 Thessalonians 4 to comfort them, at least not yet, and it sounds like they were worried that the Christians who had died would miss out on the return of the Lord.

Not so, Paul says, and by way of illustration of what would happen to the departed brethren, he points to what already has happened with Jesus.  Because God raised Christ from the dead, we can be certain that He will raise us from the dead too.  Note that this has significant implications for what the resurrection will be like.  Jesus wasn’t resurrected in some spiritual sense; instead, His body rose from Joseph’s tomb.  So too, we can anticipate a resurrection that is not merely spiritual, but is bodily too.

On the glorious day of the Lord’s return, not only will the dead be raised and then rise to join Him in the clouds, but the Christians who are still living will also ascend.  Interestingly, in our studies of authority, we often make the point that the universal church, the assembly of all believers, is an abstraction.  On the day of resurrection, though, that abstraction will become a reality.  All Christians from all places and all times will be united with one another in one joyous congregation, and they will be united with Jesus in a convocation of love that will never end.  That, friends, is why we do what we do, why we put so much time and effort into serving Jesus.  We seek this great hope, of spending eternity with one another and with Him.

Of course, THE DAY OF THE LORD isn’t going to be all sweetness and light, either.  After that beautiful, poetic description of the resurrection of the dead, Paul explains what the consequences will be for many others in 1 Thessalonians 5:1-3.  Note first of all what he writes here about the timing of the final judgment.  He isn’t going to try to assign a time to it.  The Thessalonians themselves already know that the return of the Lord is unpredictable.  As Jesus Himself taught, no one except God the Father knows when that great and awesome day will come.  This, then, is one of those many passages of Scripture that people who try to predict “the end times” should read.  Such predictions are nothing more than wasted effort.

Everyone is going to be surprised when Jesus appears.  For all too many, though, the surprise will be the worst one of their existence.  They’re going along, doing their ungodly thing, not afraid of anything, when wham!  There Jesus is.  Before they know it, they are swept off to eternal destruction.

This should remind us, friends, that God’s love is infinite, but His patience is not.  He wants all of us to repent and return to Him.  However, if we persist in wickedness like the children of Israel did in the wilderness, just like they ran out of chances, we’re going to run out of chances too.  Whether we die or whether the Lord returns, salvation is a limited-time offer for all of us, and the consequences of waiting too long are nothing short of disastrous.  Let’s be sure that we don’t take His patience lightly!

Indeed, Paul tells us what we ought to do to STAY ALERT for the Lord’s coming.  Let’s read here from 1 Thessalonians 5:4-11.  First of all, the apostle points to the difference between Christians and worldly people.  They’re in the darkness, so that the day of the Lord will overtake them like a thief, but we are in the light.  This doesn’t mean that we’ve got God’s timetable down, so that all of us know to repent five minutes before Jesus’ big entrance.  Instead, it means that we are walking in the light as He is in the light.  When the Lord comes back, He’s not going to catch us practicing evil because we don’t practice evil.

Paul explains this difference as the difference between being asleep and drunk on the one hand and alert and sober on the other hand.  The people of the world are hopelessly unprepared for Jesus, but we’re ready for His return all the time.

Paul then points to the breastplate of faith and love and the helmet of our salvation as the symbols of our readiness.  This might strike us as a little bit bizarre.  After all, this language reminds us of the whole armor of God in Ephesians 6, which has us thinking in terms of soldiers and warfare.

That’s not quite where Paul is going here, though.  Remember, just a few verses up, Paul has compared Jesus to a thief in the night, not in character, of course, but in stealth.  Today, a homeowner who has reason to expect a break-in might stay up late with all the lights on and a loaded pistol or shotgun in his lap.  Back in the day, of course, there were no firearms available, but a homeowner who similarly expected a home invasion might equip himself with the gear that was available to him, like a helmet and a breastplate.  In hand-to-hand combat, a man who is awake and fully armored is a much tougher proposition than an unarmed man who is suddenly awakened out of a sound sleep.  Just like that long-ago homeowner would prepare for the thief with armor, we prepare ourselves for the appearance of Jesus by putting on faith, hope, and love.

Of course, as Paul next points out, the analogy does break down.  The thief is the homeowner’s enemy, but Jesus is the best friend we will ever have.  Indeed, He was willing to die so that all of His brothers and sisters, whether alive or dead, could find salvation through Him.  He will come to rescue and redeem us, not to rob us.  Because this is our hope, we should make sure that we are ready ourselves and also do everything we can to keep the other Christians we know alert and prepared for the coming of the Lord.

Before the Lord’s Supper, let’s think about these things.  In these few verses, Paul outlines our greatest blessing and our greatest responsibility.  On the one hand, we are blessed beyond measure in the death of the Lord Jesus on the cross, because it is through Him that we may have eternal life.  On the other hand, if we want to be sharers in His resurrection, we must also share in His death.  This sharing begins when we are buried in baptism, but it continues as we die to ourselves daily in His service.  Is that how we’re living, or would the day of the Lord catch us unprepared too?  Let’s reflect on this as we partake.

**Righteous Behavior**

Finally, Paul describes the kind of righteous behavior that will ensure that we don’t meet the Lord unprepared.  In fact, we can almost read the rest of the book of 1 Thessalonians as a list of bullet points, a Christian to-do list.  However, Paul also organizes these bullet points by category, and the first category concerns our behavior TOWARD OUR BRETHREN.  Look here at 1 Thessalonians 5:12-15.  Paul’s basic point here is that in the church, we should constantly seek the good of others rather than our own good.  He then identifies three specific groups of brethren who often give us trouble with this:  church leaders, weaker brethren, and brethren who have wronged us.  Let’s start with the first category first.  Even though Paul doesn’t explicitly use the word “elder”, he’s most likely talking about elders, because elders are the only Christians who can be said to have charge over other Christians in the Lord.  Obviously, I’ve never been an elder, but I’ve worked closely with a couple different elderships.  Let me tell you—serving as an elder is one of the most difficult, thankless jobs a man can take on.  The Scripture describes elders as shepherds and ordinary saints as sheep, but oftentimes, the work of the elder more closely resembles herding cats.

These challenges are in no way lessened by other members of the congregation who want to engage in back-seat shepherding.  Even though they aren’t elders, they think they understand eldering better than the elders do, so they demand to know all the unpleasant secrets that elders learn, and, whether read in or not, feel free to question the decisions that the elders make.  Brethren, God’s plan leaves no place for small-minded behavior like that.  I don’t think any of us have enjoyed not having elders at Joliet for these past several years.  However, hopefully the experience has taught us how important elders are.  Should we be so blessed to work under elders again, whether here or elsewhere, let’s honor them as they deserve.

Second, Paul encourages every Christian to take on the role of the elder in at least a small way, by caring for struggling Christians.  This requires both love and judgment.  If we don’t love these people, we’ll simply allow them to go their own way without any intervention from us.  If we don’t have good judgment, though, our good intentions will go awry.  As Paul points out, patience is essential in every case of spiritual trouble, but every situation is different.  Some weaker Christians need a helping hand.  Others need a kind word.  Still others need a swift kick where it will do the most good, and applying a swift kick to somebody who needs a kind word, or vice versa, can be worse than doing nothing.  We are commanded to do this, so we must do it wisely, carefully considering the circumstances of our brother before we act.

Third, even when a Christian wrongs us, we still have a responsibility to love them and treat them accordingly.  This is never easy.  They do some horrible thing to us, then come to us and offhandedly ask our forgiveness, and they expect us to forgive them, just like that!  Well, friends, we need to forgive them just like that.  When we have been forgiven in so much, who are we to deny forgiveness in so little?

Next, Paul looks at our responsibilities TOWARD GOD.  Consider 1 Thessalonians 5:16-22.  These responsibilities break down into three main categories:  talking, listening, and obeying.  In the first category, God expects us to glorify Him through praise, thanksgiving, and prayer.  After all, no matter what our earthly situation may be, the humblest Christian is still infinitely better off than the wealthiest sinner.  Also, when we seek God’s blessing, we demonstrate our trust in Him.  He deserves all this, and we should provide it.

Second, we should listen.  Today, we don’t have miraculous spiritual gifts anymore, but we can still quench the Spirit and despise prophetic utterances, and we do so every time we turn away from the Scripture.  After all, the Bible contains the complete record of every prophecy God wants us to have.  When we refuse to study and pay attention to the Bible, we are despising that prophetic utterance.  Instead, we need to carefully examine everything the Scripture says, knowing that it is indeed the inspired word of God.

Third, once we have learned God’s will, we are responsible for obeying it, by clinging to what is good and rejecting what is evil.  Some people have reached an erroneous conclusion about v. 22 by missing the point in the KJV, which reads, “Abstain from the appearance of evil.”  From this, they conclude that we have to avoid not only things that are wrong, but things that simply look wrong.  I agree that this is a Scriptural principle, but it isn’t taught here.  Instead, the point is that whenever evil appears, we should abstain from it.

As he generally does, Paul finishes off the epistle with a few CLOSING COMMENTS.  We find them in 1 Thessalonians 5:23-28.  First, as always, he emphasizes the importance of prayer by praying for the Thessalonians’ spiritual welfare and asking them to reciprocate.  There’s an important lesson here for us.  We commonly pray for the physical welfare of one another.  When a member here is sick or traveling, they will often make an appearance in the public prayers we offer.  There’s nothing wrong with that.  However, the bodily welfare of the members here is far less important than our spiritual welfare.  Let’s remember to pray, as Paul does for the Thessalonians, that we too will be preserved complete at the coming of the Lord.

Finally, Paul gives us a glimpse into a couple of different ways that the situation of the first-century church was different from ours.  The first involves the oft-repeated instruction to greet one another with a holy kiss.  Culturally speaking, people 2000 years ago were much kissier than we are today.  For instance, when Judas betrayed Jesus, he did so with a kiss of greeting—not because he was particularly concerned about following God’s commandments at that point, but because that was just what people did.  Today, a kiss means something much different than it did, but we should still greet one another with the expressions of platonic affection that are common in our culture.  Likewise, even though we don’t get letters from apostles anymore, we should still give attention to the reading of the ones we have, just as we have done this morning.
