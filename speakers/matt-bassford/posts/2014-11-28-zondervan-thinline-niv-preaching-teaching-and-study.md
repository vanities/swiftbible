Few Bibles that I review have gotten as much of a workout as
the Thinline NIV.  During its two-week
rotation at the Joliet church building, I used it to preach two sermons and
teach four Bible classes (though our adult Bible class at the moment is using a
lesson book that contains the entire text of the gospel of Matthew without
chapter or verse numbers—more on that later, probably).  I also used it to teach adult classes during
our week of vacation Bible school .

Finally, when my family and I went on vacation (to a
hymnwriting seminar, actually, but we’ll call it “vacation”), I used it to
preach two more sermons.  One of those
was actually a topical sermon that I had intended to use slides for, but there
were technical difficulties with the PowerPoint, so I ended up doing a bunch of
page-flipping in the Thinline NIV instead.  In sum, the Bible saw A Lot of use.  Here’s how I thought it did:



**PREACHING.** The Thinline NIV is right in the sweet
spot for Bible size.  I can tuck a
half-sheet outline into it, and only a little fringe of paper emerges on one
edge.  It’s as small as it can be without
annoying me.

In layout, the Thinline NIV is a two-column, paragraphed
Bible.  In many ways (unusually generous
leading, citations for prophecies from the Old Testament at the bottom of the
page), its layout resembles that of the Crossway Thinline (ESV).  I don’t know how thoughtful the designers of
the Thinline NIV were in their adaptation, or if they just copied the Crossway
design elements, but as with the Thinline ESV, the tops of the superscript
verse numbers in the Thinline NIV protrude above the tops of the capital
letters.

I don’t know why it would be so, but for me, this layout
quirk is extremely functional.  In a
normal paragraphed Bible (in which the tops of the superscript verse numbers
are level with the tops of the capital letters), I miss verse citations left
and right.  I don’t cite the right verse,
and I often continue reading past the point where I mean to stop.

This was emphatically not the case in my use of the Thinline
NIV.  In the four sermons (two of which
were the double-length behemoths I preach during our consolidated Sunday
services at Joliet), I only remember one citation problem.  As I look at it, though, it appears to be the
result of user error.  I meant to cite
Acts 5:27-29 but cited Acts 5:27-28 instead. 
I think, though, that I pulled the verse citation from Luke 6:27-28,
which was the preceding point in my sermon outline.  They were so similar that I merely repeated
the citation I had just typed.

Regardless, one missed citation in nearly three hours of
preaching ain’t half bad.  Usually, a
double-column paragraphed Bible spells doom for me in the pulpit, but the
Thinline NIV performed quite well.

The only thing that bugged me about the Thinline NIV as a
preaching Bible was, oddly enough, the format of Proverbs.  After every proverb (which, through much of
the book, means after every verse), the Thinline NIV inserts a line break (as,
probably, do all NIV11’s).  Also, it
seems like most of the proverbs are a leetle too long to fit on one line, so
that the layout is rife with one-word widows. 
As a result of these things, it seems like the page layouts in Proverbs
contain more white space than they do text, which made it difficult for me to
locate verses.

**TEACHING.** As I’ve said before, the smaller a Bible
is, the better I like it for teaching a Bible class.  Here, the Thinline NIV shines.  It’s not a Bible with a lot of extras.  It doesn’t have a concordance or a map
section.  There’s only one page between
Revelation 22 and the back endpaper.

As a result of leaving out those things (which, honestly, I
don’t miss), the Bible is as light as it can be.  It weighs in at only a touch more (well, four
ounces more, which is significant in a burger but not in a Bible) than a
pound.  Anybody who can’t comfortably
hold the Thinline NIV for 45 minutes probably isn’t going to be able to hold a
paper Bible, period.  He probably isn’t
going to be able to stand up to teach a Bible class, either.  Basically, the Thinline NIV is as small and
light as we can reasonably expect a Bible to be.

Once again, the study aids in the Thinline NIV went unused,
but they were there, kind of.  The
Thinline NIV is what I’m about to start calling a tweener Bible.  It isn’t really a text Bible, but it isn’t
really a reference Bible either.  Bibles
like this will have a few references (the Thinline NIV keeps them at the bottom
of the page; the Holman LPCR puts them at the ends of paragraphs), but there
are few enough of them that they don’t demand a center reference column all to
themselves.

I love tweener layouts. 
For me, they present the best of both worlds.  They preserve the text-column width that is
so important in double-column Bibles, and they also contain the references
(Old-Testament prophecy citations, along with citations for parallel passages
in the Synoptics) that I am most likely to use. 
As I said, I didn’t use any references from the Thinline NIV, but if I
had wanted to use a reference, it would probably have been there.

**STUDY.** The Thinline NIV also served well as a
preacher’s-office Bible.  The size was
fine.  I also didn’t have any issues with
readability.  The typeface in the
Thinline NIV, as with most/all NIV11’s, is Utopia (as far as I can discern),
which my photographer tells me is commonly used in newspapers.  The boldfaced headings stand out dramatically
from the text.  The leading is something
on the order of 14-point (I don’t see a colophon, so I am utterly guessing
here), which makes the 8-point text even more legible.

Even though 8-point print is typical for a thinline, I
suspect that the Thinline NIV is more readable than the norm, so someone who
wants a thinline but can’t handle the usual thinline print size might be well
advised to give it a try.  I don’t have a
problem with text above 6 points or so, so the Thinline NIV gave me no issues
whatsoever.  Nothing negative stands out
to me about its time in my office, and I suppose that means it did a good job.

**CONCLUSION.**  Even though it’s a thinline, the Thinline NIV
punches above its weight as a preacher’s Bible. 
Translation issues aside (and I shall adhere to my policy of leaving
translation issues to those more qualified than I to discuss them), the Bible
served me well.  I think it deserving of
a 4.5/5.
