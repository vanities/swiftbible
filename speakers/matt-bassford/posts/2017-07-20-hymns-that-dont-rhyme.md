In hymns, “structure” is just another way of saying
“repetition”.  Every structural element
is a repeated element.  Meter is a
regular, repeated number of syllables per line. 
Rhythm is a repeating pattern of strong and weak stresses.

Hymns contain some kind of repeating element because they
don’t work if they don’t.  Worshipers
like repetition.  It makes a hymn
predictable and intuitive, so that they can focus on worship instead of
worrying about what’s going to happen next.

However, regular meter and rhythm aren’t enough to get a
hymn off the ground by themselves.  Hymns
always need some kind of extra structural element to keep them from feeling
shapeless.  I’m not aware of any hymns
that we use that have regular meter and rhythm and nothing else.

Traditionally, the most common additional structure comes
from rhymes.  Rhyme too is a form of
repetition.  It involves repeated sounds
at the end of lines.  The more similar
these repeated sounds are to each other (full rhyme versus half rhyme) and the
closer they are to each other, the stronger the structure and the more likely
worshipers are to buy into the hymn.



**Pure Repetition**

However, rhyme is not the only way to add structure.  There are other ways to get the job done, and
the most obvious of these is pure repetition. 
If you are repeating not only rhythm, meter, and concluding sounds, but
whole lines (and repetition is most powerful when a whole sentence is
repeated), you are obviously adding a huge amount of predictability!

In practice, though, pure repetition is a two-edged
sword.  It has a dramatically limiting
effect on content.  After all, if you
repeat a line twice, you’re saying half as much as if you weren’t using
repetition at all.

The extent of the problem this causes will vary drastically
depending on the content of the repeated phrase.  An older hymn that uses pure repetition is
Fanny J. Crosby’s “Though Your Sins Be As Scarlet”.  It works just fine because the repeated
phrases in v. 1 (which determines whether the congregation will buy in or not)
are straight quotations from Isaiah 1:18 in the KJV.  As a rule, powerful phrases from Scripture
will bear up very well under repetition.

However, there are limits even to this.  For instance, for the past several days, I’ve
had [this praise song](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fwHp693qOIc) stuck in my head.   I'm actually OK with that.  The
music is appealing (even if the end-of-line melodic ornamentation is a little
dated at this point), and quoting from Psalm 113 (again in the KJV) gets buy-in
from me.  I would even lead a version of
this that read:

*From the rising of the sun*

*To the going down of
the same,*

*The name of the Lord
shall be praised;*

*From the rising of the
sun*

*To the going down of
the same,*

*The name of the Lord
shall be praised.*

*So praise ye the Lord,*

*Praise ye the Lord;*

*From the rising of the
sun*

*To the going down of
the same,*

*The name of the Lord
shall be praised.*

After that, though, I start losing interest.  “From the rising of the sun. . .” can hold my
attention for three repetitions.  Four or
more is too many to keep me focused.

I’ve run into similar reactions to my own work.  For example, a few years back, I floated
this:

*A single sheep, a
single sheep,*

*The shepherd goes to
find a single sheep.*

*How his neighbors will
rejoice*

*For the sake of a
single sheep!*

*A single coin, a
single coin,*

*The woman searches for
a single coin.*

*How her neighbors will
rejoice*

*For the sake of a
single coin!*

*A single son, a single
son,*

*The father watches for
a single son.*

*How his household will
rejoice*

*For the sake of a
single son!*

*A single soul, a
single soul,*

*Our Father watches for
a single soul.*

*How His angels will
rejoice*

*For the sake of a
single soul!*

It didn’t go.  The
reaction I got was, “This would make a great children’s song!”  In other words, there was too much repetition
and not enough content to hold the attention of adults.

The further away a repetition is from Scripture, the more
quickly this reaction will appear.  The
poster child here is the radio hymn “It Won’t Be Very Long”.  The repeated phrase isn’t in the Bible, and
I’m not even sure that it’s true.  We don’t
know the day nor the hour, after all. 
The hymn’s a lot of fun to sing (what with the snappy rhythms in the
chorus and all), but it doesn’t offer much for the thoughtful worshiper.

Pure repetition is structurally powerful.  Many great hymns use it.  However, if overused, it can defeat the
purposes of Colossians 3:16.

**Parallelism**

The other alternative that appears in already-existing hymns
is parallelism.  Lines in hymns can be
parallel in thought (they say similar things), parallel in structure, or
both.

Nadia Hearn’s “Jesus, Name Above All Names” gets its
structure from thought parallels (admittedly, the hymn does rhyme, sort of, but
the rhyme scheme is so diffuse that it doesn’t add much).  Every line in the hymn after the first is a
name or title of the Christ, which allows worshipers to anticipate what will
come next.  Departing from that content
would break the structure, and the hymn wouldn’t work.

Parallel structure also works as a rhyme substitute.  Among other hymns, this is apparent in Glenda
Schales’s “You Are My Strength”, the first verse of which reads,

*You are my strength;*

*You are my shield;*

*You are my help, my
deliv’rer;*

*You are my rock;*

*You are my hope,*

*You are my all, my Redeemer.*

Once again, this is a hymn with light rhyme, but the rhyme
isn’t the key structural element. 
Instead, the hymn holds together because of the repeated grammatical
structure of “You are my \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_.”  The
various descriptors of God aren’t necessarily similar (rock isn’t much like
hope), but they’re tied together anyway by the grammatical frame in which they’re
presented.  This difference is actually
important to the hymn.  It makes it much
more interesting than repeating “You are my strength” six times would be.

**Other Possible Alternatives**

There may well be other ways to structure a hymn.  Anglo-Saxon poetry used alliteration, but I’m
not aware of any attempts to write a hymn (or even a more modern poem) that
uses alliteration as a structural mainstay. 
Even Gerard Manley Hopkins’s ["The Windhover"](http://www.bartleby.com/122/12.html) mixes heavy alliteration with complicated rhyme.  A factor complicating the use of alliteration
is that in order to sing well, the alliteration must be exact.  Inexact alliteration literally produces tongue-twisters
(as with “sweeps smooth” in “The Windhover”). 
Alliteration also often has a humorous effect, which isn’t exactly the
typical object of a hymn.

By contrast, it would probably be possible to structure a
hymn using strong assonance.  Our guide
here is not the way that others have written functional hymns before, but
rather rap music.  I’m not kidding.  Hymns are folk poetry; so is rap.  Though I’ve never seen anybody try (at least
in hymns as a genre rather than in Christian rap as a genre), the structural
elements should translate.

Basically, what matters is not whether a hymn rhymes.  What matters is whether it has enough
structure to cohere for a congregation. 
That’s the bar that every successful hymn must clear.  How you clear it is your business.
