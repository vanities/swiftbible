This ought to be my final installment about a post I saw
circulating on Facebook last week.  Once
again, for the sake of clarity, here it is:


*Hello beloved saints,*

*I’m
sure you know that NIV was published by Zondervan but is now OWNED by Harper
Collins, who also publishes the Satanic Bible and the Joy of Gay Sex.*

*The
NIV has now removed 64,575 words from the Bible including Jehovah, Calvary,
Holy Ghost and omnipotent to name but a few. . .*

*The
NIV has also now removed 45 complete verses. 
Most of us have the Bible on our devices and phones.*

*Try
and find these scriptures in NIV on your computer, phone or device right now if
you are in doubt:*

*Matthew
17:21, 18:11, 23:14; Mark 7:16, 9:44, 9:46; Luke 17:36, 23:17; John 5:4; Acts
8:37*

*.
. . you will not believe your eyes.*

*Refuse
to be blinded by Satan, and do not act like you just don’t care.  Let’s not forget what the Lord Jesus said in
John 10:10 (King James Version)*

In Part 1 and Part 2, I explained why this isn’t true and
pointed to evidence that it was written by somebody who believes that only the
King James Version is inspired.  However,
there’s one more issue here that’s worth talking about, and it’s the other half
of the reason why Facebook friends of mine whom I consider to be intelligent,
competent people were taken in by it.

The key lies in the original poster’s emphasis on electronic
versions of the Bible.  The poster notes,
“Most of us have the Bible on our devices and phones.”  He encourages the reader to “Try and find
these scriptures in the NIV on your computer, phone or device right now if you
are in doubt.”  Note, by the way, that
the poster apparently does not believe in the Oxford comma.  I do, which I suppose is another point of
philosophical contention between us.

Oxford commas aside, his focus on the NIV in Bible apps is
no accident.  As I’ve already observed,
The NIV does indeed omit Matthew 17:21. 
In paper NIV’s, the verse number for Matthew 17:21 is bracketed and it
has a footnote reference next to it.  The
corresponding footnote explains that the verse is only found in some
manuscripts.  In a paper Bible, there is
no mystery about what’s going on here.

However, on an electronic device, it’s a different story, as
my wife and I discovered.  She actually
saw the original post before I did and looked up Matthew 17:21 on her
smartphone.  What she saw puzzled her
enough that she beckoned me over to look at it. 
Matthew 17:21 was still gone on the screen, but the verse number wasn’t
bracketed, and it didn’t have an explanatory footnote.  To the suspicious reader, it might indeed
look like the publishers of the NIV arbitrarily decided to remove the
verse.  We also checked the NASB, which
brackets the verse, but even in the NASB, it takes a screen tap to bring up the
explanatory footnote.

This points to something that we need to acknowledge in our
age of technological marvels.  An
electronic Bible is not the same thing as a paper Bible.  In the interests of full disclosure, let me
hasten to acknowledge that I’m a paper-Bible fan.  I’m also something of a Luddite.  My wife and I looked all this up on her
smartphone because I own neither smartphone nor tablet.  There’s no doubt where my sympathies lie
here.

Let me also hasten to acknowledge that electronic Bibles
have a number of advantages.  They’re easier
to use while you’re trying to manage a squirming toddler.  They’re lighter.  They can offer super-duper large print
without sacrificing small size.  They
also offer a study depth that no paper Bible can.  Depending on how much you’ve spent on your
Bible app, whole libraries of commentaries and Bible helps may well be only a
few taps away.  I’m sure there are other
advantages that I’ve forgotten that e-Bible proponents might add.

However, in exchange for that depth, electronic Bibles
necessarily sacrifice breadth.  A paper
Bible is an object in a way that an e-Bible is not.  When you use a paper Bible, you are holding
the whole thing in your hands.  By
contrast, the only part of an e-Bible that has a physical existence is what is
actually displayed on the screen.  The
rest has been banished into the electronic ether until it is next summoned
forth.

Likewise, there are limits to what an e-Bible can display on
the screen, particularly when it comes to smartphones.  I’ve written a lot about design constraints
in paper Bibles.  I would imagine that
designing the screen display for a Bible app is equally challenging.  You can include links to 57,000 different
things on the screen, but if you do that, the actual text of the Bible vanishes
into the clutter.

Alternatively, you can take the NIV approach and display the
text and only the text.  That’s clean and
elegant, but it omits important explanatory footnotes and leaves you vulnerable
to intellectually dishonest KJVO fanatics who are looking for an excuse to bash
modern translations.

I’m sure that thoughtful app designers agonize over striking
a balance between these two extremes, but their options will always be limited
by the amount of real estate available on the screen.  You can find a lot more information in a
Bible app, but you have to actively go digging for it.  The paper Bible designer, by contrast, puts
the explanatory footnote right there on the page because he can afford to and
has no reason not to.

This points to an even more significant issue with
e-Bibles—the issue of context.  Any
opening of any paper Bible contains more of the Bible than a tablet or
smartphone screen can.  All that context
is right there on the page, awaiting only the inquisitive eye.  By contrast, if you want the context on a
smartphone, you have to go scrolling for it, and once you go scrolling for it,
you lose the verse you were originally looking at!  Furthermore, it is possible to do a
single-verse search in an e-Bible and pull up that verse without any context at
all.

It’s a subtle thing, but I worry that it will have unhelpful
consequences for the way that Christians understand the Bible.  A Bible is not a dictionary.  Each verse may well have meaning, but that
meaning is clarified and amplified by the logic of the whole context.  We’re good at understanding the former, but
we’re not so good at understanding the latter. 
We’re much better at proof text than context.  Everybody can quote John 3:16, but few indeed
are those who can explain Jesus’ overall argument in John 3.  When we already need to work less on our
proof texts and more on our contexts, a method of displaying the Bible that
emphasizes proof text over context can only make the problem worse.

My point here is not that we should imitate the Ephesians of Acts 19:19 by piling up our iPads
and burning them.  After all, I’m still on
record as saying that the best Bible for anybody is the Bible that he will use
the most.  However, we do need to be
mindful of the way that our choice of paper or plastic shapes the way we use
the Bible.  Every paper Bible comes with
a set of drawbacks.  Otherwise, we’d all
have giant-print compact wide-margin reference Bibles printed on completely
opaque paper.  So too does the choice to
use an electronic Bible.  Whatever choice
we make, we need to be aware of what we’re giving up.
