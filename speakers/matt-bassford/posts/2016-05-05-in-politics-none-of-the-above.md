It’s kind of a strange feeling to watch a bunch of my
friends and brethren showing up where I’ve been for years.  I’m used to being weird, and this sudden mass
agreement is unsettling me.  I refer, of
course, to the spate of disgusted declarations from Facebook friends that if
their two choices in the general election are Donald Trump and Hillary Clinton,
they’re going to abstain.

From the perspective of godliness, I don’t have any trouble
understanding their unhappiness.  So far
as I can tell, both major candidates are as crooked as a corkscrew.  Both of them worship devoutly at the shrine
of money and power.  These are not good
people.  I wouldn’t trust either one of
them to babysit my children, much less run my country.  And yet, here we are.



**Conflicted
Christianity**

However, I don’t think there’s anything unprecedented about
the present distress.  Instead, I think
it’s bringing into clearer focus a problem that has always existed.  Christianity is about ideals.  It’s about conforming oneself to the image of
Christ.  Politics is about
compromise.  It’s about building a
coalition with a bunch of people you don’t agree with to defeat another bunch
of people you REALLY don’t agree with.

Political Christianity, then, has always been something of
an oxymoron.  If you don’t compromise
what you believe, you can’t win elections. 
If you do compromise, you forfeit your ideals.

Others can and will disagree in good conscience, but this
has begun to look to me like a 2 Corinthians 6:14-18 issue.  “Do not be unequally yoked with unbelievers,”
Paul says.  “Go out from their midst, and
be separate from them.”  Making common
political cause with those who are not Christians in New-Testament terms
doesn’t look like going out and being separate. 
It looks like being unequally yoked. 
Voting for the lesser evil is still voting for evil.

**Value Creep**

In addition, the more we make common political cause with
others, the more we come to share their values. 
I do not, for instance, see the New Testament as a robust endorsement of
laissez-faire, dog-eat-dog capitalism. 
Instead, New Testament figures from Jesus on down are much more
concerned with the plight of the poor. 
In Scripture, love of money is the root of all evil, not the necessary underpinning
of society.

From the way that many Christians talk about poverty,
though, one might almost get the impression that *Atlas Shrugged* is one of the books of the Bible.  The poor are lazy, greedy good-for-nothings
who deserve only contempt from us hard-working types.  That’s politics corrupting our morality, not
our morality influencing our politics.

**Lost in Translation**

In reply, many will say that they believe in helping the
poor personally, but that they are skeptical of government intervention to do
the same.  There’s some validity to that
argument, but it also highlights another problem with political
involvement:  the values of Christianity
translate poorly to politics.  The Law of
Moses was unquestionably a political document. 
It contained the rules for running an Israelite theocracy.

However, the law of
Christ is not.  It is concerned primarily
with the behavior of the individual, secondarily with the behavior of the local
church.  When it comes to government, the
New Testament speaks of obedience, not political involvement.

The attempt to translate these individual values to the
political sphere is awkward at best. 
Discipleship is voluntary; government is coercive.  I am 100 percent convinced that Jesus calls
me to be sexually pure, both in action and in thought.  If I’m politically involved, does that mean
that I should call on the state to outlaw fornication and pornography?  That’s a really tough question, not least
because coerced righteousness is not actually righteousness at all.  Should the government permit fornication and
pornography while opposing the practice of homosexuality?  It’s awfully difficult to come up with a
principled basis for that distinction.

How about the poor people from a couple paragraphs up?  I’m unquestionably supposed to help them on
an individual basis.  Does that mean that
the government should help them too? 
These questions are much more difficult than many politically involved
Christians have allowed them to be.

Discipleship is not easy, but at least it is simple.  Discipleship in politics is neither easy nor
simple.

**Politics as a
Distraction**

Furthermore, I fear that political activism often serves to
distract us from our real work.  We
cannot win the world for Christ in the voting booth.  The government may be able to change actions,
but only the gospel can change hearts. 
Sadly, changing hearts is much more difficult than pulling a lever.  It requires boldness, faith, patience, and
hard work.  It’s hardly surprising, then,
that many Christians opt for the easier route.

In a world of limited resources, this may well have a
disastrous effect on the work of the kingdom. 
Most of us are aware that the number of self-proclaimed Christians in
the U.S. is in decline.  This is true
despite the massive political power that such people still wield.  In fact, their political efforts have often
led their political opponents to condemn Christianity in general.  Coercive Christianity hasn’t had its intended
consequence, and it’s had a lot of unintended ones.

**Learning from Chinese
Christians**

By contrast, consider the explosive growth of Christianity
in China.  According to this article http://www.telegraph.co.uk/news/worldnews/asia/china/10776023/China-on-course-to-become-worlds-most-Christian-nation-within-15-years.html
(one of the most heartening I’ve read in a good while), if demographic trends
continue, by 2030, China will have more Christians in it than any other
country.  This has happened despite the
utter lack of political influence that Christianity has in China.  Indeed, the flow of influence runs the other
way.  The Chinese government seeks to
regulate and restrict Christians.

Perhaps the key is that in a society where Christians know
they have no influence, they devote themselves to being Christians.  Nobody likes to give up power.  Nobody likes being told what to do.  However, everybody likes people who are
cheerful, kind, hard-working, and selfless. 
The force of godly example is far more powerful than any slate of
political initiatives ever can be.

Why don’t we, then, take a page from the playbook of Chinese
Christians?  If the political process is
loathsome, don’t invest in it.  Instead,
invest in godliness.  Find a neighbor to
help.  Find some poor people to
feed.  Find an extra mile to go.  Rejoice, even in the midst of trial.  Be a light. 
Above all, be open about where you hope to go and the One whom you’re
following to get there.

I don’t know where our nation is headed.  50 years ago, who could have imagined the
spiritual flowering in China today?  I do
know this, though:  If there is hope for
our nation, it does not lie in the outcome of any election.  It lies in the lives of thousands of humble
people who choose to take up their crosses and follow Jesus.

Let us go and do likewise.
