We live in a world where the text of the Bible is accessible
to nearly anybody.  It’s probably true
that even people who aren’t Christians at all have the Bible in an app on their
smartphone someplace.  As a result, it’s
easy for us to lose sight of the fact that first-century Christians didn’t have
individual copies of the Scriptures, and that the New Testament was originally
written to be read out loud.  As we
prepare for the Lord’s Supper this morning, then, let’s do so with an extended
reading, broken up with only a few comments from me.  Let’s consider Mark’s account of the death of
Jesus in Mark 15:21-16:8.



The first section of this reading is about **HIS CRUCIFIXION.** This is described in Mark 15:21-32.  There’s so much in here to discuss, but this
morning I want to focus on one thing in particular, and that is the mental
torment that Jesus endured while He was being crucified.  The Romans didn’t crucify people in secluded,
out-of-the-way places.  Instead, they
crucified as publicly as possible, especially alongside major roads.  There were a couple of reasons for this.  First, they wanted the dying man to serve as
an example of what happened to those who dared defy the might of Rome.  Second, they wanted that death to be as
humiliating as possible.

Here, as though it were not enough to slowly suffocate, we
see that Jesus must endure the taunts of His enemies, who have come out of the
city to fill His final hours with misery. 
Ironically, some of what they say is true in a way that they don’t
realize themselves.  Jesus cannot save
Himself precisely because He is in the midst of saving others.

Second, the Scripture describes **HIS DEATH.** Let’s read from
Mark 15:33-39. This reading begins with Jesus’ famous cry, “My God, My God, why
have You forsaken Me?”  This, of course,
is not an original sentiment.  Instead,
Jesus is quoting the first verse of Psalm 22, and I think He does this for two
reasons.  First, it was to point His
audience to the many amazing similarities between a psalm 1000 years old and
His own suffering and death.  Second, it
was to remind them that even though Psalm 22 begins with a cry of abandonment,
it concludes with deliverance and thanksgiving, which is where Jesus’ story
will conclude too.

Also, let’s consider the strange occurrence of the curtain
of the temple tearing in two from top to bottom at the moment of His
death.  This is no small thing!  Contemporary historians report that the
temple curtain was four inches thick.  I
don’t think any force but God could have torn it in pieces.  God did it to illustrate that even though
Jesus was as far from Him as He had ever been, by His death, the whole human
race now had the opportunity to draw near.

Third, we see **HIS
BURIAL.** It’s described in Mark
15:40-47.  The thing to appreciate about
this reading, I think, is the witnesses. 
It describes His female followers, who, along with others not mentioned
here, looked on as He died.  Some of
these same women were also with Joseph of Arimathea when he buried Jesus in his
own tomb.

At the time, these witnesses didn’t know what they were
doing, but they were setting the scene for the resurrection.  Unless Jesus is known to be dead, it makes no
sense to proclaim that He has risen. 
Unless His followers have seen where He is buried, then they cannot find
that the same tomb is now empty.  What we
see here are the building blocks for a miracle.

Finally, we come to **HIS
RESURRECTION.** Let’s read here from
Mark 16:1-8.  People who know nothing
about the Bible would probably expect that the grim mood of the story of the
crucifixion will lighten when the story of the resurrection is told, but that’s
exactly what doesn’t happen.  These first
witnesses that Jesus has risen don’t initially experience joy.  Instead, they’re filled with confusion and
fear.  They were expecting to mourn one
whom they loved, but instead, they have been confronted with the strangest and
most spectacular miracle of them all.

Today, we should be no less awed.  The empty tomb and the words of the angel
mean that on that first day of the week, the rules for what it meant to be a
human being changed forever.  The
resurrection of the Christ means that we can solemnly anticipate our own.
