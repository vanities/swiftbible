I’m a guy with lots of ideas about how things should be
done.  Just ask my wife.  I also like to write.  The conjunction of the two means that I spend
a lot of time on the Internet writing lengthy posts and e-mails to people about
how I think they ought to do things. 
Generally, these get ignored.

I was delighted, then, to receive a lengthy and gracious
response from Foundation to my screed about how they should handle their
re-set.  It came from Pike Lambeth,
Foundation’s executive vice president.  I
read through it, decided it would be of interest to the Bible-dork community,
and received permission from him to paraphrase it for my blog.



**First, Pike confirmed
that Foundation is going to be using ITC Weidemann for all of its future
settings.**  In my opinion, this is a
solid choice.  Weidemann was originally
developed for use in a German Bible in the 1980s, so it works well at smaller
sizes.  I find the 7.8-point Weidemann in
my Zondervan Thinline NASB to be very readable, for instance.  Weidemann also scales well.  It still looks good at 14 points in the
current Foundation Giant-Print Reference. 
Third, the decision to set everything in Weidemann will add cohesiveness
to Foundation’s lineup, just like all of Crossway’s Bibles are in Lexicon No. 1
and all of Zondervan’s NIV editions are set in Utopia.

In addition, I’m glad that Foundation has resisted the
current trend of setting Bibles in sans-serif typefaces.  Sans-serif text may work well on a computer
screen, but it’s harder to read on the printed page.  Also, I believe that a timeless text deserves
a classic setting, and I suspect that in a few years, those trendy sans-serif
settings will have become dated.

My one unhappiness with the decision to use Weidemann is
that I don’t think Weidemann has text figures for numbers, which means that
cross-references and so forth will be in titling figures.  However, there is certainly an argument to be
made that cross-references *should* be
in titling figures, so my grumbling here is slight.

**Second, he informed
me that Foundation will employ subtle text headings.**  They will be set in a sans-serif font, smaller
than the text itself, and flush left.  Even
though I don’t particularly care for text headings myself, this is a decision I
can appreciate.  There are certainly
Bible aficionados who like in-text headings as much as I don’t like them, and
to me, headings are at their worst when they are big, bold, and break up the
flow of the text.  The smaller the
headings are, the less obtrusive they will be.

I do have one concern about the smaller point size of the
headings, though.  The current Foundation
Side-Column Reference, which was set in 1996, already employs smaller
headings.  Because these smaller headings
take up less vertical space than a normal line of text, they throw off the
line-matching.  As much as I like the
idea of smaller headings, I’m not willing to sacrifice line-matching for their
sake.  Hopefully, Foundation will be able
to adjust the leading on lines containing headings to keep this from being a
problem.

**Third, all the new
Foundation editions will be left-aligned.**This is an unusual choice that may well be unique.  Every Bible that I’m familiar with is
justified, with the text “stretched out” to align with both left and right
margins (or with the gutter, in the case of double-column Bibles).  The reasoning here is simple.  It looks pretty.

Theologically appropriate though it may be, it is
likely that on practical grounds, Bibles should not be justified.  As I noted last week, studies have shown that
left-aligned text is easier to read than justified text.  The culprit is spacing.  Spacing between words is consistent from line
to line in left-aligned text, but the “stretching” of justified text means that
one line may have great big spaces between words, while the next has little
tiny spaces.

This problem is most obvious in Bibles with narrow columns,
like the current Foundation Giant-Print Reference.  As I commented in my review of the GPR, some
lines of text look like they have been double-spaced because the words have
been stretched out so much.  The GPR is
still a good Bible (and one I recommend), but the spacing issues distract me
when I’m reading from it.  Similarly, a
Bible-dork buddy of mine won’t use double-column Crossway Bibles at all because
the spacing varies from line to line and drives him crazy.

By left-aligning all its text, Foundation is going to remove
these subtle obstacles to reading comprehension.  I doubt that typical readers will notice the
change, but they will benefit from its effects.

**Fourth, Pike revealed
that the new Foundation settings will have more prominent book titles.**  This is purely an aesthetic issue, but it is
good news nonetheless.  Many of
Foundation’s current settings are the typographical equivalent of sensible
shoes.  They’re as practical as they can
be, but they do little to delight the eye.

The almost-casual treatment of book titles in the SCR is
symptomatic of this problem.  For
instance, the title of 2nd Corinthians in the SCR appears halfway down the
page, following the last dozen verses of 1st Corinthians 16, in the same
typeface as the text, not boldfaced, and with only a miserly margin of white
space around it.

Now, there’s nothing wrong with that.  Nobody’s soul is going to be imperiled
because the title of 2nd Corinthians doesn’t look pretty.  However, there could be so much more right
with it.  2nd Corinthians is one of the
foundational documents of the Christian faith. 
It should be given the opportunity to announce itself with dignity, in
the midst of a lordly expanse of white space. 
The costs here are small.  66
generously laid-out book titles still aren’t going to take up much room (we
aren’t selling used cars here, after all), but they will set the tone for the
entire text.

**Fifth, Foundation
will work to minimize red-letter settings.** 
This too is good news.  Of course,
neither Foundation nor any other publisher can do away with red-letter settings
entirely.  As the comment sections on
Amazon and Christianbook attest, lots and lots of folks still want their Words
Of Christ In Red.  However, red-letter
Bibles are hard to execute well. 
Generally, mass-market Bibles offer The Words Of Christ In Orange or The
Words Of Christ In Pink instead.  Those
can be extremely difficult to read, especially for Christians with aging
eyes.  It’s much simpler to produce a
readable black-letter Bible, and Bible snobs tend to find the look of
black-letter to be classier anyway.

Pike noted that Foundation still has a lot of decisions to
make.  I suspect, for instance, that they
are still debating whether they want to line-match their entire lineup (a leap
that no mass-market publisher has made yet). 
However, it sounds to me like many of the decisions they have already
made are good, and that they are working to correct problems with their
existing lineup.

This should be welcome news to all fans of the NASB.  Issues with cockling aside, the quality of
Foundation’s products has been consistently good.  I believe they went to Smyth-sewing the vast
majority of their Bible lineup way back in the late 1990s, and nobody publishes
a better genuine-leather Bible than Foundation. 
If they’re able to combine this tradition of durable construction with
fresh, attractive new settings, students of the word everywhere should benefit.
