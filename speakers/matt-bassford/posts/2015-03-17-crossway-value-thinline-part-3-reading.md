For various reasons, the Premium Gift Bible got a longer-than-usual tour of duty as my morning reader.  I read from it daily for about two and a half
weeks.  Here’s what I thought of it:

**SIZE.**Because it is a thinline, the Premium Gift Bible is moderate in terms of
Bible size and light in terms of weight. 
This makes it great for reading. 
I didn’t even bother bracing it against my breastbone when I used
it.  I merely one-handed it in my off
hand and never gave the heft of the Bible a second thought.  Even though it’s a little larger than a
paperback, I would still say that its form is as comfortable as a paperback’s.

**LAYOUT.**  The Premium Gift Bible is a double-column, paragraphed Bible, just like every thinline that Crossway produces.  As a rule, I prefer Bibles like this for my
reading Bibles, and generally, the Premium Gift Bible doesn’t disappoint.  Because it is a text Bible, without
center-column references, there’s lots of room on the page for nice wide
columns, and indeed a column in the prose sections of the Premium Gift Bible averages something like 43 characters. 
That’s plenty for my eye to take in at one glance.  Also, the column width helps the Thinline
text block avoid the wide variations in spacing that are common in Bibles with
narrower columns.  I don’t remember
seeing any sections of text that looked like they’d been worked over by a
redneck with a .22.

Crossway’s setting, though,
differs from the norm in several different ways, some of which are good, some
of which are bad.  On the credit side of
the ledger, even though I think there are prettier layouts out there,
Crossway’s stylistic choice of unusually high superscript numbers and unusually
generous leading works well.  When I’m
preaching or teaching from a Bible, I want nice conspicuous verse numbers, and
the Premium Gift Bible’s numbering system is conspicuous enough that it catches my
eye.

On the other hand, when I’m
reading from a Bible, I don’t want the numbers to distract me.  In the Premium Gift Bible, they don’t.  I have a Nelson’s Teachers’ Edition ASV
that’s at least 60 years old, and even though it too is paragraphed and
double-column, its verse numbers are the same size as the text, and they are
set in line with it.  As a result, I find
myself reading passages like, “. . . a ringleader of the sect of the Nazarenes
6 who moreover assayed. . .”  That is,
shall we say, distracting.  The Premium Gift Bible verse numbers, though, are on the other end of the continuum.  I don’t notice them at all.

That’s good.  Less good, however, is Crossway’s treatment
of poetry in the Premium Gift Bible.  The
most common charge leveled against double-column Bibles is that the format
imposes awkward line breaks in the poetic sections of the Bible.  Too often, you’ll have 90 percent of a poetic
line on one line with a solitary word dangling below.

Even though I prefer double
column, I’ll acknowledge that many double-column Bibles do make a hash out of
poetry.  This is particularly a problem
with center-column reference Bibles; their columns are inevitably narrower, so
they cause more stragglers.  Text Bibles,
by contrast, usually have fewer problems with this.  Wider columns mean longer lines mean fewer
truncated lines of poetry.

Sadly, even though the Premium Gift Bible is a text Bible, it handles the poetry of the Bible badly.  Its typesetters made a great decision when
they settled on high-superscript verse numbers. 
However, they didn’t do as well in their treatment of poetry.  Most designers, even the designers of my
antedeluvian ASV, are conspicuously aware that one-word “widow” lines of poetry
are A Bad Thing.  In order to make as few
widows as possible, they make their poetic lines as long as possible.  A usual poetic line starts right next to the
verse number on the left and runs allll the way across the column.

Not so in the Premium Gift Bible.  Instead, every first poetic
line in a verse is indented over, almost like the first line of a
paragraph.   The second and succeeding lines in a verse are
indented over even beyond that.

The overall effect is not
terrible because Crossway’s Thinline text block is wider than those of the
other double-column text Bibles I own. 
In consequence, it doesn’t have more widows than a Zondervan Thinline
NASB.  However, some of the widows it
does have are miserable; for reasons that escape me, sometimes Crossway adopted
a hanging syllable rather than a hanging word. 
Also, I can’t help but feel that the poetic setting would have been so
much better if Crossway’s designers had simply taken advantage of the white
space that was there to use.

**READABILITY.** I didn’t have
any problem making out the text, but I suspect that others would.  The Premium Gift Bible is set in 8-point Lexicon
No. 1 with massive amounts of leading, but the letters themselves look smaller
than the 8/9 Swift in my Cambridge REB. 
Also, the paper in the Premium Gift Bible is about what you’d expect from a
thinline.  It’s most likely between 20-30
gsm, it’s not very opaque, and the setting isn’t line-matched.  As a result, there’s plenty of ghosting.  I wasn’t particularly troubled by it, but if
you prefer that your Bibles not have infestations from the spirit world, you
probably would be.

Interestingly, I had low
expectations for the Premium Gift Bible as a preaching Bible, but it proved to be
quite capable in that employment.  In
this section of my review, I had the opposite kind of surprise.  The Premium Gift Bible should have been an
excellent reader, but it was merely very good. 
In particular, its poetry sections could have been and should have been
better laid out.  I didn’t have any
problems with the prose sections, but nonetheless, I award it a 9/10.   Overall, then, this Bible, which is probably
the cheapest one I’ve ever reviewed, nets a very respectable 26/30.
