I’ve been known to observe from time to time that the church
would be a wonderful institution, if only it didn’t have people in it.  Every church problem starts with
Christians.  They can be lazy, selfish,
contentious, and just plain evil.  If you
can think of a way to wreck God’s plan for the church, some Christian somewhere
has done it.

As a result, it’s easy for us to read about how the church
ought to be, compare that to the way that our local congregation is, and get dissatisfied
about the difference.  Brethren should be
doing this, and this, and this, and they aren’t doing any of it!  Instead, too many of them are thoughtlessly
or even eagerly doing the very things that they ought not to be doing!  When we compare our own lifelong record of
martyrdom for the Lord’s sake, it’s tempting to throw our hands up and say, “If
they’re not going to work, I’m not going to work either.”



As Daffy Duck might say, this is a case of pronoun
trouble.  We’re right to compare, but
we’ve picked the wrong comparison. 
Instead of comparing ourselves to them, we ought to be comparing
ourselves to Him.

Once we do that, all of the other church problems fade away,
except for the one church problem that we ought to be focusing on—our own behavior.  Comparing ourselves to the other fallible
members of the Lord’s church allows us to forget that we ourselves are
fallible.  Comparing ourselves to the
infallible Lord does not.

When we use Jesus for the standard, the differences between
us and the most worthless, underperforming member of our congregation fade into
insignificance.  For us, Jesus did
literally everything He could.  Lower
Himself to do the most undignified, menial tasks?  Check. 
Persevere through every difficulty and trial?  Check. 
Deal patiently with clueless disciples? 
Check?  Love everybody, even if
they manifestly didn’t deserve it? 
Check.

The word “martyr” means “witness”, and when I (or anyone
else) compare my supposed martyrdom to the life of the original Faithful Witness,
I don’t have any doubt about how short I fall. 
Other Christians sure can be lazy, selfish, contentious, and just plain
evil, but so can I.  Indeed, so have I
been.  For that matter, so will I be in
future.  I’m not the victim of the
imperfection of God’s church.  I’m part
of the problem, and I always will be.

As tempting as it is to criticize the behavior of others,
whether directly to them or (much more commonly) to a sympathetic third party,
criticism doesn’t do anything to solve the only problem we can solve:  the problem of ourselves.  Of course, solving this problem is hard.  It requires me to lower myself to do
undignified, menial tasks.  It requires
me to persevere through every difficulty and trial.  It requires me to deal patiently with clueless
disciples.

Above all, it requires me to love everybody, even if they
manifestly don’t deserve it, which indeed is nothing less than the essence of
the Christian faith.  God didn’t love us
because we deserved it.  He loved us even
though we didn’t.  The story of the
gospel is the story of undeserved love.

This is a tall order. 
These things, and many other equally worthy ways of imitating Jesus,
require a lifetime’s worth of effort to pursue. 
In this life, we’re never going to get there.  However, no matter which body of imperfect
people we’ve chosen to associate with, the more we try to be like Jesus, the
more we will make that body better.  As
Jesus said to Peter, it doesn’t matter what anybody else is (or isn’t)
doing.  Our job is to follow Him.
