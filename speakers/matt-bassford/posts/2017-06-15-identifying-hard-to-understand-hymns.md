I believe that one of the most important attributes that a
sacred song can have is Biblical content. 
It’s hard to glorify God or teach and admonish one another unless the
hymn hands you some content with which to do it.  However, near or complete absence of content
is not the only way to go wrong here.  A
hymnist can have something to say, yet not be able to present that to the
congregation in a useful fashion.  In my
estimation, here are the five things that are most likely to make a hymn hard
to understand.



**Poor Concept**

A few months ago, I was talking with a preacher friend of
mine about a gospel meeting he attended. 
He replied, “There were two good sermons there.  The problem was that the preacher was
preaching both of them at the same time.” 
I knew exactly what he meant.  I
can easily think back to sermons I’ve heard in which the preacher tried to
address two concepts so distantly related that they couldn’t fit logically in
the same lesson.  As the two themes
battled it out for supremacy, the struggle between them left the congregation
confused.

It’s easy for novice hymnists especially to fall into the
same trap.  They love God and they love
the Bible and they love the idea of writing hymns, and there are so many
wonderful subjects to choose from that they want to put them all in their first
effort!  The result is a collection of
earnest, well-meant phrases that don’t have a whole lot to do with each
other.  It’s important to know what you
want to write about in a hymn, but it’s equally important to know what you want
to leave out.

**Poor Structure**

Just as a hymn needs one overall point, so each verse in it
needs one point.  I’m reminded here of
the infamous first verse of “Let Me Live Close to Thee Each Day”, which has been a punching bag in the Hymninar for many years:

*In the field I would
wield sickles brave and true;*

*In the fight for the
right I would dare and do;*

*Spend my days in Thy
praise all the journey through;*

*Let me live close to
Thee each day!*

Let’s see.  In the
first line, we’re harvesting; in the second line, we’re fighting; in the third
line, we’re traveling; and somehow all of these are supposed to add up to
living close to God.  “Mixed metaphor”
does not begin to describe the logical carnage!

I would logically associate none of those things with living
close to God (consider Fanny J. Crosby’s “Close to Thee” and Austin Taylor’s
“Closer to Thee” for much more intuitive treatments of the same subject).  If J. R. Baxter wanted to make the connection
between fighting and closeness, for instance, he probably should have spent at
least a verse explaining it.  As it is,
none of the first three lines do anything more to make the point of the verse
than the fourth line would have on its own. 
Unless the whole of any verse drives at the same point, it will bewilder
the thoughtful worshiper.

**Complicated Sentence
Structure**

In the English language, it’s possible to write a sentence
of any length imaginable.  If you string
enough introductory clauses and participial phrases together, you can make
Paul’s one-sentence chapters in Ephesians look like something out of *Dick and Jane*.  However, for the hymnist this is hardly advisable.  Overlong sentences will lose people.

Take, for instance, the venerable standard “How Sweet, How
Heavenly”.  Even though it’s not punctuated
this way, the first three verses of the hymn are actually one sentence, as are
the last two.  The logical structure is,
“How sweet, how heavenly is the sight when [all this other stuff happens]”  and “When [all this other stuff happens],
love is the golden chain that binds the happy souls above.”

However, I suspect it is an extremely rare worshiper who is
still thinking about the sweet, heavenly sight when they’re singing about
hiding their brother’s failings in v. 3. 
The hymn truly makes an excellent point there.  It is beautiful in the sight of God when we
bear with and forgive one another! 
However, it is obscured by the super-duper long sentence.

**Inversions**

Inversions are the bane of traditional hymnody.  Generally speaking, they’re the result of
writers not knowing how to write within the restrictions imposed by English
rhyme.  It’s hard to rhyme in English,
and so for centuries, hymnists contorted lines around so they could end them on
those all-important rhyming words.  Sadly,
the Yodaspeak does a pretty good job of obscuring meaning.  Take, for instance, the first quatrain of
“The Spacious Firmament”:

*The spacious firmament
on high*

*With all the blue,
ethereal sky*

*And spangled heav’ns,
a shining frame,*

*Their great Original
proclaim.*

That might be impenetrable to somebody without an English
degree (hint:  the last word of the
quatrain is the verb), but it’s nothing more than a paraphrase of the first
verse of Psalm 19: “The heavens declare the glory of God, and the sky above
proclaims His handiwork.”  It’s certainly
a worthy and important thought, but it’s masked by all the gyrations Joseph
Addison went through to cram rhymes into his paraphrase.

**Obscure Language**

Even though hymns are written in poetic form and look a lot
like poetry, there are many ways in which they differ from poetry.  A good poem is an expression of
individuality, but a good hymn speaks for the community of believers (which is
why few A+ hymns have been written by accomplished poets).  Similarly, a good poem is deliberately
allusive rather than explicit, inviting repeated readings in search of its
meaning.  A good hymn, by contrast, is
clear enough that the congregation can understand it after singing it once.

However, high-toned hymnists have a habit of adopting some
poetic conceits in their writing.  This
reduces clarity.  Unless they’ve studied
it, most Christians probably don’t know that “Night with ebon pinion brooded
o’er the vale,” means “Night sat on the valley like a black-winged bird on her
eggs.”  The first might be prettier, but
the second is much easier to understand.

The same holds true for obscure Biblical allusions.  “Here I raise my Ebenezer” needed much more
unpacking than Robert Robinson gave it in “O Thou Fount of Every Blessing”
(originally “Come, Thou Fount of Every Blessing”).  These days, Christians are likely to
encounter the story of Samuel and the stone of help only if they’re
specifically studying the hymn or reading through the Bible on their own.

I’m all for adopting language from the Bible, even if a new
Christian isn’t going to grasp the reference immediately.  Ideally, though, that language will make
sense even without the Biblical referent. 
I don’t think a worshiper has to recognize Genesis 8:22 and Jeremiah
31:35 in the second verse of “Great Is Thy Faithfulness” for that verse to be
meaningful.  That’s fine.  What’s less fine is when an attempt at poetry
or an allusion from some dusty corner of the word confuses the average singer.

**Conclusion**

1 Corinthians 14:15 calls us to sing praise with our spirits
and with our minds alike.  Christians
should strive for worship that is mindful as well as spirited, but to some
extent, this is out of the control of the ordinary saints in the pews.  A hazily written and poorly chosen hymn can
prevent them from understanding what they are singing, but they’re stuck with
it anyway.

Hymnists, then, need to write in a way that is easily
accessible and understandable.  Song
leaders need to make sure that they understand the hymns on their hymn
lists.  How many men through the years
have led “By Christ Redeemed” without having a clue what the third verse
meant?  When we learn to look for
obstacles to understanding in hymns, we make it much more likely that the songs
we offer will be both acceptable and effective.
