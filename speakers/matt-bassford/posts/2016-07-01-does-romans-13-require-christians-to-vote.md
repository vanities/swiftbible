About a day ago, my brother and friend Andy Diestelkamp
linked on Facebook to an article about Donald Trump’s supposed conversion and
warned that one of the dangers of politics is that it can lead to Christians
being “unequally yoked” with those whose values we do not share.  Not surprisingly, this post touched off a
great deal of discussion and debate.

One element in particular caught my attention:  a brother who argued on the basis of Romans
13 that God expects Christians to vote. 
As someone who is pretty loud about his intention not to vote, this certainly
made me take notice!  After all, if God
expects me to vote and I don’t, I’ve got a problem.



If there were anywhere in Scripture that contained such an
expectation, it would be Romans 13:1-7, which lays out our
responsibilities to the government.  As I
see it, this text divides itself up into two main sections, the first of which
is Romans 13:1-4.  Here’s how it reads in
the NASB:

"Every person is to be in subjection to the governing
authorities. For there is no authority except from God, and those which exist
are established by God. Therefore whoever resists authority has opposed the
ordinance of God; and they who have opposed will receive condemnation upon
themselves. For rulers are not a cause of fear for good behavior, but for evil.
Do you want to have no fear of authority? Do what is good and you will have
praise from the same; for it is a minister of God to you for good. But if you
do what is evil, be afraid; for it does not bear the sword for nothing; for it
is a minister of God, an avenger who brings wrath on the one who practices
evil."

Here, I see Paul making three main points.  The first is that God remains in control of
governments and nations.  If they exist,
it is because He established them.

To us, this might seem to be a bizarre claim when we
consider the very many corrupt and wicked governments in the world and the very
few upright and righteous ones.  How can
it be that the current state of the world reflects God’s will?  Of course, God’s people in Bible times would
have said (and in fact, sometimes did say in Scripture) exactly the same thing
about the Assyrians and the Babylonians and the Romans.  Despite appearances to the contrary, His will
was done then, and His will continues to be done today.  Don’t sweat it.  God still reigns in heaven.

Paul’s second point is that God has established governments
for the punishment of evildoers.  Of
course, governmental success in actually punishing evildoers varies widely from
location to location, but that’s what government is supposed to be doing.

Third, Paul argues, because the government is doing God’s
work, it’s the responsibility of the Christian to obey the laws.  In an era marked by frequent rebellions
against the Roman Empire, he wants Christians to understand that they are to
remain subject even to an oppressive government (which the Roman Empire
certainly was).

Christians are definitely supposed to be law-abiding, then,
but this tells us nothing about whether they are supposed to vote or not.  After all, voting in the U.S. isn’t
compulsory.  If it were compulsory, we
would have to vote; in the absence of such a law, we are free not to.

In fact, Paul’s argument here suggests that voting may not
actually do much good.  The course of our
nation is decided in heaven, not in the voting booth.  We may not understand why Candidate X gets
elected, but Christians in the first century probably didn’t understand why
they got stuck with Caligula and Nero as their emperors, either.  God is up to something, but if we ever think
we’ve got it figured out, we’re likely wrong.

This takes us to the second half of our context, Romans
13:5-7, which, again in the NASB, reads:

“Therefore it is necessary to be in subjection, not only because of wrath, but
also for conscience' sake. For because of this you also pay taxes, for rulers
are servants of God, devoting themselves to this very thing. Render to all what
is due them: tax to whom tax is due; custom to whom custom; fear to whom fear;
honor to whom honor.”

The point here is much simpler.  Give the government what you owe.  If the government imposes a tax on you, pay
the tax rather than seeking to evade it. 
Honor those officials whom the government has elevated to positions of
honor (side note:  Christians need to be
careful to make sure that the articles and cartoons they share online are
properly respectful).  Once again,
though, this text doesn’t reach the issue of voting.  People vote because they want to, not because
they owe it to the government.

Instead, if we’re going to look for a text in Romans to
define our attitude toward voting, it should be Romans 14, not Romans 13.  The application of Paul’s message here is
pretty straightforward.  If your conscience
drives you to vote for a candidate, do so. 
If your conscience doesn’t allow you to vote for a candidate (or any
candidate), don’t vote.  In either case,
don’t judge brethren whose convictions are different from yours.  Assume good faith on the part of your
brother, especially when you don’t agree with him.

Finally, let’s remember that the kingdom of God is not
voting or abstaining, but righteousness and peace and joy in the Holy
Spirit.  Choosing our leaders should not
be the most important thing in our lives. 
Far from it!  No soul has ever
been saved by an election.  No church has
ever been called to greater righteousness (though some have been divided or otherwise led astray because of one).  If
we invest more effort in political advocacy than in loving God and loving our
neighbor, something has gone badly wrong someplace.

To my brethren, then, I say this:  If you feel like voting is the right thing to
do, vote.  If you don’t, don’t.  If you’re looking for a write-in candidate, I
recommend Vermin Supreme.  He’s promised
everyone a pony, and free ponies for everyone are something we can all agree on!
