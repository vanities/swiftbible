As always, I used the Pitt Minion for my daily Bible
readings for two weeks.  As it happened,
I also used it to read Hebrews, James, and part of Romans while I was waiting
for my car in Jiffy Lube.  Here’s what I thought:

**SIZE.** The dimensions of the Pitt Minion are
somewhat unusual.  I suppose you would
classify it as a small thinline if you were going to try to classify it.  It’s skinny like a thinline.  However, when compared to other thinlines,
the Pitt is just as wide, but about an inch shorter.  It looks squarish instead of having the
typically rectangular thinline shape.  In
fact, in terms of length and width, of the other Bibles I own, the Pitt is most
like my Crossway Personal-Size Reference, which, as a single-column Bible, is a
very different animal.

That’s what the Pitt looks like, not what it feels
like.  What it feels like is
amazing.  I have never held a Bible I
enjoy holding more than a Pitt Minion, and that was certainly true of this
NLT.  When I have it open, it sits in my
hand like it belongs there.  The stiff
cover keeps the pages from flopping down so I can’t read the outside, and the
weight of the volume is positively birdlike. 
There are a thousand different ways to hold it, and they all feel
right.  I don’t know why this is, but it
is.

**LAYOUT.** The NLT Pitt (as all Pitts are) is a
center-column reference Bible.  In many
cases, center-column reference Bibles are disastrous readers.  It’s a characters-per-line thing.  In a Bible with typical page proportions,
about 5 ½” x 8 ½”, even a double-column text Bible is going to be a little bit
cramped.  Usually, the columns in a
double-column text Bible are going to be about 42 characters wide.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEhvKRy6y5L-DXvoUK3swQ41NzUmyzE7aVcwOosFR0JPrmBu4LJ0ObEJ-wAIvLUPULm1lrF9horP0Qu0OQCQaU-aO-JnSQ3MPCacZhR7UCqz0_K8F3iY4BmI57G8mkLJmWGl-TmBzNF8VQ/s1600/R_columnlength.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEhvKRy6y5L-DXvoUK3swQ41NzUmyzE7aVcwOosFR0JPrmBu4LJ0ObEJ-wAIvLUPULm1lrF9horP0Qu0OQCQaU-aO-JnSQ3MPCacZhR7UCqz0_K8F3iY4BmI57G8mkLJmWGl-TmBzNF8VQ/s1600/R_columnlength.jpg)



However, if you run a center column of references between
those two columns of text, it’s going to steal column width from both of
them.  The best double-column reference
Bibles will have around 37 characters per line, but I’ve seen 30 or even 25
cpl.  That works OK in prose (though I
prefer a wider column even in the prose portions of the Bible), but it’s
terrible at handling Old-Testament poetry. 
The narrow columns lead to poetic lines spilling over into two or even
three lines of text, which defeats the purpose of setting those passages
poetically.  As a result, center-column
reference Bibles rarely make good readers.

Here too, the Pitt Minion is an exception, and it’s an
exception because of its dimensions.  In
fact, if I had to guess, the Pitt Minion has the dimensions it does in order to
accommodate that center column.  If you
took the column of references out and shoved the two columns of Pitt text
together, you’d have a Bible with the same proportions as all those other
thinlines.  It would be a smaller Bible,
but it would have comparable length and width.

What the designers of the Pitt did, then, is to take that
hypothetical pint-size thinline, stretch it out sideways by about half an inch,
and then run a column of references down the blank space in the middle.   In
other words, they left the text columns at the width they’re supposed to be and
created space for references by widening the Bible.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEjL0BkfZcC5KpSWY_VYaI-6THnZsY0t7bPVyPOVvNcLJ8Tewzx1KbDJq_7cgPX0RNXXHJwQqXap44knoiBrmWxY1DCQNVPWKDZzFAN0mM0GWIAW46cyzU_Sd6niK-9DuZ5OwTqI50PTLQ/s1600/R_redletter.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEjL0BkfZcC5KpSWY_VYaI-6THnZsY0t7bPVyPOVvNcLJ8Tewzx1KbDJq_7cgPX0RNXXHJwQqXap44knoiBrmWxY1DCQNVPWKDZzFAN0mM0GWIAW46cyzU_Sd6niK-9DuZ5OwTqI50PTLQ/s1600/R_redletter.jpg)



This is extremely clever, and it saves the Pitt as a
reader.  My NLT Pitt has the best
characters-per-line rating of any double-column Bible I’ve owned.  It’s somewhere up around 45 cpl.  That would be excellent for a double-column
text Bible, let alone a double-column reference Bible!  As a result, the Pitt laughs at Old-Testament
poetry.  Much of my reading during the
trial period came from Ezekiel, which has plenty of poetic sections.  In the NLT Pitt setting of Ezekiel, there are
a few one-word “widows” created by overhanging poetic lines, but not many.  There certainly weren’t enough to break my
concentration or even to force me to notice them.  In having both references and good line
length, the Pitt Minion represents the best of both worlds.

**READABILITY.** 6.75. 
That’s the print size in the NLT Pitt Minion, and it sounds absolutely
dreadful.  It sounds like one of those
gift-Bible KJV’s you can buy at Target that have about 700 pages and are
printed on copier paper (I have one of these. 
My children bought it for me for my birthday.  I may review it at some point).  Of course, a Pitt is about half the size of
one of those Bibles and is much better made, but 6.75 points is 6.75 points.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEijMQak-QOa3csuoaHbes6cJjqOnU3YDeCMZsD31VXptxIl5Ixgiei47HDffwV37F5a4l7NTZRSwES5GYppIiUDcVhP9JEZ-qMh6qgOF4VsNl4qinBMvgkhMIWsAQrTH518KKhF_bvGIg/s1600/R_printsize.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEijMQak-QOa3csuoaHbes6cJjqOnU3YDeCMZsD31VXptxIl5Ixgiei47HDffwV37F5a4l7NTZRSwES5GYppIiUDcVhP9JEZ-qMh6qgOF4VsNl4qinBMvgkhMIWsAQrTH518KKhF_bvGIg/s1600/R_printsize.jpg)



However, the NLT Pitt is remarkably readable for a Bible
with 6.75-point print.  I think there are
two reasons for this.  The first is Blue
Heron’s sound decision to set the Bible in Lexicon No. 1.  Lexicon No. 1 is, of course, the typeface
that Crossway uses for everything these days. 
I myself don’t care for it at larger sizes, but at smaller sizes, it
shines.  As my photographer has pointed
out, it has a number of features that make it unusually readable.  Among other things, it has a comparatively
high x-height (the height of most lower-case letters) and large, dramatic
serifs (the little lines at the end of letters in some typefaces).  Those make Lexicon No. 1 read larger than it actually
is.

Also, the Pitt Minion benefits from being printed by
Jongbloed, everyone’s favorite Dutch printer. 
I don’t know how Jongbloed does it, but the print in my two Jongbloed
Bibles is noticeably crisper than the print in any of my various mass-market
editions.  This is true even of Bibles
using the same typeface.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEjRSovn74EltNvavXf05_KltGn5fwnTNuIkCxIGvbagu5fVcKzIJAlF3C63WiyBf-OqOha_2HbLz1Gk12lQgrhCTj8RIKcqSbRPpXerEtSb_ideOYs_b-p3CiAYTzNHd-0-sHbrpPFJEA/s1600/R_Closeup.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEjRSovn74EltNvavXf05_KltGn5fwnTNuIkCxIGvbagu5fVcKzIJAlF3C63WiyBf-OqOha_2HbLz1Gk12lQgrhCTj8RIKcqSbRPpXerEtSb_ideOYs_b-p3CiAYTzNHd-0-sHbrpPFJEA/s1600/R_Closeup.jpg)



For instance, I own an old-school two-column Crossway New
Testament that, like the Pitt, is printed in Lexicon No. 1, supposedly at 6.5
points.  The old quarter test reveals
that the print in the Pitt and the Crossway is similarly sized.  The quarter covers up ten lines and a smidge
in the former and about ten-and-a-half lines in the latter.  However, if you looked at the two Bibles
side-by-side, you would swear (or perhaps affirm, depending on your religious
convictions) that the print in the Pitt is at least a full point larger.  As lovely as premium covers and gilding are,
this illustrates why it may actually be more important to have a premium text
block.

Speaking of premium text blocks, the paper in the NLT Pitt
also contributes to its readability. 
It’s a nice, gentle off-white that’s easy on the eyes.  A Bible printed by Jongbloed is always going
to be more expensive, but the difference in text-block quality is pronounced.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEhi0QLq5x6Vi-oJa142gBrjrEMpwSW1mWuu4M1h-Qn6ydvnbn7mPHTdpo3BhsLqKtPUJyYnUp5amR-kCVj6-7tFit-I6fueXIs4H-0xzUQ4D4CEe8RR5hRbExhUnRruuw5_KBD3MSob3A/s1600/R_Poetry.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEhi0QLq5x6Vi-oJa142gBrjrEMpwSW1mWuu4M1h-Qn6ydvnbn7mPHTdpo3BhsLqKtPUJyYnUp5amR-kCVj6-7tFit-I6fueXIs4H-0xzUQ4D4CEe8RR5hRbExhUnRruuw5_KBD3MSob3A/s1600/R_Poetry.jpg)



Of course, even though I like the paper color, the paper
opacity isn’t all that great.  I believe
that by describing it as “India Paper”, Cambridge is implying that it’s under
30 gsm.  Any smaller Bible is going to
have thinner, more translucent paper.

However, the text block of the Pitt NLT is largely
line-matched.  Ghosting was only a
problem when there was a large clump of textual notes in the lower right-hand
corner of a page, which tended to ghost out the lower left-hand corner of the
text on the opposite side.  Also, pages
behind the page I was reading were sometimes visible.

**CONCLUSION.** It’s hard to believe, but I loved reading
out of my NLT Pitt.  The small print
didn’t bother me, and the Bible was a delight in the hand.  I certainly would not recommend it to anyone
who needs reading glasses, but for my own use, I rate it at 9/10, with an
impressive Overall Rating of 28/30.
