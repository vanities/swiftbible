**OVERVIEW.** I very much enjoyed doing my daily
readings from the Windsor.  Its small
size made it pleasant to hold, and the verse-by-verse format didn’t bother me
any.  Its column width was almost exactly
what I prefer, and I even thought the traditional KJV setting of Old-Testament
poetry in prose instead of verse worked well. 
If I were going to choose a KJV for my daily reader, I would choose this
Bible.

\*             \*             \*

| The Windsor contains its own daily reading plan. |

As I do with every Bible I review, I took the Windsor home
from the church building, set it on my desk downstairs, and used it to carry
out my daily Bible readings for two weeks. 
Here is what I observed:



**SIZE.** The Windsor is what I often call a
just-a-Bible Bible.  It’s a little bit
fatter than a thinline, but it has approximately the same length and
width.  Bibles like this strive to be
large enough to be easily readable, yet small enough to be comfortable to
hold.  The Windsor manages this perfectly.  I don’t have particularly large hands for a
man, yet I was never bothered by having to hold it up.  It felt about like reading a trade paperback,
which is a great form factor for a Bible.

**LAYOUT.** The Windsor doesn’t have the layout that
one would expect in an excellent reading Bible. 
The received wisdom on the Internet says that single-column Bibles make
the best readers, but the Windsor is double-column.  Likewise, a Bible that is paragraphed would
seem to be the most intuitive to read, but the Windsor is verse-by-verse.

However, I loved reading out of it.  As anybody who has read more than one of my
reviews knows, I’m not a big single-column fan. 
I find that lines containing more than 55 characters (spaces included)
are too long for me to apprehend with a glance. 
I have to look over to catch the last few characters, which slows me
down and takes me out of the text.  By
contrast, the Windsor’s characters-per-line count comes in at about 42, which
is nearly perfect for me.  I never had
any trouble making out words that hovered frustratingly around the edges of my
focus.

Similarly, I think that paragraphing is overrated in a
reader.  It would be awful to read a text
with no breaks in it at all (like all too many Internet rants), but the hard
returns after each verse gave me a chance to gather myself before continuing.  In theory, this might lead the reader to
place a mistaken emphasis on verses as self-contained units of content, but I
didn’t notice that being a problem.  When
I’m reading several chapters at a time, I’m thinking on the level of overall
theme anyway.  Honestly, I suspect that
verse-by-verse Bibles cause more difficulty in study than in reading.

Also, there were a couple of layout quirks unique to the KJV
that I appreciated.  First, unlike every
modern translation I’m familiar with, the KJV does not set the poetic text of
the Old Testament in verse.  Instead,
it’s all treated as prose.  Psalm 37
looks no different on the page than 1 Corinthians 8.

In theory, this would be a problem.  Because we are so used to seeing poetry in
verse, if we think about it for half a second, we knee-jerk to the conclusion,
“The poetry of the OT ought to be in verse too!”  That may be true in a single-column
Bible.  Bibles like that are able to take
advantage of the white space that stretches from edge to edge to present even
the longest poetic phrase in Scripture on a single line.  Bible poetry looks lovely in a Crossway
Legacy, for instance.

However, I think it actually isn’t true in a double-column
Bible.  I’ve never seen a double-column
Bible with a characters-per-line count out of the low forties.  Many of the poetic lines in the Bible are
longer than that, which means that Psalms and the other poetic books are
littered with “widows”, one-word line fragments that end up on the next line
down.  Widows are ugly, and they slow
down my reading.  I have to spend an
entire glance on a single word, which is just as bad as having to glance at the
last few words of a line in a single-column Bible.

Because the Windsor is entirely a prose setting, it largely
avoids this problem.  According to my
count, the 176 verses of Psalm 119 in the Windsor contain 28 widows, created by
the ends of verses.  By contrast, Psalm
119 in the Zondervan Thinline NASB, which is a good double-column reading
Bible, has 60.

I read a lot of poetry out of the Windsor; during the review
period, my reading plan had me reading nearly the entirety of Proverbs.  However, I found that reading easier to
comprehend than poetry in any other double-column Bible.  In theory, the removal of versification might
have cost me comprehension by hiding the parallel structure of the text.  In practice, that wasn’t an issue.  I’m certainly used to spotting parallel
structure in the speeches of Lincoln and Churchill, to say nothing of the New
Testament, so I didn’t find Proverbs in prose any less comprehensible or
beautiful.  In short, I have concluded
that Old-Testament poetry actually works better in prose in a double-column
Bible.

Also, prose is more compact than poetry.  There’s certainly all kinds of white space in
the typical Psalms spread in the Legacy, but there’s plenty even in my Thinline
NASB.  It’s a result both of short lines
(many poetic lines in Scripture aren’t even 40 characters long) and of all the
widows.  By contrast, the same text set
in prose takes up much less space, which results in a more compact Bible.



This can have dramatic consequences for the series of
compromises that characterizes Bible design. 
For instance, the Windsor and the Thinline NASB have comparable
dimensions.  The Thinline is actually a
little taller.  The Windsor is a little
thicker, at around 1300 pages as opposed to the Thinline’s 1100.  However, the print in the Thinline is
7.8-point, and the print in the Windsor is 9.6-point.  That’s not a typeface issue either.  The ITC Weidemann of the Thinline is similar
in proportion to the Slimbach of the Windsor. 
It’s just that the prose layout of the Windsor uses space that much more
efficiently, which leads to a more readable Bible at a given size.

Second, the Windsor follows the typical KJV layout in not
having section headings.  It has running
headers at the top of the page, but those don’t come to my attention when I’m
considering the text itself at all.  The
lack of section headings probably saves a surprisingly large amount of room,
but it also doesn’t force the translator/editor’s opinions about breaks in the
text on me as I read.  With the chapter
breaks, don’t we already have enough human impositions on the text anyway?

In yet another point in the Windsor’s favor, it’s a pure
text Bible (lacking even translators’ notes), so I’m not contending with a
giant column of hieroglyphics running down the middle of the page.  This is greatly to be preferred.

**READABILITY.** About as good as you’re likely to find in
a hand-size Bible, honestly.  The
cream-tinted paper makes for a soothing, low-key backdrop, and 9.6-point print
is venturing nigh unto large-print territory for a Bible.  The Windsor probably isn’t a great choice for
people with *really* bad eyes, but
everybody else should be able to use it just fine.

**CONCLUSION.** What can I say?  Here too, the Windsor seems to do everything
right.  It’s a beautiful Bible, and it
makes for a pleasant reading experience. 
I can’t think of a single negative thing to say about it here.  In fact, I seriously considered adopting
the Windsor as my primary reading Bible throughout 2015.  This is one of the smoothest readers I’ve
encountered in any translation.  It
richly deserves a 10/10.  Overall, this
Bible has earned a heretofore unseen 29.5/30. 
Impressive!
