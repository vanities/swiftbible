Sometimes, in my conversations with others, certain
spiritual themes keep popping up. 
Recently, one of those themes has been that many Christians apparently
are uncertain about their salvation. 
They’ve obeyed the gospel, they strive to live righteous lives, but they
have no confidence that they are in a right relationship with God.

To me, this seems both miserable and Scripturally
unjustified.  There are two problems with
it.  First, it puts too much stress on
our own actions.  Yes, God expects us to
be faithful to His covenant, but He doesn’t expect us to be perfect.  Otherwise, why send Jesus in the first
place?

Second, it reveals a distorted vision of God.  The Bible describes the devil as “the
accuser”; in fact, that’s what the Greek word *diabolos* means.  However,
this way of thinking puts God in the accuser’s role.  It’s like He is some kind of heavenly
insurance claims adjuster, scrutinizing our every move, poring over the fine
print of the contract, looking for any excuse not to pay out.  What a bizarre way to understand the One who
loved us enough to send His Son to die for us!

The only cure for such an unbiblical way of thinking is to
turn to the Bible.  This morning, then,
let’s consider the reassuring message of the first three chapters of Ephesians,
and the way that Paul describes how we have received every spiritual blessing
in Christ Jesus.



**Before the Foundation**

Ephesians opens with the startling revelation that God
developed His plan for us before the foundation of the world.  Even then, He willed that we should receive **ADOPTION AS SONS.** Let’s read about this in Ephesians
1:1-6.  Sad to say, this is a text that
makes a lot of Christians nervous.  They
see the word “predestination”, and they start wondering if Calvin was right
after all and feverishly assembling their defenses for why He wasn’t.

This morning, though, rather than stressing over what this
text doesn’t say, let’s look at what it does say.  In a way, this is Genesis 1:0, because it
tells us that even before “in the beginning,” God looked into the future, saw
us, loved us, and recognized that we would need help.  Before, “Let there be light,” God determined
that He was going to send His Son to rescue us, so that we could be adopted as His
other sons and daughters and enjoy every blessing He has to offer.  In other words, friends, underlying the very
fabric of the universe is God’s eternal love for us.

In some of our hymns, we sing that God’s motivations in this
are inexplicable.  I don’t actually think
that’s true; in fact, I think Paul explains right here.  God did all this for the praise of the glory
of His grace.  Let’s put it like
this.  When we put up a statute to
somebody, that statue is always clothed in a way that reveals who the subject
is.  Generals are in uniform and on
horseback, politicians are in suit and tie, athletes are clad in the trappings
of their sport.

This text presents us as the monument that God has set up to
reveal His nature, and what we show about Him are His grace and His love.  Grace and love are just as essential to
understanding God as that Civil War uniform is to understanding General Grant
in Grant Park.  He has made Himself known
to us by rescuing us in the ultimate act of self-sacrifice.

Next, Paul describes two extremely important things that we
have **IN CHRIST.** They appear in Ephesians 1:7-14.  The first of these blessings is
redemption.  Basically, this is about the
way that Jesus deals with our past. 
Redemption is when you buy back something that belongs to you.  In this case, God redeemed us by paying the
price of Jesus’ blood to buy us back from our enslavement to the devil.  Paul also describes this blessing as
forgiveness, which has a slightly different shade of meaning.  Forgiveness is when you cancel a debt that
somebody owes you.  God redeemed us from
the devil, but He forgave the debt that we owed Him because we violated His
law.  Finally, it is through Jesus that
God has revealed His purpose for us.  Apart
from Christ, we don’t even have a good explanation for why we exist, but through
Him we learn that God’s purpose is to use Jesus to unite us all with Him.

This slides smoothly into the second main point here, which
is that in Christ we have the blessing of an inheritance.  God’s already taken care of our past; now,
He’s taking care of our future.  Paul
uses the p-word again here; however, this one isn’t scary either.  The point is that God guided the course of
the whole Jewish nation in order to produce the pre-Acts 10 Jewish church,
which received the promise of eternal glory from Him.

In the same way, the Gentile believers of Ephesus, and by
extension we ourselves, were brought into the inheritance too.  Our choice is to hear and believe, and once we
do, we are sealed with the Holy Spirit and receive the Spirit’s guarantee.  This isn’t some kind of subjective feeling;
instead, the Greek word here, *arrabon*,
is also used of an engagement ring.  In
the first century, this spiritual engagement ring took the form of the
miraculous spiritual gifts; today, though, we receive the guarantee of our
salvation through the Spirit’s work in inspiring the word.

Paul wants the Ephesians to understand how wonderful this
is, so he prays for **A SPIRIT OF WISDOM** for
them.  We find this prayer in Ephesians
1:15-23.  As Paul describes it, this
wisdom will enlighten us so that we will understand three things:  the hope to which we have been called, the
riches of the inheritance we will receive, and the immeasurable greatness of
the power that God will exert to make sure that we are not denied any of these blessings.

The measuring stick that we are supposed to use to
understand all this is what God has already done for Christ.  He put forth His awesome might and raised Him
from the dead.  Then, He lifted Him up in
the heavenly places, gave Him absolute authority over everything, and made Him
head of the body of the church.  This,
Paul says, is what God is going to do for us and how determined He will be to
make it happen.

To us, this seems almost blasphemous.  How dare we think that God will exalt us the
way that He exalted the Lord??? 
Actually, though, Paul is going to use the entirety of the next chapter
to explain that it really is true.  The
story of Jesus is the outline for the story of every Christian.

Let’s pause for a moment to contrast what we’ve just read
with the apprehension that so many Christians have about their salvation.  Does the God of Ephesians 1 really seem like
a God who is eagerly waiting for an excuse to send us to hell?  Or, instead, is He the God who from before
the beginning has used and is using all of His wisdom and all of His power to
bring us to heaven?

You know, there’s a lot of pride in being insecure about our
hope of eternal life.  Underlying that
worry is the idea that our salvation is for us to earn, that we’re on center stage.  We aren’t. 
God is, and we need to trust Him to do His work.

**The Riches of His
Grace**

Next, we come to Ephesians 2, a chapter that is concerned
primarily with the riches of the grace of God. 
Within this chapter, the first sub-context addresses **SALVATION BY GRACE.** Let’s read from Ephesians 2:1-10.  Here too, we encounter a text that tends to
elicit a knee-jerk reaction from us.  We
read Ephesians 2:8-9, and our first response is to say, “But you still have to
be baptized to be saved!”  That’s certainly
true, but it’s not at all the point here.

Instead, Paul is beginning to explain the assertion that he
made at the end of the last chapter: 
that we can learn how much God is doing for us by considering how much
He has done for Christ.  Christ was dead
and God raised Him; we were dead in our trespasses and sins, but God raised us
up with Christ.  Similarly, God seated
Christ at His right hand, and Paul tells us here that God has also seated us
with Christ, where Christ belongs and we emphatically do not.

Why?  So that
eternally, God could reveal the immeasurable riches of His grace in us.  It’s so funny that everybody fixates on vs.
8-9, but it’s really v. 7 that is the key verse of the context, and vs. 8-9 are
merely explaining why v. 7 works.  When we
are saved, it’s not because of us.  It’s
not because we have done such a good job of serving God that God puts us where
we belong.  That wouldn’t glorify God.  It would glorify us and give us reason to
boast.

Instead, we are saved by the grace of God, through our faith
in His promise to justify us.  We’ve done
a rotten job of serving God, so that all we have earned is His wrath.  However, in order to display His mercy, His
kindness, and His love, He has lifted us up to a place where no one could
possibly imagine that we deserve to be. 
When we feel, then, that we are unworthy of salvation, yes, that’s kind
of the point!  When God exalts even
people like us, all the glory must go to Him.

We see a further glorification of God in Paul’s explanation
of how Christ **MADE PEACE.** Let’s read on in Ephesians 2:11-22.  If you’ll remember, the final work that God
did for Christ at the end of the first chapter was to give Him the church as
His body and make Him its head in all things. 
Here, Paul explains how God through Christ worked to make a place for us
in the church too.

There were several obstacles that Christ had to overcome to
accomplish this.  First, most people were
and are Gentiles, divided from the people of God by the restrictions of the Law
of Moses.  Second, both Jews and Gentiles
alike are divided from God by their sin.

In order to solve these two problems, Jesus did three
things.  First, He joined Jew and Gentile
together by doing away with the Law that divided them.  Then, He reconciled Jew and Gentile alike to
God through the blood that He shed on the cross.  Finally, He proclaimed to all the nations the
gospel of His peace, so that no matter who we are, we know that we can come to God
through Him.

The results of this transformation are profound.  Earlier, Paul described what God had done for
Christ using the metaphor of a head and a body. 
Here, he adopts a different comparison, which only makes sense.  After all, we don’t think of our bodies as
having been put together!

Instead, he compares individual Christians to building
blocks that are being assembled into a spiritual temple that will serve as a
dwelling place for God, much like the physical temple in Jerusalem did during
the time of the Law of Moses.  Before, we
were isolated strangers, now we belong with one another and with Christ, united
to glorify God together.

Before the Lord’s Supper, then, let’s reflect on everything
we’ve studied.  Let’s remember how even
before the world was created, God loved us and desired to reveal Himself to us,
and He did so by sending His Son to die for our salvation.  Now by His grace, we are blessed beyond
measure and have even more to look forward to. 
Let’s think about this as we partake.

**The Eternal Purpose**

Finally, we come to Ephesians 3 and its discussion of how
Paul and all the rest of us fit into the eternal purpose of God.  This begins with Paul’s revelation of **THE PLAN OF THE MYSTERY.** Let’s look here at Ephesians 3:1-13.  If you’ll recall, we left Ephesians 2 with
Paul’s glowing description of all that God has done for us.  However, there’s one problem.  As every business owner knows, it doesn’t
matter what you offer if nobody knows you’re offering it.  A gospel as wonderful as that one still needs
a preacher to proclaim it.

Paul begins, then, by describing his particular work.  I’ve been using the ESV a lot recently, but
this section of Scripture has one of my least favorite ESV renderings in
it.  In v. 6, the translators break up one
of Paul’s great big sentences by inserting the words “This mystery is”, but I
think they’re mistaken.  Contextually,
Paul isn’t talking here about the overall mystery of Christ.  Instead, he’s talking about his particular
insight into the mystery that was given him through revelation.  He is the apostle to the Gentiles, so it
makes sense that his insight would be that the Gentiles can be as much
partakers of God’s promise as Jews can. 
Revealing this is his special work.

From here, Paul moves on to the proclamation of the mystery
generally.  In order to understand what
it is, let’s look at the other phrases that are used synonymously with
“mystery” in this context.  They’re things
like, “the unsearchable riches of Christ”, “the manifold wisdom of God”, and
“the eternal purpose”.  After having read
the first two chapters, we shouldn’t be in any doubt about what these things
are.  Instead, it’s clear that the
mystery is God’s eternal plan for our salvation and His glorification:  hidden for ages, but now revealed in
Christ.  This is important stuff,
brethren, and it’s our responsibility to reveal the mystery today as Paul did
2000 years ago.

Finally, let’s consider Paul’s comments on **THE POWER AT WORK WITHIN US.** Consider Ephesians 3:14-21.  In order to understand the prayer that we see
in this passage, I think we need to look back to the similar prayer that we see
in Ephesians 1:17-18.  There, the prayer
is for knowledge.  Here, the prayer is
for what God will do for us to help us gain the knowledge we need.

I think too that this is our final assurance of salvation in
this text.  It begins with God
strengthening us with His spiritual power, so that Christ can dwell within us
through our faith.  This will root us in
His love and help us to understand the incomprehensible dimensions of His grace
and love, with the result that even though we are empty, we will be filled with
His fullness.

These things are not just possibilities, brethren!  They are promises.  They are what God will do for us if we seek
Him.  The only way we don’t get these
things is if we don’t seek.

In the last paragraph of this section, Paul reminds us of
why we can be certain that all of these wonderful things will happen.  They are not limited by our ability or wisdom.  Instead, if we offer ourselves to God, the
only limits are the limits of His power as it works within us, which is to say
no limits at all.  This is why Paul
observes that God will do in us not only more than we ask, but more even than
we think.  Devotion to Him will lead us
to places we never imagined we would go.

The end result of this, of course, is not our glory.  When God completes His work in us, the point
won’t be how impressive we are.  The
point will be how impressive it is that God could take people like us and make
from us a church that was fit to honor Him.
