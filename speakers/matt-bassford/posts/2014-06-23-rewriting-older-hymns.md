Perhaps more so than any other
language in history, English changes.  It
invents and absorbs new words at a frightening rate, and older words often fall
into disuse.  The same is true of
literary style.  What seemed eloquent to Victorian
statesmen often strikes us as turgid.

These changes have affected the
corpus of English hymns no less than any other artifact of the language.  Almost every hymn in English carries with it
the stylistic fingerprint of its time and place of origin.  Back before I traded my free time for a wife
and two children, I spent enough time with my nose in online hymn archives that
I could date a hymn I did not know to within 10 years of the year it was
actually written.  Even brethren with
enough sense to avoid developing such a dubious skill are able to tell when a
hymn hails from a time other than their own.



Sometimes, these anachronisms are
quaint and even delightful.  Even though
I know better, I love to sing about “ye dragons all” in “Hallelujah!  Praise Jehovah”.  They put me in mind of the sea monsters
doodled in the margins of medieval maps.

Others are less charming.  I did not mourn in the least when the editors
of *Psalms, Hymns, and Spiritual Songs* replaced
the verse of “We’ll Work Till Jesus Comes” containing the line “And lean for
succor on His breast”.  Yes, I know what
it actually means.  No, I still do not
want to sing it.

For those of us who do not wield
the august powers of the hymnal editor but have an interest in older hymns, and
perhaps some skill that could be turned to alter them, this presents a
question.  What do we do about it?  When do we alter, and when do we leave alone?

First, I think we leave alone
anything that is currently in use among Christians.  There are many hymns in our repertoire that
use the “Thee” of the King James Version” rather than the “You” of our
contemporary speech.  In many cases, it
would not be difficult to slightly rewrite the hymn to use “You” rather than
“Thee” (though in practice, this is often more difficult than it appears).

However, such changes would be
offensive to many and pointless for nearly all. 
Human beings generally don’t like change, and Christians may well be
among the most traditional of the breed. 
They don’t want to sing “Only in You”. 
They want to sing “Only in Thee”. 
It’s what they’re used to singing. 
The End.

Nor should we overlook the fact
that they are still singing it.  Despite
the presence of “Thee” and “Thy”, song leaders all over the country continue to
select “I Am Thine, O Lord”.  We don’t
need to update it to make it relevant to the culture.  It use proves that it is already relevant.

A different rule, though, applies
to hymns that are not familiar to the church. 
There are untold thousands of those. 
99 percent of them ought to remain dead. 
They’re simply not good enough to revive and sing.  It may well be worthwhile, however, to
attempt to resuscitate the remaining 1 percent (which is probably an absurdly
high estimate).

If we think a hymn deserves
another shot at life, we probably should remove at least the worst archaisms
from it.  As all experienced hymnists in
the Lord’s church know, the journey from “finished hymn” to “hymn in wide use
in the brotherhood” is as perilous as the journey from Sinai to Canaan, and the
rate of attrition is nearly as high.  Hymns
perish by the wayside all the time, often for no obvious reason.  Use of “Thee” in a new hymn is an obvious
reason for it to fail.  We’re used to
“Thee” in the comfortable world of our existing repertoire, but not really
anywhere else.  These days, even men who
do a Scripture reading out of the KJV are likely to garner a few funny
looks.  We want our new hymns to sound
new, not to sound antiquated.

It may well be, then, that if we
wish an unknown hymn to become known, a Thee-to-You switcheroo is an important
and even necessary step.  With all this
in mind, I would like to present a reworked semi-known hymn that I had been
working on last week, even before the enthusiastic discussion of this topic on
the Hymnody group on Facebook.

I first came to know it as a
child in the Northfield Church of Christ in Northfield, New Jersey, where, due
to the vigilance of my parents, *Sacred
Selections* was about as likely to become part of our assemblies as speaking
in tongues.  Even in the 1980s, we
continued to use *Great Songs of the
Church*, and a hymn I treasure from that time is “Father and Friend, Thy
Light, Thy Love”.  It does not appear in *Sacred Selections* or *Hymns for Worship (Revised),* though *Songs of the Church* and *Praise
for the Lord* do contain it.  I’ve not
heard it sung anywhere since my family moved to Missouri in 1991.  I lobbied for its inclusion in *Psalms, Hymns, and Spiritual Songs*, but
due perhaps to poor performance in the repertoire survey that preceded said
hymnal, it ultimately was not included.

I think it should be sung.  If indeed it is sung, and it has merely
eluded me for the past 25 years, I rejoice and withdraw my revision at
once.  If it is not sung, then perhaps this
Thee-to-You revision will help.

Father and Friend, Your Light,
Your Love

Father and Friend, Your light,
Your love

Beam from each work of Yours we
view;

Your glory gilds the heav’ns
above,

And all the earth is full of You.

Your voice we hear, Your presence
feel,

While You, too pure for mortal
sight,

Enwrapped in clouds, invisible,

Reign as the Lord of life and
light.

Your children shall not faint nor
fear,

Sustained by this delightful
thought:

Since You, their God, are
everywhere,

They cannot be where You are not.
