Most people in my religious tradition are very familiar with
the story of Nadab and Abihu in Leviticus 10, who “offered strange fire before
the Lord, which He had not commanded them.” 
They promptly became burnt offerings themselves, from which we conclude
that offering things that the Lord has not commanded is not a good spiritual
survival strategy.

I have to say, though, that even though I can’t remember the
first time I heard a sermon on the story, I never gave much thought to the
motivation of the unfortunate pair.  That
changed a couple years back when I had a conversation on the subject with my
sister’s husband.  “They were drunk,” he
said.  Admittedly, the text doesn’t come
out and say so, but there’s some strong indirect evidence that points in that
direction.  In the middle of the story of
the aftermath of Nadab and Abihu’s death, we find Leviticus 10:8-10, which
reads,

“The LORD then spoke to Aaron, saying, ‘Do not drink wine or
strong drink, neither you nor your sons with you, when you come into the tent
of meeting, so that you will not die--it is a perpetual statute throughout your
generations--and so as to make a distinction between the holy and the profane,
and between the unclean and the clean,’” (NASB)

Either God just felt like inserting a random statute into
the middle of a tense and tragic situation, or the statute wasn’t random.  I incline to the latter reading.  The two sons of Aaron decided it would be a
good idea to offer strange fire before the Lord because they’d been drinking
beforehand.  As a result, they failed to
make a distinction between the holy and the profane and were struck down.



Even though we live 3500 years later under a different
covenant, I think there’s a lesson here for God’s modern-day priesthood.  No, I’m not going to argue that it’s a sin *per se* for Christians to drink alcohol
on the basis of a passage in the Old Testament, Galatians 3:23-25
notwithstanding (if I want to make that kind of argument, Proverbs 23:29-35 is
always available).  However, I believe
that the thoughtful servant of the Most High would do well to pay attention.

Apparently, the Levitical priests didn’t have to be all the
way drunk in order for alcohol to interfere with their ability to serve in the
tent of meeting.  Otherwise, God would
have warned them only against drunkenness. 
Instead, any amount of drinking could be dangerous.

3500 years may have passed, but the chemical composition and
effects of alcohol haven’t changed any. 
It’s not like our decision-making ability remains normal until we hit
.08 BAC and then falls off a cliff. 
Instead, it declines with every drop of alcohol we consume, until we
reach the point where the state declares us to be drunk enough that we’ve
become hopeless decision-makers.  To put
things in Leviticus 10 terms, we gradually lose our ability to distinguish
between the holy and the profane.

Maybe there are really awesome Christians out there who have
this discipleship thing licked.  They are
doing so amazingly well in their striving against sin that they don’t even need
to be at 100 percent to defeat the devil. 
Reduced ability to determine what is holy?  No bigs.

Maybe there are Christians like that, and I’m certainly not
about to judge anybody else.  All I know
is that I’m not one.  I desperately
desire to be holy in all my behavior, but I constantly fall short.  Even when I am at 100 percent, the devil wins
a whole lot more than I want him to.  I
shudder to think how he would fare if I were at less than 100 percent.

And that’s why I don’t drink alcohol.
