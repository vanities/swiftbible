These days, the godly perspective on finance has more than a
tinge of Dave Ramsey to it.  Of itself,
there is certainly nothing wrong with that. 
The spending of the American consumer is clearly out-of-control.  It’s not wise for Christians to continually
live beyond their means, and such behavior can easily become actively
sinful.

Even though I don’t remember having read a book by him, I’m
largely Ramseyite in the way that I handle money.  I prefer to be frugal, I don’t like debt, and
my primary financial concern is for the future. 
I think those are all good things to be, and there’s Biblical wisdom
behind all of them.

However, if our concerns about money are limited to counting
down the days until we can unleash our debt-free scream, we can easily run
headlong into another money problem. 
This time, though, the problem is not blowing through our money like
fireworks on the Fourth of July. 
Instead, it is carefully accumulating money in the belief that it will
keep us safe.



As with most bad ideas, there is certainly some basis for
this one.  Solomon observes in
Ecclesiastes 7:10, “For the protection of wisdom is like the protection of
money.”  If money offers us no protection,
Solomon would be arguing that wisdom is valueless, which doesn’t exactly sound
like a Solomonic thing to say!

Indeed, simply by experience, savers know this to be
true.  If you keep a financial cushion
around, it will help you ride out life’s rough spots.  When my family’s minivan expired prematurely
last year, we didn’t have to try to get it fixed, buy a clunker as a
replacement, or take out a car note. 
Instead, we dipped into savings (albeit grudgingly and with many
grumblings on my part) for the cash we needed to buy a new-to-us replacement
(side note:  people who buy new cars
either have lots of money or are suboptimal financial decision makers).

That’s fine, so far as it goes.  Having funds set aside for when life goes
expensively wrong (and it will) is undeniably prudent.  Everyone who can manage it should keep not
just a six-months-of-living-expenses emergency fund, but also accounts where
money for predictable large expenses can accrue.

Want to go to Disneyworld? 
Start pinching your pennies. 
Don’t feel like taking the bus five years from now?  Start saving for the car you’ll probably end
up buying around then.  Whatever you do,
don’t fall into the trap of carrying a balance on a credit card.  15 percent interest will suck you dry quicker
than Count Dracula, and for every person who successfully plays the balance-transfer
game, there are 10 who get distracted and mess it up.

However, all of this careful financial planning can lead to
an extremely dangerous habit of mind: 
the conviction that with enough planning and prudence, we can control
our financial futures and the future generally. 
This is a delusion to which even the cleverest people can fall
prey.  Look at all the folks who expound
confidently on how much global warming will increase the temperature of the
planet 25 years from now, or what the demographic makeup of the American
electorate will be in 2050.

They’ve probably got an alphabet of letters after their
names, but they overlook one of the key insights of Nassim Taleb’s *The Black Swan*, to wit, that long-term
projections made by human beings are nearly always wrong.  We blithely extrapolate current trends into
the future while overlooking even the existence of unforeseeable events that
will have a dramatic effect on those trends.

Or, as somebody put it a few years before Taleb did, “Come
now, you who say, ‘Today or tomorrow we will go into such and such a town and
spend a year there and trade and make profit’—yet you do not know what tomorrow
will bring.  What is your life?  For you are a mist that appears for a little
time and then vanishes.”

We don’t know what’s going to happen, and no matter how
carefully we plan, there are no guarantees that the scope of our foresight will
exceed the scope of the disaster.  Being
a prudent man, I bought a term-life policy when I turned 30 (Side note:  when it comes to life insurance, buy term
life rather than whole life.  You want an
insurance policy, not an inefficient savings vehicle.  Insurance agents who read this will hate me,
but it’s true).  That way, if I’m hit by
a bus tomorrow, my wife and kids won’t starve.

However, what happens if I’m hit by a bus, my wife calls in
the claim, and she discovers that some underling at Prudential, rather than
cashing all those premium checks, has been embezzling them for 25 years?  Such things happen.  So do a host of other disasters ranging from
tornadoes to epidemics.  Social
scientists speculate that if we properly understood the odds of some catastrophe
happening to us, we’d all be clinically depressed!  Ultimately, of course, we must live with the
certainty that if the earth continues long enough, death will come for all of
us.

We can’t protect ourselves, and that’s true no matter how
much money we have and how cleverly we scheme with it.  The most our schemes can accomplish is to
separate us from the One who actually can restore, confirm, strengthen, and
establish us.  If our trust is in our
money, it can’t be in God, and the kind of “prudence” that leads us to make
such a mistake is the worst kind of foolishness.

Yes, be prudent.  Yes,
save.  However, don’t get to thinking
that you can take your life into your own hands rather than leaving it in God’s
hands.  The one who lays up treasure for
himself and is not rich toward God is poor indeed.
