As anybody who reads my blog knows, I’ve been on a CSB kick
recently.  In the course of my interest,
I’ve been reading the promotional material that Holman has put online.  A lot of it is pretty standard boilerplate
for a translation (“Of *course* our
translation is good for close study!!!”), but some of it’s unique.  Particularly, Holman has attempted to use
statistics to prove that the CSB is the best Bible.

This project was apparently undertaken by Dr. Andi Wu of the
Global Bible Initiative.  You can find
his (her?) work [here.](http://csbible.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/Quantitative-Translation-Evaluation-by-GBI.pdf)  I’ve never heard of Wu or the Global Bible
Initiative before, and the website of the latter isn’t particularly
informative.  Apparently, they are
concerned with translating the Bible into various Asian languages, but they
won’t tell me, for instance, who their financial backers are.  I like to know things like that because I am
a cynical, suspicious soul who wonders if the Southern Baptist Convention might
be involved somehow.



In any event, the study appears on the CSB promo site,
and—surprise!—the CSB wins.  However, the
means used to reach this conclusion are novel. 
Wu’s evaluation of nine total translations (CSB, ESV, KJV, NASB, NET,
NIV, NKJV, NLT, and NRSV) first takes a quantitative look at their
literalness.  It does this both at the
level of grammar/sentence structure and word choice.  Second, it evaluates their readability by
comparing both grammar and word choice to the Brown Corpus, a massive database
of English texts.  Finally, it combines those two ratings into a balance score.

According to this, the CSB is the most balanced, with a
score of 70.3%.  Then comes the ESV, with
69.3%.  It’s followed by the NASB, with
68.7%, and the NKJV, with 67.3%.  Also of
interest are the NIV, with 63.6% (which predictably gets hammered on
literalness) and the KJV, with 61.0% (which predictably gets hammered on
readability).

As mentioned above, I’m a cynical, suspicious soul, so
whenever a publisher offers up a study that shows that its pet translation is
the best, I want to know how they massaged the numbers to get there.  In this case, I think the key is in the
weighting of the sub-scores for grammar and word choice.  In every instance, the CSB scores higher on
syntactic fluency than on word choice/vocabulary rate.  I don’t think it’s a coincidence that the
study proclaims that syntactic fluency should be given twice the weight of
word-level considerations.  I’m no
linguist, but I spent a couple of hours digging around on linguistics websites,
and I couldn’t find anything to support the assertion that sentence structure
is more linguistically important than word choice.  If anything, that seems backwards to me.

As a result, I went through and recalculated Wu’s statistics
for the top four Bibles using a non-weighted average.  The table below reports my findings.

This seems more likely to me.  I’ve used both the NASB and the ESV
extensively, and the NASB has always struck me as slightly more literal, and
the ESV as slightly more readable.  Wu’s
original findings have the NASB as both less literal and less readable, which
has not been my experience.

With the new
statistics, the CSB, rather than emerging as the anointed victor, makes a
strong third-place showing.  It’s sort of
like an NIV Lite:  losing slightly in
word-for-word precision while gaining in readability.  The NKJV brings up the rear this time too,
though I wonder if some of its literalness problems are the result of it being
based on the Textus Receptus rather than more modern Greek texts.

In sum, though it doesn't look like the study proves what Holman wants it to prove, it still makes for an interesting
translation-comparison resource.  Most
comparisons that I’ve seen are entirely subjective and qualitative.  Even the reading grade-level numbers that many
sites repeat are somebody slapping a score on their hunch.

Now,
at least we have a resource that says, “Here’s how much more literal the ESV is
than the NIV.”  This confirms the
intuition of many conservative Christians that the readability gains in the NIV
and NLT are far less than what those translations lose in literalness.  Sure, the translation is easier to
understand, but the actual word of God isn’t.

Finally, even though I don’t think the argument here is as
strong as Holman wants us to believe, I think it shows that there’s a niche for
the CSB.  It has succeeded in finding a
middle ground between the basically formal-equivalent trio of the NASB, ESV and
NKJV and the dynamic-equivalent duo of the NIV and NLT.  You do have to give away some precision
to get there, but you don’t give away the farm like the NIV and NLT do.  Does this show that the CSB is some kind of
runaway winner?  No, but it certainly looks
like it might be useful.
