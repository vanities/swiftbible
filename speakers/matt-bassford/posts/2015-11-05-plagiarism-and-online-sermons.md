After I posted on Tuesday about the problem of preachers
plagiarizing sermons, I had several interesting conversations on the subject,
some public, some not.  I thought,
though, that one of the private commenters made a point particularly worth
exploring.  He observed that if every
preacher in existence stopped putting sermon manuscripts and recordings online,
the problem would go away.  Why, then, do
some preachers (of whom I am one) complain loudly about plagiarism while
engaging in precisely the behavior that enables it?



I have to say, the question made me engage in some
soul-searching!  Why do I do that?  Why do I put whole sermons up verbatim the
day after I preach them, post links all over Facebook, and closely monitor the
page views I get?  Why do I blog at all,
when many of my posts could be cut-and-pasted into a sermon by the thoughtless
or unscrupulous?

My commenter suggested that in many cases, sermon promotion
is actually a form of self-promotion.  If
we don’t put our material online, nobody will ever find out what wonderful
preachers we are!  If part of our adoring
audience is plagiaristic preachers, we’ll wag a finger at them with one hand
while giving them more sermons to plagiarize with the other.  We’d rather they gave us credit from the pulpit
so that even more people could be impressed by us, but if they don’t, at least
they’re giving us clicks.

In my case, at least, there’s some truth to that
portrait.  I don’t preach to be
ignored.  I don’t blog to be
ignored.  I do both because I think I’ve
got something to say that’s worth hearing. 
If people begin reading my posts because I wrote them, rather than
because the click-bait headline intrigued them (“These 10 Plagiarism Facts Will
Leave You Speechless!”), so much the better. 
Yes.  I do want as many readers as
possible.  No question.

However.  If page
views were most important to me, I don’t think I’d be doing what I’m
doing.  In neither my preaching nor my
writing am I conscious of altering my content in order to please others.  When I’m approaching a difficult subject, I
will wordsmith my argument to a fare-thee-well, but I won’t dodge the
subject.  Nor does anyone require me to
blog about spiritual things at all.  I
suspect that if I wrote reviews of computer games instead of Bibles, I’d get
ten times the page views!

The point, then, seems to be my message rather than myself.  I want people reading what I write because I
think it’s useful and true, and especially in the case of my sermons, because
it reflects the word of God.  That word
will not return to Him empty, and I’ve come to realize that there are some very
surprising people who read my blog.  To
the extent that I desire prominence for myself, it is because I desire
prominence for the gospel.

If a plagiarist takes one of my sermons and reads it
verbatim from the pulpit, it doesn’t bother me that I’m not getting “credit
where credit is due”.  If Paul could
rejoice in the proclamation of the gospel by those who were filled with envy,
strife, and selfish ambition, I can rejoice in the proclamation of the gospel
by plagiarists.  In fact, if they’re
going to plagiarize something, I’d rather have them plagiarize something I’ve
written.  I’m confident in the soundness
of my sermons (otherwise I wouldn’t have preached them!), but I’m not so sure
about the soundness of some random dude online.

When it comes to plagiarism, it’s not the offense to me that
I’m concerned about.  It’s the offense to
congregations who are deceived about the authorship of the material they’re
hearing, congregations that may well be paying a man for work he isn’t
doing.  It’s the offense to the soul of
the man doing the plagiarizing.  He’s
allowing laziness and pride to lead him down a path that has no good end.

Most of all, though, it’s the offense to God, whose gospel
ought to be proclaimed straightforwardly, rather than from false
pretenses.  Even the envious,
strife-filled, and selfishly ambitious can glorify God by their preaching, but
He is best honored when envy, strife, and ambition are absent.  So too with plagiarism.  Let’s not be so distracted by the good that a
plagiarized sermon can do that we overlook all the ways that a sermon without
plagiarism is better.
