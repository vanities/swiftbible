After my post last week on [darkness in hymns](http://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2014/09/darkness-in-hymns.html), a discussion arose on Facebook about the way different hymns interact with our emotional state.  During this discussion, I compared "Joyful, Joyful, We Adore Thee" and "Sing and Be Happy".  A brother replied, "Matthew, the lightning may strike you for daring to utter 'Sing and Be Happy' in the same sentence with 'Joyful, Joyful, We Adore Thee'."

He spoke in jest, of course, but even the humor raises a fascinating point.  Neither one of those hymns was written by a brother, and they were imported into our worship from two very different musical styles.  "Sing and Be Happy" was originally written for use by gospel quartets like [this](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Mz7X6S0ISDg), but "Joyful, Joyful" is a high-church hymn intended for choir-and-organ performance like [this](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g3RhvVfCOII).

When we consider this contrast, we might well observe along with Kipling, "Never shall the twain meet."  It's hard to imagine the Mormon Tabernacle Choir exiting the stage to make room for the Continental Divide Gospel Quartet.  Robes and rounded vowels do not go with blue jeans and band-saw twangs.  Any church that tried to bring the two together would produce a worship service more jarring than harmonious.  Many denominational churches facing a similar culture clash have adopted the expedient of divided traditional and contemporary services, often even in different auditoriums.  They just can't get the pipe organ to work with the electric guitar and drum set.

The great exception to the rule, of course, is the Lord's church.  I wish I could remember where, but a few weeks ago, I read a blog post written by a Catholic about the worship of churches of Christ, and he particularly commended our ability to assimilate different musical styles into our tradition while still maintaining continuity of worship.  Not one of us would even raise an eyebrow if a song leader led "Joyful, Joyful" and "Sing and Be Happy" in the same assembly.

Indeed, many song leaders intentionally introduce hymns with different origins.  I still remember Craig Roberts, the brother who taught me to lead singing, telling me to mix it up.  You don't want to lead too many high-church hymns; you don't want to lead too many gospel hymns.  That way, everybody gets to sing something they appreciate.

We are able to include such a variety because of the unique adaptability of congregational a-capella worship.  Provided that a hymn or praise song has a strong melody, an intuitive rhythm, and a fairly consistent lyrical meter, we can sing it.  Anything from "A Mighty Fortress" to "I Will Call Upon the Lord" is going to work, and all those hymns are going to work with each other too.

This unique ability to assimilate shows the wisdom of the Lord's pattern for the church.  As with so many other areas in our corporate work and worship, an ordinance that seems to be restrictive actually proves to be liberating.  Our commitment to a particular form of worship frees us to borrow from practically any worship tradition, often within the bounds of a single service.

Our recognition of our liberty in Christ should also free us to avoid the worship wars currently raging in the wider religious world.  Our answer to the question "Traditional or contemporary?" should be "Both"!  There is both good and bad in the praise songs of today, just as there is good and bad in the quartet songs of 75 years ago or the Anglican standards of 150 years ago.  We should look neither to freeze nor to abandon our musical tradition, but to enrich it with the best of what each new year brings.  There should be a place in our repertoire for new and old alike--because God in His wisdom has provided that such a place can exist.
