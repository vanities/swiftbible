Some time ago, my brother Shaun Calix asked me to define the
various genres of hymn for him.  This
business of asking me questions is a fraught one.  Generally, it means that you will provoke
either a sermon or a blog post, and that your reply won’t emerge for weeks,
months, or possibly ever.  Today, though,
is Shaun’s lucky day!



Hymn genre is an issue that most Christians don’t
think too much about.  If anything, they
probably divide hymns into “Hymns I can’t remember learning” and “Hymns I can
remember not knowing”.  Ultimately, this
isn’t really a problem.  We can worship
God just fine with a hymn whether we know its origin or not.

However, it’s at least useful for song leaders to have an
understanding of genre.  Whether we
realize it or not, all of us tend to play favorites when we select hymns.  Back when I still prepared the 30-hymn orders
of services for the annual singing that the Joliet church hosts, I tried to
vary hymn style as much as I could.  Yet,
to my critical ear, half the hymns in the service came out sounding just the
same anyway (“There we go into the V/V at the end of the second phrase *again*.”), and monotony is a worship
killer.

Just as song leaders can combat monotony by choosing hymns
with different moods and tempos, so too they can add variety by selecting hymns
from different genres.  This also pleases
the congregation.  If you’re a song
leader who leads nothing but stately Lutheran anthems, you’ll probably have the
couple in the back that loves Southern gospel grinding their teeth.  If nobody gets all the hymns from the genre
they prefer, then everybody will probably get something from the genre they
prefer.

I haven’t done any formal study on the subject of genre;
this is just the way that I’ve come to organize things in my head after 20
years of hymn geekery.  From oldest to
newest, here are the hymn genres that I identify:

**Lutheran Hymns.** In the churches of Christ, we sing
basically nothing that was written before the Protestant Reformation.  Chronologically, the first group of writers
that has had an impact on our repertoire is the German Lutheran hymnists, who were
most productive from around 1525 to 1700. 
Their work was then translated into English over the next couple of
centuries, though the original German tunes were generally retained.

Stylistically, these hymns are distinguished by their great
formality.  Their content is strong,
sometimes to the point of not being easy to understand.  Their rhyme schemes are often elaborate.  Musically, they are simple rhythmically but
complex harmonically, commonly changing chords on every beat.  Lutheran bass lines are famously hard to sing
because they were originally written for the organ and other instruments, not
the voice.  Led poorly, Lutheran hymns
can become dull and ponderous.  Led well,
they are stately and powerful.

Examples:  “A Mighty
Fortress”, “O Sacred Head”, “Ah, Holy Jesus!”

**Golden-Age
Hymns.** Before 1700, few English
churches sang hymns in worship.  Instead,
they relied on psalm paraphrases or simply chanted psalms without
paraphrasing.  However, around the turn
of the century, some writers, most notably Isaac Watts, began to argue that the
psalmody of the Old Testament couldn’t give full expression to those who believed
in the gospel of the New Testament. 
Watts wrote his share of paraphrases, but he also wrote more than 1000
original hymns.  As the century
progressed, other writers, including Charles Wesley, John Newton, and William
Cowper, also turned their attention to hymns. 
However, we don’t usually sing the work of these 18th-century
authors to tunes written by 18th-century composers.  Instead, in our repertoire, they are paired with tunes written in the 19th century by composers such as
Lowell Mason and William Bradbury.

Today, the hymns that survive from this era are notable for
their extremely high quality.  This is
not because all the hymns from the Golden Age of Hymns were golden; it’s
because hundreds of years of hymnal editors have weeded out all the ones that
weren’t.  I own a hymnal from the early
19th century that contains about a thousand of Wesley’s lyrics.  The dozen we sing are good; 99 percent of the
rest are terrible.  However, any hymn
that continues in use for more than 200 years must of necessity be doing
everything right.

Content and technique are all strong (though some
words that used to rhyme perfectly in 1750 no longer rhyme today because the
way we pronounce them has changed).  Like
most hymns written before the past 25 years, these hymns use “Thee” and “Thou”,
which may make them sound a little stilted to us.  Also, they generally don’t have choruses
(unless a later composer grafted one on) and tend to be shorter.

Examples:  “When I
Survey the Wondrous Cross”, “Amazing Grace”, “Soldiers of Christ, Arise!”

**Victorian Hymns.** I’m perfectly aware that Queen Victoria
reigned from 1837 to 1901, but I prefer to be sloppy about things and class all
19th-century British hymns as Victorian.  That allows me to bring in hymns like “Holy,
Holy, Holy!”, which was written in 1826 but is pretty quintessentially
Victorian.

Along with Lutheran hymns, hymns from this era make up the
most high-church portions of our repertoire. 
Lyrically speaking, the writing is formal, and some of the authors in
this genre display an impressive amount of technical skill.  Content is, once again, generally
strong.  If hymns in this category have a
lyrical weakness, it is that sometimes they focus on technical correctness at
the expense of clarity.  They will twist
the English language into a pretzel in order to hit a rhyme.

The music also reveals a high degree of technical skill and
sophistication.  The bass line moves a
great deal and is often melodic in nature. 
Chords change with every beat instead of with every measure.  There are lots of secondary dominant and
augmented chords.  Choruses are scarce to
non-existent.  These tunes have a softer,
more complex sound than tub-thumping gospel tunes and tend to rely on
interesting harmony rather than strong melody. 
Singers who aren’t strong sightreaders (or who don’t already know the
tune by heart) can struggle with them.

Examples:  “Holy,
Holy, Holy!”, “Abide With Me”, “In the Hour of Trial”
