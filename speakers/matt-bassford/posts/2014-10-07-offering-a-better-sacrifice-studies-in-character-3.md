*By faith Abel offered
to God a more excellent sacrifice than Cain, through which he obtained witness
that he was righteous, God testifying of his gifts; and through it he being
dead still speaks.*

*--* Hebrews 11:3

Abel is not one of the more extensively discussed characters
of the Bible.  He only appears for about
half a dozen verses at the beginning of Genesis 4 before his brother Cain kills
him.  We don’t know where he lived; we
don’t know how long he lived.  We don’t
even know how he knew that God wanted an animal rather than a vegetable
sacrifice.



We do know, though, that he offered a better sacrifice, and
he did it because of his faith.  This
attitude that Abel got right is one that God’s people have been struggling to
recapture ever since.  Just like God
expected sacrifices from Abel, He expects sacrifices from us:  the sacrifice of praise, the sacrifice of
doing good, the sacrifice of giving, and so forth.

Too much of the time, though, we treat these sacrifices like
Cain, not Abel.  We ask what we have that
we don’t mind giving up and turn around and give those things to God.  The hymn that we offer up without
understanding or worshiping, the contribution check that has little impact on
our bank accounts or our hearts, the life filled with excuses instead of good
deeds, all of these are symptoms of the same disease.

All of them, in fact, show a spiritual focus on how little
rather than how much.  They reflect a
check-the-box approach to discipleship, in which, as long as we’ve made some
gesture toward pleasing God, we expect Him to be satisfied with that.  Then, we can go our way and devote our time
and money and attention to the things that truly matter to us.

That’s not the mindset of the better sacrifice.  The better sacrifice is different, not
because it is perfect and has succeeded in being worthy of God, but because it
so badly wants to be.  The better
sacrifice looks up at the perfection and holiness of the Almighty and nearly
despairs, because it is so far away from being right, but summons up all its
strength and will and does the best it can. 
And fails.  Miserably.  But then Jesus takes that sacrifice in His
hands, with all of its warts and blemishes and flaws, but sees the hope and
unconquerable faith within it, and transforms it into an glorious offering,
worthy of God.

That’s the kind of better sacrifices we need to be.
