Non-hymnists probably don’t think about this a whole lot,
but the sounds and structure of the English language have an extremely powerful
effect on the body of hymns that we sing. 
Hymns mean things, but if you want to write effectively for the
congregation, they also have to say things in a certain way.

For example, in our hymns, the concept of
love has a close association with heaven, not because the Bible constantly
makes that association, but because “love” rhymes most usefully with
“above”.  As a result, in our song
worship, the connection is pervasive and inescapable.  We probably all think that love has a lot to
do with heaven because of the rhyme.



Some Scripturally important concepts get less play than they
deserve because the rhymes are unfortunate. 
“Hope” rhymes with. . . “Dope”? 
“Mope”?  “Cope”?  “Elope”? 
All that works very well if you’re writing musicals in the 1950s (when
it is perfectly acceptable to be stuck like a dope with a thing called hope)
but less well if you’re writing hymns.

Sometimes, though, the influence goes even deeper than that,
where whole hymns are determined by the rhythms of the language.  Consider, for instance, the first verse of
“Awake, Thou That Sleepest”, an obscure hymn written in 1876 by gospel preacher
Love H. Jameson (better known as the author of “Night With Ebon Pinion” and
“There Is a Habitation”).

Awake, thou that sleepest;

Arise from the dead;

Shake off the dull slumber

That circles thy head;

The deep, chilly shadow

Of death’s gloomy night

Will fly at the rising

Of Christ, the “true Light”.

Now, consider the first verse of the equally obscure hymn “O
Sleeper, Awaken”, which I wrote in 2015 (you can find the whole hymn [here](http://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2015/08/o-sleeper-awaken.html)):

O sleeper, awaken

And rise from the dead,

No longer forsaken

But joyful instead,

For over your sorrows

The Savior will shine

To bless your tomorrows

With mercy divine.

I managed to create a fairly good approximation of Jameson’s
earlier effort—without being aware that it even existed.  Why? 
Because both of us started in the same place and were constrained by the
same language.

In both cases, the starting point was Ephesians 5:14.  In the KJV (almost certainly the translation
Jameson used), it reads, “Wherefore he saith, Awake thou that sleepest, and
arise from the dead, and Christ shall give thee light.”  In the NASB, the translation I typically use,
the text is, “For this reason it says, ‘Awake, sleeper, And arise from the dead,
And Christ will shine on you.’  Jameson
may even have been aware, as I was, that scholars consider the text beginning
with “Awake” to be a quotation from an early Christian hymn.

In any case, for whatever reason, both Jameson and I wanted
to write from the passage, and both of us sensed the same thing in the
passage:  in English, the text has a
certain native rhythm and meter.  It
implies a hymn with the meter 6.5.6.5D (where 6, 5, etc. indicate number of
syllables per line and D stands for “doubled”) and a basically anapestic (weak
weak STRONG) rhythm.

Jameson fit the quotation into that meter more easily than I
did.  He only had to remove an “and” to
get there; I had to add an “O” (probably from the NLT) and an “-en” to
“Awake”.  Plus, I also opted to lose the
“a-“ from “arise” rather than the “and”. 
However, when it comes to doing surgery on a Biblical quotation in order
to get it to fit into a hymn, believe me when I say that that barely counts!

So.  Both Bro. Jameson
and I have our first two lines.  Both of
us know, though, that “And Christ shall give thee light” and “And Christ will
shine on you” are going to resist every effort we make to fit them into the
same rhythm and meter.  They want to be
in a completely different hymn, so rather than jamming them in where they don’t
want to go, we’re just going to borrow content and trust that Christians who
recognize the first two lines will get the picture.

In Jameson’s hymn, the content shows up with Christ the
“True Light” (a nod to 1 John 2:8, I’m guessing, though John 1:9 is also
possible).  In mine, the same content
expresses itself with the shining Savior. 
I thought “shine” was the most important piece of the NASB rendition of
the verse, plus it’s easy to rhyme with!

Speaking of rhyme, the two biggest differences between
Jameson’s verse and mine are structurally determined.  I rhymed every line; Jameson didn’t.  In subsequent verses, I repeat the opening line, which Jameson doesn’t.  I
also have an Internet full of hymn editors with sharp knives, which Jameson
didn’t, and that most likely explains the difference.  My peers expect me to rhyme every line, so I
do.

Both of those decisions add considerable structure but also
increase the technical difficulty of the hymn considerably.  This is obvious in Jameson’s heavy use of
adjectives ("dull", "deep, chilly", "gloomy").  He doesn’t have a whole lot
else to say (“And Christ shall give thee light” isn’t much content), so he
needs to pad it out a little bit, and he doesn’t have to hit rhymes all the
time.  In English hymnody, 11 syllables
between rhymes is *a lot* of room to
write!

By contrast, I have five and a half syllables between
rhymes, and in English hymnody, that’s almost no room, particularly with a
triple bounce like these hymns have.  I
don’t have much to say either; as long as “Shine, Jesus, shine!” is in there,
I’m good.  Instead, my goal is to hit
rhymes every five and a half syllables without resorting either to clichés or
sounding like Marvin the Martian (“Take over this puny hymn we will!”).

When I’m writing the first quatrain, I also have to keep in
mind that “awaken” will be returning in vs. 2 and 3 for me to rhyme
with again (and "awaken" is not a kind rhyme), so I can’t blow the strongest rhyme in v. 1.  I solve the problem by
using the verse to write about the emotional impact of Jesus shining on us (once you rise from the dead and Jesus shines on you, you're no longer forsaken.  Ding!).

To summarize, every skilled hymnist is more sausage maker
than poet.  When presented with the same
meat, they’re going to use it to make the same kind of sausage.  Love Jameson was an extremely good sausage
maker; as far as I’m concerned, “There Is a Habitation” is the best hymn about
heaven ever written.  Even there, though,
he says half of what he says because he wants to say it and half because the
English language made him say it. 
Sometimes, the results of being made to say something are
spectacular.  “Pearly portals” and
“glorified immortals” is a killer rhyme! 
However, he got there not because it popped into his head, but because
he knew how to write.
