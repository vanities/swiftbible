As I anticipated, my post yesterday on “Just As I Am” and “I
Come Broken” gave rise to all sorts of spirited reaction and even
disagreement.  As long as people don’t
start getting mad and taking things personally, I think this is
beneficial.  We ought to think about the
songs we sing and why we sing them.

However, as I read through all the comments pro and con, one
pattern emerged.  It’s tough to make a
case for using “I Come Broken” when “Just As I Am” is in the repertoire.  After all, one of two things must be
logically true.  Either the original is
better than the reworking, or it is worse.



Let’s take the first case first.  This is the argument that I made in my
original post.  When I look at “Just As I
Am”, I see a hymn with stronger content, better technique, and more
congregationally suitable music than “I Come Broken”.  Most of the counterarguments that emerged
focused on congregational suitability. 
Some insisted (accurately, I’m sure) that they sang “I Come Broken” just
fine, without any problems.  Others
described experiences similar to mine, claiming that “I Come Broken” only
worked with great effort on the part of the congregation, or not at all.  Of course, nobody complained that “Just As I
Am” was too difficult to use in worship, and I think this resolves the question
of congregational suitability pretty decisively.

In any event, if this is the case, there’s self-evidently no
reason to use “I Come Broken”.  Why
exchange the greater for the lesser?

Note, by the way, that this does not mean that “I Come
Broken” is valueless and bad.  If there
were a stand-alone praise song that was basically the newly written parts of “I
Come Broken”, I wouldn’t complain about it. 
I might not teach it to my congregation (the same questions about
musical difficulty remain), but it wouldn’t aggravate me enough to provoke a
blog post.  The basic problem here is
that “I Come Broken” is a parasite on “Just As I Am”, cannot be separated from
it, and suffers by the comparison.

Next, let’s consider the opposite perspective, the conviction
that “I Come Broken” is better.  The
arguments for such superiority are generally taste-based.  Lots of people have said, “I think we should
sing it because I like it.”  We must
recognize, though, that this is driven by trendiness, not quality
(quality-driven arguments support the use of “Just As I Am”).  People like “I Come Broken” because it sounds
more like pop music than “Just As I Am” does.

There are two problems with using trendiness as a measure of
what should be used.  First, the history
of English hymnody reveals that trendiness has led to the use of many hymns
that should never have been adopted.  “I
Come Broken” is trendy today; 75 years ago, “Drifting Too Far From the Shore”
and “Did You Repent, Fully Repent?” were trendy.  However, now that the Southern-gospel era has
passed, it’s obvious to everyone that such hymns were never very good to begin
with.  I believe, in fact, that “Did You
Repent, Fully Repent?” teaches false doctrine. 
75 years from now, musical fashion will have shifted again, and hymn
critics will be saying the same thing about the vast majority of the praise
songs of today while singing a new batch of trendy-but-inferior work.

All that aside, trendiness is still a dangerous guide to
follow.  Ultimately, the argument from
trendiness is saying, “The most important thing about our song worship is that
it must conform to the current cultural impulses of the world around us.”  Never mind that Charlotte Elliott was a
better thinker and writer than Travis Cottrell; never mind that WOODWORTH was
written with the congregation in mind, while the tune to “I Come Broken” is at
best an awkward import to the congregation. 
“I Come Broken” sounds like what I hear when I turn on my car radio, and
that’s what matters most!

Would we be comfortable placing this priority on the values
of the world in any other area of our worship and service to God?  We don’t have to imagine what this would look
like; it’s evident everywhere in modern-day American religion.  Take, for instance, Joel Osteen.  It’s quite evident from his sermons that
cultural relevance is his guiding star. 
Without crossing the line and becoming crass (middle-class Americans don’t
like crass), he consistently appeals to the greed and pride that define our
present age.  Never mind the Bible, which
usually doesn’t make for pleasant hearing. 
He’s going to give the people what they naturally want!

Don’t get me wrong.  I
don’t think hymnists today should ignore the creative vocabulary of today.  To be honest, I don’t think it’s possible for
them to do so even if they wanted to.  Nor
do I think that “I Come Broken” is nearly as far down the road of cultural
accommodation as Osteen is.  Among the
ranks of contemporary worship music, there are far better candidates for that
distinction than “I Come Broken”!  However,
the principle remains the same.  When we
act like cultural preferences are the most important thing, we contribute to
the Osteening of song worship.

Obviously, I don’t care much for worldly relevance as the
primary criterion for evaluating hymns. 
However, for the sake of argument, I’ll accept it.  By this standard of evaluation, “I Come
Broken” is clearly better than “Just As I Am”.

Even that, though, is not a reason to use “I Come Broken”.

Let me explain.  Let’s
say that “I Come Broken” is one-quarter Cottrell and three-quarters Elliott and
Bradbury.  The vast bulk of the song is
the product of people who died more than 100 years ago, people who wrote lyrics
with Thee-and-Thou stodginess, who wrote in a musical idiom that has nothing to
do with Top 40.  They are as culturally irrelevant
as they can possibly be.

Why, then, are we bothering to use any of such a past-it
hymn?  According to this way of thinking,
the best part of “I Come Broken” is the 25 percent that’s new.  Why not adopt a praise song that is 100
percent new, 100 percent culturally relevant, that isn’t dragged down by the
snore-inducing handiwork of a couple of Victorian fuddy-duddies?

Either “Just As I Am” is better than “I Come Broken”, or it
isn’t.  If the first is true, we shouldn’t
use any of “I Come Broken”.  If the
second is true, we shouldn’t use any of “Just As I Am”.

One final note:  None
of this addresses what I think is the best argument for using “I Come Broken”,
which is that in a repertoire where “Just As I Am” is unused and forgotten, “I
Come Broken” is a way to revive the use of an overlooked treasure that deserves
to be revived.  There’s logic there, but
I think it raises other issues that deserve to be discussed in a post all their
own.  Maybe tomorrow!
