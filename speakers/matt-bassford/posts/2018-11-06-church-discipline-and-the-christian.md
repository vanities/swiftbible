During my last Sunday-evening sermon, I looked at what the
Bible has to say about the purposes of church discipline.  This evening, though, I want to address a
slightly different question:  what are we
supposed to do when somebody we know, or even somebody we’re related to, has
been withdrawn from?

There are a couple of things I want to mention, though,
before I get into answering this question. 
First, this is a sermon that is explicitly about church discipline.  This is about when a congregation withdraws
from an active member.  The Bible has
nothing to say about withdrawing from fallen-away Christians as a family, or
about withdrawing from fallen-away Christians as an individual.  Because the Bible doesn’t address those
subjects, I don’t have anything to say about them either.

Second, as we go through this, we must keep God’s purposes
for withdrawal in mind.  As we saw two
weeks ago, from an individual perspective, those purposes are twofold.  First, withdrawal is designed to protect us
from being corrupted by wayward brethren. 
Second, it’s designed to win those brethren back to the Lord.  The more clearly we focus on these purposes,
the less difficulty we will have with some challenging passages.  Let’s consider this evening, then, the
subject of church discipline and the Christian.

I want to begin with some guidance **FOR EVERYONE.** Let’s begin by
looking at two passages that on one level, appear to contradict each
other.  The first is 1 Corinthians
5:11.  Here, the Corinthians are plainly
told not to associate with this brother who has his father’s wife.  In fact, they’re not even to eat with him.  Pretty simple, right?

However, look at Luke 5:29-32.  Rather than shunning sinners, we see Jesus
eating with them.  In fact, He’s willing
to catch flak from the scribes and Pharisees for eating with sinners.  These are irreligious Jews, people who have
broken their covenant with God, and yet Jesus is feasting with them.  He seems to be doing exactly what Paul tells
the Corinthians not to do, and yet we are to be imitators of Him.  What gives?

I think the key lies in context.  Certainly, it’s true that the Bible is for
all times and places, but it is equally true that it was written for people in
a specific time and place.  What we see
in the text is often the application of a general spiritual principle to their
particular situation.

I think that’s what’s going on with the Corinthians.  We see a few verses up from what we read that
Paul clearly has both the goals of protection and redemption in mind.  We also see that the Corinthians were not
very spiritually minded at all, to the point of celebrating this member’s
immorality.

In those circumstances, I think what we see in v. 11 is Paul’s
conclusion about what they should do.  These
are weak Christians who are vulnerable to corruption by wayward brethren, and
if they are around sinful brethren, they’re most likely going to encourage them
on to further sin.  That being the case,
Paul’s commandment to the Corinthians is to keep away.  Because of who they are, having nothing to do
with that brother is the best thing they can do for him and them both.

Jesus, on the other hand, was in no danger of being
corrupted by a crowd of tax collectors, nor was He going to encourage them in
their sin.  Instead, He’s explicit about
His reason for attending the feast.  He
came to call sinners to repentance, and He believes that eating with these
sinners is the best way to accomplish his goal.

This leads us to the conclusion, then, that when it comes to
associating with a fallen-away Christian, we too must choose our course with
God’s purposes in mind.  Maybe this is
somebody who will lead us astray too.  If
so, we need to stay away.  Maybe we will
be tempted to act like nothing’s wrong, and so encourage them in their
sin.  If so, stay away.  However, if they pose no spiritual danger to
us, and if, like Jesus, we see an opportunity to call the sinner to repentance,
that’s what we should do, even if that opportunity comes in the form of a meal.

I’m fully aware that the principle I’m setting out here can
be abused.  I have no trouble imagining a
weak Christian who will see what I’m saying as a loophole to associate with the
fallen-away brother he’s always wanted to associate with.  However, the same thing is true of every
principle, every judgment call, in Scripture. 
They can all be abused.

However, that’s no reason to replace them with a simplistic
reading of the text that appears to free us from making judgment calls.  We’re supposed to grow in judgment.  We’re supposed to grow in wisdom.  We’re supposed to grow in the essential skill
of being honest with ourselves.  When we
take these virtues and apply them to the question of how to treat the
withdrawn-from, we will arrive at the best answer in each case.

Similar considerations apply in the case of family members
who have been withdrawn from by a congregation. This is an even more
complicated analysis because everything I’ve said above applies to family members
as well.  Additionally, though, there are
passages that speak to the specific implications of a family relationship.

This is most obvious when it comes to **SPOUSES**.  The Bible tells us
first of all what the spouse of a fallen-away Christian is not to do.  Consider 1 Corinthians 7:12-13.  For some reason, I’ve always read this
passage as applying to those whose spouses are not Christians and never have
been Christians.  However, that’s not
actually in the text.  The text itself
only talks about husbands and wives who are unbelievers, which applies equally
well to non-Christians and to fallen-away Christians alike.

Here, the Scriptural witness is plain.  If such an unbelieving spouse is willing to
live with the believer, the believer must not divorce them or send them
away.  Instead, they must continue to
live with them as husband and wife, with all that relationship implies, even if
they have been withdrawn from.

In terms of positive instruction, look with me at 1 Peter
3:1-2.  This text is addressed to
Christian women whose husbands are not obedient to the word, again, for any
reason.  In that situation, the woman of
God is not supposed to nag.  She’s not
supposed to fight her husband for headship. 
Instead, she’s supposed to be the best example of godliness that she
knows how to be.  Words won’t work.  Instead, she’s supposed to try to win him, or
win him back, without a word.

Similar standards apply to men with disobedient wives.  I think men can get away with being more
vocal, but even then, the most powerful weapon in the husband’s spiritual
arsenal is the selfless love of Christ. 
When he shows her the love of Jesus, even when he’s disappointed in her
spiritual decisions, even when she hasn’t earned it, that’s when he shows her
Jesus.

Finally, let’s consider the subject of **OTHER RELATIVES.** I want to
begin with the subject of natural affection. 
It’s based on the Greek word *storge*,
which only appears in the Bible in a negative form, in Romans 1:31 and 2
Timothy 3:3.  Natural affection is the
feeling that families are supposed to have for one another, and being without
natural affection is a sin.  We all know
what natural affection is from experience, and we know how awful it is when it
doesn’t exist.

The presence of natural affection is why the church is so
often compared to an earthly family, and we are commanded to treat our
spiritual family members in the same way we naturally treat our earthly family
members.  Look, for example, at 2
Thessalonians 3:14-15.  If my
flesh-and-blood brother does something wrong, he doesn’t become my enemy.  He’s still my brother, someone for whom I
ought to feel natural affection.  If I
start hating him like my enemy, that’s unnatural.  In the same way, Paul says, we are supposed
to continue to admonish our spiritual brothers when they stray into sin.

He uses the same kind of comparisons in 1 Timothy
5:1-2.  We are to imagine that Christians
in various life stages are our actual family members, and we are to treat them
accordingly.  How much more, then, are we
supposed to treat our actual family members like family?  Even a fallen-away brother in Christ is still
a brother in Christ, and no matter what our relatives may do that we disapprove
of, they continue being our relatives, with all that implies.

Of course, natural affection can express itself in a
relationship in a number of different ways. 
Most of us would agree that a mother who gives her drug-addicted son
money to buy more drugs is treating him neither with wisdom nor with genuine
love.  On the other hand, the parents who
show tough love for their rebellious teenager by putting him out of the house
are still showing love.

There are no easy answers here, no bright-line rules.  There’s only the question, “Am I treating my
fallen-away relative with love that is genuinely seeking their good?”  Here too, we can’t lie to ourselves.  We don’t dare mistake the anger of men for
the righteousness of God.  Instead, we
seek what is best for those we love, not only in this life, but in the life to
come.
