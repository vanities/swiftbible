A while back, I wrote [a post](http://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2014/11/use-bible-you-love.html) entitled “Use a Bible You
Love”.    Basically, I urged people to forget about the
paper-or-plastic wars and use the Bible that was right for them.  I still agree with that, but another year and
a half of reflection on Bibles and their uses has taught me that loving your
Bible is more important in some applications than others.

I don’t think, for instance, that it much matters what Bible
you use if you’re using the Bible as a reference work.  If you’re trying to figure out where that
passage is that’s stuck in your head, or you’re trying to follow along with the
sermon in church, any Bible will do.  In
both cases, there is some external force directing you to the Bible, and as
long as the Bible that’s nearest to hand has all 66 books in it, it will solve
your problem for you.  It’s also the case
that interactions with the Bible as a reference work tend not to be
sustained.  You glance at a verse, look
up for a while, then glance back down again.



However, the experience of reading as opposed to studying
the Bible is very different.  In me, at
least, when I’m listening to a sermon and the preacher calls out a Scripture
citation, I turn to it by Pavlovian reflex. 
However, I don’t have a Pavlovian reflex to do my daily Bible
reading.  It’s something that I have to
decide to do (though if I skip a day, I will miss it).  Also, my interaction with the Bible from
which I’m reading tends to be lengthy and continuous.  Back when I was reading through the Bible in
90 days, I spent about a half hour each day on the reading.  Throughout all that time, my attention was
focused on the Bible.

As a result, even though any Bible will do for looking up a
verse, not any Bible will do for keeping up with a daily reading plan.  Just because our minivan does fine
accelerating on the interstate on ramp doesn’t mean that we should enter it in
the Indy 500, and just because a Bible does fine for following the preacher
does not mean it will do well as a daily reader.  The requirements for the latter are a lot
steeper.

I’m reminded, in fact, of that Japanese saying that’s been
floating around in Facebook memes recently. 
It holds that you should only keep possessions that bring you joy when
you hold them.  I think that’s the right
test for a reading Bible.  If you want to
successfully read through the Bible, you must use a Bible that brings you joy
when you hold it, because that remembered pleasure will remind you to do your
daily reading.

It sounds silly, but I think that the most important aspect
of this joy-in-holding experience is the cover. 
A Bible cover that appeals to our senses of touch, sight, and even smell
is a powerful inducement to read the Bible.  Here, I think, is where premium Bibles (or at
least nice mass-market Bibles) come into their own.  It is, in my experience, more satisfying to
read from a Bible with a nice cover, which makes me more likely to do it.

This was certainly true the first time I ever completed a
Bible-reading program beginning to end. 
The two Bibles I used most were a Zondervan NASB Thinline rebound by
Leonard’s in brown goatskin and a brown genuine-cowhide Holman NKJV Large-Print
Personal-Size Reference Bible.  Those are
a couple of yummy covers.  I still use
the Zondervan Thinline regularly today because I enjoy holding it—nice thick
soft goatskin over bookboard that has been beaten into perfect flexibility by
long use.  Every time I pick the Bible
up, I flex it and run my thumbs over the cover.

Recently, I resolved that I wanted to do my readings out of
the KJV, so I turned to my only copy of the KJV, a black-calfskin TBS
Windsor.  This sounds promising, but the
reality is somewhat different.  I love
the looks of the cover, and I like the way it smells (yes, I sniff Bibles), but
the feel is wrong.  My edition of the
Windsor, at least, was printed in the Netherlands by Jongbloed, and it has that
austere feel that some Jongbloed Bibles can have.  The leather is thin, the bookboard is very
stiff, and the edges of the Bible are almost sharp.  I was hoping that the Bible would break in
and soften up as I read it more, but it really hasn’t.

As a result, I found myself skipping readings.  I simply wouldn’t be motivated to pick up my
unlovable KJV and read from it in the mornings. 
 I’d catch up by reading several
days at a time in my office, and as I went through my collection there,
flitting from Bible to Bible, trying to find one that felt right, I lit on my
black genuine-cowhide Holman HCSB Large-Print Ultrathin Reference Bible.  Recently it came home with me, and I haven’t
skipped a reading day since.

Let me be blunt:  this
decision had nothing to do with the HCSB as a translation (I’m not really an
HCSB guy), and only a little bit to do with the layout of the text.   It had everything to do with the cover.  Holman only sells a couple Bibles bound in
that black genuine cowhide, but it is luxurious stuff!  The way that the cover feels makes me want to
lie down on it and take a nap.  I will
read from that Bible because reading from it gives me an excuse to hold it.

All this probably sounds stupid to people who don’t have
much experience with Bibles with nice covers, but let me assure you—it makes a
huge difference!  We are created to enjoy
using beautiful, well made things, and a high-end Bible certainly falls into
this category.  If that’s what it takes
to keep me going on a Bible-reading plan, I will happily put the flesh into the
service of the spirit.

If you’re one of those Christians who has always wanted to
read through the Bible in a year but has never managed it, it might be that
you’re trying to use the wrong Bible.  I’m
generally loath to advise people to buy premium Bibles, but this might be one
time when the price tag is worth it. 
Read/watch reviews, paying special attention to what they say about the
cover, then pony up for one that really sounds appealing to you.  It might even be worthwhile to get your
favorite Bible rebound in cow or calf or goat. 
All of the options are going to be pricey, but if buying a premium gets
you reading daily where you weren’t before, it’s well worth it!
