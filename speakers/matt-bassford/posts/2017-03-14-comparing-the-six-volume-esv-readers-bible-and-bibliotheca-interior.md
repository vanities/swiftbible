In my first two posts comparing Bibliotheca and the ESV
Reader’s Bible, Six-Volume Set (henceforth “Six-Volume Set”), I talked about
the origin and advantages of a multi-volume Bible format, and about the
exterior of both sets.  This time, I will
discuss what actually matters—how each one reads.  In my evaluation, I read Joshua and Psalms
1-11 in Bibliotheca, and Judges and Psalms 12-22 in the Six-Volume Set.  Here’s what I thought:

|  |
| --- |
|  |
| Six-Volume Set |

**OVERALL DESIGN.** Once again, Bibliotheca’s design choices
are extremely understated.  There’s not a
whole lot to these Bibles but text.  They
don’t have a preface from either the original translators of the ASV or from
Greene’s team explaining their goals and purpose, so once you get out of the
front matter, you’re off into the Bible. 
Each book is identified by a sparse title page, in which the book title
itself can’t be more than 9 or 10 points high. 
There are page numbers, but there are no running headers.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEhhHn2ei8vlYD4csHp17ehO4s_lJjTc_Y7Ggf61nslFRoAIM_-U-PqMkwJaBDiLOuYFTQGBk_SuaCsOzWkmjM8fRw56MNY8BHP5uYOmb7mCFtDsiXOAvQR1igSpKZgbQRwpLKkmcyxFHA/s640/INT+Biblio+text+column.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEhhHn2ei8vlYD4csHp17ehO4s_lJjTc_Y7Ggf61nslFRoAIM_-U-PqMkwJaBDiLOuYFTQGBk_SuaCsOzWkmjM8fRw56MNY8BHP5uYOmb7mCFtDsiXOAvQR1igSpKZgbQRwpLKkmcyxFHA/s1600/INT+Biblio+text+column.jpg)



By contrast, the Six-Volume Set is much more lushly
designed.  It does include the standard
ESV preface, and it reveals that section headings will be in red.  Book titles, also in red, are about 14 points
high.  Finally, each section heading (and
there aren’t many of these; for instance, Genesis only has six) begins with a
line break and a drop cap, once again in red. 
There are headers (except on the first page of each book), and the
headers on the left-hand pages identify the book, while the headers on the
right-hand pages identify the section. 
It’s not a big deal, but I feel that the headers do make the layout feel
more finished and help me locate a particular passage.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgS4bTzgKylwqRCEHUDx4f_qjLS8_CKc8a8Y0CoBFHYNxm3K9o2gaJvxS2NHeZnZx4GtGS9lWYXeRJYRRQwnElCZJrs_jUzsT8bww_xqdKcrQzuH3W3Nwmb6nbikem7Fqcswkk7WRmPQg/s640/INT+red.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgS4bTzgKylwqRCEHUDx4f_qjLS8_CKc8a8Y0CoBFHYNxm3K9o2gaJvxS2NHeZnZx4GtGS9lWYXeRJYRRQwnElCZJrs_jUzsT8bww_xqdKcrQzuH3W3Nwmb6nbikem7Fqcswkk7WRmPQg/s1600/INT+red.jpg)



What is more of a big deal to me is that the Old Testament
in Bibliotheca doesn’t follow the ASV in adhering to the traditional Protestant
canon.  Instead, Greene chose to follow
the Jewish canon, which contains the same information, but divides and orders
it differently.  The Jewish canon has
only 24 books rather than 39, with 1 and 2 Samuel, 1 and 2 Kings, 1 and 2
Chronicles, and the 12 minor prophets all being aggregated into a book
apiece.  Also, the Jewish arrangement of
Law-Prophets-Writings moves things around considerably (and to me
unpredictably), so that the last book of the Old Testament is now Chronicles
instead of Malachi.

That’s cool and all, but it leaves me at sea.  I know the order of the Protestant canon by
heart, but I have neither reason nor desire to memorize the order of the Jewish
canon.  As a result, if I want to find a
particular book of the Old Testament in Bibliotheca, either I use the table of
contents or thrash around and hope to get lucky.  The lack of headers makes thrashing even less
appealing than it would otherwise be.

The Six-Volume Set, however, is up to no hijinks.  It uses the Protestant canon, just as God
intended (tongue planted firmly in cheek). 
If I want to find Ruth, I don’t have to thrash my way to Volume 3.  Instead, it’s right where I expect it to
be.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEg1H-DehFYv_WuWs6UD9l9ceAYtkPA0lYt5mlHOSvWgBcL2QiASAvjyWcJP6W5SDkctq3dxnulUxR0mrvDyJNuhhL9mXowE34DhD2tWy2HyLEqCNmODBgDXwjF2uvFhYAhDlhkEF80p7A/s640/INT+canon.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEg1H-DehFYv_WuWs6UD9l9ceAYtkPA0lYt5mlHOSvWgBcL2QiASAvjyWcJP6W5SDkctq3dxnulUxR0mrvDyJNuhhL9mXowE34DhD2tWy2HyLEqCNmODBgDXwjF2uvFhYAhDlhkEF80p7A/s1600/INT+canon.jpg)



**PROSE.** In prose, both of these Bibles are very
good readers.  They have all the room in
the world, so both are set with about 60 characters per line in nice big font (the
Six-Volume Set is in 12-point Trinité) with plenty of leading.  They are smooth readers, about like a
novel.  Because Bibliotheca is about an
inch and a quarter taller than the Six-Volume Set, the text column in each
Bible is proportioned differently.  As a
result, I found it disorienting to move from Bibliotheca to the Six-Volume Set
(and vice versa), but quickly became accustomed to what I was reading.

|  |
| --- |
|  |
| Six-Volume Set |

One place where I do prefer Bibliotheca, though, is in the
alignment of the text.  The Six-Volume
Set is justified (not by faith, but by the work of the typesetter), but
Bibliotheca is left-aligned.  I like this
for two reasons.  First, I think the ragged
right edge of text is aesthetically pleasing. 
Second, left-aligned text is marginally easier to read than justified
text because the minute differences in word spacing in the latter confuse the
eye and throw off the rhythm of reading. 
Because the text lines in the Six-Volume Set are fairly long, this
difference isn’t significant (unlike double-column Bibles that have 30 cpl but
are justified anyway, which can result in lines with two words and massive gaps
between them).  However, it’s a trick
that the Six-Volume Set missed that Bibliotheca didn’t.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEjQWHZ7SdH1OJJJfrFdaXLi2Mu76kETS8NdWA5EeqClLyRne1DhkAPxOz7lX6T1zcnXzoVaD-Fvb4jbctcJFmazWkjWTujXn0xWr5dTjnQt53I49g9Td-jOXhVwL1Ympi4UC24SAr0PRg/s640/INT+Biblio+ragged+right.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEjQWHZ7SdH1OJJJfrFdaXLi2Mu76kETS8NdWA5EeqClLyRne1DhkAPxOz7lX6T1zcnXzoVaD-Fvb4jbctcJFmazWkjWTujXn0xWr5dTjnQt53I49g9Td-jOXhVwL1Ympi4UC24SAr0PRg/s1600/INT+Biblio+ragged+right.jpg)



There were also some differences in translation.  Bibliotheca contains a derivation of the ASV,
but the Six-Volume Set uses the ESV.  Of
the two, I found the modified ASV to be considerably quirkier.  There were some things about it that I really
liked.  For instance, it uses the
Tetragrammaton in place of the ASV’s “Jehovah”, and with the demise of the
HCSB, there is no longer a major translation that even gestures in that
direction.  Similarly, in some places,
Bibliotheca is even closer to the Hebrew original than the ASV.  One such place that particularly struck me
was in Joshua 22:15, where Bibliotheca translates more directly in saying “God
of gods, YHWH!” as opposed to the ESV’s less enigmatic “The Mighty One, God,
the LORD!”

However, not everything that Bibliotheca retained or even
added is as useful.  It follows the ASV
in using words that were acceptable in polite company in 1611 but are not so
acceptable now, and as amusing as it might be to read such things, they are a
distraction from the text.  Similarly,
even though some of the bizarro vocabulary of the ASV got processed out, some
didn’t.  I do pretty well on vocabulary
tests, but even I have to scratch my head over the likes of “contemn”.

In comparison, the ESV is much less volatile, both for bad
and for good.  It’s the same solid,
conservative translation it always is, though as always, it tries to “help” me
a little more than I prefer.  One thing
that it is not, however, is a distraction.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEi2w-KTavLM_tHWY8HCeKaYQmyNZU0hXqa2Xmst7FG1DrxjI4W663hyphenhyphenGJJyYCx5XJEBTxxBMbsu41NIp7SfevzRytj0aK2r5CLEnlyyFsBBj2FOFbUaVrmfS8vbJX7h2uwyFpQjXVVAqw/s640/INT+ESV+prose.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEi2w-KTavLM_tHWY8HCeKaYQmyNZU0hXqa2Xmst7FG1DrxjI4W663hyphenhyphenGJJyYCx5XJEBTxxBMbsu41NIp7SfevzRytj0aK2r5CLEnlyyFsBBj2FOFbUaVrmfS8vbJX7h2uwyFpQjXVVAqw/s1600/INT+ESV+prose.jpg)



Sadly, neither Bibliotheca nor the Six-Volume Set took
advantage of an obvious opportunity to make the text even more readable.  Despite all the room in the world, neither
one broke out dialogue so that each speaker gets their own paragraph, as one
would see in a novel.  It’s always
baffled me that this isn’t part of the formatting of the ESV as a translation,
and despite all the changes to standard ESV formatting in the Six-Volume Set,
this wasn’t one of them.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEi2w-KTavLM_tHWY8HCeKaYQmyNZU0hXqa2Xmst7FG1DrxjI4W663hyphenhyphenGJJyYCx5XJEBTxxBMbsu41NIp7SfevzRytj0aK2r5CLEnlyyFsBBj2FOFbUaVrmfS8vbJX7h2uwyFpQjXVVAqw/s1600/INT+ESV+prose.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEi2w-KTavLM_tHWY8HCeKaYQmyNZU0hXqa2Xmst7FG1DrxjI4W663hyphenhyphenGJJyYCx5XJEBTxxBMbsu41NIp7SfevzRytj0aK2r5CLEnlyyFsBBj2FOFbUaVrmfS8vbJX7h2uwyFpQjXVVAqw/s1600/INT+ESV+prose.jpg)



**POETRY.** I was expecting poetry in both multi-volume
Bibles to work extremely well.  However,
as things turned out, I had a significant bone to pick with both of them.  I selected my poetic readings from the
Psalms, and I was happy with the way Bibliotheca set them.  In Bibliotheca, the Psalms are left-aligned,
with the same 1⅛” outer
margin that the prose texts got.  As a
result, there are no widows (one-word remnants of lines that wrap down to the
next line) in the first 11 psalms of Bibliotheca.  That’s good, and indeed, that’s what should
happen.

However, in the spirit of Psalm 1:4, I must report that with
the Six-Volume Set, it is not so.  I’ve
grumped for years about the way that Crossway sets poetry.  They indent it all to begin with, even when
an entire book contains nothing but poetry (at which point it becomes less an
indentation than a wider margin).  Then,
they indent alternate lines even further.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgKm5z9A_uYQ2nDhL_6V1mIAwAE9Ww1Btmj5rKxfVokVP0SyT0k9K7ZCs_RZIvHU76o0WFnvuBy-ul0mviTLDyCSaY_jpkgr4QSSB83thVrFnNX2CewmSvTmiKN-jz-g0_UxHkF9v76_w/s640/INT+ESV+poetry.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgKm5z9A_uYQ2nDhL_6V1mIAwAE9Ww1Btmj5rKxfVokVP0SyT0k9K7ZCs_RZIvHU76o0WFnvuBy-ul0mviTLDyCSaY_jpkgr4QSSB83thVrFnNX2CewmSvTmiKN-jz-g0_UxHkF9v76_w/s1600/INT+ESV+poetry.jpg)



The result is, appallingly, that the Six-Volume Set, which
as a single-column setting should have few to no widows in poetry, instead has
44 widows in Psalms 12-23.  44!  Admittedly, this is considerably better than
the 90 widows in the Crossway Thinline in the same portion of Scripture, but
the Crossway Thinline is a double-column, one-volume Bible.  A single-column, multi-volume Bible should
use its freedom to have far fewer.  In
the same reading, Bibliotheca once again has none.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEi7ZKEWSP7nAW5mJAeskEeDGg4SZk2gi-8hywjk6yCqBp_s3bdcXjuRZw2DaRQwwa0Obo4XPeZd10siMF1OfmJEvl61jrhOOUAicKNIlxQ0FqLIUjCLXXPjcF9jngwrmnZR4u5OygXAGQ/s640/INT+ESV+poetry+widow.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEi7ZKEWSP7nAW5mJAeskEeDGg4SZk2gi-8hywjk6yCqBp_s3bdcXjuRZw2DaRQwwa0Obo4XPeZd10siMF1OfmJEvl61jrhOOUAicKNIlxQ0FqLIUjCLXXPjcF9jngwrmnZR4u5OygXAGQ/s1600/INT+ESV+poetry+widow.jpg)



However, that latter point is kind of hard to determine,
because Bibliotheca doesn’t number the Psalms. 
I think this is a mistake.  I’m
completely on board with taking out chapter numbers in the Bible generally.  After all, they’re artificial breaks
introduced into what was written as an organic whole.  However, that’s not true in the Psalms.  Each Psalm (with a few exceptions) was
written as a self-contained unit. 
Formatting that introduces a break in thought at the beginning of each
Psalm is appropriate because there is a break in thought.  However, each Psalm in Bibliotheca is set off
only by choral instructions (if there are any) and a pilcrow.

I get why Greene decided to do this.  It allows him to combine psalms that are two
psalms in our English Bibles but may have been one psalm originally (like
Psalms 9-10 and Psalms 42-43).  However,
it does impart a wandering-in-the-wilderness feel to reading through the book.



**CONCLUSION.** Neither Bibliotheca nor the Six-Volume
Set got everything right.  I suppose
that’s to be expected. These are the first mass-market efforts to set the Bible
in more than one volume in my lifetime, after all.  There isn’t the same body of institutional
knowledge on how to do this right as there is with single-volume Bibles (and
Crossway’s recent single-volume designs rarely miss a trick).  However, even a work-in-progress multi-volume
reader is better than a nearly perfected single-volume reader (and in the
Schuyler Caxton, I own one of those). 
It’s good to be aware of the quirks of both Bibliotheca and the
Six-Volume Set, but if you pick the version that most closely aligns with your
preferences, you won’t be sorry.
