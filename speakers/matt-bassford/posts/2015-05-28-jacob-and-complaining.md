*And Jacob said to
Pharaoh, "The days of the years of my pilgrimage are one hundred and
thirty years; few and evil have been the days of the years of my life, and they
have not attained to the days of the years of the life of my fathers in the
days of their pilgrimage."*

*--Genesis 47:9*

The story of Jacob’s life is the longest-running in the
entire book of Genesis.  It runs from
Genesis 25 all the way through Genesis 49, and because of this, we see as much
of Jacob as we do of any character in Scripture.  This leaves us with some mixed impressions of
the man.  Sometimes, he gets it exactly
right and excites our admiration, but at other times, he shows us that he’s
just a man too, with all the same faults that we are heir to today.



After Joseph rescues him from the famine in Canaan and
brings him to Egypt, to spend the rest of his life in wealth and comfort.  When he arrives, he is granted an audience
with Pharaoh, and Pharaoh asks him politely how old he is.  This provokes the reply that we see in
Genesis 47:9.  At this point, Jacob is
130 years old, he’s going to live for another 17 years, and yet he’s
dissatisfied because he’s not going to live as long as Abraham and Isaac did.

Even worse, Jacob describes those years not just as few, but
as evil.  To us, this seems like an
ungrateful way for him to describe a career so obviously blessed by God.  When he was a young man, he fled Canaan with
little more than the clothes on his back, but when he returned in middle age,
he had great material wealth.  God had
given him twelve sons to carry on his lineage, protected him from his enemies
in the promised land, given him back the son whom he had presumed dead, and
rescued him from famine in old age.  And
yet, Jacob can only describe this providential existence as “evil.”

Today, it’s not difficult for us to fall into the same
error.  Like Jacob, we are richly and
providentially blessed.  We live in the
wealthiest land ever to exist, and we share in its riches, so that just about
all of us have material gifts far beyond anything our ancestors could have
imagined.  Our government guarantees us
freedom of worship, so that we need not fear the secret police bursting in to
one of our assemblies.  Even more than
that, we are heir to all the spiritual blessings and promises God grants to His
children.

In short, we ought to be counting our blessings daily, and
yet often, we focus on the minor things that aren’t right in our lives instead
of all the major things that are.  We
whine about car trouble, instead of being thankful we have a car.  God’s probably used to that kind of
ingratitude by now, but that doesn’t make it right.  Let’s give Him the glory that is appropriate
for all we have received.
