About six months ago now, the Internet went berserk debating
what color the dress in the above picture was. 
Some people said it was white with gold stripes; others, blue with black
stripes.  Today, it looks blue to me, but
at the time, I seem to remember thinking it was white.  Weird.

Nowadays, the Internet is going berserk with religious
debate, about same-sex marriage and a host of related issues.  As different as the positions are, they all
share a strange similarity.  Just about
every ranter on every side justifies his/her position by referring to. . .
Jesus.  Universally, they argue, “I’m
right because Jesus said. . .” 
Sometimes, there are even Scriptural citations appended.



Logically speaking, this should not be.  There’s no point in citing Jesus as an
authority unless we believe He was a real person with a coherent set of
beliefs.  If He wasn’t, His words and
example have no persuasive force. 
However, those words and example are often being used to support two
contradictory, diametrically opposite positions.  It’s like Jesus is the color-change
dress.  Some people see a
white-with-gold-stripes Jesus, others see a blue-with-black-stripes Jesus, and
never shall the twain meet!

In both cases, the difference in conclusions are the result
of a difference in perspective.  Some
people have a brain that’s hardwired to see a white dress, others, a brain
that’s hardwired to see a blue dress. 
Similarly, the Jesus that we see has as much to say about our
preconceptions as it does about Jesus. 
Those with left-wing sympathies generally see a left-wing Jesus, a Jesus
who:

* Promoted love as the highest good.
* Warned against materialism.
* Despised the religious elite of His day.
* Sought out the irreligious, marginalized, and excluded.
* Praised the poor.
* Blasted the rich.
* Urged His followers to unity.
* Flipped social convention on its head.



By contrast, conservative folks tend to have a conservative
image of Jesus, a Jesus who:

* Took a strict view of the sanctity of marriage.
* Had high expectations for sexual morality.
* Demanded self-denial.
* Zealously defended the law of God.
* Warned about the dangers of judgment and hell.
* Promised that if His nation did not repent, it would be
  destroyed.



These are two very different lists.  One can almost imagine them as personality
sketches of two talking heads who are about to square off on CNN.  They’re really going to get into it with each
other, too!  The host is probably going
to have to shut them up when time runs out.

And yet, every single thing on both lists was true of
Jesus.  I could provide a
book-chapter-and-verse citation for every item. 
The problem is that almost all of us have a nasty habit of focusing on
the attributes of Jesus that we find most agreeable and ignoring the rest.

You have to be a *real* hardcore
conservative to read everything that Jesus says about marriage in Matthew
19:3-12 without squirming.  Likewise, if
you’re comfortable with what Jesus says about wealth, prosperity, and earthly
happiness in Luke 6:20-26, you probably have political views somewhere to the
left of Bernie Sanders.

In both cases, we tend to address the discomfort by making
the plain words of the Lord mean something else.  Jesus isn’t *really* defining marriage as one-man-one-woman and condemning
divorce, is He?  Jesus isn’t *really* pronouncing woes against the
rich, well-fed, happy, and popular, is He? 
We explain away the words of Jesus that we don’t agree with, until only
a Jesus congenial to our prejudices is left.

In reality, that’s exactly the opposite of what we ought to
do.  Conservatives need to take a long,
hard look at Liberal Jesus, and liberals need to turn their attention to
Conservative Jesus.  Obeying Jesus only
when we agree with Him isn’t discipleship. 
It’s selfishness.

The parts of Jesus that challenge us, that make us
uncomfortable or even defiant, are the parts that highlight where we most need
to grow.  I see some tough questions
there for myself.  If I’m so much like
Jesus, how come I spend so much time with my fellow religious elites and so
little with the dispossessed and excluded of today’s America?  Where is my concern for the poor?  Do I put more trust in my retirement fund
than I do in God?

Every one of us should be able to compile a list of similar
questions, and they should be just as painful. 
If they aren’t, we’re missing the point of Jesus.  The Scriptural presentation of the Lord is
extraordinarily rich and complex.  He didn’t
come to make us comfortable.  He came to
emphasize the vastness of the gulf between us and God’s perfect righteousness,
and then to span that gulf in a single shocking act of grace.  Finally, He says to us, “Follow Me.”

Our role is to follow as best we know how, but never, ever
to believe that we have succeeded.
