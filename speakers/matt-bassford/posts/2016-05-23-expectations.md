More than anything else, there is one factor that determines
our earthly happiness.  It is the
difference between our expectations and reality.  I first learned about this as a Mizzou
football fan.  If, one year, I was
expecting the Tigers to have a losing record, and they finished 8-4, I was
overjoyed.  However, if in another year,
I expected them to contend for the conference or even the national title, and
they finished 8-4, I would be disgusted and mad.  I would have two very different reactions to
the same identical record entirely because my expectations were different.

Sadly, this isn’t just a football thing.  This is a life thing.  For some reason, even the pessimists among us
tend to have an optimistic tinge to their expectations.  We expect that our jobs will be like this,
our families will be like this, and our church will be like this, and when the
real-world jobs and families and churches don’t measure up, we react with
disappointment and even resentment.  We
can get in huge fights with the people we love the most because of standards
that may not be realistic and that we may not even have told them about.  With these things in mind, then, let’s
consider the power of expectations.



**Jesus and
Expectations**

This is not a new problem. 
Mankind has had great difficulty with flawed expectations since the
beginning, and, in fact, throughout the most important series of events ever to
take place on the planet.  Let’s look now
at many people’s expectations of Jesus.

Even though it seems strange to us today, one of the most
popular unjustified expectations of Jesus was that He would be **A WAR LEADER**.  Look at the comments by the two disciples on
the road to Emmaus in Luke 24:19-21.  At
first glance, this comment seems reasonable. 
After all, to this very day we sing songs about Jesus as our
Redeemer.  However, that’s obviously not
what these two men are talking about. 
This conversation is taking place on the Sunday of Jesus’ resurrection,
so by this point, in a spiritual sense, Jesus already had redeemed Israel.

That spiritual redemption, however, is not what these two
disciples were expecting.  They wanted
Jesus to redeem Israel from her physical slavery by leading a rebellion and
driving out the Romans.  They, and many
like them, took all the prophecies about David and the kingdom of God and
misread those as saying that the Messiah would bring about the earthly triumph
of Israel.

In reality, though, leading a rebellion against the Romans
was the last thing that Jesus wanted to do. 
His kingdom was not and is not of this world.  In fact, Jesus spent a great deal of time
warning His people that if they rose up against the might of Rome, they were
going to get smashed.  Sadly, the Jews
didn’t listen, and in a few decades, millions of Jews died as a result.

Second, and more simply, many in Jesus’ time looked to Him
as **A MEAL TICKET.** Read with me from John 6:26.  These were apparently people who had been
around for the feeding of the 5000, or who had at least heard about it, and
they followed Jesus because they were hoping to get more of the same.  They thought that there was such a thing as a
free lunch.

We may smile at the naïveté and greed of these Jewish
peasants, but it is ironically true that our nation today is full of people who
follow Jesus for the sake of material reward. 
They’re encouraged in this belief by silver-tongued false teachers who
promise them that whatever they put in the collection plate, they’ll get back
many times over.  Of course, the only people
who get rich off this scheme are the false teachers.

Brethren, it is true that God has promised to provide for
those who are faithful to Him, but we need only look at the example of Paul and
so many other first-century disciples to see that earthly wealth is not part of
the bargain.  We need to look to Jesus
for the true riches of eternal life, not for the riches of this world that
never satisfy and never last.

Similarly, it’s obvious from their words that the religious
elite of the Lord’s day expected Him to act like **A PHARISEE.** Consider the
indignant question of Matthew 15:1-2.  Even
though the Law of Moses never commanded the Israelites to wash their hands
before every meal, the elders of the Jews adopted this as a tradition, and
their descendants sought to bind it on others. 
When Jesus didn’t conform to their expectations, the Pharisees thought
this was a huge problem.

Today, expecting Jesus to line up with a human tradition is
no less of a problem.  However, this more
often takes the form of emphasizing the parts of Jesus’ life that we like while
ignoring the parts that we don’t.  A few
weeks ago, the online religious-satire site *The
Babylon Bee* posted an article about a socialist feminist deconstructionist
religious scholar who concluded that Jesus was one too.

We might laugh at that, but the humor points to a very real
danger for us.  It’s all too easy for us
to define discipleship in the way that we find most convenient.  We are, mostly, upwardly mobile suburbanites
with steady jobs and quiet lives. 
Without much trouble at all, we can cherry-pick Scriptures that prove
that Jesus wants us to be upwardly mobile suburbanites with steady jobs and
quiet lives, so as long as we’re the best suburbanites we can be, we’re
following Jesus.  Brethren, I’m afraid
that such a model of discipleship looks an awful lot like our worldly neighbors
and very little like the Lord.  If our
vision of Jesus does not provoke, challenge, and transform us, it probably
isn’t Jesus whom we are following at all.

However, not everybody’s expectations of Jesus were off the
mark, as we see when John the Baptist calls Him **THE LAMB OF GOD.** This takes
place in John 1:29.  Of course, having
the spiritual gift of prophecy certainly helped John see through the confusion,
but the apostles had the same advantage yet managed to miss the mark all the
time.  John recognized that the coming of
the Messiah wasn’t about any kind of earthly advantage or tradition, but about
the saving of the soul.

Two thousand years later, our attitude toward Jesus should
be the same.  He didn’t come to earth to
solve what we thought were our biggest problems.  He came to earth to solve what He knew really
was our biggest problem.  When we try to
plug Jesus instead into all those earthly wants, we’re missing the point.  Our misconceptions will only keep us from
getting what He does offer.

Before the Lord’s Supper, then, let’s reflect on our own
expectations of Jesus.  If John’s words
leave us in any doubt about His purpose, His atoning death on the cross does
not.  We remember His sacrifice every
week, but if we really understand it, it is vitally relevant beyond the
confines of the Lord’s Supper.  Jesus
died so that we could have the opportunity to be conformed to His image.  How are we doing on that?  Let’s consider ourselves as we partake.

**Flawed Expectations**

As we’ve seen, flawed expectations gave the Jews of Jesus’
day all kinds of problems.  Let’s
consider now some of the trouble that such expectations can cause in our
lives.  Let’s first contemplate the
problems that can arise from our expectations **OF WEALTH.** Look at Solomon’s
warning in Proverbs 23:4-5.  The point
here, I think, is not that we can’t get rich, even though most will not.  I think the point is that we can’t get rich
enough to make us happy.

This is hardly an idea that fits in well with modern
American society.  The people around us
believe, as Solomon sarcastically notes in Ecclesiastes, that money is the
answer to everything.  The
multibillion-dollar advertising industry exists to tell us about products that
we might want to buy, and if we didn’t want to buy them, to create the desire
in us.

The reality, of course, is that money and stuff never
satisfy anybody.  How many of us have
closets and crawlspaces and basements full of stuff that we optimistically
bought yet never even touch?  How many
people living in houses crammed with possessions are depressed and
miserable?  In fact, it is often the case
that all the clutter makes us unhappier, not happier.

My point here is not that money is bad.  We need money to provide for our families,
give to the Lord’s work, and help others in need.  My point is not even that it’s wrong to buy
something we like now and again. 
However, if our lives revolve around getting money so we can get stuff,
we’re wasting our lives.  We’re putting
expectations on material things that they can’t possibly bear.

Second, let’s re-evaluate our expectations **OF BRETHREN.** Look at Paul’s bad experience with other
Christians in 2 Timothy 4:16-17.  When he
was on trial before Caesar the first time, all of his faithful companions were
so afraid that they skipped out on him. 
With one exception, the New Testament is the story of imperfect people,
and we certainly see it here!

The first-century church was full of imperfect people, and
guess what?  The twenty-first-century
church is still full of imperfect people. 
If we expect them to be uniformly humble, patient, kind, wise, and
loving, we are going to be badly, badly disappointed.

I think every one of us would agree with this statement, and
yet in practice, that’s not how our expectations work, is it?  Instead, we expect to be surrounded by dozens
of Jesuses, and when one of those Jesuses lets us down, lies to us, gossips
about us, or is rude to us, we are filled with bitterness and outrage.  For that matter, we’re outraged when a
brother or sister appears to be indifferent to us, failing to treat us with the
enthusiasm we think we deserve.

All of those things are obviously wrong.  We can find book, chapter, and verse for all
of them.  However, if we expect our
brethren to perfectly follow the standard of the Bible, we’re expecting too
much.  After all, if there were a perfect
church, none of us would get to be members!

Similarly, we must be careful with our expectations **OF SPOUSES.**We see a truly sad case of unrealistic expectations in Genesis
29:31-32.  Poor Leah had son after son,
hoping that this time, the man to whom she was bound for life would start to
love her.  It never happened.

Today, I fear that there are many husbands and wives with a
worse problem.  All poor Leah wanted was
for her husband to love her; rarely are we so modest in our wants.  Instead, it’s easy for us to take fictional
images from the society around us and attempt to impose those on our
spouses.

Let’s start with husbands. 
There are many, many ways in which pornography is harmful, but one of
the most subtle of those harms is the way that it changes men’s expectations
for women.  Porn usually involves real
people, but it is not remotely real.  It
presents women not as they are, but as the basest, most vile part of men wishes
they were.  When a husband takes the
expectations formed by lustful fantasy and applies them to a flesh-and-blood
wife, disaster is the result.

Sadly, this is a two-way street.  Wives will use things that aren’t real to
form unjust expectations every bit as much as husbands will.  I call this the Edward Cullen effect.  Pornography is filled with women as designed
by men, but there are all kinds of books and movies that are filled with men as
designed by women.  The fantasy here is
not primarily one of physical intimacy, but of emotional intimacy.  These fictional heroes are unfailingly kind, thoughtful,
caring and patient.  If they have any
flaws at all, they quickly change because their beloved wants them to
change.  To be honest, characters like
that make my skin crawl, but a lot of women eat that stuff up.  However, when the perfect fantasy meets the
imperfect husband, resentment can be the only result.

Finally, we need to watch out for the flawed expectations we
can hold **OF GOD.** In the book of Job, Eliphaz doesn’t get a
whole lot right, but he does a great job of stating the expectations of many in
Job 4:6-7.  Basically, Eliphaz is saying
that we can expect our lives to go according to what kind of people we
are.  If we are righteous, God will make
sure nothing bad happens to us.          This
expectation, even if it is unspoken, almost always underlies the anger of
people who are angry at God.  Here they
are, being as good as they know how to be, and wham! this horrible thing
happens to them.  They get really upset
about this, not only because of the horrible thing that has happened, but
because they think God hasn’t been faithful to them.  They think they had a deal with God,
righteousness in exchange for protection, and God has clearly not held up His
end of the deal.

The thing is, though, that these deals with God never have
any reality to them except in our own minds. 
There are all kinds of things that God promises to Christians, but I
guarantee that earthly health and earthly safety are not on the list.  When we demand that God spare us from
tragedy, we are trying to hold Him to a promise that He never made.  He loves each one of us, but if the Scripture
is any guide, our earthly well-being is way down the list of His priorities.

**Managing Expectations**

In the last portion of our study together, let’s ponder a
few ways we can manage our expectations. 
The first is by **FOCUSING ON OURSELVES.** Look at the advice that Paul gives in
Galatians 6:1.  He’s writing specifically
in the context of talking to a brother who has left the faith, but I think his
advice here applies generally.  Anytime
we are considering somebody else and their behavior and their spiritual
condition, we must remember to look to ourselves first.

This is important for a couple of reasons.  First, of all the people in the world, the
only person we can control is ourselves. 
In the final analysis, my expectations for somebody else don’t matter a
whole lot, but my expectations for myself should determine the course of my
life.  If I want to make a difference, I
make that difference by choosing to become more like Christ.

Second, just about all of us have a nasty habit of holding
ourselves to a lower standard than the one we apply to others.  I might demand that my wife be the perfect
wife, but if I sense that she’s demanding that I be the perfect husband, I’m
likely to get indignant about that.  How
unfair!  Doesn’t she understand that I’m
only human???  We are most inclined to
make excuses for ourselves, but our own shortcomings are those we should find
least excusable.

Similarly, we need to learn to **BE CYNICALLY SYMPATHETIC.** Consider
the attitude of the Lord in Mark 14:27-31. 
All of the apostles are insisting, loudly, that they are so loyal to
Jesus that they are willing to die with Him. 
That’s a reasonable thing to expect from people who believe that He’s
the Son of God.  However, Jesus knows
that they aren’t going to live up to their words.  He knows that every last one of them is going
to flee and that Peter is going to deny Him.

However, even though Jesus anticipates their failure, that
doesn’t embitter Him or lead Him to reject them.  These are still men whom He loves, and after
His resurrection, He gives a second chance to everybody who asks for one.  I believe He even would have forgiven Judas.

In the same way, friends, we must remember that everyone
around us is fallible.  If we know them
well enough, we can probably predict where they are going to fail.  However, our cynicism shouldn’t lead us to
contempt, but to sympathy.  All of us
know a thing or two about failure ourselves. 
When others let us down, let’s remember that and treat them kindly.

Finally, we must learn to **TRUST GOD’S FAITHFULNESS.** The
Hebrews writer sums this up nicely in Hebrews 10:23.  The key is that He who has *promised* is faithful.  We’ve already talked about how unreasonable
it is to hold God to promises He didn’t make. 
In fact, the Scripture reveals that God believes a certain amount of
trial is necessary to our spiritual growth.

Don’t we understand this as parents?  I don’t try to make life as easy for my
children as possible, because I don’t think it would be good for them.  If I picked up their toys for them instead of
making them pick up the toys themselves, if I gave them everything they asked
for, if I hovered over them and protected them from every hardship, I’m pretty
sure I’d raise a couple of spoiled, worthless brats.  Now, there are things that I do protect them
from.  I don’t want them to learn to look
both ways before crossing the street by being hit by a car.  However, I think that struggle in measured
amounts is what will help my children learn to be responsible, successful
adults.

I think God’s viewpoint is the same.  He doesn’t promise to protect us from every
harm because that kind of protection isn’t what is best for us.  However, the things that He does promise are
best.  We need wisdom.  We need forgiveness.  We don’t need trial erased from our
lives.  Instead, we need Him to go with
us through that trial, and He promises to do exactly that.
