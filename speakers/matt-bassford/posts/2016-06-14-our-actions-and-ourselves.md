One of the major stories in the news last week was about a
young man on the Stanford University swim team who was convicted of raping an
unconscious woman during a late-night frat party.  This story has attracted attention for three
main reasons:  first, the prosperous
suburban background of the rapist, second, the extraordinarily light sentence
handed down by the judge, and third, an equally extraordinary letter written to
the judge by the man’s father.

In this letter, the father insisted that his son was a
basically fine, decent human being who made one mistake but should be judged on
the basis of who he was rather than this single thing that he had done.  Even though he sexually assaulted a woman, he
should be put on probation because he’s a good guy.  To our ears, this might sound a bit off, but
what really matters is not the way that we view the connection between what we
do and who we are.  It’s the way that God
views that connection.  Let’s consider,
then, what the Scripture says about our actions and ourselves.



**The Biblical Standard**

In this study, let’s first ponder the Bible’s standard for
evaluating ourselves.  Let’s begin by
considering what our Lord taught about **LEARNING
FROM FRUIT.** Read with me from
Matthew 7:15-19.  Specifically, this is a
text about false prophets.  These are men
who pretend to be sheep but are actually wolves.  However, Jesus tells us, in order to figure
out what kind of men they really are, we must consider not what they say about
themselves, but what they actually do. 
No good tree is going to bring forth bad fruit.

This principle applies not only to prophets, but to
everyone.  One of the great
science-fiction novels of the 1980’s, John Steakley’s *Armor*, begins with the epigram, “You are what you do when it
counts.”  In the eyes of God, every
action is important, so this can be presented, even more succinctly, “You are
what you do.”

Modern American society struggles with this one.  For years, I’ve been hearing arguments that
people who say racist things and behave in racist ways aren’t actually
racists.  The underlying premise of the
argument seems to be that racists are bad and he’s not bad, so he can’t be a
racist.  Brethren, the fond illusions
that we might cling to about ourselves don’t count for much.  What counts is the way that we actually
live.  If we do evil, then we are evil.

Judgments like this may seem harsh, but they’re fair because
**OUR ACTIONS REVEAL OUR HEARTS.** Here, consider Matthew 15:18-20.  This is Jesus once again, in the middle of a
takedown of the Pharisaical tradition of washing one’s hands before
eating.  He observes that what you take
into yourself doesn’t corrupt you. 
Instead, what comes out of you does. 
The problem is that the evil words we say and the evil things we do can
only come from evil in our hearts.

This is a truth that we often don’t want to face.  We consider some sin that we’ve committed and
we say to ourselves, “That’s not really me. 
That’s not who I am.”  Oh, yes it
is!  That sin is every bit as much an
accurate representation of our character as the most godly thing we’ve ever
done.  We didn’t sin because somebody put
a gun to our head and forced us to sin. 
The devil might have enticed us, but he didn’t compel us.  We sinned because there was a part of us that
wanted to, and the part that wanted to won. 
That is our sin, and we need to own it. 
When some evil proceeds out of us, that can only be because the evil had
already found a home within us.

Because our actions fairly represent who we are, it’s also
fair that we will be **JUDGED ACCORDING TO
OUR WORKS.** We learn that God will do
exactly that in Romans 2:9-11.  Let’s pay
careful attention to what the text says here. 
Paul doesn’t say that on the Day of Judgment, there will be glory and
honor and peace to everyone who really was a nice person inside and had good
intentions.  He doesn’t say that there
will be tribulation and distress only to the really awful people who have spent
their lives steadfastly doing awful things. 
He says that there will be glory and honor and peace to everyone who
does good, and tribulation and distress to everyone who does evil.

One of the great revelations of parenting to me has been how
early the excuses start.  Neither one of
my children is tall enough to ride on the big-kid rides yet, but both of them
are masters of excuse-making.  The other
one started it.  The bad thing I did
wasn’t as bad as it could have been. 
Look at how wonderful the results of my bad action are!  And on and on.

I’m sure that the excuses will be flying thick and fast on
the last day too.  However, God is not
going to go on a tour of our good intentions as guided by us.  He’s not going to be interested in why we
committed the sin, or who led us into the sin, or what good things came from
our sin.  He is going to look at the bare
fact of our evildoing itself and judge us accordingly.

Finally, we must acknowledge that **SIN IS NOT OFFSET BY RIGHTEOUSNESS.**Here, look at James 2:10-11. 
This is not how the Muslims do things. 
I’m certainly no expert on Islam, but my understanding is that on the
Islamic Day of Judgment, what’s supposed to happen is that Allah is going to
weigh our good actions against our bad actions, and if we have more good
actions than bad actions, we will pass into paradise.  In other words, the Muslims think they don’t
need a Savior who will justify them because they believe that they will justify
themselves.

That’s simply not what we see in the New Testament.  Instead, God’s judgments will be much more
like those of a human court.  Stanford
rapist’s dad to the contrary, if we commit a crime and are tried for it, we
don’t get to argue to the judge that we shouldn’t be punished because of all of
the good things we’ve done.  The judge is
not interested in our good behavior. 
That was expected.  He will be
interested only in whether we committed the crime.

So too it is with God. 
We will be judged according to the standard of His perfect law.  If we want to be found righteous according to
that law, the only way for us to get there is by being righteous in every
point.  If I go through my life being
faithful to my wife, that doesn’t earn me any extra credit in the eyes of
God.  I’m doing what was expected.  It certainly doesn’t spare me punishment if I
kill a guy.  Once we dig that hole with
our sins, we never get out of it on our own.

**The Standard and
Jesus**

The idea that our words and actions define us is the
standard that the Bible uses to evaluate people.  Let’s see what we learn when we apply it to
Jesus.  We see Him first reveal His
character by **COMING TO EARTH** in the
first place.  Read with me from
Philippians 2:5-7.  The Bible claims that
Jesus was a completely selfless man, devoted to the good of others.  Any of us could make the same kind of claim about
ourselves, but we see the necessary evidence only in the life of Jesus.

Jesus was and is God. 
He existed before the foundation of the world.  He has power far beyond our wildest
imaginations.  Nothing could offer any
threat to Jesus while He was in heaven. 
Basically, anything that a thinking being could want, Jesus already had.

However, Jesus turned His back on all that because the
Father wanted Him to and we needed Him to. 
He didn’t insist on His privileges or seek to weasel out of the divine will.  Instead, He obediently laid down His high
position and took on flesh to become like one of us.

It’s incredible to think that the Creator would do that for
His creation, yet Jesus did.  He really
was more important than we are, but He didn’t act like it.  What a contrast His behavior provides to
ours, when we cling fiercely to imagined rights that are nothing next to what
He willingly abandoned!  Paradoxically,
it’s our Lord’s humility that reveals His true greatness to us.

Next, let’s evaluate Jesus according to His actions in **LIVING SINLESSLY.** Look with me at Hebrews 4:15.  A few moments ago, we talked about the nature
of the judgments of the Lord.  According
to God’s perfect justice, there is nothing we can do to cancel out our own
sins.  There is no leniency shown to
first offenders.  Instead, there are two
alternatives.  Either we live perfectly,
without a single sin, and so are righteous, or we sin, even once, and are condemned
as sinners.

Every one of us has failed to meet the challenge.  Not Jesus, though.  He perfectly kept every command of the Law of
Moses, so that unlike us, He was justified by His own righteousness.  Jesus didn’t need anybody to die in His place
because He never did anything wrong.

During the last segment, we observed that the sins we commit
reveal something about our hearts.  They
show that no matter what we might like to believe about ourselves, we carry
evil within us.  Similarly, Jesus’
sinlessness reveals something about His heart. 
It reveals that there wasn’t and isn’t any evil in there.  The devil is able to entrap us by calling to
the part of us that wants to be evil, but when he tempted Jesus, he found that
there was nothing in the Lord that wanted to answer the call.  Jesus’ perfectly holy life could only be the product
of a perfectly holy heart.

Finally, Jesus revealed who He is by **DYING FOR US.** Consider how
Paul puts this in Romans 5:6-8.  Ever had
one of those friends who would praise you to the skies and loudly proclaim
their loyalty to you, yet when you needed them, they were nowhere to be
found?  The life and death of Jesus prove
Him to be the opposite.  When He died for
us, we weren’t His friends.  Instead, by
our sins, we had proven that we were His enemies.  However, even though we were the enemies of
God, people who had done nothing but steadfastly offer insult to our Creator,
Jesus loved us anyway.  He saw that we
were never going to get out of our self-inflicted disaster on our own, so He
resolved to help us.  He, who deserved
life, died so that we who deserve death could live.

There never has been, nor will there ever be, another
example of love that measures up to the love of Jesus.  Here too, we must evaluate ourselves.  We talk a good game about loving our brother
and our neighbor, but what do we actually do? 
When our brother needs help, are we there?  When our neighbor is lost and in need of the
gospel, do we love him enough to tell him about it?  Make no mistake, friends; underneath every
command of the Lord is the command to love. 
Every time we fail to obey, it is because we didn’t love God enough,
didn’t love our neighbor enough, or both. 
Jesus never failed to love, even when the price of success was His life.

Before the Lord’s Supper, let’s reflect on the character of
our Lord.  Jesus wasn’t given to boasting
and hot air.  Instead, He proved who He
was by what He did.  He didn’t merely
claim to love us.  He revealed His love
by dying to save us.  Let’s remember Him
now as we partake.

**Honoring the Standard**

Finally, let’s consider what we can do to honor the standard
that God will use to judge us.  The first
thing that we must learn is to **BEWARE
SELF-DECEPTION.** Interestingly, James
has a lot to say about this topic, and we see his thoughts on self-deceit in James
1:26.  Few indeed are the people out
there who are convinced that they are evil. 
Most of us think we’re pretty good, and there are many in the world who
are so convinced of their own goodness that they don’t feel the need to come to
church and make things right with the Lord.

It’s fairly easy for us to spot the fallacy in that kind of
thinking and to wag our fingers at those who practice it, but James here isn’t
talking about self-deception among the irreligious.  He’s talking about self-deception among the
religious.  These are the folks who show
up at church every Sunday and think they’re so holy while in reality living
lives that are anything but.

You don’t have to be a murderer or adulterer to fall into
this category, either.  James himself is
particularly concerned with those who sin with their speech.  Let’s focus in on that sin most beloved of
the churchgoing, the sin of gossip.  Do
we gossip?  Do we talk nice to people at
church and then come home and savage them while we’re eating Sunday dinner?  When our brother offends us, instead of going
to him, do we go straight to a third party? 
Do we reveal a sister’s faults to a third party in the guise of looking
for ways to help her?  All of those
things are gossip, and they are no less sinful and indicative of a hateful
heart than murdering our brother would be. 
If we’ve got a problem with gossip, let’s not lie to ourselves about how
serious the problem actually is.

Next, let’s be sure to **AVOID
ARROGANCE.** This comes from James
again, this time in James 3:14.  This
shows up when we start trying to deny the connection between our bad behavior
and who we really are.  Like the Stanford
rapist’s dad, we would rather pretend that some evil action inexplicably
materialized in our lives from somewhere else, instead of very explicably
coming from evil we’ve been nurturing in our hearts.  I think James’ language here is very
precise.  He calls it “arrogance”.  In other words, we are claiming a level of
spirituality we do not actually possess.

We are in danger of falling into arrogance whenever we start
engaging in special pleading:  “I’m
really an honest person, but I lied to my boss because it he made it too hard
for me to tell him the truth.”  “I keep
my temper under control, but my spouse was so obnoxious that they drove me to
blow up at them!”  And so on.  We refuse to acknowledge that our behavior
says anything significant about what kind of people we are.  Because we’re good people, the argument goes,
we ought not be punished and don’t have to change.  The reality, of course, is precisely the
opposite.  We have to own the truth of
what our actions, even under extreme circumstances, reveal about us.

Third, we should learn from our own records to **BE HUMBLE.**Look at where Paul takes this in Titus 3:1-3.  Among other things, this text urges us to be
peaceable, gentle, and considerate when others are hateful and obnoxious to us.  Why? 
Because we remember that we ourselves used to be the same way!  It’s easy for us to get indignant about
somebody else’s sins when we have selective amnesia about our own behavior,
sometimes even our very recent behavior. 
How dare these other people do the same kind of thing that we ourselves
have done!

By contrast, when we approach someone else’s sins from an
awareness of our own sin, our viewpoint becomes very different.  We’re sympathetic instead of
condemnatory.  Of course, that sympathy
must never lead us to deny the evil of sin, but it ought to incline us to deal
more gently with the sinner.  We
ourselves have every reason to appreciate how easy it is for evil to take root
in the heart, and how difficult it is to uproot that sin.  We can be kind and patient with those who
have been ensnared by the devil because of the memory of our own struggles.

Finally, we must **RELY
ON GRACE.** Consider Paul’s words in
Ephesians 2:4-10.  As I think all of us
have found, the subject of what our actions say about us is not a pleasant
one.  I don’t want to believe that I’m
the person my sins say I am, and yet, in a world without the grace of God,
that’s exactly who I would be.  We all
want to think we’re good people, but we aren’t.

Here, though, we come to that crucial phrase at the
beginning of v. 4:  “But God. . .”  But God, indeed!  For every Christian, it is the grace of God
that takes us and makes us into something we could never otherwise be.  Because of the grace of God, every one of His
children is counted righteous in the only court that matters.  We don’t have to spend our lives weighed down
by the burden of our sin against our Creator because it is the Creator has
forgiven us.  We can face the truth about
ourselves because our righteousness no longer depends on ourselves.

It is also true that the grace of God gives us a second
chance.  We took our first shot at
existence and blew it.  Once we blew it,
nothing else good that we did from that point on mattered.  However, once we are forgiven, we are free to
serve and do good.  Indeed, because we
have received so great a gift, we are responsible for spending the rest of our
lives in works of service and love. 
There’s nothing we can do to repay God, but we still ought to do the
best we can.
