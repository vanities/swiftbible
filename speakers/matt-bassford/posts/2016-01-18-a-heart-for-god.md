As I think most of us would agree, we live in a society that
is drifting farther and farther away from God. 
Even when the people around us recognize the importance of some
spiritual topic, it’s often the case that their perspective on it is all
wrong.  For instance, consider the
typical American’s take on the heart. 
They see the heart as being central to pleasing God, and they’re right.

However, rather than using the standard of the word to
evaluate their hearts, they make their hearts the standard.  As long as they feel good about some thought
or action, they conclude that it is good. 
They describe this as spirituality, but it’s really lawlessness with an
emotional gloss.

As always, if we want to go to heaven, we need to be wiser
than the people around us.  We need to
consider not only the impulses of our hearts, but what the Bible says about
those impulses.  Only then can we bring them
in line with it.  Let’s ponder what it
means to have a heart for God.



**Jesus and the Heart**

Let’s begin, as we often do, by considering the Lord Jesus
and His take on the importance of the heart. 
Much of what the Scripture reveals about this comes as the result of a
confrontation with **THE PHARISEES**.  It is
presented in Matthew 15:1-9.  It’s often
said that the Law of Moses was about outward acts, but the law of Christ is
about the heart.  That’s plainly not
true; the Old Testament has all kinds of things to say about heartfelt
obedience to God.  However, it is true
that by the time of Christ, many in the religious sect of the Pharisees had
turned their service to God into a bewildering tangle of traditions and exceptions
that had nothing to do with the heart at all.

This confrontation begins with the Pharisees marching up to
Jesus and blaming Him for failing to teach His disciples to wash their
hands.  This was something that wasn’t in
the Law, but was in the traditions of the elders of the Pharisees.  Already, we see signs of the Pharisees’ heart
problem.  They aren’t using their
religious understanding to help and teach others.  They’re using it to try to score political
points against Jesus, whom they consider an ideological threat.

As usual, Jesus isn’t exactly caught flat-footed by their
accusation.  He points out that following
the tradition of the elders allows the Pharisees to dodge the Law.  Even though they’re commanded to provide for
their parents in old age, according to the Pharisees’ little rules, if you vow
your money to God instead, you can tell Mom to go eat rocks.

We too have to be careful about developing our own little
rules, both individually and as a church. 
The law of God is to be obeyed from the heart, not evaded.  If we read Scripture in such a way that it’s
filled with all kinds of exceptions and asterisks that let us do what we wanted
to all along, we’re no better than the Pharisees were.  Our devotion to God is nothing more than a
sham.

Next, Jesus makes matters worse, at least from the
Pharisees’ perspective, with what He says to **THE CROWDS**.  Here, look at Matthew 15:10-14.  This is nothing less than an attack on the
foundations of the Pharisees’ belief system. 
They were all about ritual purity, ritual cleanliness.  As long as you did the ritual right, it
didn’t matter what your heart was like. 
Jesus says that’s exactly backwards. 
Washing your hands before eating might be a sound sanitary practice, but
it has nothing to do with your purity in God’s sight.  Instead, it’s the things you do that He cares
about.

Predictably, the Pharisees don’t like this, and some of
Jesus’ disciples report their unhappiness to Him.  One gets the impression that the disciples
are really worried about this development. 
Jesus isn’t.  Let the Pharisees go
do their dumb thing by themselves, He says.

Herein lies a valuable lesson for all of us.  By all means, persuade the lost to obey the
gospel, but don’t argue about religion with people whose hobby is arguing about
religion.  That’s the biggest waste of
time on the planet, especially when it happens online.  It doesn’t take too long to spot somebody
who’s just there for the argument.  They
don’t want the truth; they just want an audience.  No matter how wrong they are, no matter how
many hours we expend crafting carefully reasoned arguments to convince them,
they still aren’t going to listen to us. 
If that’s their attitude, we might as well skip to the conclusion and
miss out on the argument.

Finally, Jesus explains His point to **THE DISCIPLES**.  This explanation appears in Matthew
15:15-20.  It’s interesting that this
story begins with Peter, usual fall guy for the apostles, prompting Jesus to
reveal the meaning of His “parable”. 
Jesus obviously doesn’t think an explanation should be necessary, and
from 2000 years later, it’s easy to see His point.  It’s tough even to paraphrase Jesus’ comment
about defilement without explaining it.

What we need to understand, though, is that what is to us a
duh comment has shaken the disciples’ world. 
They’ve completely bought in to the Pharisees’ ritualistic way of
looking at serving God.  Now Jesus comes
along and tells them that what they thought was important truly isn’t.

Instead, Jesus points out that the true source of defilement
is not eating with unwashed hands.  The
true source of defilement is an evil heart. 
As the Pharisees illustrate, if you’ve got an evil heart, it doesn’t
matter how thoroughly washed your hands are. 
Even if you’ve got all your religious t’s crossed and i’s dotted, your
evil heart will still have you leading an evil life.

This is no less true for us than it is for the
Pharisees.  There are all kinds of
churchy things that we might do.  We come
here, sing hymns, listen attentively to sermons, make comments in Bible class,
partake of the Lord’s Supper, and so on. 
Those are good things to do, but they do not prove that we’re the real
deal as disciples.  Instead, we show our
discipleship by the way we behave the rest of the week, the way we treat our
co-workers, the things we say to our families, and above all, the things we
think and feel in our hearts.  As was
true for the Pharisees, if the Lord isn’t evident in our lives in all of those
things, those pieties we think are so important are just wasting our time.

Before the Lord’s Supper, let’s reflect on this.  As David says in Psalm 51, God doesn’t
delight in the mere outward show of sacrifice. 
He desires truth in the innermost part. 
We partake of the Lord’s Supper together, but there’s a sense in which
it’s very lonely too.  There should be
nothing else in our minds but Jesus, what He has done for us, and what we ought
to be doing for Him.  What’s the truth
about us?  Let’s answer honestly as we
partake.

**Defiling Our Hearts**

During the next section, let’s spend some time contemplating
the unpleasant subject of defilement of our own hearts.  As we’ve seen, the problem with defilement of
the heart is that it soon becomes defilement of our lives, and none of the
heart-defiling sins we’re going to look at can produce any good fruit.  The first of them is **LUST**.  We see Jesus’ take on lust in Matthew
5:27-28.  In blunt terms, lust is sexual
desire for somebody you’re not married to. 
Merely noticing somebody’s attractiveness is not necessarily lustful;
instead, we lust when we become preoccupied with that attractiveness and allow
evil thoughts to take over.

This is a tree with all sorts of bad fruit.  First, and most obviously, every single
sexual sin out there--fornication, adultery, impurity, etc.—every single one of
them always begins with lust.  Lust is
what opens the door for sexual sin, and if we keep our hearts pure, we keep
that door closed.

However, even if lust never reaches that point, it still
generates plenty of spiritual problems. 
First, lust is always unloving. 
It reduces another human being to a collection of body parts.

Second, whether we’re married or not, lust will cause
problems in our relationships.  It
focuses the minds of the unmarried on physical things, which is a terrible
mistake when marriage is for life.  In
marriage, lust focuses the devotion that should be directed toward our spouses
toward a figment of our imaginations instead. 
No marriage will benefit from that. 
In our sexually obsessed society, the struggle against lust is a
difficult one, but keeping it out of our hearts is worth the effort.

Next up comes **COVETOUSNESS**. 
The Lord indicts covetousness in Luke 12:13-15.  This one is much tougher to define than
lust.  The dictionary says that to be
covetous is to be “inordinately or wrongly desirous of wealth or possessions,”
which is completely unhelpful.  The way
that I think of it is this:  covetousness
is when you love money more than you love people.  It’s lawful to bargain for a fair deal when
we’re selling a car.  It’s unlawful to
take the other guy to the cleaners, because then our good fortune is coming at
his expense.

The same thing is true for gambling.  Unlike most economic transactions, gambling
is a zero-sum game.  The only way for me
to win, whether I’m playing poker or buying a lottery ticket, is if everybody
else loses.  Therefore, I’m hoping that
they lose.  The more they lose, the
happier I am.  That’s hard to square with
“Love your neighbor as yourself.”  As
with lust, we don’t actually have to achieve our goal for this to be a
spiritual problem.

In addition, covetousness has plenty of second-order
effects.  Sometimes, it reveals itself
when we love money more than we love our families.  It’s great to work hard to provide for our
children, but sometimes, what our children most want us to provide is time with
them.  Similarly, the more our love for
money grows, the more we are willing to do for money, and the lower our
standards fall.  Love of money has led to
everything from lying to murder.  As with
lust, it’s a tree that will bear all sorts of fruit in our lives, but the fruit
isn’t anything we want.

The final member of our dismal trio is **PRIDE**.  Paul defines pride in Romans 12:16.  Pride is thinking we’re the cat’s meow when we’re
really a lot more like the pig’s oink. 
Pride is when we believe we’re better than everybody else, particularly
the losers with whom we worship.  Pride
is especially dangerous because of its sneakiness.  It’s hard to lust without knowing it, but I
daresay that the proudest Christian in every congregation is the one most
oblivious to the problem of pride.

Here too, there is a lot of evil fruit to inspect.  Pride is particularly destructive when it
comes to our relationships.  Lust and
covetousness will rear their ugly heads from time to time, but pride never goes
away.  It’s evident in every word that
the proud man or woman utters, particularly about themselves.  It’s evident in the way that they disdain and
shun the humbler members of the congregation. 
It’s evident in their insistence that their view and their view alone of
the Scripture is the correct one.  It’s
evident in their unwillingness to engage in the small courtesies and acts of
grace that lubricate the functioning of any church.

In fact, to continue the mechanical analogy, proud people
are like grit in the gears.  They wear
down the working parts of the congregation and in time cause them to fail.  Behind every bitter church dispute and split,
you may be sure that you will find a proud so-called Christian.

**Guarding Our Hearts**

When the dangers posed by the corruption of our hearts are
so serious, it’s obvious that we need to make every effort to guard our hearts
against it.  The first element in our
heart-protection scheme should be **DILIGENCE**. 
We see this emphasized in Proverbs 4:23. 
Other translations here say “vigilant”, which seems like a different
idea, but it really isn’t.  Think of a
soldier standing guard on a castle tower, peering off into the distance every
day, watching for the enemy.  If he isn’t
up there all the time, he’s not doing his job. 
If he isn’t diligent, he also isn’t vigilant.

We have to do our jobs, then, by constantly being on the
alert for threats to our purity of heart. 
Sometimes, these threats can be external.  It could be some TV show we watch.  It could be some friend we associate
with.  On the other hand, sometimes these
threats emerge internally.  Satan sows
some temptation in our minds, and unless we’re watching for it, we will find
ourselves being overwhelmed by some ungodly thought or emotion.  This is work, no doubt about it!  However, if we aren’t willing to put in the
mental effort necessary to be diligent, our spiritual fate will be much the
same as the fate of the castle where the watchmen fall asleep.

It’s important for us to defend our hearts, but it’s equally
important for us to work to promote inward purity.  If all we have in our hearts is a spiritual
vacuum, sooner or later, Satan is going to find a way to fill it with
garbage.  We can avoid this by focusing
on **GODLY THOUGHTS**.  Paul gives us some
wise advice on this subject in Philippians 4:8.

This passage should cause us to seriously consider what we
do in our leisure time.  Too often, the
only question that we ask is whether some recreation is too bad for us to
pursue it.  This TV show is over the
line, so I won’t watch it, but this one isn’t quite there, so I’ll watch it
instead.

Brethren, that’s another example of drawing near to the
line, not the Lord!  We shouldn’t be
asking whether some show or website is too bad. 
We should be asking whether it’s actively good.  Are my choices in my free time drawing me
closer to God?  If not, then I am not
following Philippians 4:8.  Obviously,
this is not an absolute rule.  Not
everything in our lives has to promote godliness.  However, the things that do promote godliness
need to come first.  They need to be the
roast beef in our free time, and the neutral things need to be the gravy.  If the opposite is true, the best those
neutral things can do is create a vacuum for the devil to exploit.

Similarly, we need to focus ourselves on **GODLY
EMOTIONS**.  Paul points us in this
direction in Colossians 3:12-13.  This is
the kind of thing that most Americans think of when they think of the
heart.  Culturally, we view the heart as
the seat of the emotions.  However, most
Americans also view those emotions as unalterable.  How I feel is how I feel, and I can’t change
it.

Neither one of those concepts is Biblically founded.  The Biblical “heart” isn’t just the
emotions.  The authors of Scripture
regarded emotions as being seated in the bowels.  Instead, “heart” was the union of thought and
feeling, which isn’t really a tool that Westerners have in their intellectual
toolboxes.  Similarly, the Scripture
instructs us to control not only our thoughts, but also our emotions.  We are responsible not only for thinking a certain
way, but for feeling a certain way. 
Out-of-control emotions are as spiritually problematic as out-of-control
thoughts.

We’re supposed to crowd out those ungodly emotions with
godly ones.  Every one of the virtues
listed in these two verses is either a straight-up emotion, like compassion, or
has a strong emotional component, like forgiveness.  We ought to be serving God not because we
have to, but because we want to, because righteousness has a strong emotional
hold on us.  We accomplish this by recognizing
negative emotions and refusing to dwell on them, while simultaneously
encouraging positive emotions to flourish. 
As we meditate on these things day by day, soon our hearts will be
filled with the grace and gentleness of Christ.

Finally, we keep our hearts for God by making **WISE
INVESTMENTS**.  Consider Jesus’ thoughts
about this in Luke 12:33-34.  Note that
in this text, Jesus offers two reasons to be generous.  The first is that paradoxically, only the
treasure that we give away is the treasure that we get to keep.  Second, though, Jesus points out that we set
our hearts where we have deposited our treasure.  If we lay up treasure in heaven, that’s where
our hearts will pull us.

This text is certainly about money, but it’s not only about
money.  Wherever we invest anything we
have, whether money, energy, or time, our hearts will follow our
investment.  For instance, consider the
effect of Mormon missions in the Mormon Church. 
It basically requires that when its members turn 18, they have to spend
two years of their lives off in some forsaken corner of the globe, trying to
convince people to become Mormons. 
That’s where the Mormon “elders” who come to our doors come from.

I’ve thought about this. 
I don’t think the Mormon leadership will ever admit it, but I’m
confident that the primary point of the missions is not evangelism.  Instead, it’s to firm up the commitment of
the youngsters doing the evangelizing. 
After you’ve given two years of your life and thousands of dollars to
being abused and spat on for the sake of the Mormon Church, you’re not ever
going to leave that church.  Otherwise,
your sacrifices will have gone for nothing. 
The same is true for us.  The more
we give up for God, the more strongly our hearts will be drawn to Him.
