**OVERVIEW.**  The Large-Print Compact has some interesting
features, but ultimately, I didn’t care for it as a reader.  Others who prefer single-column layouts may
well have a different impression.

I had trouble sticking to my daily Bible-reading schedule
because I’m also reading through another Bible on another plan.  However, I did two weeks’ worth of reading
out of the LPC, though a lot of the reading came in big catchup chunks.  That’s not necessarily bad, though; it helped
me evaluate the LPC at a marathon pace as well as at a sprint pace.  Here’s what I thought:



**SIZE.** For whatever reason, I find myself
wanting to prop the LPC on my chest, even though I don’t want to do the same
thing with a Pitt Minion or the Crossway Large-Print Compact.  I hold both of those up in the air like a
paperback; apparently, the LPC is just a tad too big for that treatment.  There’s no comparison, though, between
reading out of the LPC and the behemoth Crossway Giant-Print.  This is still a light, handy Bible.  Holding it won’t wear much of anybody out.

**LAYOUT.** Lots to talk about here.  First of all, the LPC has a unique design
feature.  Unlike every other single-column
Bible I’ve ever reviewed, the text in the LPC is left-aligned rather than
justified.  It has a ragged edge along
the right rather than a clean line.

This looks strange, but I think it’s a good idea.  I’ve read studies that suggest that left-aligned
text is easier to comprehend than justified text.  Word-processing programs justify text by increasing
the space between words in lines with fewer characters.  Apparently, when the spacing is inconsistent
from line to line, this confuses the eye. 
It’s probably true that more Bibles should be left-aligned, and I’m glad
Foundation has stepped forward on this issue.

However, the LPC is a single-column text Bibles, and like
all single-column text Bibles, it has a high characters-per-line count.  I make it out to be somewhere around 78
CPL.  Generally, I start having trouble
with line length at about 65 CPL.

More than any other kind of Bible, single-column Bibles
benefit from having a reference column, because reference columns drive the CPL
count down.  I can’t help but wonder, in
fact, if the designers of the Tyndale Select/Schuyler Caxton text block
included a reference column for that reason. 
The LPC doesn’t have one, so it’s got a sky-high CPL count.

I noticed, though, that I didn’t have as much trouble with
comprehension as I was expecting.  I
wonder if the number of characters per line is high enough that my eye
naturally wants to apprehend the line in two glances instead of one.  In Bibles with a CPL count of around 70, I
can tell that my eye is missing the last five characters on a line because it
doesn’t want to glance over for five measly characters.  However, there may well be enough meaning in
13 characters that my eye doesn’t resent the trip as much.  The CPL count is so bad it’s good!

Of course, the LPC benefits, as all single-column Bibles
will, in the poetic sections of Scripture. 
For comparison’s sake, I read Exodus 13-15 in both the LPC and in my
Zondervan NASB Thinline.  The Thinline is
a double-column text Bible and my favorite NASB reader.  However, it does share a translation and a
typeface with the LPC, so I thought it would make an interesting
benchmark.

Through Exodus 13-14, the Thinline was my clear
favorite.  However, in the poetry of
Exodus 15, I found that I had an easier time reading the LPC.  There’s more prose than poetry in any
translation of the Bible, but single-column Bibles will have an advantage in
the latter.

Also in its favor, the LPC has a nice big gutter.  I make it out to be about half an inch wide,
which is extremely generous for such a small Bible.  In fact, like the Select/Caxton, it has wider
inner margins than outer, which are only a quarter inch.  This is a thoughtful, practical design choice
that keeps text from sliding into the ravine even in Ezekiel and other books in
the middle of the Bible.

However, the LPC is a red-letter Bible, and it brings no
credit to the tribe.  I will admit that
the color of the ink itself isn’t a problem. 
It appears to be a nice dark brownish red rather than pink or orange
throughout.  However, there are two
serious problems with it.  First, like
most red-letter Bibles, the LPC has registration problems.  There are places where the red lettering
doesn’t line up very well with the black lettering.

Also, even though I like the ink color, there isn’t very
much of it.  The print in the LPC is
generally very light, which makes the strokes in each letter very fine.  I found this hard to read without my glasses
on, at least in part because. . .

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEg7QFjdiMNiu6TivZ069l8Bb2W8q8MBBXlS7LLTlxajm0KBmmZIp6gBJV0Q2U0Ws0VhpIgaGiINBvHfwCJNDHUtkNblFwb3Ais19hyqJGEXYPfzbzEM3uJtBcgq7-CDYtFQt5d2IO_oPw/s1600/R+redletter.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEg7QFjdiMNiu6TivZ069l8Bb2W8q8MBBXlS7LLTlxajm0KBmmZIp6gBJV0Q2U0Ws0VhpIgaGiINBvHfwCJNDHUtkNblFwb3Ais19hyqJGEXYPfzbzEM3uJtBcgq7-CDYtFQt5d2IO_oPw/s1600/R+redletter.jpg)



. . . the LPC isn’t line-matched.  This is a big problem, especially when
combined with thin paper and faint printing. 
Admittedly, I generally tried to read the LPC in my office, underneath
the glare of a subtly flickering fluorescent light.  That’s not ideal, but even in that kind of light,
I shouldn’t have to work to distinguish lettering, particularly red lettering,
from the reddish-grayish haze underneath. 
Bibles like the Cambridge Clarion and Select/Caxton are functional
single-column readers despite lightweight paper because of their
line-matching.  The absence of
line-matching here is a significant problem.

There were even problems with print size in the LPC.  The headline font size here, 9-point, sounds
like it should be more than adequate. 
However, the designers do not appear to have added leading, and
Weidemann is a typeface without a lot of leading built in.

This makes the LPC smaller than it would otherwise be (less
leading means more lines per page means fewer pages), but it comes at a cost in
readability.  It’s striking to me that
the extremely well designed Clarion and Select/Caxton both have significant
leading.  The Select/Caxton in particular
is set in Lexicon 8.75 with leading at 11.5. 
Those long lines in single-column settings function best with a lot of
white space separating them.

I really felt the lack of white space when reading the
LPC.  In fact, even though the NASB
Thinline is set with substantially smaller characters (7.8/8.8 for the Thinline
versus 9/9 for the LPC), I thought the Thinline was generally more
readable.

**PRODUCTION
QUALITY.** I’ve already mentioned my
issues with character boldness.  This may
well have to do with the way the Bible was printed, particularly when the
characters in my Thinline are darker and bolder despite being thinner.  I’ve criticized Foundation Bibles in the past
for having overly dark, smeary printing. 
In this case, the printer (and this is another China-print Bible)
appears to have gone too far in the opposite direction.  There are also places where both the red and
the black ink appear darker than in others.

Similarly, the paper is nothing to write home about.  Generally, I don’t expect much from the paper
in a thinline or a compact, and the LPC is right in this same range.  I get that Foundation didn’t want to produce
a Bible that was two inches thick, but more opaque paper would have mitigated
the issues caused by lack of line-matching.

**CONCLUSION.** Single-column Bibles are supposed to make
the best reading Bibles.  Sadly, that
isn’t true of this one.  I give it a 6/10
as a reader.  However, the other
characteristics of the LPC are impressive enough that it scores an overall
24.5/30, which is a long way from terrible.
