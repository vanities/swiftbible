**OVERVIEW.** The form of the Windsor is
impressive.  It’s smaller, which makes it
suitable for use as a carry Bible, but it’s still quite readable.  It has the sewn binding that every Bible should,
and its (probably) calf-split cover is much nicer than I would expect at the
Windsor’s price point.

\*             \*             \*

This is another review Bible.  I asked; TBS sent.  As with all of the Bibles that are part of my
normal pattern of review, the Windsor spent two weeks at the church building,
two weeks at my desk at home for my morning reading, and two weeks roving about
the Joliet area.

**VITAL STATISTICS**

Translation:  King
James Version (1769 standard text)

Cover:  Calfskin (probably a French Morocco)

Binding:  Sewn

Trim Size:  5⅛” x 7⅝”

Text Block Width:  1⅛”

Weight:  1 pound, 8
ounces

Column:  Double

Layout: 
Verse-by-verse

Font Size:  9.6

Words of Christ: 
Black

Cross-References:  No

Ribbon Marker:  2

At its price point, this is probably the most impressive
Bible I’ve ever owned.  This begins
before I even take it out of the box. 
For the Windsor, TBS decided to employ that most delightful of
Bible-packaging options—the slipcase.

This is a small thing, but it makes a big difference.  I’m a Bible user, not a Bible collector.  I don’t keep them on my shelf because I think
they look pretty.  I keep them there
because I use them and want to refer to them.

For this purpose, the traditional Bible stack is a great big
pain.  If I want to look at the
non-updated NASB that is second from the bottom, I have to pick up the four
Bibles that are sitting on top of it, find someplace else to put them (which,
in our bookshelves, is no small feat!), take out the Bible in question, find
what I wanted to see, and put the whole thing back together.

On the other hand, the process is much simpler for Bibles
stored in slipcases.  There, I simply
have to reach up, take the Bible down, remove it from its slipcase, read it,
and put it back.  Plus, I think that all
the slipcased Bibles sitting next to each other look classy.  I’ve even begun contemplating making my own
slipcases, even though that looks like a tedious process.  Thankfully, TBS has saved me the trouble.

**SIZE.**  Though I wouldn’t think so once I open it,
the Windsor is actually among the smaller Bibles that I currently own.  It’s chunkier than my Zondervan Thinline NASB
(which is small for a thinline), but it’s about an eighth of an inch shorter.

Nearly anywhere a thinline can go, then, the Windsor will go
too.  Neither one will make it into my
hip pocket (of course, I probably couldn’t read the full Bible that would make
it into my hip pocket), but either will fit neatly into the glove box of our
minivan.

However, the Windsor is arguably an even better travel Bible
than the Thinline NASB.  The print in the
NASB runs to 7.8-point.  In the Windsor,
it’s 9.6.  I’d imagine there are more
than a few people who could read the Windsor who wouldn’t be able to handle the
Thinline.

This is not because TBS has discovered the secret of
n-dimensional print Bibles.  Instead,
it’s because of the format of the KJV versus the NASB.  The NASB formats the poetry sections of the
Old Testament (Psalms, Proverbs, most of the prophets) as poetry, breaking out
individual lines, but in so doing creating lots of white space.  That white space takes up space.  By contrast, the Windsor, like most KJV’s, is
formatted entirely in prose, which is significantly more compact.

This means that all other things being equal, a KJV is going
to have larger print than a similarly-sized Bible in a modern translation.  Those who prefer the KJV, or are even able to
tolerate it, and have trouble with small-print Bibles might be well served to
grab a Windsor or one of its brethren for travel use.

**BINDING.** When the Windsor first appeared in 2009,
it met with a tepid reception from early reviewers.  Part of the problem was that early editions
of the Windsor were printed in Belarus by who-knows-whom, with predictable consequences
for quality.

That is no longer the case. 
My Windsor, and indeed all current Windsors, are printed in the
Netherlands by Jongbloed.  For those of
you who aren’t Bible dorks, this is significant.  Jongbloed is renowned as the highest-quality
printer of Bibles in the world.  Cambridge
University Press has Jongbloed print the vast majority, if not indeed all, of
its fine Bibles.  R.L. Allan, which is
renowned for the quality of its bindings, will sometimes commission Jongbloed
to print high-quality text blocks, or even text blocks and bindings.  In short, Jongbloed is as good as it gets.

The Windsor certainly does nothing to diminish Jongbloed’s
reputation.  In fact, had the front
matter not told me, I may well have guessed that it was a Jongbloed
binding.  It reminds me of the French Morocco binding on my Cambridge Pitt Minion NLT.  It has the same pronounced stamped grain, the
same stiff boards, and generally the same feel in the hand.  I’m guessing this calfskin cover is actually a French-Morocco cover, derived in some way from some part of a calf.

However, do not be alarmed by reports of stiffness!  I’ve certainly done my share of fussing about
the plastic that some publishers try to pawn off as “genuine leather”, but the
Windsor’s cover isn’t in that category. 
There’s nothing that can be done with those plastic Bibles, but I’m
quite confident that a couple of weeks of breaking in would leave the covers of
the Windsor as pliable as could be desired.

Likewise, even though the Windsor will stay open in Genesis
1 and Revelation 22, the cover won’t lie flat in either case.  I don’t care too much about whether a Bible
lies flat or not (I never read Bibles from a pulpit or a stand), but if you do,
I’m quite certain that after enough use, the Windsor will slump over easily.

The Windsor also resembles a French-Morocco Pitt in the way that
the cover is attached.  Instead of being
edge-lined, it has a paste-down binding. 
Once again, though, this is well-executed.  The endpapers are leatherette instead of
being merely thick paper.  The ribbon
markers in the Windsor are nothing special, but they’re long enough to use
easily, and there are two of them.

**TEXT BLOCK.** Once again, the rule “just like the French-Morocco Pitt Minion” applies.  The
edges are gilded rather than art-gilded, which is what one would expect from a French-Morocco-level Bible.  The Bible is
sewn, and because it is printed with the grain and not bound too tightly, it
has zero cockling.

The Windsor has off-white paper, and though I’m not normally
a fan, I find the cream tint here to be subtle and elegant.  Because the layout is line-matched (more on
that later), the paper can be—and is—fairly light in weight.  Here too, I’d put it in Pitt-Minion
territory, probably somewhere close to 25 gsm. 
This leads to the only complaint I have about the Windsor’s form.  Because the paper is so light, it folds and
crumples easily.  I accumulated several
dog-ears in just a few weeks of use, and the title page crumpled almost from
the time I opened the Bible.

**VALUE.** Basically, then, the Windsor is a
second-rank fine Bible.  It’s what you
would expect to see from the lower tier of Cambridge fine Bibles or from the
nicest mass-market editions.  However,
there is one significant area in which the Windsor differs from its
peers—price.  The French-Morocco Pitt Minion
to which I’ve been referring is a slightly smaller Bible with exactly the same
level of quality.  It retails for
$100.  The sticker price on the Windsor
is $43.

The only other Bible I can think of in which you get so much
for so little is Crossway’s Value Thinline ESV. 
The Value Thinline is a bargain-basement sewn Bible.  Whenever people ask me to recommend a Bible
for their children, I generally respond with the Value Thinline.  However, it’s not a Bible I would choose for
myself.  I like things like
bookmarks.

Honestly, I have to tip my hat to TBS for this.  I know it’s cheaper to publish KJV Bibles
because the KJV is in the public domain, but I can’t imagine that Jongbloed is
printing these for TBS at much less than $43 a Bible.  Besides, unlike most KJV’s, the Windsor is a
modern setting, and a good one.  I’m
pretty sure that the typesetters (the Windsor does NOT have the feel of a
homebrew setting) didn’t work for free either.

Many Bible publishers are non-profit organizations, but the
folks at TBS seem to take their non-profit status literally!  Good for them.  If they want to get folks interested in the
KJV, offering a fine Bible at mass-market prices is a good way to do that.  Besides, I fundamentally believe that Bibles
ought to be well-constructed.  Aren’t the
contents worthy of at least that much respect?

**CONCLUSION.** All hail the TBS Windsor!  This Bible gets a 9.5/10 for physical form.
