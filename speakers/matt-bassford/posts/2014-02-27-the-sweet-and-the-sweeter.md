*They are more desirable than gold, yes, than much fine gold;*
*Sweeter also than honey and the drippings of the honeycomb.*
-- Psalms 19:10 (NASB)


Last year before Christmas, I was feeding my children breakfast one morning, and because they had done a good job of eating everything I wanted them to eat, I gave them each a peanut-butter blossom from our stash of Christmas cookies.  Yes, I sometimes give my children cookies at the end of breakfast.  No, I am not a bad parent.  Probably not a bad parent.



At any rate, I was surprised when both of them declared shortly afterward that they were done with breakfast and asked permission to get down and play.  I’m not about to require them to clean their plates when cookies are involved, so I let them escape and inspected the remaining contents of their plates.

The Hershey’s Kisses atop the peanut butter blossoms were gone, as I knew they would be.  However, the two cookies themselves remained, each with a single small bite taken from its perimeter.  Once my children had consumed the super-sweet chocolate first, they lost interest in anything else, even if the anything else was an only slightly less sweet peanut-butter cookie.

Sometimes, Christians approach the Bible the same way.  All of the word is sweet to our taste, but some parts are a little sweeter—the single verses that seem to speak specifically to our situations and our problems.  As a result, we are tempted to devise for ourselves a diet of spiritual Hershey’s Kisses—an uninterrupted series of topical studies, consisting only of the snippets that resonate most with us.

Conversely, we are inclined to ignore the peanut-butter-cookie context in which those spiritual sweets are nestled.  It’s not as immediately rewarding, so we search for the next Hershey’s Kiss instead of taking the time to savor the cookie.

This is certainly more spiritually worthwhile than catching up on the latest news from Hollywood, but it is not a mature approach to the text.  If God had wanted to make the Bible a series of proverbs about marriage and personal crisis, He certainly could have written it that way.  The fact that He didn’t should tell us that studying His word ought to be about more than hunting solutions for our earthly problems.  If that’s all we use the Scripture for, it becomes nothing more than a divinely inspired self-help book, with the emphasis on “self”.

Instead, in our study, we need to do a better job of listening, not asking leading questions.  We need to consider the entirety of the text rather than cherry-picking the applications.  It may not be as immediately relevant to the needs of the moment, but it will fulfill our greatest need—the need for life that only God can give.
