Last week, we walked through the arguments advanced by the
book *The Case for the Resurrection*.  In this book, the authors, Gary Habermas and
Michael Licona, contend that you do not need to accept the inspiration of the
Bible in order to conclude that the resurrection of Jesus is a historical
fact.  Instead, they use five “minimal
facts”, facts accepted by either a consensus or a clear majority of scholars,
to establish that Jesus rose from the dead.

They further contend that no other theory can explain these
five facts.  They acknowledge that a
comparatively neat naturalistic explanation would be preferable to the
miraculous explanation, but they say that no such explanation exists.  Once you accept the five minimal facts, no
other theory remains possible.  Let’s see
how this works today as we consider resurrection objections.



**Stories**

As we work our way through the alternate theories to explain
the evidence this morning, we’ll address each in the same way.  We’ll point out what facts the theory does explain,
highlight what it does not explain, and examine additional pieces of evidence
that cast further doubt on it.

The first alternate theory that Habermas and Licona consider
is the theory that the resurrection of Jesus is **A LEGEND.**  This theory
contends that Jesus never actually rose from the dead, but over time, in oral
tradition, the story arose that He did. 
Giving credit where credit is due, this theory is consistent with the
idea that Jesus died on the cross.

However, it explains nothing else.  The early disciples were not repeating a
legend they had been told by someone else. 
As we saw last week, the historical evidence is that they themselves
believed they saw Jesus after He rose from the dead.  Similarly, Paul explicitly denies that he
became a Christian because of a legend. 
Look at Galatians 2:11-12.  The
gospel that Paul preached, that Christ was risen, was not something that
anybody else taught him.  Instead, he
believed that he himself had seen a risen Jesus Christ.  Likewise, James the brother of Jesus did not
become a disciple because he heard a story from somebody else about his
brother.  Who of us would believe that a
close family member had risen from the dead unless we saw it with our own
eyes?  Instead, James believed that Jesus
appeared to him personally after His death, and he changed his life on that
basis.  Finally, the legend hypothesis
does nothing to explain the empty tomb. 
As explanations go, the argument that the resurrection was a legend
doesn’t explain much.

Second, skeptics will argue that the accounts of the
resurrection are in **A NONHISTORICAL
GENRE**.  In other words, the accounts
of the resurrection are like parables. 
They’re nice stories with a spiritual point, but that the original
authors did not intend to be taken literally. 
It’s like when after Macy died, Lauren and I went to see that worthless
grief counselor, and she told us that we should be comforted because Macy was still
alive in our hearts.  Note, by the way,
that my reaction was not to found a religion based around Macy.  Instead, it was to become furious about the
mush-mouthed counterfeit of hope that this woman was pushing on me.

Regardless, let’s assume that the early disciples went in
for mush-mouthed counterfeits of hope. 
Giving this alternate theory all the credit we can, we will concede that
it explains Jesus’ death by crucifixion, and it explains the expressions of
belief, at least, of the early disciples.

However, it simply doesn’t explain Paul’s own words about
the resurrection.  Consider 1 Corinthians
15:16-19.  Notice particularly that Paul
says that if Christ is not raised, then those who have fallen asleep in Jesus
have perished.  If the only place where
Christ lives on is in the hearts of believers, then what in the world does that
have to do with the fate of people who are already dead?  A Savior who has not actually been raised
gives no one any hope for a literal resurrection.  Unless Paul believed that the appearance of
Jesus to him was an actual historical event, his argument here makes no
sense.  The nonhistorical genre theory is
not consistent with Paul’s own belief.

Likewise for James. 
It’s obvious from the gospel accounts that James was familiar with his
brother’s teaching, just as all of us are at least somewhat familiar with what
our siblings are up to.  Jesus told all
sorts of nice moving stories, and they left James unmoved.  Why, all of a sudden, is James going to
transform his life because of a nice moving story about a man he never
accepted?  James thought Jesus was a
false teacher.  Why would he care if
Jesus lived on in anybody’s heart?

Finally, the nonhistorical genre theory does nothing to
explain the historically empty tomb. 
This explanation scores a little bit better than the legend theory, but
it still flunks.

Similarly, many have argued that the resurrection of Jesus
is **A MYTH** cobbled together from
other resurrection myths in other religions. 
This has a superficial appeal, but it fails to reckon with the
historicity of the Bible.  Pretty much
everyone who has seriously studied early Christianity accepts our five minimal
facts as historically accurate.  Jesus
was a real person who was really crucified. 
Peter, Paul, James, and all the rest were real people who really believed
what they preached.  They didn’t think
that the resurrection of Jesus was a myth that they invented.  Similarly, most scholars agree that the empty
tomb was not mythical.

The same, however, can’t be said for the historicity of all
the other resurrection accounts.  Nobody
today thinks that Osiris and Isis were real people, or that Adonis was.  The evidence even for their existence is
legendary, much less the evidence for their resurrection.  Because it is based on recorded eyewitness
testimony, the resurrection of Jesus is unique among such accounts.  There’s no reason to equate the story of the
resurrection with any myth.

**Fraud**

The next set of alternate theories involve fraud.  The first is literally the oldest explanation
in the book, that **THE DISCIPLES STOLE
THE BODY.** As with the nonhistorical
genre theory, it explains two of our five facts.  It deals well with both Jesus’ crucifixion
and the absence of His body from the tomb. 
However, after that, it falls flat. 
If the disciples stole the body, why did they sincerely believe that
they had seen Jesus after He rose from the dead?  Once again, their sincerity is established by
their willingness to suffer and die for the gospel.  No one would face the stocks, the sword, and
even the cross for the sake of a story they knew wasn’t true.  Peter, John, and all the rest could have
saved their lives by turning state’s evidence and saying “We made it all
up!”  The chief priests would have loved
to have somebody like that they could parade around.  However, all the evidence we have indicates
that rather than recanting, the disciples died. 
To their last breath, all of them were convinced that the risen Lord had
appeared to them.  These are not
body-snatchers.

Likewise, if the disciples stole the body, Saul of Tarsus
would not have sincerely believed that he saw the risen Lord.  There would have been no risen Lord to appear
to him.  We can say the same thing of the
skeptic James.  If the disciples stole
the body, what convinced him?

As a variation of this, some argue that **SOMEBODY ELSE STOLE THE BODY.** We’re
going to consider this alongside the very similar claim that the disciples went
to the wrong tomb.  Once again, this
checks out with our first and fifth minimal facts.  However, every account of the resurrection we
have makes it clear that the empty tomb by itself didn’t inspire much faith.  Consider Mary Magdalene’s reaction in John
20:11-13.  The text presents her as
having a conversation with angels in the empty tomb, and her first assumption
is still that somebody stole the body! 
The same is true of Peter considering the empty tomb in Luke 24:12.  He marvels, but he doesn’t believe on the
basis of an absent body.  Even in the
accounts written by Christians, only the apostle John is ever presented as
believing on the basis of the empty tomb. 
The empty tomb by itself doesn’t appear to have convinced much of
anybody that Jesus had risen.  Like we
would if we showed up for the funeral of a loved one but didn’t find a body,
pretty much all of them assumed at first that someone else had taken it.  This does not account for their later faith
in resurrection.

Also, why would our two unbelievers, Saul and James, believe
only on the basis of the empty tomb? 
Their first thought would be much like our thought if we heard that the
body of some false teacher had vanished. 
They would have assumed that the disciples were trying to perpetrate a
fraud.  An empty tomb by itself has no
power to convince unbelievers, much less to make them claim that the risen
Jesus in fact appeared to them.  In
addition, if the disciples simply went to the wrong tomb to begin with, people
like Saul and James would have been the first to correct the mistake.

Finally in this section, let’s consider the **APPARENT DEATH** theory.  This one holds that Jesus did not actually
die on the cross.  Instead, He fainted or
swooned, woke up a couple days later, and made His own way out of the
tomb.  This one satisfies the minimal
fact of the empty tomb, but it doesn’t satisfy anything else.  First, the fact that Jesus was crucified
makes it nearly certain that He died on the cross.  After His scourging, He was in terrible shape
to begin with, and being nailed to a cross and stabbed in the side with a spear
would not have improved His condition.

Also, it’s relevant here to note precisely how people who
are crucified die.  Doctors who have
studied crucifixion have concluded that the usual cause of death, other than
heart attack and the like, was asphyxiation. 
Once you have been crucified, the only way that you can exhale is to
pull yourself up by your crucified wrists and push yourself up on your
crucified feet.  When you no longer have
the strength to do that, you suffocate on your last lungful of air.  This is why the Roman soldiers broke the legs
of men whom they wanted to die quickly. 
Once that happened, the crucified man could only use the strength of his
arms to breathe, he would tire quickly and die.

This constant, agonizing pulling up would have been very
obvious to onlookers.  Any man who stopped
pulling up, instead hanging limply from the nails, could no longer breathe and
would be dead in minutes.  Jesus died
surrounded by His enemies.  They would
not have been satisfied until they saw convincing evidence that Jesus was dead.

Even if that’s not so, even if we grant that Jesus could
somehow have survived the cross, it strains credulity that He could have
survived the tomb.  At the very least,
when Joseph and the rest take Him down from the cross, He’s in such terrible
shape that His friends are convinced He is dead and bury Him.  In this near-death condition, He spends 36
hours in a tomb with no food, no water, and no medical care.  At some point, though, He summons up the
strength to roll the heavy stone from the door of the tomb with His crucified
hands, and stagger to safety on His crucified feet.  And this theory is advanced by people who
find the resurrection hard to believe!

Even granting all this, the apparent death theory simply
cannot account for the beliefs of the disciples, Saul, and James.  Sure, if the disciples had encountered this
bleeding, mutilated Jesus, they would have recognized that He was alive, but
they would not have concluded that He had risen from the dead.  Instead, He clearly would have been somebody
who had not died yet, but was close.  Nobody
is going to mistake a man at death’s door for the recipient of a miracle!

The same would have been true for James, and the skeptical
brother would have remained a skeptic. 
Finally, even assuming that a normal, human, recovered Jesus meets Saul
of Tarsus several years later, that would never have convinced Saul that He had
risen from the dead.

**Hallucinations and
Combinations**

The next set of alternate theories we’re going to consider
has to do with the disciples’ mental state. 
It’s been argued, though not as much recently, that the postmortem
appearances of Jesus were **HALLUCINATIONS.** After all, grief-stricken people have
been known to hallucinate that they see their loved one alive again,
particularly if they’re drunk or under the influence.

The nicest thing I can say about this theory is that it
accounts for Jesus dying on the cross, but it doesn’t account for anything
else.  Let’s start with the disciples’
conviction that Jesus appeared to them postmortem.  The classic statement of this belief appears
in 1 Corinthians 15:3-7.  What’s striking
about this list is that it includes not only individual appearances, but also
collective ones, in which whole groups of people believed that they saw the
Lord simultaneously.  The three groups
mentioned are the twelve, 500 disciples at one time, and finally “all the
apostles”.

The big problem here is that groups never share a
hallucination.  Hallucinations occur
within an individual’s brain.  There’s
nothing real there, so it’s impossible for more than one person to “see” it
simultaneously.  Even in situations where
members of a group hallucinate simultaneously, they aren’t going to hallucinate
the same thing.  Whatever it was that the
twelve, for instance, saw at the same time, it wasn’t a hallucination that
Jesus had risen.

As for James and Saul, it’s unlikely that the first, and
impossible that the second, would have a grief-based hallucination.  James was a devout Jew who believed his
brother was a false teacher.  Why would
somebody like that hallucinate that a false teacher was the risen Lord?  As for Saul, before he converted, the thought
of Jesus’ death would have filled him with joy, not grief!  Finally, once again, hallucinations cannot
explain the very real empty tomb.

OK, but how about **A
DELUSION**, like the delusions at the center of various modern cults?What
if Peter was such a smooth talker that he was able to convince his weak-willed
fellow disciples that they had seen Jesus after He rose from the grave?  Again, we’ll be generous and concede that
this might explain the first two points, though it’s far from clear about the
second.

That still leaves three issues.  What about James?  James was a skeptic.  Peter wasn’t going to convince him that Jesus
was the risen Messiah any more than David Koresh could have convinced any of us
that he was the Messiah.  Delusions only
overtake those who want to believe.

The same goes for Saul. 
As we’ve seen already in Galatians, Paul denied that anybody taught him
about the gospel at all when first he obeyed it.  Also, his antipathy to the gospel at the time
of his conversion went well beyond mere skepticism.  It’s hard even to imagine a strong-willed
persecutor of the church like Saul falling prey to a charismatic, deluding
teacher.  Finally, and unsurprisingly,
the delusion theory stumbles over the empty tomb.

Next, let’s consider **COMBINATION**theories.  These are theories that
attempt to explain the resurrection by combining two or more of our failed
alternate theories together, along with perhaps some theories about the
conversion of Saul that we don’t have time to delve into.  Let’s say that somebody else stole the body, Peter
had a hallucination and deluded the other disciples, James hallucinated too,
and Saul became a Christian because of his lust for religious power.

This appears strong at first glance, but there are several
problems with it.  First, combination
explanations like this don’t only inherit the strengths of their
sub-theories.  They inherit the
weaknesses too, and if any one of the sub-theories falls, the whole explanation
falls too.  In this case, if the evidence
shows that Saul did not convert out of lust for power, the combination loses
its explanatory power because it no longer accounts for Saul.

Second, combination theories look awfully *ad hoc*. 
They look like they are coming from somebody who has already made up his
mind and is looking for an excuse to cling to his conclusion, rather than like
somebody who is studying the evidence and following where it leads.  There’s no reason to suppose all of this
complexity when the resurrection elegantly explains all the known facts.  This is particularly true because most of the
possible sub-theories are assertions with no evidence.

Finally, we come to the position held by most modern
critics, which is that **SOMETHING
HAPPENED**, but what it is will never be known.  As the authors observe, this is not a
rejection of the evidence.  It is a
rejection of the conclusion.  It is being
presented with 2 + 2 and refusing to agree that they add up to 4.  Their problem is not what we don’t know.  It’s what we do know.

Before the Lord’s Supper, let’s reflect on this.  We’ve taken a whirlwind tour of the best
alternate theories that enemies of the gospel have ever been able to come up
with, and we’ve seen how miserably they all fail.  The Lord is risen indeed.  Let’s reflect on that as we partake.
