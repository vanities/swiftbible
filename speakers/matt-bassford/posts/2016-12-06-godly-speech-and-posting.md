As just about all of us are aware, elections have a way of
bringing out the worst in mankind.  This
is particularly true on the Internet, where the filters that keep us from being
rude and obnoxious in person seem to disappear. 
I suspect there were times when all of us came away from our Facebook
feeds disappointed in the human race and even in our brethren.  As such, this strikes me as a useful time for
us to remind ourselves that even though there was no online world 2000 years
ago, the Bible still applies to it. 
Let’s consider God’s wisdom on godly speech and posting.

 **Don’ts**

Let’s start with a short list of don’ts, things that
Christians should avoid whether they’re talking with their mouths or their
keyboards.  The first of these is that we
must learn not to **SPEAK HASTILY.** Consider Solomon’s sobering reminder in
Proverbs 29:20.  Even though we aren’t
likely to admit it, all of us can think of people we consider fools, the kind
of people who couldn’t pour water out of a boot if there were instructions on
the heel.

Friends, if we’ve gotten into the habit of engaging our
tongues before our brains, that fool that we sneer at and look down on is
actually better off than we are.  They
will actually do less damage to their lives with all their bad decisions than
we will do to ours by blurting out whatever comes to mind.  Usually, when we wreck relationships, it’s
not because we went into it wanting to create a disaster.  It’s because we didn’t think about what we
were saying, we didn’t think about the consequences, and so we ended up in the
middle of a disaster we did not intend.

The same thing is true of hasty posting.  Just because something sounds really true to
us, just because we feel it really strongly right now, doesn’t mean that we
should click “Update” or “Share”. 
Instead, we should think first. 
We should ask how others are going to take it.  We should check to make sure that the source
for the meme isn’t vulgar, as many of them are. 
Basically, we should ask whether this will make us look like disciples
or the opposite.

Second, we shouldn’t **ENGAGE
WHILE ANGRY.** James has some apt
counsel on this point in James 1:19-20. 
In particular, we must remember that the anger of men does not approach
the righteousness of God.  This is so
difficult because when we’re angry, we feel that we *do* approach the righteousness of God!  “I’m so right, and you’re so wrong, so I’m
going to get you told so you can see just how wrong you are!”  Once we’re angry, it’s very hard for us to
keep from sinning.

This is all the more problematic because when we’re online,
it’s very easy to become angry.  In
person, we can manage to remain civil. 
When we see the wrong meme or the wrong headline or the wrong comment on
our computer screens, though, our tempers often go volcanic.  We just have to make sure that other person
knows what a moron he is, even though in the back of our minds, we know that
having an angry conversation with him is going to accomplish evil, not good.

I’ve heard that in the world, some people make a rule out of
not texting while they’re drunk.  In the
same way, I think it’s a good rule for us not to say anything to anybody while
we’re mad.  As soon as we feel our blood
boiling, that is exactly when we need to step away from the computer, put the
smartphone down, and go for a walk. 
We’ll be happier and better off if we do.

Similarly, we must learn never to **GET IN ARGUMENTS.** This is
the point of, among many other passages, 2 Timothy 2:24-25.  This doesn’t mean that we can’t ever have
disagreements with anybody.  It doesn’t
even mean that we can’t ever talk with them about those disagreements.  It does mean, though, that we have to know
the difference between a disagreement and an argument.

We come to a disagreement acknowledging the possibility that
we might be wrong.  We come to an
argument with the certainty that the other guy is wrong.  We come to a disagreement with the goal of
persuasion.  We come to an argument with
the goal of expressing our opinions.  We
come to a disagreement willing to listen. 
We come to an argument willing to listen only so that we know just where
to stick the knife in during our rebuttal. 
Resolving a disagreement is a godly activity.  Blessed are the peacemakers, after all!  Starting an argument pleases only the devil.

Just as our online interactions seem particularly likely to
anger us, so too they seem particularly likely to entangle us in
arguments.  The reality is that texting
or typing is a really terrible way to try to persuade somebody and a great way
to get into an argument.  I love to study
the Bible with people in person, even if there are issues we have to work
through together, but I only reluctantly, if at all, get involved in Scriptural
disagreements online.  The risk is too
great.

Finally, we must not **REPEAT
LIES**.  Consider Jesus’ sobering
warning in Matthew 12:36-37.  We don’t
actively have to craft an untruth and repeat it, knowing that it’s false, in
order to be condemned for our words. 
Instead, all we have to be is careless in what we say, repeating
something without knowing whether it’s true or false because it suits our
purposes to do so.

This is certainly a problem in real life.  Gossips recklessly pass around untruth all
the time, and if anybody ever corrects them on it, they defend themselves with
“Well, that’s just what I heard!” 
Brethren, that’s no kind of defense at all.  Unless we know for certain that something is
true, we shouldn’t be repeating it, and a lot of the time, we shouldn’t repeat
it even then!

This is a problem in real life, but it’s an epidemic
online.  Some days, it seems like half
the shares I see on Facebook are of politically motivated lies, and an awful
lot of those shares are coming from Christians! 
We’ve got these political convictions, we feel very strongly that our
side is right and the other side is wrong, so we repeat whatever makes the
other side look Really Bad with no concern for its truth.  Let me tell you, brethren:  that kind of stuff really puts the virus in
“viral”.  And no, just because it comes
from some site with a name that looks truthy does not mean that we can believe
it!  Before we share anything, we need to
fact-check it six ways to Sunday first.

**Do’s**

In the last part of our time together, let’s consider
several godly and wise things that we ought to do in our speech, whether
literal or virtual.  The first of these
is to **REMEMBER WHO WE ARE.** Paul describes our relationship to the
world in 2 Corinthians 5:20.  It’s no
small thing for us to adopt the name of Christ. 
Once we do, we can be certain that others will judge us by His standard
and judge Him by us.  We can only avoid
this by hiding our light under a basket and never acknowledging to anyone that
we are Christians at all.

In our interactions with anybody, then, our role is to serve
as ambassadors for Christ.  An ambassador
doesn’t get to shoot their mouth off about whatever they feel like saying.  Instead, they speak for the head of their
home country, and as a result, their words can have consequences that extend
far beyond themselves.  For example, back
in 1990, the U.S. ambassador to Iraq, in a conversation with Saddam Hussein, appeared
to give him tacit permission to invade Kuwait, which has led directly to 25
years of disaster for both countries. 
Ambassadors have got to be careful!

Exactly the same holds true for ambassadors for Christ.  If we forget ourselves, if we speak without
considering the consequences first, we probably aren’t going to start a war,
but we can easily cause a soul to be lost. 
Before we speak, we must ask whether our statement is something that we
want others attributing to Jesus Himself, because they will.

Second, we must **KNOW
WHEN TO SAY NOTHING.** Here, we must
consider the rather caustic advice of Proverbs 17:28.  This is another one of those proverbs that
gives the fool a backhanded compliment. 
You’re worse off than a fool if you speak hastily, and if the fool has
the sense to keep their mouth shut when you don’t, they’ll get a better
reputation than you.

Obviously, we shouldn’t react to this proverb by taking vows
of silence and never saying anything for the rest of our lives, but it should
teach us to discern the times when silence is the best policy.  Just because we want to say something to
somebody by no means establishes that we ought to say it!  Instead, we should constantly weigh the
consequences of engagement versus the consequences of staying silent.  Sometimes, as when we see a brother in sin,
we have a responsibility to speak up.  At
other times, though, the wisest course may well be to say nothing.

This is particularly true when we disagree with another’s
words or actions but have little faith in our ability to persuade.  Maybe it’s because the conversation is
playing out on Facebook, and it’s axiomatic that nothing said on Facebook ever
changes anybody’s mind.  Maybe it’s
because we’re dealing with somebody in person, but we know from experience that
ain’t nobody changing their mind on this! 
In times like those, the best thing we can do is love and let it go.

Third, we must **SEEK
TO BUILD UP.** We learn this from
Ephesians 4:29.  Once again, the point
here is that because we’re Christians, we’re different.  It’s not enough for us to remember that our
speech will reflect on Christ.  Instead,
we must use it to lead others to Him.

This doesn’t mean that everything that comes out of our
mouths has to be an invitation to a Bible study, but it does mean that
everything we say must give others the opportunity to see Jesus in us.  I’ve heard it said that people who come to
the Lord fall in love with Christians before they fall in love with Christ, and
experientially, I think there’s a lot of truth to that.

We are called then, to be lovely people, not lovely in our
appearance, but lovely in our souls.  The
people who know us should look forward to interacting with us because we are
unfailingly kind, gracious, encouraging, and polite.  Christians are supposed to be Labrador
retrievers, not pit bulls!  Even if the
darkness of the world feels overwhelming, and the best-chosen words we can say
will only lighten the darkness a little bit, we’ll still say them.  We should always have in the back of our minds
that we want everybody we talk to to obey the gospel, but even if the
opportunity hasn’t arisen yet, we can still lay the ground work with speech
that is edifying and gracious.

Finally, we must remember to **SPEAK IN LOVE.** Never has
this been put better than Paul did in 1 Corinthians 13:1.  We tend to dwell on the evil that the tongue
can work because that evil is so easy to accomplish.  However, it’s easy to accomplish great good
with words spoken in love.

Loveless speech, by contrast, has nothing that is beautiful about
it.  I think the problem is the
self-centeredness.  When you’re talking
to somebody who only cares about themselves, it has a distinctive sound to
it.  They’re concerned only with
expressing their opinion and getting what they want.  As long as those things happen, they’re happy
and couldn’t care less about you.

The only way for us to avoid the lovelessness trap is to
enter every conversation, whether in person or online, primarily concerned with
the other.  Is there something they need
to hear from me?  How can I present that
in the most useful way possible?  How can
I encourage them?  How can I make their
day better?  Here too, this is an
attitude that is hard to hide.  We can
all think of people who reflect their concern for others with each word that
they say.  When we cultivate the same
selfless attitude in ourselves, our speech truly begins to glorify God.
