Last week, I wrote about how the Ashley Madison scandal
should move us to compassion, even though the revealed (would-be) cheaters
aren’t particularly likable sinners.  In
response to this, several readers made interesting comments here and there, but
one of the most interesting raised the subject of righteous anger.  Are we angry about things like AM?  Should we be? 
How do we tell the difference between that and plain old self-righteous
contempt?



I can think of two main New-Testament examples of righteous
anger.  The first, of course, is Christ’s
cleansing of the temple, recorded, among other places, in Mark 11:15-18.  The second is Paul’s epistle to the Galatians,
in which his anger is perhaps best captured by his expressed wish that the
Judaizing false teachers would emasculate themselves (Galatians 5:12).  Presumably, there is some difference between
this kind of speech and behavior and the “all bitterness and wrath and anger
and clamor and slander” that Paul himself says we ought to put away from
ourselves in Ephesians 4:31.  How do we
reconcile these things?

We can distinguish the sinful anger of Ephesians 4 from
Jesus’ and Paul’s angry outbursts because the latter were motivated by
love.  Of course, love for God is always
present, but so too is love for others.  Consider,
for instance, the account of the words of Jesus the day before the cleansing of
the temple in Luke 19:41-44:

*When He approached
Jerusalem, He saw the city and wept over it, saying, "If you had known in
this day, even you, the things which make for peace! But now they have been
hidden from your eyes. For the days will come upon you when your enemies will
throw up a barricade against you, and surround you and hem you in on every
side, and they will level you to the ground and your children within you, and
they will not leave in you one stone upon another, because you did not
recognize the time of your visitation."*

In Luke’s account, the story of the cleansing of the temple
follows immediately after this anecdote. 
Jesus is angry in the temple, yes, but His rage is well mixed with
grief.  He knows that the greedy
religious leaders who had profaned the temple courts are going to lead the
people to reject both the Father and the Son, with the result that Jerusalem
itself will be destroyed and the Jews scattered.  The chief priests only care about money and
power, hundreds of thousands (if not millions) will die because of their
wickedness, and there’s nothing Jesus can do to stop it.  He drives out the moneychangers because He
can’t bear to stand by and watch this slow-motion disaster continue.  Not coincidentally, the Jewish power brokers
succeed in having Him crucified in less than a week.

Similarly, it’s evident that Paul’s anger in Galatians has a
lot of love behind it.  For instance, in
Galatians 4:19-20, Paul says, “My children with whom I am again in labor until
Christ is formed in you—but I could wish to be present with you now and to
change my tone, for I am perplexed about you.” 
Paul’s predicament is one that is familiar to every earthly
parent—somebody you love is determined to be foolish, and you’re driven to
distraction trying to figure out a way to get them to wise up.

This suggests, then, a pair of principles that should govern
our righteous anger.  First, our anger
can only be righteous when we are angry about sin rather than about our
think-so’s.  Our opinions can be wrong,
and indeed often are, and in any case lack the moral weight of the law of the
Lord.  As James observes in James 1:20,
“The anger of man does not achieve the righteousness of God.”

Second, even when we’re angry, we must speak in love within
the context of a genuine relationship. 
Let’s explore this using one of the favorite rants of church dragons
everywhere:  the young sister in Christ
who comes to the assembly wearing an immodest, revealing dress.  The usual course of “dealing” with this is
for Christians to express their purse-lipped disapproval to each other when
she’s not around.  This may feel like
righteous anger, but it’s actually an ungodly, gossipy expression of
contempt.  When we are truly righteously
angry, we will go to the person who needs to change, not to a third party who
already agrees with us.

At other times, a particularly brave church dragon may take
it upon herself to haul the offending sister into a classroom after services
and give her what-for, even though they have never exchanged 10 words before
that Sunday morning.  Even though this
kind of confrontation may produce a change in behavior, it often has negative consequences
down the road.

By contrast, consider the following scenario.  Instead of being somebody we barely know, the
sister is someone we know and know well. 
We talk to her frequently after services, and we’ve had her in our home
on a number of occasions.  We love her;
she loves us.  Indeed, we know her well
enough to know that even though she’s a good kid, she can also be headstrong
and stubborn at times.  A less direct
rebuke is something she’s going to be able to brush off.

Then (and perhaps only then) are we justified in expressing
our righteous anger to her.  She’ll be
able to hear not only the anger but also the love in what we’re saying, because
both are truly there.  That kind of
wake-up call has the power to change the heart along with behavior.  Jesus and Paul didn’t resort to righteous
anger because they needed to vent and didn’t care about the consequences.  Instead, they used it because it was the best
way to make an important point with people whom they cared about.  If we are wise, we will do the same.
