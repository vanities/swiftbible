*[My last hymn-based post](http://hisexcellentword.blogspot.com/2017/04/a-critique-of-traditional-hymns.html), on the problems with traditional hymns, elicited a number of thoughtful
responses.  None of these, however,
caught my attention more than the comments made by my brother and friend Mike
Rogers.  Mike’s approach to sacred song
is a potent blend of the academic and the practical.  He’s a professor of music at Abilene
Christian University, and he has spent many years writing and arranging music
for the church, particularly in company with Keith Lancaster of Acapella
fame.  Regardless of whether or not you
agree with Mike, his expertise demands respectful attention.*

*Originally, I asked
Mike for permission to quote from his comments in a reply post from me.  However, as I began to develop that reply, I
soon realized that even quoting at paragraph length wouldn’t do Mike’s
perspective justice.  I intend to explore
Mike’s arguments at length later, but for now, I think it’s worthwhile simply
to present them in their entirety, especially for those who aren’t members of
the Facebook group where he originally made them.  He writes as follows:*



Matthew, perhaps one reason that people view you as anti-modern
music is your labeling of all Christian music as "hymns."

The term "hymn" in today's vernacular suggests
specific stylistic traits that represent 99% of what was written by the last
generation. Of the music being composed today, I would approximate that less
than 1% of it may properly be categorized as hymns.

By referring to modern Christian music as hymns, you likely
come across to some as anachronistic and biased.

It might help your public perception if you began to look at
songs as songs and hymns as hymns, correctly distinguishing styles within the
genre.

I would say that (musically) hymns are Christian songs
written in homophonic texture and characterized by common practice tonality
with regular phrasing, non-syncopated melodic lines, and a preference for
contrapuntal independence of the voices.

Regarding the text elements, my opinion (from a
non-linguist's point of view) is that hymns are typified by standard poetic
forms and rhyme schemes, often dense in their theology.

Contemporary Christian songs, on the other hand, are a mix
of homophony and polyphony, characterized harmonically by a modern variation of
common practice tonality. Syncopated melodic lines are the norm, and they often
feature irregular phrasing as well. Contrapuntal independence in this style is
less emphasized.

Lyrically, modern Christian songs often implement standard
poetic forms and rhyme schemes, but these forms are often only loosely
followed. Many times, these songs follow no preconceived poetic form at all,
and are typically theologically less dense.

Contemporary praise songs are as different from hymns as
football is from baseball. Both are indeed sports, but each with very different
criteria as to what makes it unique. Both may be correctly generically
categorized as sports, but referring to both as “football” would certainly be a
mistake.

Generically categorizing ‘everything we sing in church’ as
“hymns” is simply incorrect (and when I hear it, it sounds to me just as
strange as someone referring to baseball as football). It would be correct to
call everything we sing in church as “songs.” Musically, hymns are a very
specific style within the “songs” genre. I truly believe that this lack of
distinction lies at the heart of the problem here.

I will speak from a music perspective here, because my
training is in music, but the point I will make may also be applied to text
(syntax, poetic forms, rhyme schemes, etc.) as well.

Every style of music is different. And every style of music
has its greatest practitioners and masterworks, from which we extract the principles
that distinguish a great piece (or composer) from an average or bad piece (or
composer) in a particular style.

If we apply the stylistic principles valued by J.S. Bach
onto, say, Debussy, then Debussy would be considered to be a poor composer. The
same is true if we apply the stylistic principles valued by Debussy onto J.S.
Bach. Bach would then be viewed as an uninitiated neophyte if judged under the
musical principles valued by Debussy and his contemporaries. As it is, they are
each considered one of the best if not the best of their generation. And to
apply the same value criteria to both is surely a blunder, for each were
writing in a specific style which valued certain elements of music differently
from the other.

The same is true of evaluating modern-style worship songs.
If we apply a criteria valued by hymn writers to non-hymns, we err. The
elements that make a great hymn are not necessarily the same elements that make
a great praise song in the modern style. Although there will be overlap, we
need to distinguish between criteria that make each effective. (This
distinction is the reason for my last post, showing that they are made from
different elements).

My overall point is this: If we fail to distinguish the
styles, we run the risk of failing to recognize the different criteria that
make each effective in its own style. And to be more specific, if we apply the
criteria that make a great hymn onto a non-hymn, then we will likely consider
every non-hymn a poor piece of music. We ought to rather strive to find what
makes a modern song great and create a revised set of value criteria rather
than placing new wine into old wineskins, for they will surely break.
