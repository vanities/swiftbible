Once again, I am back in the business of preaching a sermon
based on a sermon request, and once again, the sermon request came from one of
my children.  This time, though, the
requester is Marky, who wanted me to preach a sermon on what it took to be on
God’s side.

Though I don’t know that I would have phrased the question
quite that way, the topic is one of extreme importance.  Our physical world looks extremely complex,
with all sorts of different groups of people doing different things.  However, the spiritual world is much
simpler.  There aren’t a whole bunch of
sides and a whole bunch of competing interests. 
Instead, there are two sides. 
There’s God’s side, and there’s the other side, and everybody is on one
of the two.

The side we’re on matters a lot.  It’s not like watching the Super Bowl and
picking a team even though you really don’t care, and the team you don’t care
about loses.  Instead, this is literally
a matter of life and death.  God’s side gets
eternal joy; the other side gets eternal punishment.  Let’s consider, then, how we must order our
lives to be on God’s side.



**What It Takes**

Let’s start off this morning by looking at what being on
God’s side takes.  What are the
requirements?  The first of these, I
think, is **WISDOM.** Consider Peter’s thoughts on the subject
in 2 Peter 3:3-7.  I think this text
captures a very common viewpoint.  It’s
about people who aren’t worried about the future, even though they’re not
making any effort to live for God.

It could be that these are people who doubt that the Lord
will return.  After all, the earth’s been
around my whole life!  Why would that
change?  It could also be that these are
people who simply don’t consider their own mortality.  Despite abundant reason to do so, they’re not
interested in pondering the subject of their own inevitable deaths and what
they should do to prepare.

Sadly, however, death and judgment aren’t the sorts of
things that go away when you ignore them. 
They will still happen to us whether we invest one iota of thought in
them or not.  As a result, Peter has no
problem with describing the questioners as scoffers with evil desires.  They’re foolish people taking a foolish
position that ultimately is going to destroy them.

Wisdom, by contrast, says, “I see the train coming, and
before it gets here, I’m going to get off the tracks!”  Once we recognize that it is appointed to man
once to die, and after that comes the judgment, the next question in our minds
ought to be, “What do I have to do to get ready?”  Foolishness has no concern for the future,
but wisdom wants to be prepared.

Second, being on God’s side requires **FAITH.** We see the Hebrews
writer’s classic definition of faith in Hebrews 11:6.  Here, we see a two-part definition of faith,
as well as one consequence of faith. 
Let’s start with the definition first. 
Faith, first of all, is believing that God is.  Maybe this comes from looking at the
mountains, the ocean, or the starry night sky and saying to ourselves,
“Somebody made all this stuff.”  Maybe it
comes from studying the Scriptures and examining the evidence of fulfilled
prophecy or Jesus’ resurrection from the dead. 
Both inside and outside the word, there are many paths we can take to
accepting the existence of God.

The second part of the definition is trickier, because it
requires us to believe that He rewards those who seek Him.  If I start from the creation, at least, I
could come to believe in a deist God, one who made all of these things but is
too powerful and remote to be interested in me. 
If that’s my belief, it will make no practical difference in my
life.  However, I can also consider the
word, what it reveals about the character of God, and the stories it tells of
those who trusted Him and were not disappointed, and come to a completely
different conclusion.  I can say, “If I
trust in God, I can be sure that He’s going to reward me too.”

If that’s what I believe, I’m going to act on it, and just
as unbelief makes it impossible to please God, this kind of active faith makes
pleasing Him possible.  By faith we
diligently seek Him, by faith we come to Him, and by faith we receive His
reward.

It’s clear, though, that this kind of faith isn’t mere
mental assent.  Instead, it demands **OBEDIENCE.**Paul emphasizes the importance of obedience in Romans 6:15-18.  Notice that Paul isn’t saying here that we
have to obey perfectly if we want to be saved. 
Our salvation is by grace through faith, not through the works of the
Law of Moses.  However, trying to take
obedience away from saving faith is kind of like trying to take sunshine away
from the sun.  If you’ve got one, you’ve
got the other.  Never in Scripture do we
see the two opposed to each other.

In fact, Paul says, our obedience is an important marker of
our ownership.  Everybody belongs to
somebody, even the macho types who brag about how they make up their own
rules.  Either we are slaves of God and
obey Him, or we are slaves of the devil and we obey him.

This has little to do with our self-understanding.  We’re very good at lying to ourselves, and
the world is full of people who think they’re serving God but could not be more
wrong about it.  Our subjective
impressions don’t matter much.  What does
matter is our actual conduct, measured by the standard of the word.  Only if our lives reveal God’s direction do
we belong to Him.

Finally, if we want to be on God’s side, He requires that we
**LOVE.**Perhaps nowhere in Scripture is this core truth stated better than
in 1 John 4:7-8.  If we think that
somehow we’ve arrived at a loveless version of Christianity, we’re half
right.  We’ve almost certainly got the
lovelessness part down pat, but we’re about a million miles from where Christ
wants us to be.

What does it mean, then, to be loving?  The best explanation I can come up with is
that love is like a pair of eyeglasses for our souls.  It transforms the way that we see the
world.  It keeps us focused on who God
wants us to be.  When we consider our
lives through the prism of love, things that would seem confusing and unclear
to worldly people become perfectly clear to us. 
We know what to do because we start with love of God and our neighbor,
and we work forward from there.

In practice, this means that every time we ask the question,
“What is right?”, we also ask “What is loving?” 
This doesn’t mean that love and the law of God can conflict; instead, it
means that every law must be understood as an expression of love.  If we can’t affirm that the right thing to do
is also the loving thing to, it’s a sign that we’ve misunderstood the
situation.  This is often not easy; in
fact, none of us will completely master it in our lifetimes.  However, the more accomplished at this we
become, the more we will imitate Christ.

**How Jesus Did It**

Next, I want us to consider how Jesus planted Himself so
firmly on God’s side and remained there throughout His life.  In addition to all of the other things we’ve
already talked about, Jesus succeeded because of His **SUBMISSIVENESS.** Look at how
He expresses this in John 5:19. 
Basically, Jesus didn’t have an original idea about His life.  His goal was only to faithfully translate
God’s nature and character into a context that we could understand.  Because we have seen Him in the pages of
Scripture, we have seen the Father, and only His submissiveness makes that
possible.

One of those pop-culture sayings that I’ve been talking
about a lot recently is, “God has a plan for your life.”  This saying generally shows up whenever life
isn’t going well for us and somebody wants to reassure us.  The girlfriend we really liked broke up with
us, so one of our friends says, “God has a plan for your life,” by which they
mean that He’s got an even better girlfriend than that one stored up for us
someplace.  God’s plan for us is to give
us what we want.

The thing is, I don’t think that such things are God’s plan
for our lives at all.  God’s plan is not
for us to get great jobs and great families and great health.  That’s our plan for our lives.

God’s plan for our lives is for us to become like Jesus and
glorify Him.  We don’t get there because
God showers us with goodies.  We get
there because we submit ourselves entirely to Him.

Likewise, Jesus showed His allegiance by His **FAITHFULNESS.** The Hebrews writer explores this topic in
Hebrews 3:1-6.  His purpose here is to
compare Moses and Jesus and to establish that Jesus is the greater of the
two.  He observes that both men were
found faithful, but Moses was found faithful as a servant, where Jesus was
found faithful as a Son.

In this passage, though, I think the writer uses
faithfulness in two senses.  The first is
the familiar one, of keeping a commitment, like being faithful to one’s
marriage vows.  The second, though, is of
responsibly using something that has been entrusted to us.  Think here of the faithful servants in the
parable of the talents, who proved themselves to be good stewards.

Today, how are we doing as stewards?  As the story of the rich young ruler
demonstrates, our material possessions can come between us and God.  We usually deal with Jesus’ call to sell
everything by saying that we just have to understand our possessions as
belonging to God, so that we will use them for Him whenever He asks us for
them.

That sounds good in theory, but how does it work in
practice?  We say we have our houses in
trust from God.  Great!  When was the last time we hosted a Bible
study or invited some brethren over so we could get to know them better?  We claim that we hold our money in trust from
God.  Wonderful!  Do we contribute more to the Lord’s work than
we spend on, say, our vacations?  I’m
afraid that too much of the time, we say that our stuff is God’s for the
asking, but when He does ask, we pretend we can’t hear Him.  That’s awfully convenient, but not very
faithful.

Finally, we see Jesus aligning Himself with God in His **SELF-DENIAL.** This appears perhaps most clearly in His
anguished prayer in the garden of Gethsemane in Mark 14:32-36.  What a scene this must have been!  Here, we see the eternal Son of God so
oppressed by His grief that His legs literally give out from under Him and He
collapses to the ground in prayer.  I’ve
only ever had that happen to me once in my life, and it was when my daughter
Macy died.

And yet, Jesus’ grief isn’t about the present.  It’s about the future.  He can still save His own life, either by
calling 12 legions of angels or, more prosaically, by fleeing into the night
before the mob shows up.  However, He
doesn’t do that.  Even though His own
will is for this awful cup to be removed from Him, He prays that God’s will,
not His will, be done.

This calls us, I think, to consider our spiritual limits, to
ask ourselves how far we are willing to go because God is calling us
there.  For Jesus, there was no
limit.  He didn’t belong to God in name
only.  He belonged to God in reality, to
the point of going to the cross for Him.

Of course, none of us measure up to that.  Every one of us has lines we will not cross
for God.  If we didn’t, we would be
precisely like Jesus and never sin.  What
we need to do is to admit to ourselves that those lines are there and then
bring our issues with us to Gethsemane. 
There, as we consider the prostrate, suffering Son of God, we can
reconsider what we think of lines.

Obviously, this sets the stage wonderfully for the Lord’s
Supper.  Jesus was devoted to God with
all the intensity and passion of His great heart.  He had known His Father since before the
foundation of the world.  What’s more, He
was devoted to us.  He loved us even
before we existed.  As a result, His
death on the cross will forever be a symbol, not of ugliness and human hatred,
but of love.  Let’s remember that and
consider ourselves as we partake.

**What It Feels Like**

Finally this morning, let’s consider what the experience of
being on God’s side feels like.  What’s
it like to live a life where we make that choice consistently?  First, I think it feels **LONELY.** Jesus gives it to us
straight in Matthew 7:13-14.  The other
day, I read an article online about the 10 things that churches must not do if
they want to attract and keep millennial members.  One of these taboo things was to criticize
the culture around us.  The author argued
that millennials are sick of hearing that culture is the enemy.  Instead, he said, we’re supposed to take our
cues from Jesus and be optimistic about the culture and engage with it.

Now, I’m all about engagement with sinners who need to hear
the gospel, but to say that Jesus was optimistic about His culture is to
completely ignore the record of His ministry. 
He Himself said that He lived in the midst of an adulterous and sinful
generation that was headed for destruction and was not going to listen to Him,
even though He was its only hope.  Jesus
didn’t draw disciples by offering cozy platitudes about how everything was
wonderful.  Instead, He told the few who
would hear Him what they had to do in order to get out from under the coming
disaster.

In many ways, our situation is similar to that one.  We live in a society that is not merely
evil.  It is doomed.  If it continues down its present track, it
will destroy itself, as sure as sunrise, in both a material and a spiritual
sense.  If we just go with the majority,
we will meet the same fate it does.  Only
by standing for God and against it can we hope to escape.

However, we will also find that being on God’s side is
strangely **PEACEFUL.** Consider Jesus’ declaration in John
14:27.  Part of what it means to stand
with Jesus is to know His peace.  This
isn’t hippie peace.  It’s not the
go-along-to-get-along kind of peace. 
Instead, it’s the peace that comes from knowing that because we have
devoted our lives to God, He will care for us. 
No matter how bad the times get, we can be certain that He isn’t going
to forsake us.

This is a priceless gift. 
To appreciate its value, just consider the emotional climate of our
country today.  Last year’s presidential
election was defined by fear.  Most
people cast their votes because they were afraid of what the other side’s
candidate would do if elected.  Even now,
there are tens of millions who live in fear because of what they think the
current administration is going to do to them. 
Sooner or later, there’s going to be a political backlash, and all those
people are going to unleash their fears on those whom they believe are the
enemy.  The cycle of fear will continue.

Sounds scary, right? 
Not really.  At least, not for
us.  In the words of Paul, our lives are
hidden with Christ in God.  If we put God
first, there is no future event that can touch us.  The first-century Christians were intimately
familiar with persecution, but even when confronted with prison and the sword,
they never had to surrender their peace. 
In Christ, we have the same blessing.

Finally, being on God’s side is **HOPEFUL.** Look just a page or
two ahead of our last Scripture, at John 16:33. 
Interestingly, even though this is a positive passage, the only promise
in it is a negative one.  Jesus tells His
disciples that in this world, they’re going to have tribulation.  The same holds true for us.  We have tribulation because we follow Him
when others don’t, and we have tribulation simply because we live in a fallen,
imperfect world where awful things can happen.

However, despite these tribulations, Jesus exhorts us to
take heart.  Our struggles are ongoing,
but their outcome is not in doubt.  Jesus
has overcome the world, and through His love, all of us will be more than
conquerors too.  This was true in the
first century, when scattered congregations of brethren throughout the
Mediterranean world were menaced by the might of Rome.  It’s true today, in our crooked and perverse
generation, and it will be true tomorrow. 
The actors may change, but the play does not.  The same side always wins.

We are not called, then, to live our lives on the Lord’s
side oppressed by fear and doubt.  We are
called to lives of hope and courage.  We
are to be the ones who live lives of holiness and righteousness, even when
Satan is whispering in our ears that there is no point.  We’re the ones who are to speak out even when
the whole world is poised to shout us down. 
We’re the ones who are called to love, even the hateful, even the
unlovable, because God has first loved us. 
This is our high calling, and with the help of the One who has overcome
the world, we will overcome too.
