Even though it’s true that the Internet has a way of sorting
us into micro-communities according to our interests, by the same mechanism, it
tends to throw us together with people who are different from us, despite some
shared interest.  This has been my
experience when it comes to the various hymn-related groups of which I am a
member on Facebook.  For instance, in the
normal round of my existence, I have next to nothing to do with contemporary
Catholic hymnody.  The most recent hymns
of Catholic origin that I’m likely to sing in worship (“The Church’s One
Foundation”, “Faith of Our Fathers”, etc.) are now nearly 200 years old.



However, the worship wars that are being waged elsewhere in
the Christian diaspora are certainly present in the Catholic community as
well.  Just as I have my list of least
favorite contemporary hymns, so too do the Catholic traditionalists.  Rarely are these the hymns that nobody
sings.  Instead, they’re the hymns that
have been embraced by Catholics nationwide and perhaps worldwide, except of
course by traditionalists.

I am extremely interested in hymns and hymnody, but I am by
no stretch of the imagination Catholic, so I enjoy being able to study these
hymns with a detachment that I can’t bring to the sacred music of my own
religious tradition.  After all, for good
or ill, none of us can look at our own families the way an outsider would.

Sometimes, I see where the traditionalists are coming
from.  Honestly, I wouldn’t want to sing
“Here I Am, Lord” in worship either.  At
other times, though, I don’t understand the vitriol that is directed toward
some contemporary hymns that seem pretty good to me.

So it is with “One Bread, One Body”.  Admittedly, the title is eminently spoofable
(there was a Facebook thread a few days ago entitled “One Bread, One Body, One
Bath and Beyond”), but that’s about the worst thing that I have to say about
it.  For those who aren’t familiar with
it, here are the lyrics:

*Refrain*

*One bread, one body,*

*one Lord of all,*

*one cup of blessing
which we bless.*

*And we, though many,*

*throughout the earth,*

*we are one body in
this one Lord.*

*1. Gentile or Jew,*

*servant or free,*

*woman or man, no more.*

*(Refrain)*

*2. Many the gifts,*

*many the works,*

*one in the Lord of
all.*

*(Refrain)*

*3. Grain for the
fields,*

*scattered and grown,*

*gathered to one, for
all.*

*(Refrain)*

As soon as I finished listening to and reading it, I said to
myself, “I want to sing this!”  I’m not
at all invested in the twangy folk-guitar style of the music, but there’s a
great deal in the words that appeals to me. 
This is obviously written as a Eucharistic hymn, and even though we call
it the Lord’s Supper rather than the Eucharist, my religious tradition places
almost as much emphasis on the communion as does the Catholic tradition.  I already know when and why we would sing
“One Bread, One Body”.

However, the real clincher for me is the content of the
hymn.  We are a very Bible-centric
tradition, so when I look at the refrain, I immediately see 1 Corinthians
10:16-17.  In the ESV, it reads, “The cup
of blessing that we bless, is it not a participation in the blood of Christ?  The bread that we break, is it not a
participation in the body of Christ? 
Because there is one bread, we who are many are one body, for we all
partake of the one bread.”

It quickly becomes apparent, then, that even the easily
parodied title is straight from the pen of the apostle Paul.  To me, that’s important.  That’s really important.  Indeed, I’ve tried to write hymns based on 1
Corinthians 10:16-17 myself, thus far without success.  There is nothing that I would rather sing
than a hymn that explores Biblical concepts using Biblical language.

Nor is that all. 
Probably just about any topic in the New Testament would resonate with
me, but the subject of the Lord’s Supper as an expression of Christian unity is
particularly resonant.  I meditate on
that very idea every single Sunday.  Visitors
aside, I know and am friends with everybody I worship with, and I am deeply and
passionately committed to remaining united with them.  One bread, one body?  Amen!

The first verse is readily identifiable as a paraphrase of
Galatians 3:28, which reads, “There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither
slave nor free, there is no male and female, for you are all one in Christ
Jesus.”  As my wife noted, these days the
phrase “Woman or man, no more” carries with it the specter of Caitlyn Jenner,
but that aside, the content is still powerful and resonant.  I’m blessed to be a part of a demographically
diverse congregation, and (even though I admittedly didn’t have a whole lot to
do with it) I’m fiercely proud of that fact. 
Black or white, rich or poor, male or female, anybody who wants to serve
the Lord can find a family with us.

In the second verse, we have more Scriptural
paraphrase.  This time, the text is
clearly taken from 1 Corinthians 12:4-5, which reads, “Now there are varieties
of gifts, but the same Spirit.  And there
are varieties of ministries, but the same Lord.”  This too is a point that appears with some
regularity in my preaching.  You don’t
have to be a preacher to serve.  You
don’t have to be a song leader to serve. 
Even if your work is visiting the sick or cleaning the church building,
you are no less important a part of the body of Christ than its most visible
members.  Ultimately, every kind of
service gets back to serving Jesus.

The third verse is not quite as strong.  There are echoes of various Biblical texts in
it (Matthew 13:3-9 and John 12:23-26 among them), but it’s not a straight-up
book-chapter-verse paraphrase.  It seems
to me that what the author is trying to extend the one-bread-one-body
comparison.  As Christ assembles us into
one body, so too He plants us, causes us to grow, and gathers us in.

I think this doesn’t quite work for a couple of different
reasons.  First, Biblically speaking, the
harvest of Christ isn’t gathered into the church.  It’s gathered into heaven.  None of us are harvested until either we die
or the Lord returns.

Second, bread recalls to us the Lord’s body, both in the
literal and ecclesiastical sense, but I don’t know of anywhere that the Bible
explicitly compares the church to bread. 
I think that the attempt to do so is to extend the metaphor too far.  Even though we are united in Christ, we still
retain our individuality.  This is
apparent from Paul’s great discussion of the members of the body in 1
Corinthians 12.  We aren’t all the same.

However, in the bread-making process (implied by the hymn,
though not directly stated), the individual grains of wheat are ground up and
converted into homogenous particles of flour, which then is baked into
homogenous loaves of bread.  At that
point, there isn’t much individuality left! 
It also reminds me vaguely of the giant at the top of the beanstalk
promising that he will grind Jack’s bones to bake his bread.  Fee fie foe hymn?

Regardless, the shortcomings of the third verse should not
obscure the point that by and large, this is a strong hymn.  It explores a Biblically important topic using
the language of the Bible.  If a hymn
gives me the opportunity to do that during worship, I’m satisfied.

It also, I think, highlights a larger point about the
worship wars.  There is certainly a case
to be made against many contemporary hymns. 
Either they have nothing to say, or they say the wrong thing.

However, I don’t see that there is a case to be made against
contemporary hymns, period.  As we’ve
seen, some of them do what hymns are supposed to do.  They may not do it in a familiar musical
style, but at least in my book, adherence to a particular musical style is not
a matter of faith.  It’s a matter of
taste.  I may be strongly committed to my
musical tastes, but they don’t provide me with a basis for advancing a reasoned
argument.

From what I’ve seen, there is good and bad in the sacred
music that comes from any era.  The
repertoire of past centuries only appears stronger because the dross of the
mediocre and bad has already been smelted off by the passage of time.  No matter our religious tradition, we should
take advantage of that repertoire.

However, we should
also take advantage of the new that is good. 
If we do battle against the likes of “One Bread, One Body” simply
because it is new, we create two problems. 
First, we injure our worship. 
Second, we leave ourselves open to an even more grievous
counterstroke.  We allow the proponents
of contemporary worship the rhetorical room they need to reject the old simply
because it is old.
