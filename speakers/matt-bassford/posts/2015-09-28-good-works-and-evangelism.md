Just about every Christian out there is aware that we have a
God-given responsibility to evangelize. 
We’re supposed to go and make disciples. 
We’re supposed to proclaim the word. 
This is a simple concept. 
However, as Clausewitz once observed about war, just because it’s simple
doesn’t mean it’s easy, and evangelism certainly belongs on the list of
simple-yet-difficult things.

Few indeed are the brethren, myself included, who are happy
with their evangelistic production.

There are a number of different things that brethren do in
response to this problem.  Some
congregations try to off-load their evangelistic responsibilities onto the
preacher.  The problem, of course, is
that Jesus doesn’t want our hired substitutes. 
He wants us, and if we try to push serving Him off onto somebody else,
He won’t be pleased with that.

Likewise, other congregations try to gimmick their way into evangelism
via some program that demands little but offers much.  However, only a disciple can make a disciple,
and we can only hope to find evangelistic success by investing ourselves in the
process.

Instead, we will be much better off if we turn not to human
wisdom but to the word.  The
first-century church was the most effective evangelistic organization in
history.  If we study and learn from it,
we can succeed too.  Let’s look, then, at
good works and evangelism.



**Multiple Witnesses**

Our exploration of the topic has to begin with an
understanding of the importance of multiple witnesses.  We see this concept being **INTRODUCED** in Deuteronomy 19:15.  At first glance, this seems like a weird
passage for us to go to.  After all, we
no longer live under the Law of Moses. 
Why should a requirement of the Mosaic judicial system matter to us?

In reality, though, this verse is one of the most important
verses in the entire Old Testament for our understanding of the New
Testament.  In significance, it’s right
up there with “Seeing, they may not see,” and “The just shall live by faith.”  The speakers and writers of the New Testament
treated this passage not merely as the way to convict a criminal, but as the
way to convict a heart.

This is only good logical sense.  After all, if we see one man accusing
another, and the second denying the accusation, that’s just one man’s word
against another.  We don’t have enough
evidence to make a decision yet.  On the
other hand, if there are two witnesses bringing the same accusation, it starts
to look like the guy doing the denying is a liar.  Similarly, if the gospel were only proclaimed
by one man without support, sinners could brush him off.  However, if the gospel he preached was
supported or confirmed by another witness, then they’d give him a listen.

We first see this idea of multiple witnesses being used **BY JESUS.**Read with me from John 5:31-40. 
In many ways, this passage is a microcosm of the whole book of
John.  Over and over again, John
introduces multiple witnesses to support his contention that Jesus is the
Christ.  Here, Jesus cites four witnesses
in support of Himself:  John the Baptist,
the miracles that Jesus worked, the Father, and the Scriptures.  All of these witnesses testified to Jesus.

However, it’s important to note that not all of these
witnesses are people.  Some of them are,
or at least entities with a personality. 
Both the Father and John the Baptist could and did speak to testify that
Jesus was the Son of God.  However, there
are other items on this list that aren’t beings.  Miracles don’t literally talk.  Neither does the Scripture.  However, the fact that Jesus could work
miracles confirmed His claim to be the Christ, and so too did the prophecies
about Him.

Similarly, **THE
APOSTLES** also used this multiple-witness style of argumentation in their
defense and proclamation of the gospel.  They
didn’t do so as overtly as Jesus did it, but we still see it appearing quite
clearly in the first gospel sermon.  Look
at Acts 2:5-6, 14-15, 32.  Here, three
witnesses work together to prove the same point.  The first witness is the witness of miracle.  Once the apostles speak in foreign languages,
the multitude realizes that something strange is going on.

However, notice that the first witness by itself isn’t
enough.  Some in the crowd are able to
dismiss it by charging that Peter and the rest are drunk.  In response, Peter brings in the second
witness:  the witness of Scripture.  Citing both Joel 2 and Psalm 16, Peter shows
that the Jews should have expected two things: 
First, an outpouring of the Holy Spirit as a sign of salvation.  Second, that God would raise His Holy One
from the dead.  After this, Peter summons
his third witness, the human testimony of the apostles.  Together, the twelve proclaim that God has
already raised His Holy One from the dead, and that the Holy One is Jesus, who
has now been made Lord and Christ. 
Logically, this threefold witness is powerful and difficult to refute.

Of course, all of this is important not because of the
structure of the logic, but because of what the logic proves.  We see **THE
POINT** in John 20:30-31.  Richard
Dawkins once said that Darwinism made it possible to be an intellectually
fulfilled atheist.  Whatever we think of
that particular claim, it is undeniable that the New Testament allows us to be
intellectually fulfilled Christians.  It
is no small thing to believe that Jesus is the Son of God or that by believing
in Him, we can receive life in His name. 
That’s not the kind of thing you ought to believe because you got a
funny feeling in your tummy one day. 
Instead, our faith is based on these witnesses, on the meticulously
crafted arguments of Scripture that lead us to the truth.

We see, then, that the gospel had such success in the first
century because of the power of the multiple-witness strategy.  Scripture plus miracle plus personal
testimony was tough for the devil to overcome. 
Today, though, what does this mean for us?  We can’t work miracles and none of us have
seen the risen Lord for ourselves, so all that’s left to us is the Bible.

In theory, the multiple witnesses present in Scripture ought
to be enough to get the job done, and they are. 
If somebody with an open heart will sit down with me and study, I can
generally persuade them to obey the gospel. 
That’s not the problem.  The
problem is that Bible students with open hearts are hard to come by, and as
many of us can testify, Scriptures by the dozen will just bounce off the
surface of a heart that has been hardened against the truth.

**The Witness of Good
Works**

In short, we’ve got a witness problem.  People will generally reject the witness of
Scripture by itself, just like the Jews on the day of Pentecost rejected the
witness of tongue-speaking by itself.  We
need reinforcements.  We need an
additional witness, the witness of good works.

The Lord Himself describes this witness as **LETTING YOUR LIGHT SHINE.** Turn, of course, to Matthew 5:14-16.  First of all, let’s be clear about what this
witness is not.  This is not what the
denominational world describes as “witnessing”. 
This is not, “You ask me how I know He lives? He lives within my
heart!”  To be blunt, who cares if
somebody thinks Jesus lives within his heart? 
That’s utterly subjective and proves nothing.  Likewise, who cares if I talk about how Jesus
has made my life better and happier? 
Others say the same thing about Buddha and Mohammed.

However, if I demonstrate that Christ lives in me by living
like Him, that’s a whole ‘nother kettle of fish!  Whether people in the time of Christ loved
Him or hated Him, they couldn’t ignore Him. 
His words and His life both demanded a response.  The more like Him we become, the more
difficult we also become to ignore. 
Nobody can brush off the example of a dedicated disciple, and that
example has a way of prying open the doors of even the most tightly sealed
heart.

A couple of things are true of the witness of good works
when it’s genuine.  The first is that it
involves **IRREPROACHABLE CONDUCT.**Let’s consider 1 Peter 2:11-12.  This text points out something about our
first-century brethren that we don’t often realize.  There was a tremendous amount of prejudice
directed at them.  It was common for
early Christians to be condemned as rebels, atheists (because they didn’t
acknowledge all the gods), and even cannibals. 
That’s definitely a case of having the deck stacked against you!  As many of us have experienced, if someone
has it in for you, they will seize upon any scrap of bad behavior and use it to
justify their low opinion.  Any kind of
moral failing in Christians would cause others to believe all those allegations
were true.

Today, even if nobody is calling us atheists or cannibals
yet, there are plenty of slanders directed against us.  From various directions, we’re called
hateful, bigots, cultists, and Pharisees. 
The only way to avoid those slanders is to hide our faith entirely, at
which point we aren’t going to save anybody. 
On the other hand, any weakness in us will only make those charges
stick.

As Peter points out, the only way to escape the trap is to
live up to our high calling.  If
somebody’s going to be putting our lives under a magnifying glass, we have to
make sure that they’re not going to find anything no matter how hard they
look.  Sooner or later, they’ll have to
admit that they were wrong about us in the first place, and when they do,
that’s an opening for the gospel.

As important as our influence is on others, though, we must
make sure that our first goal is **PLEASING
GOD.** Look at how Paul puts this in 1
Thessalonians 2:4-6.  Here, he’s
describing his initial evangelistic work in Thessalonica.  He’s able to rely on the witness of his good
works now that he’s writing to those early converts, but what’s especially
interesting is his description of his motives. 
Even though his behavior had the effect of impressing them with his
sincerity, that wasn’t why he lived in such a way.  Instead, he was concerned only with pleasing
God.

We’re supposed to consider the effect that our conduct has
on others, but most of all, we must remember that we are serving our
Creator.  If, by contrast, our goal is
just to impress people so that we can cut notches on our Bibles, that’ll go
wrong in a couple of different ways. 
First, people can tell when we’re just putting on a good front for
them.  Second, if man-pleasing is our
goal, that will distort our conduct in a number of subtle but ultimately
disastrous ways.  After all, the gospel
will not please most people, so if we want to please most people, we’ll have to
abandon the gospel.  Instead, we are to
offer our witness to others but our lives to God.

**Works that Witness**

Finally, we’re going to consider four Biblical examples of
good works that bear witness to others. 
The first of these is **A CHEERFUL
SPIRIT.** Consider Paul’s words to the
brethren in Philippi in Philippians 2:14-15. 
Ever since the beginning of time, humankind has been given to whining
and complaining and quarrelling.  If you
have any doubt that these things are still a significant problem, just spend
about 15 minutes reading your Facebook feed! 
It’s fair to say that these behaviors are universal among people in the
world.

If we want to stand out, then, one of the best ways to do
that is by living whine-free, contention-free lives.  This is exactly what we are being called to
do.  After all, Paul says, “Do *all* things without complaining and
arguing.”  This includes men’s meetings
at church.  This includes conversations
with our co-workers.  This includes
family gatherings.  It even includes
posting on Facebook.  Ever lived near a
major freeway that was shut down while you were living near it?  Let me tell you—the absence of that constant
noise is downright eerie!  In the same
way, friends, the absence of that spiritual noise from our lives will stand out
and call us to the attention of others.

Second, we reveal Christ in us through **A QUIET SPIRIT.** Peter raises
this point in 1 Peter 3:1-5.  This, of
course, is a text about evangelism, but it is explicitly written to women of
God who have non-Christian husbands. 
Some of these things apply to women only, but some of them apply to all
disciples, and the admonition to develop a quiet spirit falls into the latter
category.

Because this is written to women, we may be tempted to read
it in the same light as the commandment in 1 Timothy 2 that women are not to
teach or exercise authority, but I don’t think that’s the point that Peter is
trying to make at all.  This isn’t about
external quiet.  Instead, it’s about
internal quiet.  Those are not
necessarily the same thing.  I can
remember times when certain people weren’t willing to say a word to me, yet
were clearly seething internally with anger. 
Those were people who had quiet mouths, but not quiet spirits.

Instead, the point here is much closer to the point of
Jesus’ parable about the house built on the rock.  We don’t have quiet spirits because we’re
naturally phlegmatic, though that may be true. 
Instead, they are quiet because we have committed our lives to Him, and
if those lives are truly founded on the Rock of Ages, no external storm can
move us.  We can face hardship and
tragedy not without flinching, not without mourning, but with hope and trust
that He holds the future.

When this is true of us, the results can be awe-inspiring.  There is no more impressive sight than a
Christian bearing up under immense sorrows not because he is strong, but
because his Lord is strong.  That will
open people’s eyes.  It shows them what
they’re missing if they don’t have Jesus.

Third, and more prosaically, **HOSPITALITY** is a way to bear witness through good works.  Among the many passages in Scripture that
commend hospitality to us, let’s read Hebrews 13:2.  Hospitality was a big deal in ancient
times.  It’s not such a big deal to us;
indeed, I’ve read that the average American family only practices hospitality
about one time a year.

However, we can’t any more imitate our society’s example
when it comes to hospitality than we can when it comes to materialism or
illicit sex.  We’re called to follow the
word, not the world.  We need to be
regularly inviting strangers into our homes and sharing meals with them,
particularly non-Christians.  Often, Christians
throw up all kinds of excuses as to why they don’t do this.  “Oh, my house is too messy!”  Look at it this way.  Either your guests don’t care about
housekeeping, and they won’t notice, or they do, and you’ll give them an
opportunity to feel good about themselves!

Another popular excuse is, “I’m an introvert, and that’s
outside of my comfort zone.”  That’s
completely irrelevant.  It’s not just
Christians who are good singers who are called upon to sing, it’s not just
Christians who love to read who are called upon to study the Bible, and it’s
not just Christians who are extroverts who are supposed to be hospitable!  Maybe we aren’t five-talent Christians when
it comes to hospitality, but that doesn’t mean we get to bury what we’ve got.

This is so important because hospitality is such a valuable
tool for developing relationships. 
There’s something almost mystical that happens when you share a meal
with somebody.  You understand them, and
they you, in a way you didn’t before. 
These days, I will almost never study the Bible with an outsider unless
I’ve had the chance to eat with him first.

Finally, we shine our light through **SECOND-MILE SERVICE.** Our
text here, of course, is Matthew 5:41-42. 
Although Jesus is speaking explicitly here about the way we treat our
enemies, the same rule should apply with even more force to those who aren’t
our enemies.  Back when I was still in
the preacher-training program at Dowlen Road, Max Dawson told me, “Always give
the brethren more than they expect.” 
That’s sound advice for a preacher, but it’s equally sound advice for
all of us whenever we are serving others. 
When we go out of our way to help somebody unload a moving van or work
on their car, they remember that.  It
makes them recognize that there is something different about us, and that’s
exactly the way we want them thinking.

Of course, as important as all of this is to evangelism,
it’s not actually evangelism.  It’s not
sowing the seed; it’s preparing the soil. 
We can’t do good works and then wait expectantly.  Even when we’ve got somebody who has an open
heart, it’s still our role to make the first move, Bible in hand.  However, by diligently shining our lights, we
will open as many hearts as possible.
