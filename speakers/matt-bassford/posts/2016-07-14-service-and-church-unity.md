Last week, I read [an article](http://www.msn.com/en-us/news/opinion/one-way-we-can-repair-our-toxic-partisan-politics-national-service/ar-AAi4gb6) that urged the United States to institute a mandatory-service requirement for
young adults.  Everybody, once they turn
18, has to spend two years in the military, the Peace Corps, Americorps, or
similar.  He argues that this will force
young people to work with and appreciate others from diverse backgrounds,
thereby reducing the toxic polarization that is so much a part of American
society today.

I must admit that I’m skeptical about the “mandatory”
part.  I’ve read enough about the U.S.
Army during the Vietnam War to suspect that any organization made up of people
who don’t want to be there is going to have some serious problems.  The military has wanted nothing to do with
draftees ever since, and while I think the willing can learn a great deal from
public service, I fear that the unwilling will remain stubbornly unenlightened.



However, I believe that the author’s intuition about the
connection between service and unity is correct.  It’s human nature that the more we give of
ourselves to any organization, the more we identify with it and with its
members.

This applies particularly to the church.  Just about all of us have had experience with
discontented Christians.  They might
complain about “the Church of Christ” generally (whatever that is).  They might be unhappy with their local
congregation and the work that it’s doing. 
They might even be getting into it with other Christians.

As diverse as these
criticisms can be, there tends to be one thing that all the critics have in
common.  The discontented are also
disconnected.  Rather than being right
there in the midst of serving the Lord, they tend to have withdrawn themselves
from the work (at least somewhat) in order to complain.

In fact, their critical attitude often hinders them from taking
a larger role.  After all, who would want
to be more involved with such a deficient church/congregation/group of
people?  When the critics leave, as they
often do, in the words of 2 Chronicles 21:20, they depart with no one’s
regret.  Their absence is noted when
attendance is taken on Sunday morning, but not really otherwise.  Their contribution was so small that it is
easily made up for by the efforts of others.

All this is not only an organizational problem.  It’s a spiritual problem too, and it can easily
be one for us.  Who has not been unhappy
with something about the way we think the churches of Christ do things (though
the practice may not be as widespread as we think)?  Who has not thought that our local
congregation could do better?  Who has
not thought that some brother really needs to step up his spiritual game?

The devil is happy to encourage these thoughts in all of us,
and he will lead us to fall away because of them if he can.  Even if he can’t get us that far, he will
diligently use us to stir up strife in our local congregations.  That may well serve his purposes better than
if we were to leave entirely.

Unless we relish the thought of becoming a tool of the devil
(emphasis on “tool”), we can’t allow this spiritual problem to flourish.  If service and discontent are opposed to each
other, we can diminish our difficulties with discontent by serving more.

In many cases, we can do this by stepping up and solving the
problems we see.  The members of the
church aren’t being hospitable enough? 
Invite everybody over!  The
shut-ins are being neglected?  Go
visit!  The brethren are mean-spirited
and selfish?  Give them an example of selfless
love!  If I can solve a spiritual
problem, the problem isn’t somebody else’s. 
It’s mine.  Either I work toward a
solution, or I indict myself by my own complaints.

Even if we can’t tackle a problem directly, increased
involvement in the work of service will still improve our attitude.  Every congregation out there is
imperfect.  Some of them are even
church-in-Corinth or church-in-Sardis kind of bad.  If we’re of a mind, we can probably compile a
list of all the problems where we worship that would leave us feeling
stressed-out and depressed.

If  we want to see everything
to get fixed (and this is usually the desire of critics, whether they realize
it or not), that’s an impossibly tall order. 
Trying to perfect a congregation is like trying to hand-weed a two-acre
garden.  By the time you work your way
down to one end, the weeds at the other are already three feet high!

We can’t win the war on our own, but there are battles we
can win.  Maybe the song leading is
wretched, but we can’t be of any help (either because we’re female or because
we can’t carry a tune in a bucket).  It
is what it is.  Get out there and be salt
and light anyway.  Even if the song
leading stays wretched, better children’s classes or a steadier stream of
visitors from the community will make for a better church.  Over time, who knows?  Maybe the overall increase in enthusiasm will
nudge the song leaders to do a better job too.

Besides, the more effort we invest in the church, the more
strongly we will identify with the church. 
I know the people in my family aren’t perfect, but that doesn’t
matter.  They’re my family!  When we similarly identify with our brethren,
even if problems in the church continue, they will no longer strike us as quite
so important.
