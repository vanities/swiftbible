One of my favorite things about using *Psalms, Hymns, and Spiritual Songs* at Joliet is the opportunity it
often gives us to sing new verses to familiar hymns.  Most Christians don’t realize it, but many
older hymns were originally written with more than the canonical three verses.  We know them in a three-verse form because
previous generations of hymnal editors mutilated them to fit, but the editors
of *Psalms, Hymns, and Spiritual Songs* restored
the best of the excised verses to their proper place.

In any event, one of the hymns that swelled from three to
four verses is “O Worship the King”.  The
newly restored v. 2 reads:

O tell of His might, o sing of His grace,

Whose robe is the light, whose canopy space;

His chariots of wrath the deep thunderclouds form,

And dark is His path on the wings of the storm.

Honestly, I don’t see how you could love God and not love
singing that.  It does a magnificent job
of capturing the power and the grandeur of the Almighty.  When I put on my hymn-critic hat, the only
thing that I don’t like is the inversion that leads to “form” at the end of the
third line, but when I reach the fourth line, all is forgiven.  “And dark is His path on the wings of the
storm” makes the hair on my arms stand up.

It takes a quality that we don’t often associate with
God—darkness—and uses it to stand for God’s alienness, inscrutability,
wildness, and power.  These days, I can’t
watch a thunderhead boiling up on the horizon without thinking about this
verse.  Dark is His path on the wings of
the storm, indeed!

So.  Awesome verse,
yes?  Yay hymns!  However, that’s not all this one has to
offer.

A couple weeks back, I was doing my daily Bible reading with
my HCSB that has the luscious top-grain cowhide cover that smells so nice and
feels so wonderful.  “Why read out of a
Bible you don’t love?” says I.  Anyway,
I’m reading along, and I come to Psalm 104:2-3, which reads:

He wraps Himself in light as if it were a robe,

Spreading out the sky like a canopy,

Laying the beams of His palace on the waters above,

Making the clouds His chariot,

Walking on the wings of the wind.

Oh.  Ohhhh!  Back in 1833 when Robert Grant wrote “O
Worship the King”, he wasn’t only writing beautiful poetry about the
Creator.  He was using Psalm 104 to write
beautiful poetry about the Creator.

I am not the first to pick up on this (the editors helpfully
glossed “O Worship the King” as “based on Psalm 104” in tiny print down at the
bottom of the page where I didn’t notice it), but for me personally, it was a
spiritually meaningful discovery.  Now, I
can’t sing “O Worship the King” without thinking of Psalm 104, and I can’t read
Psalm 104 without thinking of “O Worship the King”.  My experience of both psalm and hymn is the
richer for it.

This, I think, is the most resonant argument for using
Scripturally rich hymns.  When hymnists
employ beautiful thoughts and phrases from the word, they do two things.  They give people who love the Bible something
to love in hymns, and they give people who love hymns something to love in the
Bible.

I’ve followed this both ways, actually.  I’ve loved “It Is Well With My Soul” since I
was a teenager, and my favorite verse is this:

My sin—o the bliss of this glorious thought—

My sin, not in part but the whole,

Is nailed to the cross, and I bear it no more.

Praise the Lord, praise the Lord, o my soul!

At some point when I was 14 or 15, the thought occurred to
me, “I bet that last line is from the Bible someplace,” so I went looking for
it.  I found not only Psalm 146:1, from
which the line is directly quoted, but also Psalm 103:1, with its very similar
injunction to “Bless the Lord, o my soul!” 
What’s more, I can’t escape the suspicion that 150 years ago, H. G.
Spafford included the line because he loved the verse and wanted to leave it
there for me and others like me to find.

The problem with thoughtless, content-light hymns and praise
songs (besides, of course, the fact that they’re thoughtless and content-light)
is that they can’t do that.  If an author
relies on his own resources to write, his hymns will never lead worshipers
anywhere higher than where he is.

It’s no accident, I think, that so many contemporary songs
are couched in the terms of romantic infatuation rather than religious
devotion.  If you haven’t developed a
mature relationship with God through His word, the only language that you have
to express profound emotion is the language of romance.  If that’s all you’ve got, then naturally
Jesus is going to come off sounding like your boyfriend instead of your
Lord.

Songs like that have a superficial appeal because the
experiences they draw on are nearly universal, but they’re not going to provoke
spiritual growth or a closer walk with God. 
They meet us where we are rather than taking us where we ought to
be.  It’s the spiritual equivalent of
eating cotton candy—enjoyable, but hardly nourishing.

I have a high view of Scripture.  I affirm along with 2 Timothy 3:16-17 that it
is inspired by God and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction,
and for instruction in righteousness.  I
find it to be as much computer program as book. 
Once we hide it in our hearts, it begins to transform us in ways that
are beyond our comprehension.

I believe in the transformative power of song worship too.  Whenever I leave an assembly of saints who
have poured out their hearts before the Lord, I leave a changed man.  These are two of the main tools that God uses
to reshape our lives.

When we sing songs that aren’t Scripturally rich, though,
we’re taking away half the toolbox. 
Sure, the music may move us, but it isn’t moving us anywhere
useful.  Far better for us to turn our
attention to “O Worship the King”, “It Is Well With My Soul”, and the hundreds
of other hymns, both old and new, that are similarly worthy.  It is when the Bible is working in and with
our hymns that the work of those hymns is perfected.
