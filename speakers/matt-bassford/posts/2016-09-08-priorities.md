I shared the meme yesterday, but I think the point is worth
repeating today.  Our souls and our
families’ souls aren’t most at risk from social pressures to do things that are
obviously wrong.  Instead, we are most in
danger from the social pressures that aren’t obviously wrong, and in this
latter list, we must include the American cult of high achievement.

This one is tough to see for the (mostly) white,
middle-class suburbanites who make up the Lord’s church in the United States
these days.  It’s tough to see because
it’s so fundamental.  In the circles in
which we move, it’s taken for granted that the purpose of life is to accumulate
as much money and social position as possible, and to teach our children to do
the same.  Our lives are about diplomas
and promotions and nice houses and good school districts.



Certainly, the Bible encourages diligence and hard
work.  In Ecclesiastes 9:10, Solomon
says, “Whatever your hand finds to do, do it with all your might, for there is
no activity or planning or knowledge or wisdom in Sheol where you are
going.”  I know this because my dad
quoted it to me.  Frequently.  To this day, I’m terrified that somebody is
going to think I’m lazy.

However, there are at least two caveats to the rule.  The first is that the Scriptures spend as
much time encouraging rest as they do work. 
The Sabbath cut productivity by a seventh every week.  Ecclesiastes 4:6 reads, “One hand full of
rest is better than two fists full of labor and striving after wind.”

There aren’t too many dads quoting that one to their
children, though.  Instead, we pick and
choose the passages that support what people in our society are inclined to do
anyway.  This kind of unbalanced reading
of the Bible creates problems wherever it is employed, and the American cult of success is no different.

Nonetheless, the pressure to conform is strong.  People don’t brag about how well-balanced
their schedules are, so that they have plenty of time to relax while still
providing for their families.  They don’t
boast about how they leave their children to fend for themselves for hours at a
time.

Instead, they trumpet how busy they are, how they’re
constantly rushing from work to practice to concert to PTA meeting.  These narratives are generally couched in the
form of a complaint (“Here’s how my week has gone, and I’m exhausted!”), but
they’re really a form of cultural bonding. 
They prove to others that we have paid our dues and deserve membership
in America’s in-crowd.

Though unspoken, this norm is real and powerful.  When we tell others the right stories about
ourselves, we get sympathetic nods and a sense of belonging.  What happens, though, when we tell the wrong
stories?  What happens when we say, “I
haven’t done anything this week.  I let
the kids spend their evenings watching TV while I lay on the couch playing
Candy Crush for hours at a time”?  Our
friends and neighbors would doubtless smile politely, while thinking to
themselves, “What awful people! 
Tomorrow, they’re probably going to have a car up on blocks in their
front yard or something.”  We don’t want
that reputation, so we conform.

Of course, if it’s
that important to be busy in pursuing socially acceptable goals, it is not only
our free time that falls by the wayside. 
It is also our other priorities. 
This is the second caveat.  The
Bible warns us that anything we love too much will become an idol, and the
American ideal of hard work and success is certainly robust enough to attract
worship.  Once we begin to worship it,
the worship and service we owe God will inevitably suffer.

I get the sense sometimes that brethren think some aspects
of discipleship are more important than others. 
It’s really important to have your kids there for Sunday-morning
worship, kind of important to get them up an hour earlier for Bible class, and
meh important to have them there on Wednesday. 
In fact, considering how early they have to get up for school Thursday
morning, it’s probably best to skip Bible study altogether.

As for all the ways we might serve outside the assembly—showing
hospitality, visiting shut-ins, teaching our neighbors, and so on—ain’t nobody
got time for that.  Between our
overwhelming work schedule and all of our children’s extracurriculars, there
simply aren’t enough hours in the day. 
Surely God understands how good that chess-club membership in fourth
grade will look on a college application!

And yet, somehow, we manage to sing “All to Jesus I
surrender” with a straight face.

I understand the drive to excel, maybe better than
most.  I have two bachelor’s degrees and
a law degree from a Top-15 law school. 
That makes me the most poorly educated of my siblings. Since graduation,
we’ve all been industrious about adding to our curricula vitae.  My brother has his own Wikipedia
page.  In the rat race, Bassfords
generally make for pretty fast rats.

Know what it’s all going to be worth 1000 years from
now?  Unless it was for God, not a thing.

The point here is not that we should quit our jobs, yank our
children out of all of those after-school activities, and move next door to the
local meth dealer.  The point here is to
remember that God wants it all.  Sure, He
wants us to conform to His will in all the ways that “nice people” do.  He wants us to be hard workers, good parents,
and good citizens.

However, He also wants us to put Him first, to make our
lives about more than being hard workers, good parents, and good citizens.  If that means that we lose according to the
metrics of salaries, property values, and school-district ratings, so be
it.  It’s important to raise our children
to be productive members of society, but it’s more important to raise them to
be productive members of the body of Christ.

It’s most important of all that we seek that life ourselves.
