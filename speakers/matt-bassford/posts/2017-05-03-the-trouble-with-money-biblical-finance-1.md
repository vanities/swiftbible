In James 3:2, the Lord’s brother observes that anyone who
doesn’t sin in his speech has the sin problem licked in every other area of his
life as well.  If you can control your
tongue, you can control everything else too. 
Hint:  James does not believe that
anyone actually succeeds in doing this.

There are few other things in the spiritual realm that cause
this much trouble for everyone, but one of them, I think, is money and how to
handle it.  We live in a materialistic
society that is addicted to conspicuous consumption and consumer debt.  All of us know this; many of us live it.

In response to the besetting sin of the age (and it is
sinful; make no mistake about that!), countless finance writers, secular and
Biblically informed alike, urge us to craft a budget, limit our spending, save
for emergencies, save for retirement, and generally control our relationship
with money in a hundred different ways.  I
think this is valuable advice, so far as it goes, but it brings with it a
collection of new but equally serious spiritual challenges.  If I ruthlessly discipline my spending, will
I still be inclined to share with those who have been undisciplined with their
money and now need help?  What are my
responsibilities to the poor of the community where I live?  How will I ensure that my laying up treasure
for myself won’t lead to poverty toward God?



More subtly, in a world where “money is the answer to
everything”, how do we avoid making everything about money?  The problem reminds me of one of my favorite
Buddhist parables:  A monk and his
disciple came to a river crossing.  While
there, they met a lady, who asked them to carry her across the river.  The student refused with pious horror, but
the master agreed, picked the lady up, and safely brought her to the far
bank.

Then, the two Buddhists resumed their journey.  After a few miles, the disciple could contain
himself no longer and burst out with an angry, “Master!  How could you???”

The master replied, “Are you still carrying the lady?  I put her down three miles ago.”

When it comes to money, even the most frugal may find
themselves still carrying the lady. 
There are obvious spiritual problems with out-of-control finances, but
the love of money may be every bit as abundant in the heart of those who have
their finances perfectly under control. 
The Scripture tells us famously that it leads to every form of evil, and
contempt for the improvident, trust in the security of one’s own wealth, and
preoccupation with being good with money are no less evil than offering one’s
life as a sacrifice to the debt gods.  Jesus
is the pathway to the abundant life, not money, no matter how we use it.

And yet, for all this, money itself is not evil.  In fact, it is an essential tool for our
service to God.  We can’t escape it, and
we shouldn’t if we could.

This is complicated stuff! 
Not surprisingly, the Bible is full of guidance on how to handle money,
from Proverbs’ pithy advice on debt to Paul’s suggestion that we work partially
so that we can be generous to the needy. 
However, because this guidance doesn’t fit neatly into any worldly
schema, it is often either considered piecemeal or ignored.

For the next little while (in other words, for as long as
people are reading it), I intend to address this lack.  Since I was a young adult, I’ve been
interested in personal finance (I even like income-tax law!), and I think that
a lot of the advice dispensed by the Dave Ramseys of our day has a Biblical
basis.  I’ll point that out where it’s
useful.  However, I intend too to explore
the question of how we as Christians should think about money.  Unless we adopt a godly attitude, then
financial wisdom inevitably starts to take on worldly overtones.  Financial freedom is important, but it is the
Son who makes us free indeed.

Thus for my latest project.
