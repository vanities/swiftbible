As Paul notes in 1 Corinthians 1, it’s not appropriate for
us to identify ourselves by the name of Paul, Apollos, or any other man when it
is Christ who was crucified for us and in whose name we are baptized.  We’re Christians, not Paulists, Apollonians,
or any other such designation.  In fact,
whenever we see a church that takes its name from some man, that’s a sure sign
that it’s gone off the rails someplace.  If
anybody else’s teaching has replaced Christ’s, you’ve got a serious problem!

However, if we’re going to call ourselves Christians, we’d
better act like Christians, and a big part of that is listening to and learning
from Christ.  Claiming to be a follower
of Jesus while remaining ignorant of His teaching and character is kind of like
claiming to be a Blackhawks fan while never watching a single game.  We need to know the story of Jesus as well as
we know anything.  With that in mind,
then, let’s turn to the gospels this morning to consider a set of linked
stories that have been arranged together to reveal the Christ.



**Peter’s Confession**

All of the stories we’ll be looking at appear in Mark 8-9,
and the first of them is about Peter’s confession.  This begins when Jesus asks His disciples **A QUESTION**.  The resultant conversation appears in Mark
8:27-30.  When we read this, one of the
first things that stands out to us is what’s not here.  Mark does not repeat the story from Matthew
16 about how Jesus says to Simon that his name is Peter, on this rock He’ll
build His church, and so on.

This is particularly interesting when we consider what the
Church Fathers said about the origin of Mark. 
According to these men, who lived and wrote in the first and second
centuries and therefore were fairly close in time to the writing of the New
Testament, Mark was Peter’s follower and interpreter.  He wasn’t present for the ministry of Jesus
himself, so he got his information from it about Peter.  If, as some assert, Jesus meant to make Peter
supreme among the apostles and establish a succession of men who were likewise
supreme, how come Peter didn’t talk about this? 
If God put me in charge, I’d make sure everybody knew about it to avoid
confusion down the road.  Peter’s silence
on the subject implies that he didn’t understand this event that way at all.

Before we move on, let’s also consider Jesus’ strange
reaction to Peter’s statement here. 
Clearly, there’s a lot of confusion about Jesus’ identity.  He’s the Christ, but all of these other
people think He’s somebody else. 
However, rather than urging His disciples to go out and set all these
folks straight, Jesus tells them not to tell anybody.  He does the opposite of what we would expect.

I think this is best explained by Jesus’ awareness of His
mission.  He knows that He has to die,
but He also knows that He has a bunch of work to do first.  If the rumor that He is the Christ leaks out
too soon, the Jews or the Romans might grab Him and kill Him before He is able
to finish.  Jesus here is controlling the
spread of information so He doesn’t die too early.

In fact, Jesus goes on to show that this is what’s on His
mind by making **A SHOCKING
STATEMENT.** Look at Mark
8:31-33.  When Peter identified Jesus as
the Christ, he said something that was both true and secret.  Here, Jesus is responding with another secret
truth.  Even though He is the Christ, He
is going to be taken and killed by His enemies. 
However, the conclusion will be even more shocking, because after three
days, Jesus will rise from the dead. 
This is the first time in His ministry that Jesus predicts either His
death or His resurrection.

From the text, there’s no doubt that His disciples
understand Him.  He’s not speaking in a
parable.  For once, He’s being absolutely
straightforward and plain.  Not
surprisingly, Peter, the same man who just proclaimed Him the deliverer of
Israel, is horrified.  If your Messiah
gets killed by His enemies instead of killing them, the hope of Israel will
never be brought to fruition.  Peter
pulls Jesus aside and begins to read Him the riot act for saying such awful
things.

Jesus responds in stinging fashion.  In denying the truth of what Jesus said,
Peter is doing nothing less than putting his own interests before God’s and
serving the devil.  This implies that
when Jesus dies, it won’t be because of the plans of the enemies of God.  It will be because of God’s plan.  Indeed, it implies that Jesus’ purpose in
coming to earth has always been to die.

It is extremely important that Mark records Jesus saying
this and His disciples not understanding it before Jesus is crucified.  No false Messiah would make such a bizarre
claim about Himself.  False Messiahs tell
their followers they’re going to win! 
Even beyond this, the resurrection of Jesus, which is plenty important
on its own, becomes even more important when we see that Jesus predicted
it.  Only the Son of God can foretell His
own death and resurrection.

Finally in this section, Jesus says some more appalling
things about **COMING AFTER HIM.** Look at Mark 8:34-9:1.  This is significant, so Jesus calls in not
only the disciples, but the multitudes to listen.  According to Him, He isn’t the only one who
is going to have to die.  Instead,
everybody who wants to follow Him is going to have to pick up a cross to do it,
sometimes literally, always figuratively. 
To us, carrying a cross brings to mind one of those dudes in white robes
in a high-church service leading the procession with that staff with the cross
on it.  To Jesus’ audience, carrying a
cross meant that you were about to die the agonizing death of a robber or
rebellious slave.

That sounds like a bad idea, but Jesus goes on to reveal
that the alternatives are even worse. 
People who willingly die for Jesus are going to live, but people who try
to save their own skins are going to end up suffering the very fate they
fear.  Why?  Because when Jesus comes, He is going to be
ashamed of anybody who was ashamed of Him.

Here, we come to the last verse of the reading, Mark
9:1.  There are good chapter breaks and
bad chapter breaks in Scripture, and I’m strongly of the opinion that this is
one of the bad ones.  I don’t think it
has anything to do directly with the story of the Transfiguration in Mark
9.  Instead, I think it defines at least
one of the incidents Jesus is talking about in the last verse of Mark 8.  During the last part of His ministry, Jesus
spent a great deal of time predicting that He would come in judgment against
the wicked city of Jerusalem, and here, He is telling His audience that this
coming will happen within some of their lifetimes.  Of course, this is exactly what happened with
the destruction of Jerusalem and the temple in 70 AD.

Before the Lord’s Supper, let’s think about all this.  Let’s think about what a strange Messiah
Jesus was and is.  He didn’t deliver His
people by leading a mighty army to victory. 
Instead, He did it by dying a lonely death on a cross.  However, the deliverance that He purchased
with His blood is so great that even now, 2000 years later, we have gathered to
remember it.

Let’s also ponder Jesus’ warning.  Like the Jews of His day, we too live in an
adulterous and sinful generation.  The
judgment will come for all of us too, though maybe not within any of our
lifetimes.  However, when it comes, we
can be certain that if we are ashamed of Jesus, He’ll be ashamed of us.  Let’s reflect on this as we partake.

**The Transfiguration**

Mark’s exploration of this strange Messiah continues with
his account of the transfiguration.  It
begins with what three of Jesus’ closest disciples see **ON THE MOUNTAINTOP.** Our
story continues in Mark 9:2-8.  What Peter,
James, and John see here is one of the strangest events in the gospels.  Jesus Himself is changed so that some of His
heavenly glory starts leaking through, and two famous dead people, Moses and
Elijah, appear and start talking with Him.

This is one of the places where the Petrine origin of the
story becomes obvious.  In one of his
characteristic blurts, Peter offers to build three tabernacles, one for each of
the three prophets.  However, he doesn’t
say this because he’s thought it through and thinks it’s a good idea.  Instead, the text candidly tells us that he
says it because he’s terrified and doesn’t know what to say.  I’ve certainly had moments in my life like
that, where I had no idea what to do, so I just started talking and hoped it
would end well.  Peter, however, doesn’t
get rewarded for his thoughtlessness. 
Instead, he gets rebuked by no one less than the Father Himself, who
identifies Jesus as His Son and tells the apostles to listen to Him.  Then, Moses and Elijah vanish.

I’ve heard it said that this story represents Jesus’
supremacy over the Law and the Prophets. 
That’s certainly a glib enough explanation, but even though it could be
true, I don’t see anything in the text that demands that conclusion.  Instead, I see something else.  In His response to Peter, God is quoting
Scripture.  When He says of Jesus,
“Listen to Him,” He’s alluding to the prophecy of Deuteronomy 18:15-19.  There, Moses predicts that a prophet like
him—somebody who will be a second lawgiver—will arise from among the people,
and anybody who doesn’t listen to this second prophet will be cut off from
among his people.  God identifies Jesus
with Moses, not with Elijah.

As a result, the ensuing discussion between Jesus and the
three apostles concerns **ELIJAH.** Look at Mark 9:9-13. Just as He did with
Peter’s proclamation of Him as the Christ, Jesus urges the disciples to keep
this story quiet until He rises from the dead. 
I think the underlying logic is the same here:  if this story gets spread around, Jesus is
likely to be killed ahead of schedule. 
They don’t understand what Jesus means about rising from the dead, but
they agree anyway.

Next, though, they start asking about Elijah.  This shows that they understand the
connection between Jesus and Moses that the voice from heaven made.  Moses’ presence on the mountain makes
sense.  However, Elijah is still a loose
thread, and they want to figure that one out. 
Besides, wasn’t Elijah supposed to come and prepare the way for the
Messiah anyway?

In response, Jesus addresses both discussion topics.  Elijah has already come, and when he came,
unlike the original Elijah, he was killed by his enemies.  By now, the apostles would have had no trouble
identifying Elijah as John the Baptist, whom Antipas had executed some time
earlier.

Jesus goes on to point out, however, that it is prophesied
that the same thing that happened to John will happen to the Son of Man.  Just as Elijah Part 2 was killed, so Moses
Part 2 also will be.  Paradoxically,
Jesus is both the great Prophet to whom the people should listen and the
suffering Servant whom the people will murder.

**The Demon-Possessed
Boy**

The third of our trio of stories that reveal who Jesus is
concerns a demon-possessed boy.  It
begins with a man announcing **A PROBLEM
DEMON.** Read with me from Mark
9:14-19.  Ever been in one of those jobs
where you’re the indispensable worker, where if you aren’t around, the whole
place falls apart?  This must have been
how Jesus felt when He came down off the mountain with Peter, James, and
John.  He finds a big crowd gathered
watching His disciples arguing with some scribes, and one gets the impression
that the disciples are on the defensive.

Soon, the truth reveals itself.  A father brought his demon-possessed son to
the disciples to have the demon cast out, as they have been doing ever since
Jesus commissioned them, but they are unable to do so.  This is a big PR problem.  Once the disciples are no longer able to
perform the works of men sent from God, they open the door for their enemies to
insist that they aren’t sent from God. 
The scribes smelled blood and now have closed in for the kill.  Jesus comes in, sees the whole mess, and is
utterly disgusted, with His own disciples not least.

This moment might have diminished the faith of the
multitudes, but the fact that it’s recorded bolsters our own.  Even today, this is the kind of story that
makes Jesus and His followers look bad. 
If the power of the Holy Spirit is so great, how come the disciples
didn’t cast out the demon and send the boy and his father on their way?  Sure, the outcome of the story eventually
makes Jesus look good, but Mark didn’t have to tell this part to tell that
part.  Instead, I think this shows Mark’s
essential honesty as a historian.  It’s
much less important to him to make his side look perfect than it is to tell the
truth.  As a result, we can have
confidence in his narrative.

Next, we see Jesus confirm **THE POWER OF FAITH**.  Our
story continues in Mark 9:20-24.  As
Jesus has asked, the father brings the boy to him, and as soon as he shows up,
the demon starts acting out.  Jesus
starts quizzing Dad about the situation, and as they’re talking, the father
utters the fatal words, “if You can”. 
Let’s be honest; it’s completely understandable that the father has
developed some doubts by this point. 
After all, the disciples have already failed once.

Jesus, however, jumps on him.  He tells the father that anything is possible
for those who believe.  In response, the
father utters what might be the most nonsensical, yet heartfelt, cry in the
entire Bible.  He says, “I believe; help
my unbelief!”, and I think there are times when all of us can identify with
that.  We come to God in faith, and yet
sometimes it’s faith we’re looking for.

However, I think the key point here concerns Jesus’
statement about faith.  This is one that
modern-day faith healers love.  When
somebody comes to them with some illness, and they lay hands on the sick
person, but the sick person doesn’t get any better, they like to blame the one
who didn’t get healed.  “If only you had
had more faith,” they say, “I could have made you well.”

In reality, though, it’s not that the faith of others can limit
Jesus’ power to heal.  It’s that it can
limit his opportunities to heal.  After
all, there are times in Scripture when Jesus raises the dead, and dead people
are incapable of faith.  However, if Dad
had gotten angry at Jesus and stalked off, taking his boy with him, in that
case, his lack of faith would have prevented Jesus from casting out the
demon.  All things are possible for those
who believe not because our belief has magic power, but because when we do
believe, we go to God for help and refuse to give up.

The story concludes with Jesus **HEALING THE BOY.** Let’s
finish things up with Mark 9:25-29.  The
first thing that we see here is Jesus behaving in a way that seems at odds with
His desire for secrecy.  He waits until a
crowd has run together before He casts out the demon.  In reality, though, what seems like a
contradiction is actually Jesus pursuing another one of His purposes.  There are times when Jesus conceals big,
spectacular miracles, like His raising of the dead girl, again probably because
He’s worried about too much attention too soon. 
However, Jesus still needs to establish that He’s a genuine prophet, and
one of the ways He does that is by working public, undeniable miracles.  Now that the scribes have publicly called His
disciples’ credibility, and therefore His credibility too, into question, Jesus
has to expel the demon publicly too.

Even though the demon puts up a ferocious fight, it is
ultimately no match for the Son of God. 
After a short but excruciating ordeal, the boy is freed.  What’s most interesting here, though, is not
the exorcism, but the conversation that follows it.  Not surprisingly, Jesus’ followers want to know
why He could do what they couldn’t, and in response, He tells them that this
kind can only come out by prayer.  As
answers go, this one is not terribly satisfying.  After all, Jesus did succeed in casting the
demon out, but so far as we can tell from the text, He didn’t pray first.

There are two things here that are worth talking about.  First, we see here that there are
distinctions among demons.  Some the
apostles could use their spiritual gifts to cast out, but there were kinds of
demons that were too powerful for the apostles. 
Second, what’s underlying Jesus’ seemingly contradictory statement is
the truth that the rule about prayer applies to the apostles but not to Him
because He is not like the apostles.  The
final proof of His authority in this reading is His casting out of a demon that
would give way before no one but the Son of God.
