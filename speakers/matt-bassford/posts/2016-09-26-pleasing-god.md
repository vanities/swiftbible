Everybody’s life is about something, whether we realize it
or not.  There is some goal to which our
days are dedicated, even if that goal is nothing more profound than doing what
makes us happy in the moment. Not all of these goals are equally
worthwhile.  All of us see the problem
with the life goals of the career criminal, who seeks to get ahead by harming
other people.

However, some of the problems with life goals are less
obvious.  Our country celebrates the successful
businessman, but when he dies and leaves hundreds of millions of dollars to his
heirs, his success is revealed for the hollow thing it has always been.  We can be buried in a Corvette, but we still
can’t take it with us!  Instead, as
individuals and as a congregation, we must ask whether pursuit of our goals
will benefit us not only in this life, but also in the life to come.  If we achieve success with these goals, only
then will we have gained something worth having.  With this in mind, then, let’s turn our
attention to where all spiritual goals inevitably lead:  to pleasing God.



**Figuring It Out**

However, before we can start working on pleasing God, we
must learn not only what God-pleasing activities are, but how to figure out
what they are.  We see **THE QUESTION RAISED** in Ephesians
5:8-10.  As this text makes clear,
learning what pleases God lies at the heart of Christianity.  It’s a necessary part of walking as children
of light.

To grasp its importance, just think of the difficulty in
trying to walk as children of light without knowing what pleases God!  It would be like dressing me up in a cricket
uniform, putting me on a cricket pitch, and telling me to play cricket.  I’m no great athlete in any sport, but at
least if I’m playing basketball or football, I know that the ball is supposed
to go in the hoop or over the goal line. 
By contrast, I do not know anything at all about the rules of cricket,
so if you try to get me to play cricket, I’m going to stand around like an
idiot while the rest of my team is trying to win.

Friends, if we don’t know what pleases God, then our
ignorant attempts to do so will be no more effective than my ignorant attempts
to play cricket.  Nor is it something we
can figure out on our own, any more than I’m going to figure out how to play
cricket sitting in my office staring at the wall.  If we want to be effective servants of God,
we’re going to have to learn how from somewhere.

The first and most obvious place to learn is from **OTHER PEOPLE.** This is, after all, a common way for us
to learn how to do things.  Let’s
consider cricket again.  Back when I
lived in Austin, one of my friends lived in an apartment complex that happened
to have a lot of Indian tenants.  Cricket
is very popular in India, so when the weather was good, many of them would
gather on a nearby field to play cricket. 
I saw them doing it, but I never spent more than a moment or two
watching them.  However, if I had decided
that I wanted to learn to play, sat watching them for a while, and possibly
even asked questions or participated myself, I could have figured it out.

Biblically speaking,
though, there appears to be a problem with this approach.  Consider the words of the Lord in Matthew
15:7-9.  This tells us that pleasing God
is very different from many other activities because the risk of error is much
greater.  When the Jews of the time of
Jesus followed the traditions of the Pharisees, they didn’t succeed in pleasing
God.  Instead, they offered up worship
that was offensive and vain.  In today’s
religious world, where there are so many different people doing so many
different things in the name of Christ, it’s certain that at least some of them
are making the same mistake, and it’s possible that all of them may be.  We don’t dare make people the standard.  Instead, we have to find a different standard
to evaluate the actions of people.

In our quest for that
standard, we might choose another popular course, learning about pleasing God
from **THE CHURCH.** However, there are problems with this one
too, and they start with the definition of the church.  We see it defined in 1 Corinthians
1:1-2.  Notice the parallel address
here.  Paul says that he’s writing both
to the church in Corinth and to those who have been sanctified in Christ
Jesus.  Those aren’t different
things.  Instead, grammatically, they’re
the same thing.  The church in Corinth is
those who have been sanctified in Christ Jesus. 
Biblically speaking, a church is not a building or an institution.  A church is an assembly of people.

This is important, because it tells us that if we are
looking to learn what pleases God from the church, we’re learning from other
people again, and we’ve already seen the problems with that one.  Just as people by themselves can be wrong
about religion, groups of people in churches can be wrong too, and judging by
the diversity of practice, most of them are.  Before we decide to go along with the crowd
and adopt the religious practices of the majority, we must remember that the
way is broad that leads to destruction. 
People, even lots of people, don’t make up a standard.

Instead, we are best served by learning what pleases God
from **THE WORD.** John makes this connection in 1 John
3:21-22.  Simply put, what pleases God is
doing the commandments of God, and we learn what the commandments of God are by
turning to the inspired Scripture.

We can feel confident in this course of action for two
reasons.  The first is that the Bible is
a trustworthy guide to the will of God. 
People can be wrong, but the word of God is infallible.  Despite countless challenges, it has stood
firm for 2000 years, and it is filled with internal evidences that prove that
it cannot be merely the work of human wisdom. 
We can trust it.

Second, we can rely on the word because of God’s faithful
and unchanging character.  People aren’t
like that.  We can change and do
change.  When I was in law school, I
didn’t vary my diet much.  Instead, I
would eat one thing at a time until I got sick of it.  I’d eat Chinese takeout every day for three
months, then switch to frozen pizza, then switch to ramen, and so on.  My tastes were not constant.  I got fed up with stuff.  God, however, is constant.  He is as He has been, He is as He will
be.  As a result, despite the age of the
Bible, it is still a faithful guide to pleasing God.

**How Jesus Did It**

In our search for how to please God, our example and guide
must be Jesus.  He perfectly succeeded in
honoring His Father, and He means for us to walk in His footsteps.  For us, this walk must begin with **FULFILLING THE LAW.** Jesus describes His purpose in this
regard in Matthew 5:17-18.  As is true in
our time, there were people in the time of Jesus who were confused about what
constituted the law of God.  They mistook
the traditions of the Pharisees for the Law of Moses, so when Jesus taught
against those traditions, they thought He was critiquing the Law too.  In reality, of course, Jesus meant to strip
away the traditions, which did more harm than good, so that the Jews could return
to following God as He intended.

Today, we are not under the same law.  The Law of Moses was nailed to the cross with
Christ, and it has been replaced by the law of Christ.  However, our attitude toward His law must be
the same as His attitude toward the law He was under.  This requires us first of all to strive to
obey it.  None of us will succeed
perfectly, but every one of us must make the effort.

Similarly, we must look to fulfill the law and only the law
by recognizing human tradition for what it is. 
As any group will, the churches of Christ have accumulated a body of
tradition about worship and other things. 
Some of those traditions may be helpful. 
Some may be harmful.  However, we
must never make the mistake of confusing the tradition of men for the law of
God.

Second, we see Jesus committed to **SEEKING GOD’S GLORY.** Here,
look at His words in John 7:18.  This is
only one of many passages I could have used. 
One of the striking features of the entire gospel of John is its concern
with the glory of God and Jesus.  Here,
we see Jesus informing the Jews that the goal of His entire existence is to
seek the glory of God, and that as a result of this goal, He lives a life that
is free from unrighteousness.

This is a critical point for us to understand.  Especially if we were raised in the church,
it’s easy for us to lapse into a because-God-says-so approach to
obedience.  This is what we do
just-because.  However, if we want to
develop spiritual maturity and avoid apostasy, we must keep in mind not only
what God asks of us, but the aims that He wants to achieve through our
obedience.

If we fail in this, we will probably fall into checklist
Christianity.  The Pharisees were great
at checklists.  They had checklists for
tithing the herbs in their gardens. 
However, because they forgot to seek God’s glory, soon they lost focus
on the most important whats too.  Rather
than pursuing the justice and mercy and faithfulness that would bring honor to
God’s name, they turned the Law itself into a vehicle for greed and oppression
of the poor.

The only way we can avoid their problem is by fixing our
eyes on the goal.  We must constantly
ask, “Will this action glorify God?”  If
it won’t, it’s time for us to rethink our views.

Finally, Jesus sought to please God by **LAYING DOWN HIS LIFE.** He’s
quite plain about this in John 10:17-18. 
Jesus knew very well what God’s expectations were for Him, and He
responded to those expectations by affirmatively acting to lay down His life
for others.

The same thing is true for us.  If we want to please God, we cannot regard our
lives as being our own.  Every day that
we roll out of bed in the morning isn’t our day.  It’s God’s day, to be used according to His
will.  The money that we earn isn’t our
money.  It’s God’s money, and when He
asks us to use it in some way, we don’t have the right to say no.  Our possessions don’t belong to us
either.  They belong to Him, and they are
to be used for His glory.  Like Jesus, we
need to act on God’s initiative, not ours. 
If we seek to do less, we are not His disciples.

Momentarily, we are going to partake of the Lord’s
Supper.  Before we do, though, there are
two things that I’d like for us to think about. 
The first is the perfect humility and submission of Jesus.  As the hymn “And Can It Be?” says, He emptied
Himself of everything but love.  If He
had it and could give it up, He gave it up for God, all the way from heaven to
the cross.  That’s astonishing, and it’s
our obligation to remember His sacrifice now.

Second, let’s consider ourselves.  Where do we stand when it comes to
humility?  Where do we stand when it comes
to submission?  Either God’s will and the
desire to please Him shine forth from our lives, or they don’t.  Let’s consider ourselves as we partake.

**The God-Pleasing
Church**

In the final part of our lesson this morning, let’s consider
what a God-pleasing church looks like. 
First, I believe that such a church **ACCEPTS
CHRIST’S HEADSHIP.** Let’s look here
at Ephesians 1:22-23.  I think the
language here is particularly important. 
Notice that Paul says that God gave Christ as head over *all things* to the church.  That is an exclusive grant of authority.  Like the saying goes:  my house, my rules.  The church is Christ’s house, so it needs to
be Christ’s rules.

Sadly, this is not the way that most churches actually
operate.  They claim to belong to Jesus,
but they don’t act like it.  When they
make a decision, their primary consideration is not what Christ has instructed
through His word.  Instead, they do as
seems best to them and work to please themselves.  Want a gym on the church property, paid for
with church funds?  Sure!  Why not! 
Never mind that there’s not a whisper in the Bible that indicates that
God would be pleased with such a thing. 
As long as they like it, that’s good enough for them, and they go ahead
and do it.

In the face of such behavior, I can only quote Jesus in Luke
6, where He asks, “Why do you call me ‘Lord, Lord!’ and do not do what I
say?”  The way I see it, friends, there’s
not much point in ignoring the will of Jesus but keeping on calling Him
Lord.  If we’re going to do that, we
might as well stop calling ourselves the church of Christ and start calling
ourselves the church of us.

As a result of this, it is also true that a God-pleasing
church **FOLLOWS THE PATTERN.** We see this pattern mentioned in
Philippians 3:17.  Paul is pretty plain
here.  He wants the Philippians, and by
extension us, to consider the behavior of Paul and other righteous Christians
and take it for their pattern.  If we do
what they did, we will do well.

This is true of our actions both individually and
corporately.  When we consider the
witness of the entire New Testament, we learn quite a bit about how the
first-century church functioned.  Early
Christians assembled on the first day of the week.  They sang psalms, hymns, and spiritual
songs.  They prayed together.  They studied the Scriptures.  They shared in the Lord’s Supper.  They contributed to the Lord’s work.  With that money, they supported preachers and
other workers for the Lord and helped needy Christians.  Whenever possible, they were led by elders
and deacons.

Basically, the picture of the primitive church is a simple
one.  Nobody much is going to deny that
early Christians did these things, and the Scripture is clear that they pleased
God by doing them.  The question before
us is whether we are going to do the same. 
It takes great restraint to faithfully follow the pattern without
attempting to improve it, but such faithfulness still pleases God.

Similarly, a God-pleasing church **REJECTS HUMAN WISDOM.** We see
this rejection in 1 Corinthians 1:18-21. 
Today, when we see the elite of our society growing increasingly
contemptuous of Christianity and the Bible, we might think that this is some
new thing.  The reality, of course, is
quite different.  Even in Corinth 2000
years ago, those who were wise and powerful in earthly terms rejected the
gospel.  They thought it was a bunch of
nonsense.  The devil hasn’t changed his
stripes in the past 2000 years, and he still wants to use these wise fools to
overawe us.

Of course, there are just as many wise fools who claim to be
Christians as there are who openly oppose the name of Christ.  They tell us that we can’t possibly survive
as a church unless we start abandoning the pattern in favor of human
wisdom.  We have to tone down the Bible’s
teaching on sexuality because it offends the young people.  We need to add interest to our services by
bringing in a praise band.  If we had a
coffee shop on the premises, we’d attract the unchurched.

Some of these ideas might sound absurd to us, while others
might be appealing.  Really, though,
whether we like them or not doesn’t matter. 
The real question is whether we would rather follow our own wisdom or
trust in Jesus.  He’s the One who handed down
the pattern we follow.  He has told us
that the gates of hell will not prevail against His church.  Either we trust Him enough to obey His will,
or we don’t, and if we don’t trust Jesus, then all of us might as well go home!

Finally, the God-pleasing church **PRESSES ON.** For an example
of this spirit, let’s go back to Philippians 3 and look at 3:12-14.  I’d imagine that not too many of us are
seriously tempted to advocate for an in-house coffee shop, although that might
depend on how hard your Sunday morning has been!  However, here I think we encounter a far
commoner temptation, and that is the temptation to stay where we are
spiritually.  We’re a congregation with a
lot of nice people in it, and we do the right things on Sunday, so let’s be
content with that and stick to the status quo.

Brethren, Christ does not call us to stick to the status
quo.  I’ve said it before, and I’ll say
it again:  in this church, right now, the
Restoration is still a work in progress. 
Neither as individuals nor as a church do we completely fulfill the
first-century pattern.  We fulfill some
parts of it, but we dare not rest on our laurels while so much work remains to
be done.

For instance, we know from Scripture what the first-century
church did when it assembled.  We do
those things, and we should do them. 
However, we also know from Scripture that the first-century church was
evangelistically dynamic.  Its members
went everywhere preaching the word.  At
times, great numbers were added to the faith every day.

Those things are part of the pattern too.  Are they part of the pattern that we are
following?  Can we honestly claim that we
ourselves are deeply involved in rescuing the lost?  I’m glad that there are dozens of saved souls
in here, but there are hundreds of thousands of lost souls out there, all
around us, every day.  Until those lost
souls eat at us the way they ate at Jesus, we’ve all got a lot of growing to
do, and until they’ve been brought to Him, we’ve all got a lot of work to do.
